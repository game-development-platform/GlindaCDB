## Why it belongs in this category

- **Spirulina as a whole is farmed** in raceway ponds [2], so the
  `spirulina` cropID stays a farmed crop.
- **Dihé is the exception.** In Chad it is gathered from wild alkaline
  pools under customary rules, not farmed [3]. It is a wild-managed
  supply route for the same organism.

## Fields a wild-managed template would need, with dihé values

- **Harvest rights / tenure:** Customary. Only Kanembu women harvest;
  men are banned from the water, in the belief they would make it barren
  [3]. The method passes from mother to daughter [3]. How pools are
  allocated between women or villages: Not sourced.
- **Social status of harvesters:** Mostly poor women of a low-status
  "blacksmith" group, according to FAO via secondary reports [3] (low
  weight).
- **Supervision:** An elderly woman supervises each harvest [3].
- **Season window:** Brackish pools form at the end of the rainy season
  [3]; daily harvesting starts early in the morning [3].
- **Harvest method:** Scoop floating algae into jars or baskets; filter on
  cloth, sieves or sand [3].
- **Yield per worker:** About 4–8 kg a day per woman [3].
- **Post-harvest:** Sun-dry on sand into thin cakes, cut into squares,
  finish drying on mats in the villages [3].
- **Market:** Family food and local sale; traders carry it to Massakori,
  N'Djamena and across the border [3].
- **Sustainable ceiling:** The FAO project coordinator estimated output
  could rise tenfold without harming the pools [3] (an estimate, not a
  measured limit).
- **Development and quality rules:** An FAO/EU project (from 2007) added
  hygiene, packaging and marketing; FAO said testing and certification
  were needed before export [3].
- **Climate pressure:** Lake Chad has shrunk about 90% since the 1960s
  [3].

## Template questions this case raises

- Rights held by a gender or social group, with taboos as enforcement,
  rather than licences or quotas.
- The same organism as both a farmed crop and a wild-managed resource,
  depending on route; the template may need to attach to a supply route,
  not only to a species.
- A quality or certification gate before a wild product can enter export
  markets.
