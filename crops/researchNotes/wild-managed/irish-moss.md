## Why it belongs in this category

- **Supply has been wild.** Canada's industry, the world's largest from
  about 1950 to 1970, ran on raked beds and storm-cast weed [1][2].
- **Farming lost on cost.** Tank culture reached 8 ha in Nova Scotia, but
  cost more than wild harvest for carrageenan. It survives only as a
  premium edible product [2][4]. Like dulse, it is a mixed case.

## Fields a wild-managed template would need, with Irish moss values

- **Harvest rights / tenure (Canada):**
  - Atlantic Coast Marine Plant Regulations (1978), under the Fisheries
    Act [2].
  - Harvest organised by Marine Plant Harvesting Districts [2].
  - Limited entry tried in 1978: licences went to everyone with two years'
    harvest evidence, not matched to stock size [2].
  - Fees $45 a year (1975–1995), $200 from 1996, when licences became
    limited and non-transferable [2].
- **Gear rules:**
  - Nova Scotia: 5 mm minimum rake-tine spacing; drag-raking limited to
    those who used it before 1977 [2].
  - Suction harvesting banned in Canada in the 1990s [8].
  - Drag rakes redesigned to kill fewer lobsters and pull up fewer
    holdfasts [1][2].
- **Season window:** Mainly May–July on PEI [2]; seasonal closures in some
  Nova Scotia districts [2]. Summer harvest recovers in 5–6 months, winter
  harvest much more slowly [8].
- **Harvest methods:**
  - Hand rakes (4–5 m) from small boats around low tide [2].
  - Drag rakes towed by "haulers" (three rakes a side) [2].
  - "Winchers": larger boats hauling three rakes while three drag,
    cropping continuously [2].
  - Storm-cast gathering from beaches, on PEI with horse-drawn rakes
    [2][1].
- **Harvest limits / good practice:** Québec guidance: at most 33% of a
  species' biomass per site [2]. Avoid harvesting *Mastocarpus* by mistake
  [2].
- **Effort and intensity:** About 100 licensees over about 875 ha in
  western PEI, averaging 22.8 drag-rake passes per hectare per season [2].
  Winching yielded 20.1 ± 9.8 kg an hour in trials [2].
- **Workforce:** About 2,000 harvesters from 47 communities in the
  Maritimes around 1990 [2]. In Nova Scotia, 800 in 1980 fell to 225 in
  1995 [2]. About 500 western-PEI families were involved in storm-cast
  gathering, drying and selling [2].
- **Post-harvest:** Dry at once to prevent spoilage; sun-dried, later
  drum-dried, with wet crop bought at the dock from 1967 [2].
- **Market dependence:** Collapsed when farmed tropical eucheumatoids took
  the carrageenan market [2]. Buyers rejected moss mixed with too much
  *Furcellaria* [2].
- **Regeneration:** Perennial, 2–6 years, with a long-lived basal crust
  that regrows fronds [8]. Spores settle within about 10 m [8]. Harvested
  beds showed reduced reproductive capacity and shorter fronds [8].
- **Stock shifts and threats:** *Furcellaria* takeover on PEI [2];
  *Grateloupia turuturu* competition and warming [5]; green crab at Basin
  Head [7].
- **Protected stock:** The Basin Head strain is protected in a Marine
  Protected Area (2005) [7].

## Template questions this case raises

- Storm-cast gathering is a harvest from the beach, not the bed: does it
  need separate rights from raking?
- Gear rules (tine spacing, drag-rake grandfathering, suction ban) are a
  distinct field from quotas and seasons.
- Bycatch of another valuable fishery (lobster) as a management cost.
- A protected local strain inside an otherwise harvested species.
