# Schema exceptions: interactions with other crops, hazards and rules

Part of the schema exceptions log. Rules, statuses and the full index are in
[`../schema-exceptions.md`](../schema-exceptions.md).

Entries marked "imported" came from earlier private notes kept before this log existed. Their **Seen in**
lists name crops, not checked Glinda docs: the facts have not yet been re-checked against the crop's
Glinda doc, and some of those crops have no Glinda doc yet. When a research run confirms one, replace the
plain name with the cropID, doc and Finding.

## 53 — Crop that hosts another crop's disease

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Invasiveness / biosecurity" covers the crop spreading. It doesn't cover a crop
  that is an alternate host or reservoir for a disease of *other* plants, with setback distances, bans or
  certified-stock rules as a result, or a crop harmed through a host plant growing nearby.
- **Seen in (imported):** blackcurrant, red currant, golden currant, gooseberry (white pine blister rust;
  setbacks around 300 m and state bans); Meyer lemon (tristeza reservoir; certified stock required);
  quince (rust carried by nearby juniper).
- **Possible home:** a "Host for other crops' diseases" line under "Invasiveness / biosecurity".

## 54 — Disease shared with neighbouring or nursery crops

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** diseases are recorded for the crop alone. Some are shared with a particular
  neighbour or nursery companion, which sets where the crop can sit.
- **Seen in (imported):** rutabaga (Phoma dry rot; keep away from oilseed rape); rosemary and lavender
  (one *Phytophthora* in container nurseries); peppermint, cornmint and oregano (the same mint rust).
- **Possible home:** a "Shared diseases" line naming the other crops.

## 55 — Disease that decides where a crop can be grown

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Climate zones" reads as purely climatic. In some regions a disease, not climate,
  rules a crop out, or a disease is triggered by a specific weather pattern.
- **Seen in (imported):** grape (Pierce's disease makes European grapes uneconomic in the US Southeast,
  where muscadine is resistant); low-chill pear (fire blight forecast from warmth and wetting during
  bloom); taro (leaf blight destroyed Samoa's crop in 1993; recovery by breeding).
- **Possible home:** a "Disease limits on range" note under "Climate zones" or "Weather response".

## 56 — Crop that persists as a weed of the next crop

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Invasiveness" covers escape into the wild. It doesn't cover tubers or corms left
  in the field that cut the yield of the following crop, or a crop that becomes a resistant weed of its
  rotation partner.
- **Seen in (imported):** Jerusalem artichoke (left tubers cut the next maize crop by 81% and sugar beet
  by 91%; 2-3 years of control); arrowhead (volunteers in rice, with herbicide-resistant strains);
  horseradish (left roots spread); tacca.
- **Possible home:** a "Volunteer persistence" line under "Invasiveness / biosecurity" (effect on the
  next crop, years to clear).

## 57 — Rotation, fallow and effects on the next crop

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Soil biology" covers replant disease. Nothing records required fallow, rotation
  intervals, nitrogen or soil-structure benefits passed to the next crop, or a crop grown only to be
  ploughed in.
- **Seen in (imported):** maca (long fallow, historically 10-30 years); Andean potato (communal fallow,
  about 3 years cropped to 9 fallow); scorzonera (a non-host rotation cuts root-knot nematodes by 80%);
  konjac, celery; field pea and jicama (nitrogen left for the next crop; jicama fixes 175-199 kg N/ha but
  nets far less after root export); salsify (a cover crop ploughed in first raised yield about 45%);
  radish (deep-rooted "tillage" types used to break compacted subsoil); vetch, cowpea, field pea, faba
  bean, lupin, winter rye, oats, buckwheat, sorghum-sudangrass, sunn hemp, annual ryegrass (cover crops
  or green manures).
- **Possible home:** a "Rotation" bullet: fallow or interval needed, effect on the next crop, cover-crop
  use.

## 58 — Intercropping, relay cropping and agroforestry

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** the template describes a crop alone in its field.
- **Seen in (imported):** maize and pigeon pea (widely intercropped); grass pea (sown into a standing rice
  crop, *utera*); konjac, arrowroot, elephant foot yam, tacca (under trees); coyo (shade tree in coffee);
  grape (religious rules on what may be sown in a vineyard, see entry 65).
- **Possible home:** an "Intercropping" line under "Establishment" (partners, arrangement, shade).

## 59 — Effects on the land and neighbours beyond the crop

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** nothing records services or harms the crop causes beyond its own yield.
- **Seen in (imported):** moth bean (binds sand); Kernza (perennial roots cut nitrate leaching); jujube
  (windbreak rows protect neighbouring fields; its nectar harms managed bees); lotus root (paddy runoff of
  nitrogen, phosphorus and organic load; heavy-metal uptake used for clean-up); American lotus (can take
  over a pond and harm fishing and boating).
- **Possible home:** an "Environmental effects" line (services and harms).

## 60 — Crop that supports another farm enterprise

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Uses and processing" lists products of the crop itself. Some crops matter as
  forage or hosts for another enterprise.
- **Seen in (imported):** red and white clover (beekeepers place hives for clover honey); Indian jujube
  (host for lac insects; pruning is timed to inoculation).
- **Possible home:** a note under "Uses and processing".

## 61 — Hazards outside the toxin, allergen and ergot classes

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Hazards" is built around toxins, allergens and ergot in the eaten product.
  Other hazard kinds have no obvious slot.
- **Seen in (imported):**
  - food-drug interactions: grapefruit; cranberry (warfarin, disputed); durian with alcohol;
  - microbes on the rind: cantaloupe;
  - worker hazards: thorns (date palm, pickers of thorny fruit); dust from seed hairs (canary seed);
    contact irritation from raw sap (taro, cocoyam, velvet bean); phototoxic sap (parsnip, celeriac);
    farm chemicals (potassium chlorate in longan, hydrogen cyanamide); smoke from burning prunings (mango);
  - plant safe but its concentrated oil hazardous, with different audiences: rosemary (pets), sage
    (people), thyme (both);
  - hazard in what the crop is served with: coriander (onion and garlic in the dish);
  - adulteration: star anise (toxic Japanese star anise in the supply chain); maca (wheat flour,
    sildenafil);
  - sprout products: radish (pathogens in sprouted seed).
- **Possible home:** widen "Hazards" with these classes: interaction, contamination, worker, concentrate,
  adulteration.

## 62 — Contamination taken up from the growing water or soil

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** hazards are recorded as properties of the crop. These come from the site.
- **Seen in (imported):** Chinese water chestnut, lotus root, water spinach, watercress (fluke cysts from
  the water; pigs and manure close the loop); water spinach (heavy metals); mustard greens and brown
  mustard (cadmium uptake); arrowroot (lead arsenate left in the soil from past spraying).
- **Possible home:** a "Site contamination" line under "Hazards" or "Soil".

## 63 — Growing or storage conditions that raise a toxin

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Hazards" gives a toxin's presence. It doesn't give the conditions that raise it.
- **Seen in (imported):** potato (light raises glycoalkaloids from 12-20 to 250-280 mg/kg); cassava
  (drought raises cyanogens); taioba (sun and heat raise oxalate; also higher in the dry season);
  celeriac (pink rot raises psoralens); sweet potato (mouldy roots form a lung toxin).
- **Possible home:** a "Raised by" field on each hazard.

## 64 — Legal limits on the product or the harvest

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Invasiveness / biosecurity" covers biosecurity rules, and entry 4 covers
  licences and IP. Other legal limits apply to the product or the harvest: content limits, pre-harvest
  intervals, quarantine movement, protected-origin caps, collecting permits and export bans.
- **Seen in (imported):** mustard oil (erucic-acid limits); head mustard (fertilizer and pesticide
  intervals before harvest); wild rocket (EU leaf nitrate limit); makrut lime (leaf movement under a
  citrus-greening quarantine); bergamot (protected-origin yield cap of 40 t/ha); maypop (pharmacopoeia
  marker limit); coontie (wild-digging permit); American lotus (collecting limits where it is endangered or
  threatened); maca (export ban on propagation material); arrowhead (a related species is a US federal
  noxious weed; corms move only under permit).
- **Possible home:** a "Legal limits" line, separate from biosecurity and IP.

## 65 — Religious and cultural rules and uses

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** the template has no place for religious or cultural requirements on growing or
  using the crop, or ritual products.
- **Seen in (imported):** citron (etrog, with rules on grafting and quality); fruit trees generally (no
  fruit in the first three years; no grafting between species, such as apple or pear on quince); grape
  (rules on what may be sown in a vineyard); date palm (closed fronds for ritual use); horseradish
  (Passover bitter herb); ahipa (a ritual cup carved from the root); radish (oversized roots carved for a
  December festival in Oaxaca); yam (no new yams eaten before the festival).
- **Possible home:** a "Religious and cultural use" line under "Uses and processing", with any production
  rules noted under the bullet they affect.

## 66 — Quality tied to a named place

- **Status:** OPEN (imported 2026-11-24).
- **What doesn't fit:** "Climate zones" describes where a crop grows, not a protected origin whose
  product commands a price only from that place.
- **Seen in (imported):** bergamot (protected origin; microclimate); arrowhead (a geographic indication,
  with intact sprouts as a grade).
- **Possible home:** a "Protected origin" note under "Uses and processing".
