# Chestnut (*Castanea mollissima*)

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Castanea mollissima* (Chinese chestnut), the species
behind the large majority of current global production by volume (China
alone is credited with roughly 73-90% of world tonnage depending on
source [1][2]) and the blight-resistant backbone of essentially all
modern US orchard plantings since *Cryphonectria parasitica* (chestnut
blight) eliminated American chestnut as a nut crop in the early 20th
century [3][4].

*Castanea sativa* (European/sweet chestnut — the species behind Italian,
Spanish, Portuguese, and Turkish "marrone"-type production and most
European culinary tradition) is modeled as a cultivar/species-group delta
within this same file, not a separate crop: the two species hybridize
freely, share essentially the same orchard system, pollination biology,
product form, and postharvest handling, and most commercial US cultivars
today are *C. mollissima* × *C. sativa* hybrids grafted onto Chinese
rootstock [5][6][7].

**American chestnut** (*Castanea dentata*) is explicitly **not** covered
here. It's functionally extinct as a nut crop — virtually eliminated from
the eastern US canopy by chestnut blight starting in 1904, now surviving
mostly as blight-suppressed root sprouts — and all current work on it
(backcross breeding, transgenic blight-tolerance trials) is
forest-restoration research, not commercial nut production [3][4]. It's a
possible future research topic in its own right (a genuinely interesting
ecological/historical story distinct from commercial production), but
isn't covered as part of this crop. Japanese chestnut (*C. crenata*) is
mentioned only where it clarifies a genome or disease-resistance
comparison; it isn't separately modeled.

*Castanea* (Fagaceae) is unrelated to walnut's *Juglans*, almond's
*Prunus*, or hazelnut's *Corylus*.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — self-incompatibility works differently again here — many
trees are simply male-sterile, not just self-incompatible.** European
chestnut is widely described as self-incompatible, so cross-pollination
is required to set nuts [8]. But the more specific and more interesting
finding is that a large fraction of trees are male-sterile (produce
catkins with no viable pollen) rather than merely self-incompatible with
viable pollen, and a 2024 study found that even on male-fertile trees,
roughly 90% of the pollen reaching female flowers is self-pollen, yet
self-fertilized fruits make up under 1% of the total — most self-pollen
is wasted on ovules it cannot fertilize, an "ovule usurpation" effect
that actively reduces yield by tying up ovules that cross-pollen could
have fertilized [9][10]. This means male-sterile pollenizer trees are
deliberately favored in modern orchard design — the opposite of walnut's
and almond's pattern (both need fertile pollenizer cultivars) — chestnut
benefits from less of its own pollen reaching its flowers, not more of a
compatible neighbor's. Cultivar identity, not just cultivar count,
strongly affects pollination success [11][12].

**Finding 2 — yield figures are the least nationally standardized of any
nut crop researched so far for this project — no official national
yield series exists at all.** Unlike walnut and almond, no official
chestnut-specific production/yield series was found; one national census
counts farms and acreage (1,587 farms, >4,200 acres per a 2017 count) but
not yield [13]. Extension budget tools instead give a spread: one
cost-of-production model assumes 2,500 lb/acre (~2,800 kg/ha) at full
production (year 15) in one version and 3,500 lb/acre (~3,920 kg/ha) for
grafted French-style orchards in another [13]; a university extension
source gives 1,000-1,500+ lb/acre (~1,120-1,680 kg/ha) at maturity for
improved cultivars [13]; a grower-conference presentation gives ~1.5
tons/acre (~3,360 kg/ha) at ~20-year maturity, with commercially viable
yields starting around year 7-10 [13]; and a regional source splits by
type — low-intensity orchards with 12+ year-old trees average ~1,200
lb/acre (~1,345 kg/ha) for Chinese chestnut and ~2,000 lb/acre (~2,240
kg/ha) for European hybrids, rising to ~6,000 lb/acre (~6,720 kg/ha)
under high-intensity management [13]. See Estimates, below, for a
reconciliation onto one kg/ha scale; no single figure is treated as
authoritative given the complete absence of an official series.

**Finding 3 — genome size and chromosome number are well-converged,
continuing walnut's and hazelnut's own pattern rather than almond's
unsettled one.** *Castanea* is diploid at 2n=2x=24 across the genus [14].
The first chromosome-level *C. sativa* assembly (cultivar 'Marrone di
Chiusa Pesio') gives haplotype totals of 715.8 and 713.0 Mb across 12
chromosomes (the haploid count, consistent with 2n=24); a k-mer-based
estimate from the same sequencing data gives ~654 Mb [15]. The related
Japanese chestnut (*C. crenata*) gives a similar k-mer estimate (~665.9
Mb) and chromosome-level assembly (~683.8 Mb) [15] — a tight,
cross-species-consistent range, in contrast to almond's own
three-independent-assembly spread.

**Finding 4 — grafting Chinese chestnut is reported as genuinely,
specifically difficult — not just routine orchard practice as it is for
walnut, almond, or hazelnut.** Grafting failure of Chinese chestnut is
common enough that a dedicated study investigated whether phloem fiber
structure in the rootstock stem is the mechanical cause [16]. Commercial
nurseries use chip-budding or whip-and-tongue grafting, with splice
grafting considered most satisfactory; the recommended graft timing is
scion collection in February with grafting in mid-April, onto seedling
rootstock at about the four-leaf stage [16][17]. An Italian trial
separately found that grafted *C. mollissima* plants have more
difficulty surviving transplant than grafted European chestnut cultivars
under the same nursery conditions [16] — a species-specific propagation
weakness not seen in walnut's or almond's own rootstock story. Rootstock
choice itself is still an open compatibility question: one university
trial used four rootstock types (*C. dentata*, *C. mollissima*, and two
hybrid generations) specifically to screen for incompatibility, without
yet reporting a clear winner [16].

**Finding 5 — three serious, distinct threats are tracked together in
the literature (blight, ink disease, gall wasp), each with a different
control strategy and none controlled by a single robust method.**
(a) Chestnut blight (*Cryphonectria parasitica*) is the disease that
eliminated American chestnut; it persists on dead and living American
chestnut, chinquapins, and spreads to many other hosts including oak,
maple, hickory, beech, and tulip poplar, defeating quarantine/destruction
efforts because of abundant wind- and animal-dispersed spores [3][4].
Decades of backcross breeding (Chinese × American, repeatedly
backcrossed toward American) have produced trees with resistance
intermediate between susceptible American and resistant Chinese
chestnut, but a 2019 field trial found a later backcross generation's
resistance still fell short of Chinese chestnut's own at the
highest-blight-pressure site, and the plantings were judged too young to
confirm durable resistance [18]. (b) Ink disease (*Phytophthora
cinnamomi* and *P. cambivora*, the more aggressive of the two) is a
soil-borne root-rot problem that has limited chestnut culture in Europe
and North America for at least 200 years; no biological control agent
exists and chemical treatments haven't shown lasting efficacy, leaving
resistant planting material as the only durable strategy; irrigation
practice itself has been identified as a driver of outbreak severity in
at least one Greek study [19]. (c) The Asian chestnut gall wasp
(*Dryocosmus kuriphilus*), native to China and since spread to Korea,
Japan, North America, and Europe, is described as the single most
injurious insect pest of the genus — its larvae deform shoots and leaves
at budbreak, causing severe crop loss and in bad cases tree death; the
imported parasitoid wasp *Torymus sinensis* has proven highly effective
as biological control in at least one region of Italy, and gall-resistant
cultivar breeding is separately under way [20].

**Finding 6 — postharvest handling turns on a moisture-curing tradeoff
that is sharper and more failure-prone than any prior nut crop's own
postharvest story.** Fresh chestnuts fall near ~50% moisture and become
vulnerable to rot once moisture drops below roughly 35% [21] — unlike
walnut/almond/hazelnut, which are deliberately dried hard (to
single-digit-percent kernel moisture) specifically to prevent rancidity,
chestnuts must be kept moist enough to avoid this rot threshold while
still curing (a starch-to-sugar flavor conversion that improves eating
quality but makes the nut keep worse the more it progresses) [21].
Fungal rot — black rot, grey rot (*Botrytis cinerea*), and brown rot
(*Gnomoniopsis smithogilvyi*) — is the dominant storage loss, with one
Italian source reporting losses exceeding 50% in a bad (wet) year [22].
Traditional water curing (a multi-day soak, also used against weevil
larvae) can itself promote mold; a postharvest handbook instead
recommends a high-CO2 shock treatment followed by controlled-atmosphere
storage, which outperformed water curing on decay control in its own
trials, plus near-freezing, high-humidity cold storage as the long-term
baseline [21]. Weevil larvae develop inside the nut and exit through a
visible hole once the nut drops, giving growers a simple post-drop
screening check [21].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial tree.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest; nuts harvested after
  natural drop from the burr at full maturity.
- **Bloom timing:** Midsummer — late June to mid-July across several
  cultivar listings, notably later than walnut, almond, or hazelnut (all
  of which bloom in late winter/early spring) [24]. No sourced
  day-length or temperature-sum trigger for bloom onset.
- **Composition:** A low-fat, low-protein, carbohydrate-dominant nut
  (specific figures vary noticeably across aggregator sites citing the
  same nominal database entry; none of the sources checked linked
  directly to the primary database, so figures here aren't independently
  verified against it).
- **Pollination:** Cross-pollination required; self-incompatible in
  practice, but the dominant limiting factor is widespread male
  sterility (catkins with no viable pollen) rather than merely
  incompatible viable pollen [8][9]. Even where self-pollen is viable,
  ~90% of pollen reaching female flowers on a male-fertile tree is
  self-pollen, yet under 1% of fruit sets from self-fertilization — most
  is wasted on "ovule usurpation" that reduces total yield [10]. Orchard
  design therefore deliberately favors male-sterile pollenizer cultivars
  (the opposite pattern from walnut's/almond's own pollenizer
  requirements, both of which need fertile pollen from the neighbor
  cultivar) [9]. Cultivar identity, not just count, strongly affects
  pollination success [11][12]. No sourced wind-vs-insect pollination
  split or bee-benefit figure comparable to almond's/walnut's own
  studies.
- **Yield basis:** In-shell fresh weight, by extension-budget convention
  — no official yield-per-area series exists for chestnut. See
  Estimates, below.
- **Postharvest storage:** Sharp moisture-vs-rot tradeoff: fresh nuts
  near ~50% moisture, rot risk rising once moisture drops below ~35%
  [21]. Curing (starch-to-sugar conversion for flavor) trades off
  against keeping quality — more-cured nuts store worse [21].
  Traditional water-curing soak (also controls weevil) can itself
  promote mold; a recommended alternative is a high-CO2 shock treatment
  followed by controlled-atmosphere storage, which outperformed water
  curing on decay control [21]. Long-term storage baseline: near-freezing
  temperature at high humidity [21]. Dominant rot organisms: black rot,
  grey rot (*Botrytis cinerea*), brown rot (*Gnomoniopsis
  smithogilvyi*); losses above 50% reported in at least one wet Italian
  season [22].
- **Establishment:** Grafting Chinese chestnut is specifically
  difficult — failure is common enough to have motivated a dedicated
  study of rootstock phloem-fiber structure as a mechanical cause [16].
  Commercial nurseries use chip-budding, whip-and-tongue, or (considered
  most satisfactory) splice grafting; scions collected in February,
  grafted in mid-April onto seedling rootstock at about the four-leaf
  stage [16][17]. An Italian trial found grafted *C. mollissima* has more
  transplant-survival difficulty than grafted European cultivars under
  identical nursery conditions [16]. Rootstock-scion compatibility across
  *C. dentata*, *C. mollissima*, and F1/backcross hybrid rootstocks
  remains an open screening question in at least one university trial
  [16]. Commercially viable yield is reported to begin around year 7-10,
  full production around year 15-20 [13].
- **Site envelope / water use:** No sourced soil pH, salinity tolerance,
  or quantified drought-response figures. Irrigation practice is flagged
  as a specific ink-disease outbreak driver in at least one regional
  study, implying overwatering or poor drainage is a real risk factor,
  but no quantified threshold was sourced [19].
- **Stand life:** Not sourced; commercial orchards are known to remain
  productive for decades (cultivars like 'Marrone di Chiusa Pesio' and
  'Marron di Val di Susa' derive from traditional, long-lived European
  plantings), but no specific stand-life figure in years was located.
- **Labor / field operations:** Grafting (establishment, see above);
  post-drop harvest (chestnuts are harvested after natural drop from the
  burr, as with hazelnut and walnut, rather than picked); post-drop
  weevil-exit-hole screening [21]; curing/moisture management as a
  distinct, time-sensitive postharvest operation [21]. No labor-hours
  figures sourced for any of these operations.
- **Seed crop:** Not applicable as a dedicated commercial seed crop —
  chestnuts propagated commercially are grafted cultivars on seedling
  rootstock, not grown-from-seed true-to-type; seedling rootstock itself
  is grown from Chinese chestnut seed without named seed-crop
  infrastructure sourced [16][17]. No hybrid-production system was
  sourced — named hybrids like 'Colossal' are clonally grafted, not
  re-grown from seed.
- **Religious/cultural notes:** No sourced sect-specific production rule
  or off-world/controlled-environment growability note was found.
  Chestnuts do have a strong regional culinary-ritual role (notably
  Italian/French winter and Christmas-season roasting and marrons glacés
  traditions), but no sourced rule rises to the level of a production
  constraint.
- **Weather response:** Chill requirement reported as 300-750 hours
  below 45°F (7°C) depending on species/hybrid/cultivar, narrower for
  'Colossal' specifically at 400-500 hours [23]. Hardiness zone listings
  disagree across sources — general guidance gives USDA Zones 6-9, one
  hybrid cultivar is listed 4-9, another 4-8; Chinese chestnut is
  explicitly described as not hardy to Zone 5 but tolerant as far south
  as Zone 10 [24]. A cold-hardiness study found that seed sources from
  cold climate zones were themselves more cold-tolerant and less
  variable than sources from warm/moderate zones — a provenance effect,
  not purely a cultivar effect [24]. No dose-response curve sourced for
  any of these.
- **Environment envelope:** No sourced soil chemistry (micronutrient
  demand), soil biology (symbiont dependence — though *Phytophthora*
  root-rot susceptibility implies real sensitivity to soil
  drainage/moisture, not yet quantified), light (shade tolerance),
  atmosphere (CO2 response), enclosed-space, or spaceflight-trial data.
  Genome size is sourced (Finding 3): ~654-716 Mb depending on
  assembly/estimate method, 2n=24.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Single stage — chestnuts are harvested
  after natural drop from the burr at full maturity; no sourced
  immature/alternate-maturity harvest stage. Single window — no sourced
  evidence of multiple separate flowering/fruiting cohorts per year.
- **Cultivars (representative spread):**
  - 'Colossal' (*C. mollissima* × *C. sativa* hybrid, 20th-century
    California) — large nut, widely planted US commercial cultivar,
    chill requirement ~400-500 hours [23].
  - 'Marrone di Chiusa Pesio' (*C. sativa*, traditional Italian) — the
    source of the first chromosome-level *C. sativa* genome assembly;
    high-value "marrone" culinary type [15].
  - 'Marron di Val di Susa' (*C. sativa*, traditional Italian) — another
    named marrone-type cultivar in commercial nursery trade [23].
  - Chinese chestnut seedling populations (*C. mollissima*) — used
    directly as blight-resistant rootstock and, increasingly, as the
    dominant orchard species in its own right [16][17].
  - Various *C. mollissima* × *C. sativa* F1/backcross hybrids — bred
    specifically for blight resistance combined with European eating
    quality [6][7].
  - No sourced data on 'Auburn Homestead', 'Prolific', or other named US
    nursery cultivars beyond their chill/zone listings; eating-quality
    deltas between named cultivars not sourced.

## Hazards

- No crop-specific human or animal food-safety issue was sourced —
  chestnuts are a low-fat, low-protein, carbohydrate-dominant nut, with
  no toxin comparable to the stone-fruit-kernel cyanogenic-glycoside
  issue found in other nuts/fruits.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh in-burr nuts → cured/dried (moisture managed
  carefully, see Postharvest storage above) → in-shell or kernel; burr
  and shell are byproducts.
- **Not otherwise modeled:** chestnut flour and marrons glacés (candied
  chestnut) processing — real downstream food products, treated as
  consumer/kitchen-level processing outside this project's scope for
  now, consistent with how other confectionery processing is scoped
  elsewhere.
- No wild-gathering basis was sourced for either *C. mollissima* or
  *C. sativa* specifically — this is a firmly orchard-cultivated crop
  (distinct from the question of American chestnut's wild/relict status,
  which would belong to a possible future note on that species, not this
  one).

## Open research questions

- No official yield-per-area series exists for chestnut; the yield
  estimate below rests entirely on extension budget tools, which
  disagree by more than 5x between low and high ends.
- The species mix behind China's ~73-90% of global tonnage isn't
  confirmed at a species-tagged production-data level (inferred from a
  general review, not a direct species-tagged figure) [1][2].
- No sourced day-length/temperature-sum bloom trigger, despite chestnut's
  bloom timing (midsummer) being the latest of any nut crop researched
  so far.
- No sourced wind-vs-insect pollination split, nor a bee-pollination
  yield-benefit figure comparable to almond's or walnut's own studies.
- Rootstock-scion compatibility across *C. dentata* / *C. mollissima* /
  hybrid rootstocks is an open question in the one university trial
  found; no resolved recommendation.
- No sourced standard graft-success-rate figure for Chinese chestnut
  specifically, despite its being flagged as unusually difficult.
- No sourced soil pH, salinity tolerance, or drought-response
  quantification.
- No sourced stand-life-in-years figure for mature orchards.
- No sourced labor-hours-per-operation figures (grafting, harvest,
  curing).
- No environment-envelope data sourced beyond genome size (soil
  chemistry, light, atmosphere, enclosed-space, spaceflight trials).
- No religious/cultural-use production constraint sourced beyond the
  general observation of a strong regional culinary-ritual role that
  doesn't amount to a sourced production rule.
- American chestnut (*Castanea dentata*) is a strong candidate for its
  own future research entry, documenting the blight collapse and ongoing
  restoration breeding — distinct from this file's commercial-production
  focus.
- The Asian chestnut gall wasp's biological control agent, *Torymus
  sinensis*, and its demonstrated effectiveness in at least one region,
  is a potential precedent for modeling pest/biological-control
  interactions generally — worth keeping in mind for a future
  pest-system pass.
- Genome-size figures (654 Mb k-mer estimate vs. 713-716 Mb
  chromosome-level assembly) differ by roughly 9%; not reconciled into
  one number.
- Chill-hour and hardiness-zone ranges disagree across sources (300-750
  hours; Zones 4-10 depending on source and cultivar) and aren't
  reconciled; treated as genuine cultivar/provenance variation rather
  than a single crop-level figure.
- Nutrition figures (kcal, protein, carbohydrate per 100g) vary
  noticeably across aggregator sites citing the same nominal database
  entry; none of the sources checked linked directly to the primary
  database.
- No sourced multiplication-ratio or named seed-crop infrastructure for
  Chinese chestnut rootstock seed production (seed collected informally
  from orchard trees per the propagation sources, not a dedicated
  seed-crop operation).

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield range reconciled to kg/ha.** Inputs: two cost-model figures
(2,500 and 3,500 lb/acre) [13]; a university extension figure of
1,000-1,500+ lb/acre [13]; a conference-presentation figure of ~1.5
tons/acre (3,000 lb/acre) [13]; a regional source's 1,200 (Chinese) /
2,000 (European hybrid) / up to 6,000 (high-intensity) lb/acre [13].
Method: 1 lb/acre ≈ 1.1209 kg/ha (1 lb = 0.4536 kg, 1 acre = 0.4047 ha).
1,000-1,500 lb/acre → ~1,120-1,680 kg/ha; 1,200 lb/acre → ~1,345 kg/ha;
2,000 lb/acre → ~2,240 kg/ha; 2,500 lb/acre → ~2,800 kg/ha; 3,000
lb/acre → ~3,360 kg/ha; 3,500 lb/acre → ~3,920 kg/ha; 6,000 lb/acre →
~6,720 kg/ha. Plausible range for a mature, well-managed grafted
orchard: roughly 1,100-4,000 kg/ha, with 6,000+ lb/acre (~6,700 kg/ha)
representing an upper bound under high-intensity management rather than
a typical figure. A real official per-hectare yield series (none
currently exists for chestnut) would replace this estimate.
