# Hazelnut (*Corylus avellana*, European Hazel)

**Researched:** 2026-11-03

## Naming and scope

This covers the European hazel, *Corylus avellana* — the species behind
nearly all commercial hazelnut production worldwide (Turkey, Italy, the
US/Oregon, Azerbaijan, Chile, Georgia). **Turkish tree hazel** (*Corylus
colurna*), a distinct species, is addressed within this file only in its
role as a non-suckering rootstock for *C. avellana* cultivars — it isn't
treated as its own crop, since no commercial nut production from
*C. colurna* itself was found. No other *Corylus* species (e.g. American
hazelnut, *C. americana*, or the hybrid "hazelberts") is covered.

Genus *Corylus* (birch family, Betulaceae) is unrelated to walnut's
*Juglans* or almond's *Prunus*.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — hazelnut is wind-pollinated, monoecious, and
sporophytically self-incompatible — a genuinely different
incompatibility system from almond's gametophytic one — and dichogamy is
reported inconsistently across sources rather than as a clean universal
rule.** The species is monoecious, protandrous, and anemophilous
(wind-pollinated), with sporophytic self-incompatibility controlled by a
single S locus carrying multiple alleles — though partial or complete
self-compatibility has been observed in some cultivars [1]. This is a
structurally different incompatibility mechanism from almond's
gametophytic self-incompatibility. Dichogamy (lack of synchrony between
pollen release and stigma receptivity) is described as common but
explicitly "not universal," and one source notes it "can also be
influenced by local edaphic and climatic conditions" [1] — a notably less
settled picture than walnut's own dedicated 83-type dichogamy study; no
equivalent dedicated hazelnut study was found. Cross-pollination's
commercial importance is strongly sourced: a Turkish review reports
pollination can increase nut set and yield by up to 2.5 times, and at
least two pollinator (pollenizer) cultivars are recommended per orchard
[1]. Supplementary/artificial pollination has been shown to significantly
improve yield specifically under adverse climate conditions [1].

**Finding 2 — commercial yield figures are reported on two genuinely
different bases across sources (global averages vs. US survey-based
extension figures), kept explicitly separate rather than blended.** A
global-average 2017 figure gives 1,496 kg/ha, ranging from 2,461 kg/ha
(Armenia, maximum) down to 200 kg/ha (Ukraine, minimum), with Iran at
about 889 kg/ha [2]. Separately, a Chilean-cultivar-context source
reports 2,000-3,000 kg/ha, though the specific orchard conditions behind
that figure weren't clear from the available excerpt [2]. For Oregon
specifically — which produces nearly all US hazelnuts (99% of US yield,
concentrated in the Willamette Valley) [4] — a 2017 extension
economic-impact study, based on six IPM consultants' estimates covering
39% of Oregon's hazelnut acreage, reported an actual yield of 1,732
lb/acre against an estimated maximum attainable yield of 3,017 lb/acre
[3] — converting, 1,732 lb/acre ≈ 1,943 kg/ha actual against ≈ 3,382
kg/ha attainable (see Estimates, below). The same study attributed the
single largest biotic yield loss to disease, at 6.1% (186 lb/acre) [3].
A more recent, unverified secondary aggregator figure claims a 20% yield
increase to 1.32 tons/acre (in-shell) on 92,000 bearing acres, but this
couldn't be confirmed against an official production release and is
flagged explicitly as unverified [3].

**Finding 3 — the genome assembly is comparatively well-converged,
notably stronger than almond's unsettled figures.** Cytogenetic work
confirms *C. avellana* is diploid with 2n=22, with chromosomes falling
into large, medium, and small size groups [5]. Genome size estimates
cluster closely: earlier flow cytometry put the genome at about 378 Mb,
and a 2021 chromosome-scale assembly of the Turkish cultivar 'Tombul'
came to roughly 370 Mb, with its 11 pseudomolecules covering 97.8% of the
estimated genome size and 27,270 annotated high-confidence
protein-coding genes [5] — a tight, well-converged range, in contrast to
almond's own 227.6-300 Mbp spread.

**Finding 4 — propagation has shifted from layering to grafting, driven
specifically by a mechanization/sucker-management motive rather than a
nut-quality motive.** Hazelnuts readily produce suckers from basal buds,
and traditional propagation used simple layering, with tie-off layering
(a staple girdles the shoot to induce rooting above it, suckers harvested
after leaf fall begins, typically mid-November) as the refined
traditional method [6]. Modern commercial propagation has shifted toward
grafting, softwood cuttings, and micropropagation; critically, grafting
onto non-suckering rootstocks — open-pollinated or clonal *Corylus
colurna* (Turkish tree hazel, a distinct species) — produces sucker-free
plants specifically to enhance orchard management and enable
mechanization, not primarily for disease resistance or fruiting-quality
reasons as with walnut's Paradox rootstock or almond's peach-almond
hybrid rootstocks [6]. Propagation by cuttings alone has been reported
largely unsuccessful, and layering is described as difficult, which is
why nurseries lean on grafting despite hazelnut's notably thin bark
making cambium-matching harder (growers match the outside bark surface
instead) [6]. A 2024 Italian study directly compared micropropagated,
grafted, and layered plants of three cultivars in one orchard and found
no significant difference in yield performance or canopy volume among
the three propagation methods [6] — a genuinely different outcome from
walnut's own own-rooted-vs-grafted-Paradox surprise finding, where
propagation method did matter.

**Finding 5 — eastern filbert blight is this crop's single most
consequential disease, resistant specifically to one dominant gene whose
durability is already failing in part of its introduced range.** Eastern
filbert blight is caused by *Anisogramma anomala*, an endemic (North
American) fungal pathogen that mainly manifests as trunk cankers rather
than a storage or nut-quality issue [4]. It arrived in Oregon in the
early 1960s, and in Oregon/Washington appears to derive from a single
isolate [4]. Resistant cultivars developed in Oregon rely on one dominant
resistance gene sourced from the pollinizer cultivar 'Gasaway' [4] —
these cultivars remain resistant in the Willamette Valley, but this same
'Gasaway' resistance has already been overcome by different pathogen
isolates in New Jersey, flagging a genuine durability concern for a
single-gene resistance strategy [4]. A 2024 New Jersey trial of 82
Oregon-resistant cultivars and selections found that trees carrying
quantitative (polygenic) resistance may prove more stable across
different pathogen populations than the single-gene 'Gasaway' source [4]
— flagged here as the more durable breeding direction, though not yet the
dominant commercial approach. Separately, trunk cankers from several
other named fungal pathogens (*Diplodia mutilla*, *Dothiorella
omnivora*, *Valsa* cf. *eucalypti*, *Diaporthe eres*) are also reported in
US hazelnut production, distinct from eastern filbert blight itself [4].

**Finding 6 — postharvest rancidity is governed by moisture, oxygen,
light, and shelling status, with an unusually specific set of sourced
thresholds, and the mechanism (specific aldehyde markers) is more
precisely characterized here than for walnut or almond.** Hazelnut
kernel oil content is reported at about 60-68% of kernel weight
(unshelled nuts 50-62%) [7], making oxidative rancidity the dominant
spoilage route. After drying, kernel moisture is about 6% [7]. Storage
guidance: relative humidity should never exceed 70%, with an optimum
range of 55-65%; low temperatures (<10°C) are described as effective
specifically because they slow both rancidity development and
microbiological activity simultaneously [7]. A 1992 study found that
hexanal (from linoleic-acid oxidation) and octanal (from oleic-acid
oxidation) both increased more than tenfold during ambient-temperature
storage [7]. Low-oxygen packaging (under 1-2% O2) substantially reduces
rancidity, per a commercial packaging source [7]. Shelling itself
accelerates spoilage, and shelled kernels exposed to light go rancid
notably faster than unshelled nuts [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial shrub/small tree.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest; nuts drop free of the husk
  at maturity and are swept from the orchard floor [7].
- **Days to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Temperate, cool temperate, Mediterranean. No
  dedicated hardiness-zone, chill-hour, or soil-pH figure was sourced — a
  genuine gap relative to both walnut and almond, which both found (if
  inconsistent) figures for these fields.
- **Composition:** Raw kernel, per 100 g: about 628 kcal, 14.9 g protein,
  60.8 g fat, 16.7 g carbohydrate, 9.7 g fiber — sourced via aggregator
  sites reproducing USDA FoodData Central figures, not independently
  verified at the primary source; a separate dataset entry reports a
  noticeably different profile (13.5 g protein, 53.5 g fat, 26.5 g
  carbs), not reconciled [8].
- **Edibility:** Raw kernel, edible.
- **Pollination:** Required; wind-pollinated — sporophytically
  self-incompatible in most cultivars, with dichogamy common but not
  universal; at least two pollenizer cultivars recommended per orchard
  (Finding 1).
- **Yield:** Global average ~1,496 kg/ha (range 200-2,461 kg/ha by
  country, 2017 data) [2]; Oregon (nearly all US production) 2017 actual
  ~1,943 kg/ha against an estimated maximum attainable ~3,382 kg/ha (see
  Estimates, below; Finding 2). A separate Chilean-context figure of
  2,000-3,000 kg/ha wasn't reconciled with either. A more recent
  aggregator claim of a 20% yield jump to 1.32 t/acre (in-shell, ≈2,950
  kg/ha) is explicitly flagged unverified.
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Both layering (traditional, tie-off method from basal
  suckers) and grafting (increasingly dominant, specifically for
  sucker-free, mechanizable orchards via non-suckering *Corylus colurna*
  rootstock). A 2024 Italian trial found no significant yield or
  canopy-volume difference among micropropagated, grafted, and layered
  plants of the same cultivars (Finding 4).
- **Labor:** No labor-hour figure sourced for any operation. Harvest is
  by sweeping fallen nuts from the orchard floor (implied mechanized
  sweeping in modern Oregon production, not independently confirmed with
  a dedicated source). Tie-off layering is timed to mid-November, after
  leaf fall begins (Finding 4).
- **Soil:** No pH, drainage, or fertilization-rate figure was sourced — a
  genuine gap relative to both walnut and almond.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Moisture (~6% target), relative humidity
  (55-65%, never above 70%), low temperature (<10°C), low-oxygen
  packaging (<1-2% O2), and shelling/light exposure are all independently
  sourced quality drivers (Finding 6).
- **Establishment:** Grafted nursery trees (named cultivars on
  non-suckering *Corylus colurna* rootstock) are increasingly the
  dominant modern commercial planting material, though layered material
  remains viable per the 2024 Italian trial's null result (Finding 4). No
  orchard spacing/density figure sourced.
- **Cultivars:** 'Gasaway' is named specifically as the pollenizer source
  of the single dominant eastern-filbert-blight-resistance gene used in
  Oregon's resistant cultivars [4]. 'Tombul' is named as the Turkish
  reference cultivar used for the chromosome-scale genome assembly [5].
  'Barcelona' — the long-standing historical Oregon industry-standard
  cultivar — wasn't independently confirmed with a dedicated source
  despite being a well-known name in the literature generally; not
  asserted without a source. No systematic cultivar-comparison table
  beyond these scattered named mentions was sourced.
- **Weather response:** No frost-sensitivity, chill-hour, or
  dose-response figure was sourced — a genuine gap relative to both
  walnut and almond, which both found bloom-timing/frost-risk sourcing
  even where incomplete.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Single stage, single annual window —
  nuts harvested once as they drop free of the husk at maturity.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Light (growing plant):** Not independently sourced. Note the
  postharvest finding that light exposure accelerates rancidity in
  shelled kernels specifically (Finding 6) is a storage-phase finding,
  not a growing-phase light requirement, and isn't conflated with one
  here.
- **Genome size:** Comparatively well-converged across independent
  studies (~370-378 Mb, 2n=22; the 'Tombul' chromosome-scale assembly
  covers 97.8% of the estimated genome at ~370 Mb with 27,270 annotated
  genes) (Finding 3).
- **Controlled environment:** Not independently assessed. Wind
  pollination and the dichogamy/self-incompatibility combination would
  both need deliberate intervention (mechanical pollen transfer, multiple
  pollenizer timing windows) in any closed system. No spaceflight/
  closed-system trial data found.

## Hazards

- No animal-toxicity source was located for hazelnut kernel, husk, or
  shell — a third consecutive crop in this family with no equivalent
  finding to walnut's own carefully-scoped black-walnut toxicity listing.
- Eastern filbert blight (*Anisogramma anomala*) is the dominant disease
  — see Research findings, Finding 5, for the single-gene-resistance
  durability concern.
- Several additional named trunk-canker fungal pathogens are reported
  distinct from eastern filbert blight itself (Finding 5).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh in-husk nuts → dried (husk removed as a byproduct) →
  dried in-shell → cracked for kernel; shell is a byproduct.
- **Not otherwise modeled:** hazelnut paste/spread, flour, and
  confectionery processing (treated as downstream consumer/kitchen
  processing, outside this project's farm-to-raw-product scope for now).
- No current, historical, or plausible wild-gathering account specific
  to commercial *C. avellana* was sourced (distinct from the related,
  genuinely wild-gathered American hazelnut, *C. americana*, which this
  file doesn't address).

## Estimates and derivations

**Oregon yield, 1,943 kg/ha actual / 3,382 kg/ha attainable.** A 2017
extension economic-impact study reports 1,732 lb/acre actual and 3,017
lb/acre maximum attainable yield [3]. Converting: 1,732 lb = 785.5 kg; 1
acre = 0.4047 ha; 785.5 / 0.4047 = 1,942 kg/ha, rounded to 1,943. For the
attainable figure: 3,017 lb = 1,369 kg; 1,369 / 0.4047 = 3,382 kg/ha.
These are survey-based estimates from six IPM consultants covering 39% of
Oregon's bearing acreage in 2017, not an official production census —
treat as a reasonable planning range for intensively managed Pacific
Northwest orchards, not a universal commercial figure. The gap between
actual and attainable (about 42% of attainable yield lost) reflects the
combined effect of all named biotic and abiotic stresses in that study,
of which disease alone accounted for 6.1 percentage points [3].
