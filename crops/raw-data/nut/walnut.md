# Walnut (*Juglans regia*)

## Naming and scope

This covers the Persian/English walnut, *Juglans regia*, family
Juglandaceae — the species that dominates global commercial walnut
production (California, Chile, China, Europe) and the species meant by
"walnut" in nearly all culinary and retail contexts.

This is explicitly **not** Black walnut (*Juglans nigra*), a North
American species that is far more commonly wild-harvested than cultivated
in orchards, has a distinct and much stronger allelopathic (juglone)
reputation, a documented ASPCA toxicity listing (unlike *J. regia*, which
has no equivalent listing found in this pass), and a thick, hard-to-crack
shell that makes it a genuinely different product. Black walnut is a
strong candidate for its own future entry in this project, given its
extensive wild-harvest trade in the US Midwest, but isn't covered here. No
other *Juglans* species (butternut, heartnut) is covered either.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — wind-pollinated and monoecious, but dichogamous, making a
deliberate pollenizer cultivar a standard commercial requirement.** Walnut
bears both male (catkin) and female flowers on the same tree, pollinated
by wind, but the timing of pollen shedding from the male flowers
frequently doesn't overlap well with female-flower receptivity — a
condition called dichogamy [1]. Commercial guidance recommends planting
roughly one row of a pollenizer cultivar for every 8 rows of the main
cultivar, with the pollenizer row placed upwind [1]. A dedicated 1995
study of 83 walnut types over several years found dichogamy to be one of
the crop's important biological characteristics, but found no
statistically significant difference between protandrous (male-first) and
protogynous (female-first) types in average per-tree yield or nut
characteristics [1] (available only at abstract-level detail).

**Finding 2 — commercial yield is well-documented for California, while
smaller-scale and non-US sources give markedly lower, less certain
figures.** USDA reports California bearing-acre yield reaching 2.14
tons/acre in 2023 (the second-highest on record) against a record 2.19
tons/acre in 2016, with a longer-run rise from 1.50 tons/acre in 2007 to
1.87 tons/acre in 2017 [2] — converting the strongest recent figures gives
roughly 4,800 kg/ha (2023) and 4,900 kg/ha (2016 record) (see Estimates,
below). By contrast, permaculture-sourced planning figures (citing Martin
Crawford's work, not a peer-reviewed orchard trial) give much lower
figures for younger or non-intensive plantings: roughly 500 kg/ha for
trees aged 3-5 years, and for trees aged 10-15 years, about 5 t/ha, with a
stated maximum of about 7.5 t/ha [3] — treated here as a non-intensive
lower bound rather than a conflicting claim about the same production
system. A separate Indian cultivar trial reported only per-tree yields
(0.93-2.15 kg/tree across cultivars, with cultivar 'Paynee' superior
across several growth and yield traits) [1] — far too low to represent
mature commercial bearing and not reconciled with the California figures.
Standard commercial orchard spacing in France is reported as 100
stems/ha at 10m × 10m [3].

**Finding 3 — the reference genome is comparatively well-converged across
independent studies.** *J. regia* has a genome size of about 600 Mb with
n=16 chromosomes [4]. The 'Chandler' cultivar's chromosome-scale reference
assembly places 16 contiguous chromosomal scaffolds accounting for 95% of
the final genome, with an N50 scaffold size of 35 Mb — a substantial
improvement over an earlier, more fragmented assembly [4]. A
flow-cytometry study across six walnut varieties found 'Chandler' at
about 520.8-543.2 Mb (two separate measurements with overlapping error
ranges) and found no statistically significant difference in genome size
across the six varieties tested [4].

**Finding 4 — commercial rootstock practice centers on an infertile
interspecific hybrid.** The *J. microcarpa* × *J. regia* hybrid
("Paradox") is a widely used commercial rootstock in California walnut
production, valued for tolerance to soil-borne diseases; because the
hybrid itself is infertile, it must be propagated vegetatively rather
than from seed [4]. Counterintuitively, one orchard trial found
own-rooted 'Chandler' (not grafted at all) had significantly greater
trunk circumference, yield, and yield efficiency than grafted 'Chandler'
on Paradox rootstock in both years of a two-year trial [4] — a genuinely
surprising finding worth further confirmation, since it runs against the
usual assumption that specialized rootstocks improve commercial
performance, and the available sourcing didn't explain why own-rooted
trees outperformed in this particular trial.

**Finding 5 — oxidative rancidity, not a pest or disease, is the single
most serious postharvest problem.** A postharvest review calls oil
rancidity the most serious postharvest physiological disorder affecting
walnut quality, tied to poor seed-storage conditions including elevated
drying temperatures, cold storage at humidity levels that keep nut
moisture low, and reduced oxygen [5]. Specific mitigations: drying air
should stay below 43°C (110°F) to avoid rancidity-causing kernel damage
[5]; kernels are ideally stored at 2-3% water content in
vapor-impermeable packaging, since higher moisture both reduces storage
life and raises rancidity risk [5]; vacuum packaging (excluding
atmospheric oxygen) and storage below 1% O2 both extend shelf life [5];
and a cultivar comparison found storage under light at 21°C gave the
worst oxidative rancidity, while storage at 5°C in darkness showed no
trace of oxidative rancidity at all [5]. Fresh, unshelled nuts
specifically benefit from cold storage below 8°C to extend postharvest
availability, with cultivar-dependent variation [5]. Nuts are harvested
at full maturity (onset of hull split) [5].

**Finding 6 — two well-documented diseases, both bacterial/fungal rather
than insect-vectored, can cause severe, quantified outbreak losses.**
Walnut blight is caused by the bacterium *Xanthomonas arboricola* pv.
*juglandis*, favored by wet weather and controlled primarily with
copper-containing bactericides such as Bordeaux mixture [6]. Anthracnose
has both a sexual form (*Gnomonia leptostyla*) and an asexual form
(*Marssonina juglandis*) [6]. Documented outbreak severity is
substantial: a 2017 anthracnose outbreak caused by *Colletotrichum
siamense* severely damaged yield and quality in Shandong Province, China
[6], and a 2022 walnut blight outbreak in Guizhou, China reached 65-75%
disease incidence across a 0.8-hectare area [6].

**Finding 7 — juglone allelopathy and animal toxicity are both real,
well-documented phenomena, but the toxicity literature concerns the
different species Black walnut, and this doesn't automatically transfer
to *J. regia*.** Both *J. nigra* and *J. regia* are confirmed to produce
juglone-related compounds (hydrojuglone and its glucoside, oxidized to
toxic juglone) [7]; one study reports *J. regia* soil juglone content at
0.25-3.25 mM depending on season, though no matching figure for *J.
nigra* soil was found to allow a direct side-by-side comparison [7].
However, black walnut is explicitly described as "the most notorious of
allelopathic trees" and the preferred species for juglone research
generally [7] — this doesn't establish that *J. regia* carries an
equivalent allelopathic reputation, since the sourcing found concerns the
genus broadly or black walnut specifically, not a dedicated
*J. regia*-specific allelopathy severity claim. On animal toxicity: the
ASPCA's "Black Walnut" listing (toxic to dogs and horses, toxic principle
listed as "unknown") concerns *J. nigra* by name — no equivalent ASPCA
listing was found for *J. regia* specifically. Separately, a 1987 study
found horses developed a toxic reaction within 12 hours of exposure to an
extract of black walnut heartwood specifically, in which juglone itself
was not detected (juglone was found only in the bark and nuts) [8], and a
2021 study concluded juglone is not the trigger for equine laminitis,
with oral juglone dosing in ponies producing only inconsistent, mild
signs [8] — together these suggest the mechanism of black walnut's own
horse toxicity isn't simply juglone, and remains only partly understood
even for the species it's most strongly associated with. For dogs, moldy
nuts/hulls (again, specifically black walnut) are described as causing
tremors and seizures, attributed by a separate veterinary source to
tremorgenic mycotoxins (penitrem A) produced by *Penicillium* species
colonizing overwintered, moldy walnuts — a mold/mycotoxin hazard, not a
hazard from the nut itself [8]. The moldy-nut mycotoxin risk is plausibly
relevant to *J. regia* as well, since the causative mold is a general
storage-mold issue rather than species-specific chemistry, but this
wasn't independently confirmed for *J. regia* specifically.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial tree.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest at hull split; harvest
  window of a few weeks per orchard [5].
- **Days to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Temperate, cool temperate. USDA-equivalent hardiness
  reported inconsistently across sources (zones 5-9 most commonly, 6-10
  in one source, with cold-hardy 'Carpathian' cultivars rated to zone 4)
  [9]. Chill-hour requirement also varies sharply by cultivar: residential
  cultivars 300-700 hours, most varieties 400-1,500 hours below 45°F,
  specific named cultivars ranging 700-1,200 hours [9].
- **Composition:** Raw kernel, per 100 g: about 654 kcal, 15.2 g protein,
  65.2 g fat, 13.7 g carbohydrate (2.6 g sugar), 6.7 g fiber — sourced via
  aggregator sites reproducing USDA FoodData Central figures, not
  independently verified at the primary source [10].
- **Edibility:** Raw kernel, edible [10].
- **Pollination:** Required; wind-pollinated. Monoecious but dichogamous;
  a dedicated pollenizer cultivar is standard commercial practice
  (Finding 1).
- **Yield:** 4,800-4,900 kg/ha at mature, well-managed commercial bearing
  (California, 2016 and 2023 figures; see Estimates). Much lower figures
  (roughly 500-7,500 kg/ha) reported from non-peer-reviewed permaculture
  planning sources for younger or non-intensive plantings, treated as a
  lower bound rather than a conflicting claim [3].
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Both seed (rootstock) and grafting (dominant
  commercial route for named cultivars like 'Chandler'). The commercial
  Paradox rootstock (*J. microcarpa* × *J. regia*) is an infertile
  interspecific hybrid propagated vegetatively, not from seed [4] (Finding
  4). One orchard trial found own-rooted 'Chandler' outperformed grafted
  'Chandler' on Paradox rootstock in trunk circumference, yield, and
  yield efficiency — a surprising finding not explained by the available
  sourcing.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour figure sourced for any operation. Harvest
  timing is sourced: full maturity, at hull split [5]. Drying must begin
  promptly (air below 43°C/110°F) to avoid rancidity-causing kernel
  damage [5] (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** pH tolerance 5.1-8.3, preferred 6.6-7.5 (one source); a
  nursery listing for a named cultivar gives pH 6.0-8.0 [9]. No
  fertilization-rate or nutrient-removal figure sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Fresh in-shell — cold storage below 8°C
  extends postharvest availability, response varies by cultivar [5].
  Dried in-shell/kernel — target 2-3% kernel moisture in
  vapor-impermeable packaging; vacuum packaging or storage below 1% O2
  extends shelf life; 5°C in darkness showed no oxidative rancidity in
  one cultivar trial, versus the worst rancidity at 21°C under light [5]
  (Finding 5).
- **Establishment:** Grafted nursery trees (named cultivars on seedling
  or Paradox hybrid rootstock) are the dominant commercial planting
  material [4]. Standard commercial spacing in France: 100 stems/ha at
  10m × 10m [3].
- **Cultivars:** 'Chandler' is named repeatedly as the dominant/reference
  commercial cultivar (used for the reference genome assembly and the
  own-rooted-vs-grafted rootstock trial) [4]. 'Paynee' was found superior
  across several growth and yield traits in an Indian cultivar trial [1].
  'Carpathian' is named as a cold-hardy cultivar group (zone 4, 1,000-1,200
  chill hours) [9]. 'Carmelo' is named with a lower chill requirement
  (700 hours) [9]. No systematic cultivar-comparison table beyond these
  scattered named mentions was sourced.
- **Weather response:** Chill-hour requirement is sourced but varies
  sharply by cultivar (300-1,500 hours depending on source and cultivar)
  [9]; no formal dose-response curve was sourced.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Single stage, single annual window — nuts
  harvested once at full maturity (hull split).
- **Soil biology:** The Paradox rootstock is specifically valued for
  soil-borne-disease tolerance [4], implying own-rooted or
  standard-seedling trees may be more vulnerable, though this comparison
  wasn't independently confirmed.
- **Controlled environment:** Not independently assessed. A large
  deciduous wind-pollinated tree with a dichogamy requirement would need
  deliberate air movement and careful pollenizer-cultivar timing in any
  closed-system setting. No spaceflight/closed-system trial data found.

## Hazards

- Juglone-related allelopathic compounds are confirmed present in
  *J. regia* itself (soil juglone 0.25-3.25 mM, seasonal), though the
  strong allelopathic reputation in the literature concerns the different
  species Black walnut specifically, not confirmed as equally severe for
  *J. regia* (Finding 7).
- No ASPCA listing or equivalent toxicity finding was located for
  *J. regia* specifically — the one located listing ("Black Walnut")
  concerns the different species *J. nigra*.
- Moldy/overwintered nuts and hulls carry a tremorgenic mycotoxin
  (penitrem A, from *Penicillium* mold) risk to dogs, documented for
  black walnut specifically; plausibly a general storage-mold risk
  relevant to *J. regia* too, but not independently confirmed for this
  species. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller animal-hazard write-up is
  deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
  health/safety data.
- Walnut blight (*Xanthomonas arboricola* pv. *juglandis*) and
  anthracnose (*Gnomonia leptostyla*/*Marssonina juglandis*, or
  separately *Colletotrichum siamense*) are both sourced with quantified
  outbreak-severity examples (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh in-shell nuts → dried (promptly, below 43°C) →
  in-shell storage or cracked for kernel; shell is a byproduct.
- **Not otherwise modeled:** cold-pressed walnut oil (no dedicated
  oil-pressing yield figure was sourced).

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield, 4,800-4,900 kg/ha.** USDA reports California bearing-acre yield
of 2.14 tons/acre in 2023 and a record 2.19 tons/acre in 2016 [2].
Converting short tons/acre to kg/ha: 2.14 short tons = 1,941 kg; 1 acre =
0.4047 ha; 1,941 / 0.4047 = 4,797 kg/ha, rounded to 4,800. For 2016: 2.19
short tons = 1,987 kg; 1,987 / 0.4047 = 4,910 kg/ha, rounded to 4,900.
These are in-shell yields, not kernel-only yields; no sourced
kernel-recovery percentage was found to convert to a kernel-only figure.
Plausible range for other growing regions/seasons: 4,000-5,000 kg/ha for
comparable intensive commercial California-style management; likely
lower for other countries' typical orchard management, per the much
lower permaculture-sourced figures for younger or non-intensive
plantings [3].
