# Macadamia (*Macadamia integrifolia*)

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Macadamia integrifolia* (smooth-shell macadamia,
Proteaceae), the species behind the Hawaiian industry and most named
commercial cultivars, with *M. tetraphylla* (rough-shell macadamia,
somewhat more cold-tolerant, dominant in parts of South Africa and
Australia) treated as a same-crop cultivar/species-group delta —
commercial orchards today are overwhelmingly *M. integrifolia* ×
*M. tetraphylla* hybrids or named interspecific cultivars, and growers
are explicitly warned never to graft across the two parent species for
propagation [1].

Two other species in the genus are explicitly **not** covered here:
*M. ternifolia* bears small, bitter, cyanogenic-glycoside-bearing nuts
that aren't eaten and have no commercial crop use [2]; *M. jansenii* is
an endangered wild species used only as a genetic-diversity/breeding
resource, not a commercial crop [3]. Both are possible future research
topics in their own right.

Genus *Macadamia* (Proteaceae) is unrelated to walnut's *Juglans*,
almond's *Prunus*, hazelnut's *Corylus*, or chestnut's *Castanea*.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — macadamia is genuinely pollen-limited, and the magnitude
of the cross-pollination benefit is larger than any other nut crop
researched so far for this project.** Flowers are insect-pollinated and
most cultivars are at least partly self-incompatible via a homomorphic
stylar self-incompatibility mechanism [4][5]. A 2022 study found trees
were pollen-limited and that supplementary cross-pollination increased
nut-in-shell yield, kernel yield, and fruit set by as much as 97%, 109%,
and 92% respectively [4] — a substantially larger benefit than almond's
own ~20% bee-pollination gain on its one well-studied self-fertile
cultivar. A separate 2020 study found 84-100% of fruit arose from
cross-pollination even 200 m (25 rows) from a block of a different
cultivar, implying pollen (insect-carried) travels much farther than
might be assumed from orchard row-spacing alone [6].

**Finding 2 — yield reporting is split across at least three different
bases (per-tree, nuts-in-shell per hectare, kernel per hectare) and the
figures vary by more than 5x depending on industry-average vs.
best-practice assumptions — continuing chestnut's own non-standardized-
yield pattern rather than walnut's/almond's more convergent figures.** A
global agricultural bulletin gives 27-68 kg of nuts per tree per year; a
Hawaii-focused source gives 3.2-3.5 t/ha/year of nuts-in-shell; an
Australian source gives a current industry average of 1 t/ha kernel
weight against a 2.5 t/ha best-practice figure and a 5 t/ha aspirational
target; a South African source gives 2 t/ha dryland vs. 3-5 t/ha
irrigated [7]. No single official national series was found (macadamia
isn't a major US crop; Hawaii figures are the closest to an official
series but weren't independently verified against a primary government
release this pass). The in-shell-vs-kernel split isn't reconciled onto
one basis.

**Finding 3 — genome size is the best-converged of any nut crop
researched so far, across all four species in the genus simultaneously,
from one comparative 2023 study.** A preprint comparing all four
*Macadamia* species' genome assemblies reports a tight range of 735-795
Mb with N50 53.7-56 Mb, all at 14 pseudo-chromosomes [8].
*M. integrifolia* cultivar '741' specifically has had three independent
assembly attempts (518 Mb draft, later improved to 745 Mb, and a separate
chromosome-level assembly at 807 Mb) [8] — showing assembly completeness
improving over time rather than genuine biological disagreement, a more
informative story than a bare range. *M. tetraphylla*'s own assembly (750
Mb, N50 51 Mb) [8] sits squarely inside the same range as
*M. integrifolia*, consistent with how freely the two species hybridize
in cultivation (Finding 1 background).

**Finding 4 — grafting macadamia is reported as a genuinely harder
beginner skill than any other nut crop researched so far, for a
different, specifically-named physical reason (hard wood), and growers
are explicitly warned against crossing species when grafting.** A Hawaii
extension guide states macadamia doesn't grow true to seed, so clonal
propagation (grafting) is required for yield/quality, but warns that
grafting macadamia "can be difficult for a beginner because the wood is
hard" [1] — a different difficulty mechanism from chestnut's own
graft-failure story (rootstock phloem-fiber structure). The same guide
warns against grafting *M. tetraphylla* (rough-shell) scion/rootstock
combinations with *M. integrifolia* (smooth-shell) [1]. No standard
rootstock is recommended in Hawaii, though cultivar '660' is a common
grower preference; a 2021 rootstock-diversity study found that including
wild germplasm and dwarf genotypes increased genetic variability
available for future rootstock breeding [9]. A Chinese grafting-method
comparison found approach grafting gave 100% "take" versus only 50% for
rooted cuttings, 40% for side-wedge grafts, and just 16% for budding —
an unusually large spread between propagation techniques for the same
species [9].

**Finding 5 — postharvest handling centers on an unusually precise,
two-stage moisture target distinct from any other nut crop's own
postharvest figures, with oil content (not starch or protein) as the
primary quality metric.** A postharvest handbook puts fully developed
kernels at ~72-78% oil, the highest oil fraction of any nut crop
researched so far (hazelnut's own oil figure was ~60-68%), with kernel
moisture dried down to a specific 1.5% target, at which water activity
(0.3) is too low to support mold or bacterial growth [10]. Husking is
recommended within 24 hours of harvest, and Australian practice dries
de-husked nuts to an intermediate 8-10% wet-basis moisture before storing
as nut-in-shell for up to 1-2 months until processing [10][11]. A Thai
drying-method study found a two-stage heat-pump process (first ~40°C,
then 50-60°C) produced milder rancidity than single-temperature hot-air
drying, and that heat-pump drying reduced fatty-acid decomposition more
than conventional hot-air drying [11][12]. Oxidation (tracked via
peroxide value and free fatty acids, though not consistently via
p-anisidine value) continues slowly during storage after drying and
roasting [12].

**Finding 6 — macadamia has two distinct, geographically-separated major
pest problems, neither shared with any other nut crop researched so far,
plus the best-known hazard of any nut crop researched so far: dog
toxicity.** The macadamia felted coccid (*Acanthococcus ironsidei*, syn.
*Eriococcus ironsidei*), native to Australia, is an invasive sap-sucking
pest in South Africa's Mpumalanga, Limpopo, and KwaZulu-Natal regions,
causing branch/tree die-back and yield loss at high density; biological
control (the imported parasitoid *Metaphycus macadamiae*) and a 2026
sex-pheromone identification (for monitoring/mating disruption) are both
in progress, but chemical control currently relies on a limited
insecticide set [13]. The macadamia nut borer (*Thaumatotibia
batrachopa*) is instead the dominant pest in South Africa's cooler
growing regions, with larvae feeding inside the husk and halting nut
development — one 2022 study found it accounted for 95% of larvae
collected from damaged nuts across all surveyed regions; pheromone-based
threshold monitoring is commercially available [14]. Separately, and
unlike any other nut crop researched so far, macadamia kernels are
reported as toxic to dogs (toxic principle unknown), with clinical signs
of weakness especially in the rear limbs, vomiting, tremors, and
depression, and documented onset in dogs ingesting macadamia at 2.4 g/kg
body weight; most dogs recover within 24-48 hours with supportive
veterinary care [15].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree (unlike walnut,
  almond, hazelnut, and chestnut, all deciduous).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest; nuts drop at maturity and
  are gathered from the orchard floor (implied by the husking/drying
  sources' framing, though no explicit "drop vs. pick" statement was
  sourced).
- **Climate / cold tolerance:** Mature trees tolerate temperatures down
  to about 24-26°F (-4 to -3°C) for short periods, but flower clusters
  are typically killed around 28°F (-2°C) and young trees can be killed
  by light frost [16]. *M. tetraphylla* and tetraphylla-derived hybrids
  show somewhat better cold tolerance than pure *M. integrifolia* [16].
  Windy sites should be avoided — branches are brittle and especially
  prone to wind damage when heavily laden with nuts, and freeze-plus-wind
  is more damaging than either alone [16]. Suited to a mild, frost-free
  climate; one nursery source gives USDA Zones 9b-11 with good drainage
  as the practical growing range [16]. No sourced chill-hour requirement
  (macadamia is evergreen and presumably has no true chill requirement,
  but this wasn't explicitly confirmed by a source), soil pH, or
  salinity-tolerance figures.
- **Composition:** Fully developed kernels run ~72-78% oil, the highest
  of any nut crop researched so far for this project.
- **Pollination:** Insect-pollinated; most cultivars at least partly
  self-incompatible via a homomorphic stylar self-incompatibility
  mechanism [5]. Genuinely pollen-limited: supplementary
  cross-pollination increased nut-in-shell yield, kernel yield, and
  fruit set by up to 97%/109%/92% respectively in a 2022 study [4] — the
  largest sourced cross-pollination benefit of any nut crop researched so
  far. Cross-pollen reaches distant blocks effectively: 84-100% of fruit
  arose from cross-pollination even 200 m (25 rows) from a
  different-cultivar block [6]. No sourced bee-specific (vs. generic
  insect) pollinator requirement or managed-hive stocking rate.
- **Yield basis:** Reported inconsistently across sources — per-tree
  (kg/tree/year), nuts-in-shell per hectare, and kernel weight per
  hectare [7]. Not reconciled to one basis (see Research findings,
  Finding 2).
- **Flowering:** No sourced bloom-timing, day-length, or
  temperature-sum trigger.
- **Postharvest storage:** See Research findings, Finding 5. Husking
  recommended within 24 hours of harvest; Australian practice dries
  de-husked nuts to an intermediate 8-10% wet-basis moisture for
  nut-in-shell storage up to 1-2 months pending processing [10][11].
  Two-stage heat-pump drying (~40°C then 50-60°C) produces milder
  rancidity than single-temperature hot-air drying [11][12]. Oxidation
  (peroxide value, free fatty acids) continues slowly during storage
  after drying/roasting [12]; prolonged O2 exposure drives rancidity, so
  oxygen protection matters more than cold storage for short-term keeping
  [10].
- **Establishment:** Grafting is required (macadamia doesn't grow true
  to seed) but is reported as genuinely difficult for beginners
  specifically because of the wood's hardness — a different difficulty
  mechanism from chestnut's own rootstock-anatomy graft-failure story
  [1]. Never graft across *M. tetraphylla* (rough-shell) and
  *M. integrifolia* (smooth-shell) [1]. No standard rootstock is
  recommended in Hawaii; cultivar '660' is a common grower default
  [1][9]. Propagation-method success varies sharply: approach grafting
  100% take vs. rooted cuttings 50%, side-wedge grafts 40%, budding only
  16% in one Chinese comparison [9]. Best grafting month given as
  December in the same (presumably Southern Hemisphere/subtropical
  China) source [9].
- **Cultivars:** No sourced representative 5-10-cultivar spread with
  eating-quality/era/delta detail; named cultivars surfaced only
  incidentally ('660' as a common Hawaiian rootstock choice [9];
  'HAES788' and 'Guire No.1' as a reported best scion/rootstock pairing
  in one Chinese grafting study [9]).
- **Stand life:** Not sourced.
- **Labor / field operations:** Grafting (establishment, see above);
  husking within 24 hours of harvest [10]; de-husking and drying as a
  distinct, time-bounded postharvest operation [10][11]. No labor-hours
  figures sourced.
- **Seed crop:** Not applicable as a dedicated commercial seed crop —
  commercial macadamia is propagated by grafting onto seedling rootstock,
  not grown true-to-type from seed [1]. No hybrid-production system was
  sourced — named interspecific hybrid cultivars are clonally grafted,
  not re-grown from F1 seed each generation.
- **Religious/cultural notes:** No sourced sect-specific production rule
  or off-world/controlled-environment growability note was found.
- **Weather response:** Default evergreen, frost-sensitive behavior
  (rather than the deciduous, chill-dependent pattern of walnut,
  hazelnut, and chestnut). Sourced deltas: mature-tree cold tolerance to
  ~24-26°F, flower-cluster kill point ~28°F, young-tree frost
  sensitivity, wind-damage amplification of cold injury,
  *M. tetraphylla*/hybrid cold-tolerance advantage over pure
  *M. integrifolia* [16]. No dose-response curve sourced for any of
  these.
- **Environment envelope:** Genome size sourced and well-converged
  across all four species in the genus: 735-795 Mb, 14
  pseudo-chromosomes (Finding 3). No sourced soil chemistry, soil
  biology, light (shade tolerance), atmosphere (CO2 response),
  enclosed-space, or spaceflight-trial data.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Single stage, single annual window — no
  sourced evidence of multiple separate flowering/fruiting cohorts per
  year or an alternate-maturity harvest stage.

## Hazards

- Macadamia felted coccid (*Acanthococcus ironsidei*) — a South African
  regional pest; biological control in progress, limited chemical
  options (Finding 6).
- Macadamia nut borer (*Thaumatotibia batrachopa*) — the dominant pest in
  South Africa's cooler regions; pheromone monitoring available
  (Finding 6).
- Kernels are toxic to dogs (toxic principle unknown), with onset at 2.4
  g/kg body weight; most dogs recover within 24-48 hours with supportive
  veterinary care (Finding 6). **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller
  animal-hazard write-up is deferred until this project has a place for
  this kind of health/safety data.
- *M. ternifolia*'s cyanogenic-glycoside bitter nuts are a
  separate-species hazard, not applicable to *M. integrifolia*/
  *M. tetraphylla* as modeled here [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh in-husk nuts → husked (within 24 hours) → dried →
  dried in-shell → cracked for kernel; husk and shell are byproducts.

## Open research questions

- Yield figures span at least three incompatible bases (per-tree,
  nuts-in-shell/ha, kernel/ha) and more than 5x between industry-average
  and best-practice assumptions; no in-shell-to-kernel conversion ratio
  was sourced to reconcile them.
- No official national yield series was independently verified (Hawaii
  figures are the closest candidate but weren't confirmed against a
  primary government release this pass).
- Global production-leader ranking (South Africa vs. Australia vs.
  China) is inconsistent across sources and years, with China's own
  figures disagreeing by nearly 50% between two cited sources; not
  resolved.
- No sourced bee-specific (vs. generic insect) pollinator requirement,
  managed-hive stocking rate, or wind-pollination contribution.
- No sourced bloom-timing, day-length, or temperature-sum trigger.
- No sourced chill-hour requirement, soil pH, or salinity-tolerance
  figure.
- No sourced stand-life-in-years or labor-hours-per-operation figures.
- No sourced representative 5-10-cultivar spread with eating-quality/
  era/delta detail.
- *Macadamia ternifolia* (bitter, cyanogenic-glycoside-bearing, not
  commercially eaten) is a possible future research topic on genus-level
  toxicity variation.
- *Macadamia jansenii* (endangered wild species, breeding/
  genetic-diversity resource only) is a possible future research topic
  on conservation status.
- No environment-envelope data sourced beyond genome size (soil
  chemistry, light, atmosphere, enclosed-space, spaceflight trials all
  open).
- No religious/cultural-use production constraint sourced.
- The toxic principle behind dog toxicity remains unidentified in
  veterinary literature as of this research pass, which is itself worth
  flagging as unusual for a well-known, long-documented toxicity.
- No wild-gathering basis was sourced for *M. integrifolia*/
  *M. tetraphylla* themselves (firmly orchard-cultivated); the
  wild-species conservation angle belongs to *M. jansenii* specifically.
- No sourced in-shell husk-removal timing tolerance (how long fresh nuts
  can sit in-husk before quality loss begins) comparable to walnut's/
  hazelnut's own "must dry within N days" figures — the 24-hour husking
  recommendation is the closest analog found, but it's a recommendation,
  not a quantified tolerance curve.
- Macadamia felted coccid and macadamia nut borer are both specifically
  South-African-regional pest problems in the sources found; no sourced
  equivalent pest-pressure figure for the Hawaiian or Australian
  industries specifically, so this file doesn't claim these are the
  dominant pests everywhere macadamia is grown.
