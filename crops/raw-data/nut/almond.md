# Almond (*Prunus dulcis*)

**Category:** Nut · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/nut/almond.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers the sweet almond, *Prunus dulcis* var. *dulcis* — the type
grown commercially worldwide for the edible kernel. **Bitter almond**
(var. *amara*) is addressed within this same file as a trait/cultivar-level
distinction, not a separate crop, since it's the same species and the
sweet/bitter distinction is controlled by a small number of genes, not a
different taxon — but it's flagged as carrying a materially different
(and much higher) amygdalin/cyanide content. No other *Prunus* sibling
species (almond-peach hybrids aside) is covered here.

*Prunus dulcis* is in the same genus as the stone fruits (plum, cherry,
peach) and shares the same cyanogenic-glycoside kernel hazard already
flagged for that group.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — almond cultivars are overwhelmingly self-incompatible (not
merely self-unproductive), making bee pollination and a planted
pollenizer cultivar a near-universal commercial requirement — and even
the one well-studied self-fertile cultivar still measurably benefits from
bees.** Almond is diploid (2n=16) and exhibits gametophytic
self-incompatibility [1]; one source states plainly that all almond
cultivars grown in California require cross-pollination to produce a
crop, and that a profitable crop depends on pollen transfer to nearly
every flower, with growers routinely planting a pollenizer cultivar
alongside the main variety [2]. Recommended stocking is 5-8 hives/ha
placed in or near the orchard when both target and pollenizer cultivars
reach 50% flower, with a separate study using 5 colonies/ha as a standard
rate for self-incompatible varieties [2]. The self-fertile cultivar
'Independence' was directly tested with and without bees: kernel yield
was about 20% higher in bee-pollinated trees (5.53 kg/tree) than in
bee-isolated trees (4.49 kg/tree) [2] — self-fertility reduces but doesn't
eliminate the pollination requirement. A later study found self-fertile
orchards need only about half the minimum bee visits per flower that
cross-pollinating varieties require for nut set, allowing fewer hives
[2]. At the breeding level, the cultivar 'Tuono' is named as the primary
historical source of self-compatibility in global almond breeding, with a
recent study finding convergent evolution of self-compatibility through
transcriptional inactivation of the S-RNase and F-box self-incompatibility
determinants [1] — sourced only at search-snippet level, not
independently verified in the primary paper.

**Finding 2 — commercial yield is reported in pounds of shelled kernel
per bearing acre, a different reporting basis from walnut's own in-shell
figures, so this doc converts carefully rather than assuming
equivalence.** The most recent figure found is a 2025 California almond
projection of 2,160 lb/acre (kernel weight), close to a reported 10-year
average of 2,123 lb/acre [3]. Earlier seasons: 2023 at 1,810 lb/acre (the
lowest since 2005); 2024 at 2,170 lb/acre; 2020 at 2,490 lb/acre, second
only to 2011's 2,540 lb/acre [3]. A separate Moroccan study of
traditional, largely non-irrigated/poorly-pollinated orchards reported a
mean of only about 0.63 t/ha (kernel), with some low-input orchards below
100 kg/ha — explicitly attributed partly to poor pollination in that
study, underscoring Finding 1's commercial importance [2]. Converting the
strongest recent California figures to kg/ha kernel weight: see
Estimates, below.

**Finding 3 — the genome-size and genome-assembly picture is genuinely
unsettled across independent efforts.** Chromosome number is consistently
reported as 2n=16, base number x=8 [4]. But reported genome size varies
substantially by assembly and method: a Texas-cultivar k-mer-based lower
bound of 238 Mb; a Rosaceae database figure of 240 Mb; a separate assembly
of 227.6 Mb against an estimated total of 235 Mb; an Ensembl figure of
about 300 Mbp; and a chromosome-level draft of the 'Wanfeng' cultivar at
267,492,931 bp [4] — not reconciled into one authoritative figure here.

**Finding 4 — hull rot is the almond-specific disease complex with the
clearest, most actionable commercial guidance found, and its control is
explicitly a harvest-timing decision, not only a chemical one.** Hull rot
is caused mainly by *Rhizopus stolonifer* and *Monilinia* species, with
*Aspergillus niger* newly identified as a causal agent in Kern County [5].
Hulls remain susceptible from the start of hull split until the hulls are
fully dry, a window that can last from 10 days to two months depending on
irrigation and fertilization practice [5]. There are no control measures
once a hull rot infection becomes visible — prevention is the only lever,
via adequate-but-not-excessive nitrogen, a deliberate water deficit at
hull-split initiation, and, most effectively, early harvest [5].
Separately, navel orangeworm is the dominant insect pest, and its
management leans heavily on orchard sanitation: "mummy nuts" left on the
tree over winter give overwintering larvae a place to survive, so winter
removal and destruction of mummy nuts is a standard recommended practice,
and is also credited with helping minimize hull rot indirectly [5]. One
study found that harvesting 15-20 days post-hull-split (5-10 days earlier
than then-current practice) reduced windfall to under 0.5% in both
'Nonpareil' and 'Independence', though a grower source noted this could
extend the hulling/shelling season beyond 100 days [3] — a direct
operational trade-off between loss reduction and processing-capacity
strain.

**Finding 5 — postharvest kernel quality is governed overwhelmingly by
moisture content, with an unusually specific, well-sourced set of
thresholds.** Almond kernels are naturally low in moisture (roughly
3-6%), which limits oxidation and rancidity; industry research suggests
keeping moisture below about 6%, ideally in the 3.5-5.5% band, can
support up to two years of stable storage [6]. Moisture gain begins at
relative humidity of 70% or higher, and visible mold appears once kernel
moisture reaches about 8% [6]. Rising moisture at elevated humidity
causes texture loss and releases free fatty acids, which initiate oil
oxidation and rancidity [6]. High storage temperature, higher moisture,
light, and certain metals (notably iron) all promote lipid oxidation and
shorten shelf life [6]. Packaging can extend shelf life by 4-18 months
depending on conditions; one study found blanched kernels in sealed bags
kept peroxide values under 2 meq/kg for 18 months across storage
conditions tested [6]. A 2022 study found that almonds exposed to
postharvest moisture before storage, even if later dried, showed
increased lipid oxidation and significantly reduced shelf life compared
to almonds that were never exposed to that moisture in the first place —
the damage from a wet exposure isn't fully reversed by subsequent drying
[6]. Roasting changes the kernel's chemical structure, making it more
susceptible to rancidity than the raw kernel [6].

**Finding 6 — the amygdalin/cyanide difference between sweet and bitter
almond is real, large, and measured inconsistently across study types.**
A Tunisian study measuring hydrogen cyanide (HCN) directly found bitter
almonds from Sfax and other origins averaging about 1,062 mg/kg (~1
mg/g), against sweet-variety averages of only about 25 mg/kg — the same
paper notes HCN content varies considerably across samples, from under 20
to over 1,000 mg/kg of dry matter [7]. A Colombian study measuring
amygdalin (not HCN directly) across 29 commercial varieties found "not
detected" up to 375.40 mg/100 g (roughly 0-3.75 mg/g), spanning sweet,
slightly bitter, and bitter types [7]. Separately, one source reports a
study with bitter-almond amygdalin concentrations of 33-54 g/kg (33-54
mg/g) by variety, against semibitter averaging 1 g/kg and sweet averaging
only 0.063 g/kg [7] — this single figure is an order of magnitude or more
above both the Tunisian HCN figure and the Colombian amygdalin figure,
and the three measurement bases (HCN-released vs. amygdalin-content, and
differing units) aren't reconciled into one number here. The fatal oral
cyanide dose range for a human is reported as 0.6-1.5 mg/kg body weight
[7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial tree.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest at hull split, shaken to the
  orchard floor [3][5].
- **Days to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Temperate, Mediterranean, semiarid. USDA-equivalent
  hardiness reported inconsistently (zones 5-7 to 9 in one nursery
  source, 7-9 in an extension-style guide, with named cultivars ranging
  5-9 to 7-10) [8]. Chill-hour requirement also varies by cultivar:
  roughly 200-500 hours for most sweet varieties per one source, 500-600
  per another; named cultivars range 200-400 ('Garden Prince') to 600-800
  ('Halls Hardy') [8]. Bloom occurs unusually early (February-March in
  mild climates, before most other stone fruits and often before the
  average last frost), making frost during full bloom the single biggest
  climate risk — a single late-frost event at full bloom can eliminate
  the entire season's crop; cold-air pooling on valley floors makes this
  worse, favoring hillside sites with good air drainage [8]. Almond
  blooms even earlier than plum, which itself blooms about a week before
  apple and faces a comparable frost risk.
- **Composition:** Raw kernel, per 100 g: about 579 kcal, 21.2 g protein,
  49.9 g fat, 21.6 g carbohydrate, 12.5 g fiber, 4.4 g sugar, 269 mg
  calcium, 3.7 mg iron — sourced via aggregator sites reproducing USDA
  FoodData Central figures, not independently verified at the primary
  source [9].
- **Edibility:** Raw, sweet kernel only; bitter kernel is not sold as a
  raw snack food due to amygdalin content (see Finding 6 and Hazards,
  below).
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (bees) — see Finding 1;
  the self-fertile cultivar 'Independence' reduces but doesn't eliminate
  the requirement (still yields ~20% more with bees) [2].
- **Yield:** USDA California figures (shelled-kernel basis) of 1,810-2,540
  lb/acre across recent seasons convert to roughly 2,030-2,850 kg/ha
  kernel weight (see Estimates, below; Finding 2). A Moroccan
  traditional-orchard figure of ~630 kg/ha (with some low-input orchards
  under 100 kg/ha) is treated as a poorly-pollinated/non-intensive lower
  bound, not a conflicting claim about the same production system [2].
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced for this crop specifically.
  By comparison with other stone fruits (plum, apricot, peach), almond is
  rated the most salinity-sensitive at an ECe threshold of 1.5 dS/m
  (compared to plum 2.6, apricot 1.6, peach 1.7) [10].
- **Propagation:** Both seed (bitter-almond seedling rootstock) and
  grafting (dominant commercial route for named fruiting cultivars like
  'Nonpareil' and self-fertile 'Independence') [1][2]. Clonal
  peach-almond hybrid rootstocks are also reported in commercial use,
  though not independently confirmed with a dedicated source.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour figure sourced for any operation. Harvest is
  by mechanical shaking at hull split [3][5]. Winter mummy-nut
  sanitation (removal and destruction of overwintered nuts) is a named,
  specific labor operation for navel-orangeworm control [5].
- **Soil:** pH preference clusters around neutral: one source gives
  6.0-7.0, another gives a wider tolerance of 5.6-7.8 with better
  performance at 6.6-7.0 [8]. Drainage matters as much as pH — almonds
  are susceptible to root rot in wet soils [8]. No fertilization-rate or
  nutrient-removal figure sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Moisture content (ideally 3.5-5.5%) is the
  dominant quality driver, with specific thresholds for mold onset (~8%
  moisture) and the humidity level at which moisture gain begins (≥70%
  RH) (Finding 5).
- **Establishment:** Grafted nursery trees (named cultivars such as
  'Nonpareil', self-fertile 'Independence', on seedling or clonal
  rootstock) are the dominant commercial planting material (Finding 1).
  No orchard spacing/density figure sourced.
- **Cultivars:** 'Nonpareil' is implied as the reference commercial
  standard. 'Independence' is the dominant self-fertile cultivar,
  directly studied for its bee-pollination yield response (Finding 1)
  [2]. 'Tuono' is named as the historical source of self-compatibility
  in global almond breeding [1]. 'Texas' is named as a self-incompatible
  reference cultivar used in genome sequencing [4]. 'Garden Prince'
  (200-400 chill hours) and 'Halls Hardy' (600-800 chill hours,
  late-blooming) are named as cold-hardy nursery cultivars at opposite
  ends of the chill-hour range [8]. No systematic cultivar-comparison
  table beyond these scattered named mentions was sourced.
- **Weather response:** Frost at full bloom is the dominant sourced
  weather risk (a single late-frost event at full bloom can eliminate
  the entire season's crop), but no dose-response (e.g. percent bloom
  kill by temperature) figure was sourced.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Single stage, single annual window —
  nuts harvested once at hull split.
- **Soil biology:** Susceptible to root rot in wet/poorly-drained soils
  [8]; no symbiont-dependence or replant-disease figure sourced.
- **Genome size:** Genuinely unsettled across independent assemblies
  (227.6-300 Mbp depending on source and method; chromosome number
  consistently 2n=16, x=8) (Finding 3).
- **Controlled environment:** Not independently assessed. Bee
  pollination and the extremely early bloom window would both need
  deliberate intervention in any closed system — bee access and precise
  frost/temperature control at bloom would be the two hardest
  constraints to replicate. No spaceflight/closed-system trial data
  found.

## Hazards

- Bitter almond (var. *amara*) carries a materially higher
  amygdalin/cyanide content than sweet almond — measured inconsistently
  across study types (HCN directly: ~1,062 mg/kg bitter vs. ~25 mg/kg
  sweet; amygdalin: up to 375.40 mg/100g across a 29-variety survey; a
  third source's 33-54 g/kg bitter-variety figure is an order of
  magnitude above both) — not reconciled into one figure (Finding 6).
  **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller human-hazard write-up is deferred until
  this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
- Hull rot (*Rhizopus*, *Monilinia*, *Aspergillus niger*) has no control
  once visible — prevention (nitrogen management, deliberate water
  deficit at hull split, early harvest) is the only lever (Finding 4).
- Navel orangeworm overwinters in unremoved "mummy nuts," making winter
  sanitation a named control operation (Finding 4).
- No animal-toxicity source was located specific to almond kernel, hull,
  or shell — a gap, not a safety claim, and notably different from
  walnut, which has a documented (though species-scoped) toxicity
  listing for the related but different species black walnut.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh in-hull nuts → dried (hull removed as a byproduct) →
  dried in-shell → cracked for kernel; shell is a byproduct.
- **Not otherwise modeled:** sweet-almond oil, almond milk/flour, and
  marzipan processing (treated as downstream consumer/kitchen processing,
  outside this project's farm-to-raw-product scope for now).

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield, 2,030-2,850 kg/ha (kernel weight).** USDA reports California
shelled-kernel yield in lb/acre. Converting the cited range: 1,810
lb/acre (2023, lowest since 2005) = 821 kg; 1 acre = 0.4047 ha; 821 /
0.4047 = 2,028 kg/ha, rounded to 2,030. For 2011's record 2,540 lb/acre:
2,540 lb = 1,152 kg; 1,152 / 0.4047 = 2,847 kg/ha, rounded to 2,850. The
2025 projected figure (2,160 lb/acre) converts to about 2,420 kg/ha,
close to the reported 10-year average of 2,123 lb/acre (~2,382 kg/ha).
These are kernel-weight yields (as reported, pounds of meat per bearing
acre), a different reporting basis from walnut's in-shell figures — no
sourced kernel-recovery percentage was found to convert to an
in-shell-equivalent figure.
