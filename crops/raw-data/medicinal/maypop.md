# Maypop / Purple Passionflower (*Passiflora incarnata*)

## Naming and identity

*Passiflora incarnata* L., Passifloraceae — maypop, purple passionflower,
wild apricot, apricot vine, maracock (from the Powhatan name) [1][10].

- Native to the south-eastern and central United States [1].
- The traded medicinal herb "passionflower" (Passiflorae herba) is this
  species [2].

## Why Medicinal rather than Fruit

The main traded product is the dried herb — official in the EU
pharmacopoeia and US Pharmacopoeia, and an EMA-recognized traditional herbal
medicine [2][3]. The fruit is a minor garden, wild, and historical food
[4]. Both paths are documented below, but the primary economic use is as a
medicinal herb, which is why this crop is filed under Medicinal rather than
Fruit.

The species is grounded against its fruiting relatives in the same genus,
passionfruit and sweet granadilla — tropical/highland evergreen vines with
no temperate die-back habit. Maypop is the only temperate passionflower
crop (see Distinguishing characteristics, below).

## Distinguishing characteristics

- **Climate and life form.** The only temperate passionflower crop.
  - A herbaceous perennial: frost kills the top growth each winter, and
    the plant regrows from a rhizomatous root crown [1][6].
  - Grown in zones 6-11 [1]; roots are reported to survive to zone 5,
    about −29°C (secondary source) [6]. Shoots emerge late in spring [6].
- **Product.** A medicinal herb crop: whole aerial parts cut at flowering
  and dried [2][5]. The European Pharmacopoeia quality limit is at least
  1.5% flavonoids as vitexin [2]. It also has an edible fruit [4].
- **Pollination.** Self-incompatible, pollinated by carpenter bees and
  bumblebees, and functionally andromonoecious [1][4]. At least two
  genetically different vines are needed.
- **Hazards.** Spreads by deep root suckers and is an agricultural weed of
  no-till crops, pastures, and hay fields (secondary source) [7]. The herb
  is a sedative with drug-interaction and pregnancy cautions — see
  Hazards, below.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a temperate, die-back perennial grown for a dried sedative
herb, with fruit as a second product.**
- Herb definition: "the fragmented or cut/dried aerial parts... It may
  contain flowers and/or fruits"; at least 1.5% total flavonoids as
  vitexin; dry extract at least 2.0% [2].
- Use: EU traditional use for mild mental stress and to aid sleep, 1-8 g
  of herb a day as tea. Liquid extracts range from a drug-extract ratio of
  1:1 to 1:8 in 25-70% ethanol [2].
- Production: cultivated mainly in India, Florida, Italy, and Spain
  (secondary source) [5]; certified organic farms in Mediterranean France
  and Italy (secondary source) [5]; dried plant exported from America to
  Europe (secondary source) [5].
- Harvest: in bloom, from flowering to the start of fruiting. Flavonoid
  content depends on the reproductive stage, and storage after harvest
  reduces it (secondary source) [5]. Commercial crops expect more than one
  harvest a season [3].
- Herb yield: 964-1,130 kg/ha of dry aerial biomass a season with manure
  [8].
- Practical consequence: two cultivation paths with different harvest
  stages — herb, cut at flowering (cut-and-come-again), and fruit,
  gathered from vines left to set (perennial bearing).

**Finding 2 — fruit: small yields, self-incompatible.**
- Egg-sized, 4-10 cm long [1][9]. Fruit develops 2-3 months after
  flowering [1]. Ripe fruit yellows, wrinkles, and drops; harvest runs
  July to October [9].
- Keeps a week or two in a cool, not overly dry, room, where flavor
  develops [9].
- Yields per vine: 10-20 fruits [1]; "a dozen or so" [9]; 5-20 in the wild
  and 30-50 expected under cultivation (secondary source) [4].
- Vines bear in 1-2 years [1].
- McGuire (1999) argued it "deserves greater use as a fruit crop": an
  herbaceous perennial vine that flowers and fruits over much of the
  growing season, with aril juice used fresh or processed [4].
- Fruit set: self-incompatible, pollinated by carpenter bees (*Xylocopa*).
  Plants are functionally andromonoecious, and low resource status favors
  male flower production and reduces fruit set [4].
- Each flower lasts about one day [1].

**Finding 3 — propagation is easy and the plant spreads aggressively.**
- Seed: germinates in 5-10 days at near 90% when sown in darkness in very
  warm soil (35-40°C) [1]; seed sown about 15 cm deep still emerges [9].
- Root cuttings: pieces 5-8 cm long root at near 100% at 21-32°C [9].
- Spread: grows about 1.8 m a season and spreads by suckers [9]. Thick,
  deep rhizomes make mechanical control very difficult in no-till crops,
  pastures, and hay fields (secondary source) [7].

**Finding 4 — historical cultivation and food use by Native Americans.**
- Captain John Smith (1612) reported Virginia natives planting the vines
  for the fruit. The name "maypop" comes from the Powhatan "maracock"
  (secondary source) [10].
- Cherokee uses: the fruit eaten raw or boiled to a syrup; young shoots
  and leaves parboiled and fried as a potherb; root poultices and root
  teas as medicine (secondary source) [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Herbaceous perennial vine; tops die back every
  winter and regrow from rhizomatous roots [1][6]. Perennial, warm-season
  growth; shoots emerge in late spring [6].
- **Harvest pattern:** Two paths — herb, cut at flowering, more than one
  cut a season [3][5]; fruit, July-October on vines left uncut [9]. Both
  paths are exclusive per shoot.
- **Days to first harvest:** Herb ~90 days (estimated); fruit 365-730 days
  (vines bear in 1-2 years) [1].
- **Climate zones:** Temperate, warm temperate, subtropical (USDA zones
  6-11 [1]; roots possibly to zone 5, secondary source [6]); Mediterranean
  (Italy, Spain, France) [5].
- **Composition:** Not researched — no composition table found for the
  fruit (kcal/protein).
- **Edibility:** Fruit is raw-edible [1][4]; herb is not (consumed as a
  dried medicine).
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (carpenter bees, also
  bumblebees); self-incompatible — needs at least two genetically
  different vines [1][4].
- **Yield:**
  - Herb (dry aerial parts, kg/ha/season): average ~1,000, good 1,130,
    exceptional ~1,500 (estimated, assumes a second cut), poor ~600
    (estimated, assumes a dry, unfertilized first year) [8].
  - Fruit (fresh kg/ha/yr): average ~1,900, good ~3,000, exceptional
    ~5,000, poor ~600 (derived from per-vine fruit counts and an estimated
    fruit weight — the weakest input; see Estimates, below).
  - Fresh-to-dry ratio for herb biomass: not sourced; estimated about
    4:1, typical of leafy herb biomass (about 75-80% water).
- **Water use:** No measured seasonal water-use source found. Estimated
  ~120 growing-season days at an average crop coefficient of 0.8 (full
  canopy vine). Deep rhizomes [7] and reported high drought tolerance
  (secondary source) [9] suggest an estimated root depth of 1.0 m.
  Drought tolerance: high (secondary source) [9]. Waterlogging tolerance:
  low — needs well-drained soil with "plenty of moisture in the growing
  season" (secondary source) [5]. Low resource status raises the share of
  male flowers and reduces fruit set [4].
- **Soil:** Tolerates acid, neutral, and alkaline soils; moderate salt
  tolerance (secondary source) [9]; needs good drainage [5]. Estimated
  optimal pH ~6.5 (range 5.0-8.0), based on general tolerance rather than
  a sourced optimum.
- **Planting / spacing:** Propagated from seed, root cuttings (5-8 cm,
  near 100% strike at 21-32°C), or dug suckers [1][7][9]. Spacing 0.9-1.5
  m [1][9]; fruiting vines need 2.8-3.7 m² of trellis support each [9].
  Seedlings or rooted cuttings planted in spring; grows about 1.8 m a
  season [9].
- **Seed viability:** Not sourced; estimated 2 years ideal storage, 1 year
  ambient, 3 years in an arid barn. Hard-coated seed that germinates
  readily when warm [1] and is sold dry by seed firms.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Fruit keeps about 1-2 weeks in a cool room,
  ripening further [9]. Dried herb keeps about a year, with flavonoid
  potency declining over storage (secondary source) [5].
- **Flowering:** No induction trigger; flowers on current-season growth
  from June to September [9]. Tops are killed by the first hard freeze;
  roots survive to about −15°C (secondary source) [9] or reportedly to
  zone 5 (secondary source) [6] — sources conflict on exact hardiness.
- **Labor (rough estimate, no sourced budget found):**
  - Herb path: weeding/upkeep, then cutting and drying (1-2 cuts a
    season).
  - Fruit path: trellis upkeep, then gathering fallen fruit.
  - All labor figures are estimates; a commercial herb-farm budget study
    would replace them.
- **Field operations:** Cutting aerial parts at flowering [5]; prompt
  drying [2][5]; hand-pollination with a paintbrush where pollinators are
  scarce (secondary source) [4]; controlling root suckers [7].
- **Disorders:** Nematodes; caterpillars of the Gulf fritillary butterfly
  (an exclusive larval host) and other butterflies defoliate the plant
  [1][9]. Poor fruit set can result from a lack of carpenter bees or from
  planting a single genotype [1][4].
- **Cultivars:** None formally researched; wild seedlings vary, and
  growers advise planting two genotypes for pollination [4]. Nursery
  selections exist but are not verified.
- **Controlled environment:** Hydroponic viability marginal (inference,
  not sourced); widely container-grown in the nursery trade (not
  researched in detail).

## Hazards

- Sedative medicinal herb: causes drowsiness; interacts with
  benzodiazepines and other sedatives; not recommended during pregnancy or
  for children under 12 [2]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller human-hazard
  write-up is deferred until Glinda has a place for this kind of
  health/safety data.
- Contains the cyanogenic glycoside gynocardin in trace amounts in the
  herb [2]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — same as above.
- Trace harmala (β-carboline) alkaloids, usually undetectable [2].
- Agricultural weed risk: deep rhizomes and root suckers, difficult to
  control mechanically in no-till crops, pastures, and hay fields
  (secondary source) [7].
- Livestock-safety notes: none found in the sources reviewed.

## Uses and processing

- **Herb chain:** Aerial parts cut at flowering → dried promptly → cut tea
  (1-8 g a day) or ethanolic tincture (drug-extract ratio 1:1 to 1:8,
  25-70% ethanol) [2].
- **Fruit chain:** Ripe fallen fruit → eaten fresh, or pulp strained for
  juice → boiled to syrup or jelly [4][10].
- **Propagation:** Seed; root cuttings [1][9].
- **Not otherwise modeled:** Young shoots as a potherb, root poultice, and
  root tea (Cherokee use) [10]; commercial dry-extract manufacturing [2].

## Estimates and derivations

These values are not directly sourced and should be replaced if better
data is found.

1. **Days to first harvest (90 / 365-730):** Herb path assumes a spring
   planting can be cut at first bloom (~90 days), which no source
   confirms directly. Fruit path uses the sourced 1-2 years to bearing
   [1].
2. **Yield tiers:** Herb figures come directly from the 964-1,130 kg/ha
   sourced range [8], with exceptional/poor tiers estimated around it.
   Fruit figures are derived from an estimated ~2,500 vines/ha (one per
   3-4 m² of support [9]) × 10-40 fruits per vine [1][4][9] × an estimated
   50 g per fruit (egg-sized [1]) — fruit weight is the weakest input here
   and a direct measurement would improve this significantly.
3. **Fresh-to-dry ratio (~4:1):** Typical of leafy herb biomass; not
   directly sourced.
4. **Water use (~120 crop-days):** Estimated from a ~150-day growing
   season and an average crop coefficient of 0.8; no measured water-use
   source was found.
5. **Seed viability (2 / 1 / 3 years):** No storage study found; based on
   general seed-hardiness behavior.
6. **Labor:** No cost/budget study found; rough figures derived by
   judgement from harvest-rate assumptions (e.g., ~40 kg fresh herb
   harvested by hand per hour).
7. **Optimal pH (~6.5):** The plant tolerates acid to alkaline soils
   (secondary source) [9]; a center value is judgement, not sourced.

## Open research questions

- **Hardiness conflict:** Sources disagree on root-crown cold hardiness —
  zone 6 [1] vs. zone 7b (secondary source) [9] vs. zone 5 / −29°C
  (secondary source) [6] vs. roots surviving to −15°C (secondary source)
  [9].
- **Fruit-per-vine conflict:** Sources report anywhere from 5 to 50 fruits
  per vine [1][4][9].
- **Not researched:** Fruit composition (kcal, protein) and average fruit
  weight; the full text of McGuire (1999) (only the abstract and secondary
  snippets were available); herb cuts per season and regrowth timing;
  drying temperature; commercial herb-farm budgets; controlled hardiness
  trials; the primary Pacheco et al. (2021) paper (seen only via secondary
  citations).
- **Weakest estimates:** Labor figures, fruit yield derivation, and
  seasonal water use — all flagged above as not directly sourced.
