# Persimmon, Asian (*Diospyros kaki*)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as *Diospyros kaki* (Ebenaceae), the Oriental, Japanese or
Chinese persimmon — one species, with four recognized astringency
types treated here as cultivar groups rather than separate entries:
pollination-constant non-astringent types (which lose astringency on
the tree and are eaten firm), pollination-constant astringent types
(which must be softened, de-astringed or dried before eating), and two
less commercially significant pollination-variant groups whose
astringency depends on whether the fruit is seeded. These types differ
in processing but share tree form, climate needs, propagation and
harvest timing, so they are modeled as cultivar groups within this one
entry rather than as separate crops.

Three other persimmon species — the American persimmon, date-plum, and
Texas persimmon — are treated as separate entries this project has
also researched, though this entry's rootstock, drying and
de-astringency treatment forms the shared family basis those entries
draw on; in particular, the American persimmon species is itself the
main rootstock option for this crop in the southern US. This entry
shares its general "fruit that must be processed before it is edible"
pattern — conceptually similar to a Mediterranean oil-fruit crop's own
debittering requirement, and to a dried-fig-type processing chain —
with other fruit crops this project has researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a crop whose global footprint is concentrated in a few
East Asian countries, with wide regional yield variation.** Native to
China and cultivated there for over 2,000 years, this crop reached
Japan by around the 7th century and Korea by around the 14th; roughly
90% of world production remains concentrated in China, Japan and
Korea, with world output around 5 million tonnes as of a recent
reference year, China alone accounting for roughly 80% of that figure.
Spain is documented as Europe's largest producer by a wide margin over
the next-largest European producer, built around one specific widely
grown cultivar developed there. Yield varies enormously by growing
region: a world-average figure sits well below 5 t/ha, pulled down by
large, lower-input production areas, while at least one Middle Eastern
producing country reports a yield several times the world average, and
a leading Chinese cultivar is documented reaching roughly 30 t/ha at
full production in intensive systems [1][4][8].

**Finding 2 — flowering happens well after bud break, which protects
flower buds from spring frost, but most commercial cultivars need
pollen from a minority of male-flowering trees anyway.** Trees bloom
in late spring on current-season shoots, several weeks after bud
break — a timing pattern that means spring frosts rarely kill flower
buds directly, unlike many other deciduous fruit trees. Trees are
dioecious or monoecious, and most commercially important cultivars
bear only female flowers; many of these still set a modest crop without
pollination at all (a trait called parthenocarpy), but at least one
widely grown non-astringent cultivar's parthenocarpic set is weak, and
it needs actual pollination from a nearby pollen-producing cultivar to
crop well. Documented pollinizer-tree ratios for that cultivar run
roughly one pollinizer tree for every fifteen to forty bearing trees.
A 2019 regional Japanese study found bumblebees and other small bees
moved more pollen than honeybees in this crop specifically [1][5][12].

**Finding 3 — astringency is a real, removable chemical property, not
simply unripeness, and several industrial-scale removal methods
exist.** Unripe fruit, and the flesh of astringent-type cultivars even
at full color, contains a soluble tannin that makes it unpleasantly
astringent to eat; this tannin disappears naturally as the fruit
softens, or can be removed deliberately using ethylene, alcohol, carbon
dioxide, or frost exposure while the fruit is still firm. Commercial
de-astringency methods documented here include sealing fruit in a high
concentration of carbon dioxide for roughly a day at room temperature
(the method used for Europe's leading cultivar, sold firm afterward
under a trademarked name), spraying fruit with an ethanol solution and
holding it sealed for about ten days (standard Japanese practice), and
using dry ice as a cheaper carbon-dioxide source that keeps fruit
firmer than the ethanol method; one comparison across 25 cultivars
found the ethanol method never outperformed the carbon-dioxide method,
and that some cultivars respond poorly to de-astringency treatment
generally [3][4][9].

**Finding 4 — a genuinely unusual chilling-injury pattern: damage in a
band above freezing, not below a single threshold.** At least one
widely grown non-astringent cultivar is damaged by cold storage
specifically within a 2-15°C range (worst around 5-7°C, causing flesh
browning, softening and water-soaking) but is not damaged at 0°C
itself — the opposite of how chilling injury usually works in
cold-sensitive fruit, where injury occurs below a threshold rather than
in a band above it. A separate widely grown astringent-type cultivar is
not chilling-sensitive at all. Standard storage for firm fruit is near
0°C and high relative humidity, giving roughly three months of ordinary
air storage life and longer under controlled atmosphere; the fruit is
also extremely ethylene-sensitive, with even low ethylene
concentrations softening it to an unmarketable state within days at
room temperature [3][5].

**Finding 5 — rootstock choice carries real scion-compatibility risk,
and the American persimmon is this crop's own best-documented
rootstock option in parts of its range.** In the southern US, this
species' rootstock of choice is frequently the American persimmon,
valued for cold-hardiness, tolerance of both wet and dry soils, and
compatibility with most scion cultivars, though it suckers and gives
less uniform vigor than other options. In California, a different
persimmon-genus rootstock species is more common, but it is partially
incompatible with at least three named non-astringent cultivars and can
raise fruit drop in at least one widely grown astringent cultivar.
Several Japanese-bred dwarfing rootstock selections give higher yield
efficiency and lower per-tree labor. Cuttings root poorly in this
crop (documented rooting rates well under 25%), so commercial
propagation is done almost entirely by grafting onto seedling
rootstock [5].

**Finding 6 — drying this fruit is a distinct, named craft tradition
across multiple East Asian countries, and a separate fermented-juice
product has its own multi-year aging process.** Firm astringent fruit
is peeled, briefly dipped in boiling water to discourage mold, and hung
on strings or skewers; the skin hardens over roughly a week, after
which the fruit is kneaded by hand every few days as it dries over
three to six weeks, until a white sugar bloom forms on the surface.
This practice has its own distinct name in Japan, China, Korea and
Vietnam respectively, and at least one Japanese region is known
specifically for skewered dried persimmons as a local specialty.
Separately, the juice of crushed unripe astringent fruit, pressed and
then fermented and aged for one to three years, yields a traditional
Japanese tannin-rich liquid historically used to waterproof and
preserve paper, fishing nets and lines, wooden vessels and cloth, as a
natural dye, and to help clarify rice wine — a genuinely multi-year
aging process for a non-food product, distinct from the fruit-drying
tradition [4][10][11][13].

**Finding 7 — real cultural and ritual use in East Asia alongside a
documented health hazard.** In Korean ancestral commemorative rites,
fresh or dried persimmon is placed in the fruit row of the ritual food
table alongside jujube, chestnut and pear, with some sources describing
positional conventions (red fruits to one side, white to the other) and
a preference for odd-numbered item counts, though a 2023 guideline from
a major Korean Confucian institution allows families to simplify the
table considerably. In Japanese Shinto New Year household custom in at
least one western Japanese region, a skewer of ten dried persimmons is
placed beneath a traditional rice-cake offering as an offering to a
New Year deity, arranged in a specific pattern tied to wordplay on
"smiling" and "being in harmony" — the skewer itself is not eaten.
Separately, unripe or firm astringent fruit of this species is a
documented cause of a specific type of stomach bezoar in humans, one of
the most common plant-based bezoar-forming foods identified in medical
literature [1][4][13][14].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial tree, reaching roughly
  10 meters [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; firm fruit is clipped with
  the calyx attached, in two to three picking passes each autumn
  [3][5].
- **Climate:** Warm-temperate, Mediterranean and subtropical climates,
  including humid East Asian summers, Mediterranean Spain and the
  Middle East, and warm-temperate parts of the Americas; tropical
  conditions give poorer yields and lower non-astringent-cultivar fruit
  quality [1][4][5].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible for non-astringent cultivars and
  soft-ripened or de-astringed fruit generally; firm astringent-type
  fruit and green fruit are not raw-edible until softened, de-astringed,
  or dried [1][4].
- **Pollination:** Not strictly required (most cultivars set fruit
  parthenocarpically), but pollination from male-flowering pollinizer
  trees meaningfully raises fruit set and reduces drop for at least one
  widely grown cultivar with weak parthenocarpic set; pollinators
  include bumblebees, other small bees, and honeybees [5][12].
- **Yield:** A world-average figure sits a little above 4 t/ha,
  pulled down by large low-input production areas; well-managed
  regional averages (for example, California's documented multi-year
  average) sit in the 12 t/ha range, and at least one Middle Eastern
  producing country and a leading intensively grown Chinese cultivar
  both reach or approach roughly 28-30 t/ha. A per-tree yield check
  (150-300 fruit per mature tree at a typical fruit weight) is broadly
  consistent with these good-to-exceptional figures at typical planting
  density.
- **Water use:** No dedicated reference crop coefficient exists for
  this species in the standard international crop-evapotranspiration
  reference; a closely analogous stone-fruit crop-coefficient curve is
  used here as the basis, adjusted upward at midseason based on
  documented Spanish research showing this crop's own summer water use
  approaching or exceeding that analogy value. Overwatering specifically
  at bloom and fruit set increases fruit drop; early-season deficit
  irrigation reduces drop but shrinks fruit, while late-season deficit
  irrigation speeds fruit coloring [5][16].
- **Establishment:** Bare-root grafted trees planted in winter, or
  potted trees planted any time (best in spring); grafted at or above
  the soil line; cut back substantially at planting. Planting density
  ranges from roughly 360 trees per hectare on a standard central-leader
  system up to roughly 720 trees per hectare on a trellised system, with
  root-zone restriction techniques allowing much higher densities in
  intensive systems [5][6].
- **Labor:** No directly sourced labor-hour figures were found;
  estimates here are built from documented task descriptions (hand
  thinning roughly three weeks after flowering, hand clipping in
  multiple picks, winter pruning and training, and additional
  hand-labor for de-astringency treatment and fruit drying) rather than
  a dedicated regional cost study.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained soil across a range of textures, pH
  roughly 6-7; the crop is documented as salt-sensitive (no specific
  threshold found), and frost-pocket sites should be avoided; windbreaks
  are recommended since fruit-loaded shoots can break in strong wind
  [5][6].
- **Stand life:** First fruit by around year three, full production by
  around eight to ten years. Overall productive lifespan is not well
  quantified in the sources used here, though at least one East Asian
  cultural reference describes the tree itself as proverbially
  long-lived. Heavy crop loads lead to biennial bearing if not
  controlled by pruning and thinning [1][5][13].
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal postharvest rot genus affecting
  stored fruit; in warm-climate US production, documented pests and
  diseases include scale insects, a persimmon-specific psyllid, tree
  borers, stink bugs, a fungal anthracnose disease, two further fungal
  leaf-spot diseases, a fungal wilt disease, and a general rootstock-
  linked decline disorder, though none of these were researched in
  further depth here [3][5].
- **Cultivars:** Non-astringent cultivars include a widely grown
  Japanese-origin type valued as the most popular non-astringent
  cultivar internationally but needing pollinizer trees for reliable
  set, and several further Japanese-origin types differing in season and
  sugar content. Astringent cultivars include a widely grown
  Japanese-origin type mostly used for drying in its home country; the
  leading Spanish cultivar, sold firm after carbon-dioxide de-astringency
  treatment under a trademarked name; an Israeli-origin cultivar
  similarly de-astringed and sold under its own trademarked name, with
  notably high yields in that country; an Italian cultivar needing a
  much longer carbon-dioxide treatment than most; and a leading Japanese
  astringent cultivar responsive to both major de-astringency methods.
  Several further cultivars are grown specifically as pollen sources for
  their male flowers rather than for fruit. Interspecific hybrids between
  this species and the American persimmon exist as named cultivars and
  are notably more cold-hardy than either parent species alone.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a fungal postharvest rot genus,
scale insects, a persimmon-specific psyllid, tree borers, stink bugs,
anthracnose, additional fungal leaf-spot diseases, and a general
rootstock-linked decline disorder.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — unripe or firm astringent fruit of this species
is a documented cause of a specific type of stomach bezoar in humans,
among the most common plant-based bezoar-forming foods identified in
medical literature. Separately, fruit and seeds of the related American
persimmon rootstock species are documented to cause colic and
gastrointestinal obstruction in horses. Both deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** non-astringent fruit is graded and packed cold (avoiding
  the mid-range chilling-injury band documented for at least one
  cultivar); astringent fruit is either treated with carbon dioxide or
  ethanol to remove astringency while staying firm, or allowed/induced
  to soften (via room-temperature ripening, ethylene exposure, or
  freeze-thaw) into soft pulp; astringent fruit is also peeled, hung and
  kneaded into the dried product documented in Finding 6, with peels as
  a by-product; green fruit is pressed into juice that is then aged for
  one to three years into the traditional tannin-rich product described
  in Finding 6; leaves are used for tea and, in at least one Japanese
  region, as a traditional food-wrapping material. Rootstock seed is
  grown into seedlings and grafted into nursery trees.
- Not covered by this research in detail: a fermented persimmon
  vinegar; a Korean spiced dried-persimmon punch; a Taiwanese
  lime-water "crisp persimmon" preparation; frozen pulp; persimmon
  wood; the wild form of this species as a distinct foraged resource.

In Jewish religious law, this crop is subject to the same first-three-
years fruit prohibition applied to fruit trees generally, counted from
the date of planting; how that count treats a grafted nursery tree was
not checked specifically for this crop. Separate from formal religious
law, Korean folklore holds that a dried persimmon can frighten away a
tiger, a folk tale rather than a ritual practice.

## Open research questions

- Sugar content for at least one widely grown astringent cultivar is
  reported quite differently across two sources (roughly 17-19%
  soluble solids versus 21-23%).
- Chill-hour requirement for this crop is reported inconsistently
  across sources, ranging from about 100 hours up to 200 hours
  depending on source and cultivar.
- World production figures for a recent reference year vary across
  sources by a meaningful margin (roughly 5.0-5.9 million tonnes), as
  does China's reported share of world production (roughly 66-80%).
- Carbon-dioxide dose for de-astringency treatment is reported
  differently across two technical sources (a lower historical figure
  versus a notably higher figure used for Europe's leading cultivar).
- Controlled-atmosphere storage duration is reported as five months in
  one technical source versus six months in another.
- Detailed labor-hour figures, soil nutrient uptake, salinity
  threshold, and overall productive lifespan all rest on estimates
  rather than direct, crop-specific measurement.
- Fresh-to-dried weight ratio for the traditional drying process
  described in Finding 6 is estimated by analogy to a dried-fig
  product's documented moisture content, since no persimmon-specific
  drying-moisture study was found.
- Detailed pest and disease management, a fermented vinegar product,
  and the wild form of this species as a foraging resource were not
  researched in depth.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 150-230 days from bud break to harvest)
is derived from documented bud-break timing (relatively late, since
this crop needs more heat accumulation to leaf out than several other
deciduous fruit trees) through to harvest, which runs from roughly
August for early cultivars through December for the latest-maturing
widely grown non-astringent cultivar.

Yield tiers are derived from a combination of sources: the world
average is taken directly from a recent-year international production
statistic; "good" is taken from a documented multi-year California
state average, converted to metric units; "exceptional" is taken
directly from a documented Middle Eastern national yield figure, cross-
checked against a leading Chinese cultivar's own documented
full-production figure; "poor" is estimated at roughly half the world
average, reflecting unthinned, unpollinated, or neglected trees with
heavy fruit drop and biennial bearing. A per-tree sanity check (typical
fruit count and weight per mature tree, multiplied by standard planting
density) falls consistent with the good-to-exceptional range.

A fresh-to-dried weight ratio (roughly 3.5-4 kg fresh fruit per kg of
dried product) is estimated by analogy to a dried-fig product's
documented moisture content, since no persimmon-specific drying-
moisture study was found; this likely has real error given the absence
of a direct source.

A seasonal water-use index is derived from a closely analogous
stone-fruit crop-coefficient curve (the closest match in the standard
international crop-evapotranspiration reference, since this crop has no
dedicated entry there), integrated across an estimated bud-break-to-
leaf-fall season length and adjusted upward based on documented Spanish
research indicating this crop's own summer water use may run higher
than the stone-fruit analogy suggests; the associated drought-buffer
estimate uses standard soil-water-holding assumptions for a
moderate-to-deep root zone.

Labor-hour estimates by operation are built from documented task
descriptions (hand thinning, multi-pick hand clipping, winter pruning,
de-astringency treatment, and drying-specific hand labor) rather than a
dedicated cost study, and are treated as a rough estimate accordingly.

Soil nutrient uptake figures are estimated from the fruit's documented
dry-matter content and a typical nitrogen content assumption, scaled up
to account for ongoing leaf and wood growth as is done for other
tree-fruit crops this project has researched, since no persimmon-specific
nutrient-removal study was found.

A yield-ramp profile (time to first harvest, ramp to full bearing, peak
daily yield) is derived from documented first-fruit and full-production
timing (year three to years eight-to-ten); peak daily yield is
calculated by dividing the "good" yield tier by an estimated multi-week
harvest window reflecting the typical two-to-three-pick harvest pattern
across a cultivar mix.
