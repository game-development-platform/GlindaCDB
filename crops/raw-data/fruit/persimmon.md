## Naming and scope

*Diospyros kaki* Thunb. (Ebenaceae), the Japanese, Oriental or Chinese
persimmon [1][2].
- **Origin:** native to Japan, China, Burma and the Himalayas and Khasi
  Hills of northern India; wild in China up to 1,830-2,500 m [1].
  Introduced to the US in the mid-1800s [1][4].
- **Fruit:** a berry with a large calyx and a few large seeds [2].
- **Astringency groups:** Kyoto University grouped 40 cultivars as
  pollination-constant non-astringent (PCNA), pollination-variant
  non-astringent (PVNA), pollination-variant astringent (PVA) and
  pollination-constant astringent (PCA) [1]. Non-astringent fruit lose
  astringency while still firm; astringent fruit hold about 2% soluble
  tannin at harvest until fully soft [1][5].
- **Scope:** one cropID; the four groups are cultivar groups.
- **Gate check (2026-10-11, re-confirmed 2026-11-23): passes.** The three
  other *Diospyros* rows are separate species with their own fruit.

**Relatives in this project:**
- American persimmon (`persimmon_american`, `persimmon-american.md`):
  the main rootstock in the southern US;
- date-plum (`persimmon_dateplum`, `persimmon-dateplum.md`): a
  California rootstock;
- Texas persimmon (`persimmon_texas`, `persimmon-texas.md`): not graft
  compatible with kaki [4].

**Proposed:** none.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — production is concentrated in East Asia.**
- **2016 (FAO via a review):** China 3.93, Korea 0.35 and Japan 0.23
  million tons [5].
- **2023 (snippets, conflicting):** world 5.07 or 5.9 million tons; China
  about 4.0-4.06 million tons; harvested area about 1.3 Mha [8].
- **Spain:** the second producer and largest exporter, built on 'Rojo
  Brillante' in Valencia (snippet) [7].
- **US:** California made 92% of the US crop in 1970 [1]. Florida had 266
  acres in 2017, half in four north-central counties [3].

**Finding 2 — late flowering spares the flowers, but fruit drop is the
real yield problem.**
- **Timing:** flowers open on current-season shoots several weeks after
  bud break, so early-spring frost rarely kills flower buds [3]. The tree
  needs moderate heat to start growth and leafs out after peach,
  blueberry and pear [3].
- **Flowers:** many cultivars bear only female flowers; male flowers are
  small and clustered [3]. Male and female flowers are usually on
  separate trees [1].
- **Parthenocarpy:** modest crops set without fertilisation, but "this
  trait is not dependable" [1][3]. Seedless fruit drop easily under
  excess fertiliser, heat, cold, water or drought [4].
- **Pollinizers:** 1:15 to 1:40 suggested in Florida ('Gailey',
  'Nishimura') [3]; Morton gives 1 male per 8 females, or 30-60
  pollinators/ha, and 1-2 hives per ha [1]. Pollinated fruit drop less but
  then need thinning [3]. Seeded fruit often have darker flesh [4].
- **Drop and biennial bearing:** a drop after petal fall, and lighter
  drops in June and later; heavy crops lead to biennial bearing [3].

**Finding 3 — astringency is removed by gas, alcohol or drying.**
- **CO2:** 80% CO2 for 24 h at 20 °C removes astringency from 'Hachiya'
  while keeping it firm [2]. Morton gives 95% for 24 h at 20-25 °C, after
  which fruit soften quickly [1]. 'Rojo Brillante' uses 95-100% CO2 for
  24 h at 20 °C (snippet) [7].
- **Ethylene:** 10 µL/L at 20 °C works for 'Hachiya' but over-softens
  fruit [2]; 3 days in an airtight container (Japan) [1].
- **Alcohol:** ethanol spray, or 10 days to 2 weeks in sake kegs (Japan)
  [1].
- **Other:** warm water (40 °C for 24 h for 'Hachiya'), lime water,
  freezing, smoke and drying [1][5].

**Finding 4 — 'Fuyu' is injured in a band above freezing.**
- **Band:** 'Fuyu'-type fruit show chilling injury between 2 and 15 °C,
  worst at 5-7 °C and slowest at 0 °C; 'Hachiya' is not
  chilling-sensitive [2]. Symptoms are flesh softening, browning and
  water-soaking after transfer to warmth [2].
- **Storage:** 0 ± 1 °C, 90-95% RH; freezing point −2 °C; about 3 months
  in ethylene-free air and up to 5 months in 3-5% O2 + 5-8% CO2 [2].
  UF gives 30 days at room temperature, 2 months at 0 °C and up to 6
  months in CA for 'Fuyu' [3].
- **Ethylene:** 1 and 10 µL/L at 20 °C soften fruit below the 17.8 N
  marketability limit in 6 and 2 days [2]; 0.1 µL/L already worsens
  chilling injury [2].
- **CA limits:** O2 below 3% or CO2 above 10% for over a month cause
  off-flavours, failure to ripen or browning [2].

**Finding 5 — rootstock is set by region, disease and compatibility.**
- **Southern US:** *D. virginiana* — wet- and drought-tolerant, cold-hardy,
  compatible with most scions, but suckers and is less uniform [3]. It
  "significantly contributes to cold-resistance" [1]. Texas requires it
  because Oriental persimmon is highly susceptible to cotton root rot
  [4].
- **California:** *D. lotus* or *D. kaki* [3][4]. *D. lotus* is partly
  incompatible with 'Fuyu', 'Jiro' and 'Izu' and raises 'Hachiya' drop [3];
  Morton reports "much lower yields" on it in California [1].
- **Dwarfing:** Japanese selections SH11, MKR1 and FDR-1 give high yield
  efficiency and less labour per tree [3].
- **Cuttings:** root poorly (9% from grafted trees, 22% from
  micropropagated trees), so trees are grafted [3].

**Finding 6 — a Botryosphaeria canker caps tree life in the Deep South.**
- *Botryosphaeria* canker is "the limiting factor for growing persimmons
  in Florida and the Deep South", often limiting tree life to about 8-12
  years; there is no good chemical control [3].
- By contrast, Texas reports persimmons "have no serious insect or
  disease problems" [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** deciduous tree, 4.5-18 m [1]; to about 12 m in
  Texas, some shrubs under 3 m [4].
- **Harvest pattern:** one crop a year, August to December by cultivar
  [3]; clipped with the calyx, in 2-3 picks [2][3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** bearing from year 3, full production at
  8-10 years [3]; 3-4 years for many cultivars, 5-6 for others [1].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Chill:** low; 100-200 h below 7 °C, and dormancy may release best
    just above 0-7 °C [3].
  - **Range:** "subtropical to mild-temperate"; "will not fruit in
    tropical lowlands" [1]. Better quality in central and north Florida;
    astringent types do better in the south [3].
  - **Cold:** Middle Atlantic trees survived −6.7 °C and fruited 40 years
    [1]. Buds that have started growth are damaged by −2.8 °C for 1 hour
    [3]. 'Fuyu' is freeze-susceptible in Texas; 'Izu' seems hardier [4].
    Overhead sprinkling protects growing trees [3].
  - **Frost as a quality event:** fruit ripens around the first fall
    frost, but frost is not needed to remove tannins [4].
  - **Elevation:** sea level to 1,524 m in Central America; above 1,000 m
    in Indonesia [1].
- **Composition (per 100 g, USDA via a review) [5]:** raw: water 80.3 g,
  energy 70 kcal, carbohydrate 18.6 g, fibre 3.6 g, protein 0.58 g, fat
  0.19 g, potassium 161 mg, vitamin C 7.5 mg. Dried: water 23.0 g, energy
  274 kcal, carbohydrate 73.4 g, fibre 14.5 g, potassium 802 mg, vitamin C
  not detected. Morton gives raw 77 kcal, 78.6 g moisture, 11 mg vitamin C
  [1].
- **Edibility:** non-astringent fruit raw when firm; astringent fruit only
  when fully soft, de-astringed or dried [1][4][5]. Morton says the skin
  "should not be eaten" [1].
- **Pollination:** Finding 2 [1][3][4].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - **California 2013-14:** 5.4 tons/acre, about 12.1 t/ha [3].
  - **Florida:** 3.6-5.4 tons/acre in the south (8.1-12.1 t/ha), higher
    in the north; lower for non-astringent types [3].
  - **Brazil:** about 15 t/ha [1].
  - **Per tree:** 22.6-40.8 kg (young) to 150-250 kg (full-grown) [1];
    150-300 fruit per mature tree [3] (see Estimates).
- **Water use:** no FAO-56 persimmon row (checked). Overwatering at
  flowering and fruiting raises fruit drop; early-season deficit cuts drop
  but shrinks fruit; late deficit speeds colour [3]. Irrigate in the
  spring flush and summer [4]. Nectarine-like stone-fruit coefficients
  are not used here.
- **Propagation:** whip grafting in dormancy or chip budding 2.5-15 cm
  above the soil [3]; seedling rootstocks from *D. virginiana* seed,
  stored moist at 2-7 °C and grafted the next winter [3]. Scions keep
  weeks at 4.4 °C or months at 0 °C [3]. Clean stock: Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. Operations: winter pruning and
  training, hand thinning about 3 weeks after flowering to 15 cm apart,
  clipping in 2-3 picks [2][3].
- **Soil:**
  - **Preferred:** upland sandy loam [3]; deep friable subsoil; "Good
    drainage is essential" [1]; any soil that does not stand in water
    (Texas) [4].
  - **pH:** 6-7; manganese and iron scarce above pH 7 [3].
  - **Salinity:** "sensitive to salinity" [3]; FAO rates *D. virginiana*
    sensitive (S), with no threshold [6].
  - **Nutrients:** 10-10-10 rising to 7.75 lb per tree in year 10, split
    50/25/25 in March, June and September [3]; Texas gives 40 lb N/acre a
    year [4] (see Estimates). Avoid heavy quick-release nitrogen, which
    raises drop [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Finding 4 [2][3]. Respiration 4-8 mg CO2/kg·h
  at 0 °C, 20-24 at 20 °C [2]. Fresh-cut 'Fuyu' slices keep 7 days in air
  at 5 °C [2].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** 4.5 × 6 m (145 trees/acre) for a modified central
    leader; palmette trellis 290 trees/acre; root restriction over 1,500
    trees/acre [3]. Texas: 4.6-5.5 m in rows 6 m apart [4]. Japan: 1,000
    trees/ha thinned to 200/ha over 10-15 years [1].
  - **Planting:** bare-root trees December-February, cut back to about
    0.75 m [3]; remove at least half the top [4].
  - **Site:** avoid frost pockets; windbreaks, since fruit-laden shoots
    break [3].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **PCNA:** 'Fuyu' (needs mild areas; 230-250 g desirable), 'Jiro',
    'Izu', 'Suruga' [1][2][4].
  - **Astringent:** 'Hachiya' (dried in Japan), 'Tanenashi', 'Tamopan',
    'Triumph', 'Eureka' (best commercial variety in Texas, self-fruitful,
    seeded) [1][4]; 'Rojo Brillante' (Spain) [7].
  - **Pollinizers:** 'Gailey', 'Nishimura' [3].
  - **Cold-hardy (Mid-Atlantic):** 'Eureka', 'Okame', 'Yokono',
    'Tsurushigaki' [1].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Late frost on new growth:** Climate zones [3].
  - **Wind:** breaks fruit-laden shoots [3].
  - **Sun:** sunburn on some cultivars [1].
  - **Stress:** heat, cold, drought or excess water cause seedless-fruit
    drop [4].
- **Water regime:** upland; no flooded regime; waterlogging avoided
  [1][4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Non-astringent:** firm, from yellow-orange to full colour [3];
    California minimum is a Munsell colour-chart standard [2].
  - **Astringent:** full colour, soft or near-soft (fresh), or mature and
    hard for curing or drying [1][3].
  - **On-tree storage:** fruit holds on the tree well into winter [4].
- **Soil biology:** cotton root rot in Texas (Finding 5) [4]; crown gall
  occasionally [4]; *D. lotus* gets root rot and crown gall in Florida
  [1]. Replant: Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** not on Florida's noxious-weed or
  invasive-plant lists [3]. Quarantine: limited trade; no specific US
  requirements for Chilean imports [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Bezoars (snippets):** tannins from unripe persimmon (shibuol)
  polymerise in stomach acid into diospyrobezoars, which are harder to
  dissolve than other phytobezoars; cola alone rarely dissolves them,
  and endoscopic fragmentation is usually needed [9].
- **Pests (Florida):** white peach scale, persimmon psylla, twig girdler
  (which may spread wilt), tree borers, stink bugs (on non-astringent
  fruit), thrips, caterpillars and scarabs [3].
- **Diseases:** *Botryosphaeria* canker (Finding 6); leaf and fruit spots
  (*Cercospora*, *Gloeosporium* anthracnose, *Colletotrichum*,
  *Pseudomonas* bacterial blast and others) [3]; Cephalosporium wilt [1];
  postharvest *Alternaria* rot, latent until ripening [2].
- **Calyx separation:** caused losses in New Zealand; avoid excess N and
  thin early [2].
- **Vertebrates:** opossums, raccoons, birds, deer, rats [4]; flying foxes
  [1].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh non-astringent):** clipped → graded → cooled to 0 °C,
  kept away from 2-15 °C and from ethylene → sold firm [2][3].
- **Chain 2 (astringent, firm):** CO2 or alcohol de-astringency → sold
  firm (Finding 3) [1][2][7].
- **Chain 3 (dried):** firm astringent fruit peeled and hung 30-50 days
  in the sun, kneaded every 4-5 days, sweated 10 days in heaps, then dried
  in wind (Chinese method for 'Hachiya' in California) [1]. White sugar
  powder forms on the surface [5]. Fresh-to-dried ratio about 3.9:1 (see
  Estimates).
- **Other foods:** pulp in desserts, jam, frozen whole or pulped;
  molasses, cider, beer, wine; roasted seeds as coffee substitute [1].
- **Non-food:** tannin from unripe fruit used in sake brewing, dyeing and
  wood preserving, and painted on paper or cloth as an insect and
  moisture repellent [1]; dark streaked wood for inlays [1] and golf clubs
  [4].
- **Not detailed here:** kakishibu ageing, vinegar, leaf tea.

## Estimates and derivations

**Yields to t/ha** [1][3]: 5.4 short tons/acre × 2.2417 = 12.1 t/ha;
3.6 × 2.2417 = 8.1 t/ha. Morton's 6.5 tons/acre × 2.2417 = 14.6 t/ha
(Morton prints 15).

**Per-tree check** [2][3]: 150-300 fruit × 0.23 kg = 35-69 kg per tree ×
358 trees/ha = 12-25 t/ha. Morton's 150-250 kg per tree × 200 trees/ha =
30-50 t/ha, so his figures fit only large, widely spaced old trees.

**Tree densities** [3]: 145 ÷ 0.404686 = 358/ha; 290 → 717/ha; 1,500 →
3,707/ha.

**Nitrogen** [3][4]: year-10 rate 7.75 lb 10-10-10 per tree = 0.775 lb N
× 0.4536 = 0.352 kg × 358 trees/ha = 126 kg N/ha. Texas: 40 lb/acre ×
0.4536 ÷ 0.404686 = 45 kg/ha.

**Fresh-to-dried ratio** [5]: dry matter 100 − 80.32 = 19.68% raw and
100 − 23.01 = 76.99% dried; 76.99 ÷ 19.68 = 3.9 kg of peeled flesh per kg
of dried fruit (peel and calyx losses not included).

**Temperatures** [1][3]: 27 °F = −2.8 °C; 20 °F = −6.7 °C; 45 °F = 7.2 °C.
