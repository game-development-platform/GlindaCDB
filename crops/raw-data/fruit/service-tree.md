## Naming and scope

*Sorbus domestica* L. (Rosaceae, tribe Maleae); segregate-genus synonym
**_Cormus domestica_** (L.) Spach [1][2]. Common names: **true service tree**,
**service tree**, **sorb**, **sorb tree**, **whitty pear**; German
**Speierling**, French **cormier** (fruit: *corme*) [1][2]. The fruit is called
a **sorb apple** or **sorb** [1]. This doc covers the `service_tree` row,
proposed from `medlar.md` [6].

The fruit occurs in two shapes — an **apple-shaped** and a **pear-shaped**
("whitty pear") form — a within-species variation, not separate rows [1][5].

**Distinctiveness (gate check, 2026-11-27): passes.** A validly distinct
species and genus with its own fruit, cultivation and uses [1][6]:
- **vs medlar** (`medlar`, *Mespilus germanica*, the proposing doc): both are
  Maleae pomes **edible only after bletting** (their shared trait and the reason
  medlar flagged it), but service tree bears **small (~2-3 cm) apple- or
  pear-shaped pomes in clusters** versus medlar's single large hollow-ended
  fruit, and it is additionally a **prized timber tree** and a **cider/apple-
  wine additive** (Findings 2-3) [1][6].
- **vs azarole** (`azarole`, *Crataegus*): a different genus, and azarole is
  eaten ripe off the tree with no bletting [6].
- **vs pear / apple / quince / rowan:** distinct genus or species; rowan
  (*S. aucuparia*) has small red berries and is not this project's fruit [1][6].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the harvested fruit is inedible until bletted, like medlar.**
Picked off the tree the sorb is **hard and astringent** [1]; it becomes
**sweet, soft and aromatic only after bletting** (over-softening), with a
flavour likened to **spiced pears**, exactly the medlar process [1]. Bletting
often **begins on the tree**, and the fruit can also be **dried and used like
prunes** [1]. Because it must be conditioned off the plant before it is food,
this is the project's bletting/off-plant-conditioning pattern (schema Entry 41),
shared with medlar [1][6].

**Finding 2 — its most distinctive use is as a tannin additive fermented into
apple wine, not as a fruit in its own right.** In Hesse, Germany, sorbs are
**fermented together with apple juice** to make a regional **apple-wine /
cider variant (Speierling Apfelwein)**: the fruit's **tannins help clarify and
preserve** the apple wine [1][3]. Reported proportions are **historically about
3:1–3:2 service-fruit:apple juice, and roughly 5-15% in modern practice** (the
latter figures are anecdotal and need firming) [3]. The fruit is otherwise made
into jam, juice, high-quality **liquors/brandy**, and was used medicinally
(intestinal complaints) [1]. So, commercially, the service tree matters as a
**minor additive to another crop's product** (apple wine) more than as a fresh
fruit — a cross-crop product linkage new to this project's schema (new
Entry 71) [1][3][6].

**Finding 3 — a prized hardwood: the tree is grown for timber as much as
fruit.** *Sorbus domestica* yields a **hard, heavy, fine-grained, valuable
timber**, used historically for **mechanical/precision parts** — wine-press
screws and gear teeth, rulers/yardsticks, tool handles, inlays, musical and
measuring instruments, and veneer [2][4]. **Knot-free timber** is grown by
pruning the lower branches of young trees, a practice that itself **slows the
tree's growth** [4]. In Central-European forestry the species is valued as a
high-value hardwood, which is a major reason it is planted at all [2][4].

**Finding 4 — very slow, very long-lived, and rare enough to be a conservation
subject.** The tree is **slow-growing and long-lived**, commonly reaching ~200
years and occasionally **400+** [5]. Time to meaningful fruiting is **slow and
inconsistently reported** — RHS gives 20-50 years to maturity, while a US
(Wisconsin) trial's catalogue claimed 2-3 years but saw **no fruit in six
years** [1]. Though spread by cultivation since **Roman times** across W/C/S
Europe, NW Africa (Atlas Mts) and SW Asia to the Caucasus, it is **rare and
endangered through much of its range**, with **weak natural regeneration**, so
conservation and breeding programmes (e.g. EUFORGEN guidelines) rely on
**planting** rather than natural seeding [2]. It is grown only on a **small
scale**, and trials find it **adaptable and notably free of pests and disease**
[1][5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous tree, slow-growing and long-lived
  (~200, to 400+ yr); fruit ripens in autumn [1][5].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; fruit gathered in autumn, often as it
  begins to blet on the tree, or laid down to blet off it (Finding 1) [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** **Slow and conflicting** — RHS 20-50 yr to
  maturity; a US trial's 2-3 yr catalogue claim was not borne out (no fruit in 6
  yr) (Finding 4) [1].
- **Climate zones:** Temperate to warm-temperate; native W/C/S Europe, NW
  Africa (Atlas Mts) and SW Asia to the Caucasus; frost-hardy [2]. No chill
  figure sourced.
- **Composition:** **Astringent/high-tannin** when unripe (hence bletting);
  a specific tannin % was **not sourced for *S. domestica*** this pass (the
  ~2.6-3% figure in `medlar.md` is for medlar, not borrowed here) [1][6].
  Per-100 g nutrition: Not sourced.
- **Edibility:** **Not edible fresh** — astringent; edible only **after
  bletting**, then sweet and spiced-pear-like; also dried like prunes
  (Finding 1) [1].
- **Pollination:** Not detailed; insect-pollinated as a Maleae pome, no
  cultivar-compatibility wrinkle sourced [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced; not expected to apply.
- **Yield:** Not sourced (small-scale, local; soft fruit hard to transport) [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced; drought-tolerant reputation but not quantified
  here [2].
- **Propagation:** By **seed** (slow, and conservation plantings depend on it)
  and by **grafting** for named/fruit selections; natural regeneration is weak,
  so establishment is largely by planting (Finding 4) [1][2]. No clonal/virus
  issue sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced; **mechanical harvest judged unlikely** (soft fruit)
  [1].
- **Soil:** Tolerant of a range, including dry and calcareous forest soils;
  pH/texture detail and **salinity:** Not specifically sourced [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** The fruit is **soft and hard to transport**, so
  trade is mostly local; keeping is tied to the bletting window rather than a
  long cold-store regime [1].
- **Establishment:** Planted as grafted or seedling trees; for timber, lower
  branches are pruned to grow knot-free wood (which slows growth) (Finding 3)
  [4].
- **Cultivars:** Apple-shaped and pear-shaped ("whitty pear") forms; named
  fruiting selections exist in parts of Europe but were not enumerated this pass
  [1][5].
- **Weather response:** Frost-hardy and drought-tolerant; bletting can be
  **initiated by frost** on the tree, as for medlar (a quality event, not only a
  hazard) [1].
- **Water regime:** Ordinary rainfall; no paddy requirement [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One autumn window; fruit picked hard and
  bletted, or allowed to blet on the tree first (Finding 1) [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced; trials report no notable disease/pest pressure
  [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native and **rare/endangered** across its
  range — the conservation concern runs toward protecting it, not controlling
  it; no invasive listing found, and no cultivation/movement restriction
  specific to it found [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced; a forest/orchard tree, not a
  protected crop [2].

## Hazards

- **Seeds contain cyanogenic compounds** (amygdalin-type), as in other Maleae
  pips (apple, pear, quince) — toxic only in quantity and not a concern for the
  eaten bletted flesh; carried as the same **TO BE ADDRESSED** seed-cyanide gap
  the project defers for pome/stone fruit (see `quince.md`), since no
  *S. domestica*-specific figure was sourced [6]. **No other hazards found in
  this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fruit, food):** hard astringent sorb → **bletted** (off the tree or
  finishing on it) → sweet soft flesh eaten fresh or **dried like prunes**; also
  → jam, juice [1].
- **Chain (apple-wine additive — the distinctive use):** sorbs → **fermented
  together with apple juice** → **Speierling apple wine / cider** (Hesse), the
  tannins clarifying and preserving it; ~3:1–3:2 historically, ~5-15% today
  (Finding 2) [1][3].
- **Chain (spirits):** fruit → fermented/distilled → **service-tree brandy /
  high-quality liquors** [1].
- **Chain (timber):** trunk → **hard, fine-grained hardwood** → precision/
  mechanical parts, instruments, rulers, inlays, veneer (Finding 3) [2][4].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** medicinal use (historical, intestinal);
  precise juice composition; named fruiting cultivars [1].

## Open research questions

- **The apple-wine blend proportions** (historic 3:1–3:2 vs modern 5-15%) rest
  on an anecdotal source and need an authoritative German cider reference
  [3].
- **Tannin content and fruit composition** of *S. domestica* specifically were
  not sourced (the medlar figures are not transferable) [1][6].
- **Bearing age** is contradicted (RHS 20-50 yr vs a 2-3 yr catalogue claim
  that failed in trial); a reliable grafted-tree figure is needed [1].
- **Timber properties** (density/hardness numbers) were described qualitatively
  but not quantified in the sources read [4].
- **Named cultivars / the apple- vs pear-shaped forms** were not enumerated or
  tied to use [1][5].
