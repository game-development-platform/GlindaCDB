## Naming and scope

*Mangifera indica* (Anacardiaceae), with several hundred to over 500
named cultivars falling into two broad genetic groups: a subtropical
Indian group, mostly monoembryonic and therefore requiring grafting to
propagate true-to-type, and a tropical Southeast Asian group, mostly
polyembryonic and therefore often coming true from seed [1][3]. These
two groups are treated here as cultivar groups rather than separate
crop entries. Green (unripe) mango is simply an early harvest stage of
the same fruit, not a separate crop.

The genus this fruit belongs to contains dozens of species, more than
two dozen of which bear edible fruit, centered in the Malesian region
[5]. A cluster of related wet-tropics *Mangifera* species (grown in
humid equatorial climates, with graft-incompatibility against this
species) is a candidate for its own future crop entry and is not
covered further here.

This entry is grounded against two other grafted or seed-propagated
tropical tree-fruit crops this project has also researched: one shares
its off-tree ripening behavior, chilling sensitivity, and propagation
questions, while the other shares a dry-spell-triggered flowering
mechanism.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — dryness makes flowers; rain at bloom ruins them.**
Dryness stimulates flowering while rain discourages it; the ideal
climate combines a substantial wet season with roughly eight dry
months [2]. Rain, dew, or fog during bloom reduces flowering and
favors fungal disease, especially anthracnose [2][3]. Commercial
growers in at least one major producing country deliberately withhold
irrigation for two to three months before the expected flowering
period, then water heavily at bloom and monthly until the rains return
[2]. Where the natural climate does not provide this dry trigger,
flowering is chemically or mechanically induced, using potassium
nitrate and a specific plant-growth-regulator spray (a technique first
developed in the Philippines in the 1970s, before which trees flowered
only every year and a half or so), soil-applied paclobutrazol, or
traditional practices such as smoke exposure, branch girdling, and
deliberately withholding water and nitrogen [1][2][15]. Trees over
roughly a decade old tend toward alternate bearing, and growers
counter this by deliberately removing half the flower clusters in a
heavy ("on") year [2].

**Finding 2 — a tender tree with no cold acclimation.** Young trees can
be killed by a light freeze; mature trees suffer leaf and twig injury
at a somewhat harder freeze lasting a few hours; and flowers and small
fruit are damaged by even a mild chill lasting a few hours [3]. Trees
do not acclimate to cold, and one major US growing region found no
measurable difference in cold resistance between its standard
varieties — though one classic reference describes a specific named
cultivar as notably cold-resistant, a conflict that is kept here
unresolved rather than picking a side [2][3].

**Finding 3 — picked mature-green, ripened off the tree, and
chilling-sensitive.** Fruit matures 100-150 days after flowering,
recognizable when the fruit's "shoulders" rise above the stem end [3][4].
It ripens at a warm room temperature over roughly three to eight days;
a short ethylene exposure speeds and evens coloring [2][3][4].
Mature-green fruit stores for two to four weeks under cool (but not
cold) refrigeration; most cultivars show chilling injury below a
specific threshold temperature, though at least one named cultivar
tolerates distinctly colder storage, and fully ripe fruit tolerates
colder storage than unripe fruit [2][4]. Disease (anthracnose and
stem-end rot) is the main limit on storage life [4]. Because this fruit
hosts fruit flies, export fruit bound for fly-free markets is commonly
treated with a hot-water immersion calibrated by fruit size [4][13].

**Finding 4 — a large green-fruit economy.** In South Asia and
Southeast Asia, a substantial share of the crop is used while still
green and unripe: pickled, made into chutney, sun-dried and ground into
a tart seasoning powder, used raw in salads, or brined [1][2]. Seedling
trees and fibrous-fleshed cultivars are often specifically grown for
this green-fruit market rather than for ripe fresh eating; green
seedling-type fruit can be held in cool storage for roughly six weeks
[2].

**Finding 5 — hazards from sap, skin, smoke, and leaves.** Sap and
peel skin cause delayed contact dermatitis in sensitive people, similar
to poison-ivy reactions, with the most severe reactions in people
already sensitized to that chemical family [1][2]. Stem sap also stains
and burns the fruit's own skin at harvest ("sap burn"), so fruit is
routinely cleaned of sap residue and washed [3][4]. Wood smoke from
burning prunings is an irritant [2]. Leaves fed continuously to cattle
(in a traditional dye-making byproduct stream) have proven fatal [2][10].

**Finding 6 — yields and labor.** A detailed agroeconomic study of a
major Latin American growing region found roughly 236 trees per hectare
at a 7×7 m spacing, a first crop in year four, a full mature-bearing
yield of about 20 t/ha sustained from roughly years seven through
eighteen, and total labor at full bearing of around 1,300 hours per
hectare per year, with irrigation and harvest together making up over
half of that labor [7]. Elsewhere, documented yields vary widely: one
major US growing state has averaged in the low-to-mid 30s of tonnes per
hectare; an irrigated Brazilian orchard reported around 45 t/ha; a
review source describes typical commercial yields as often under 5
t/ha but reaching 20-30 t/ha under good management; and a major South
Asian producing country (accounting for a large share of world output)
averages around 9 t/ha [2][8][12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A large evergreen perennial tree (reportedly
  still fruiting at several centuries old); perennial bearing with one
  main harvest season per region, picked across several passes.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single natural harvest window per cultivar in
  most regions; induced flowering can shift or add off-season crops in
  the tropics, and some cultivars reportedly give a double crop, though
  no sourced yield data for a second window was found (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Tropical and subtropical, frost-free, with a
  seasonally dry climate pattern strongly preferred for reliable
  flowering (Finding 1).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible when ripe; green fruit is also widely
  eaten raw, typically with salt, for its sourness (Finding 4).
- **Pollination:** Flower clusters are mostly male with a smaller,
  cultivar-dependent share of hermaphrodite flowers, pollinated mainly
  by flies and thrips with some contribution from honey bees; bagging
  flowers to exclude insects measurably lowers fruit set, so insects
  clearly help, though true pollination dependency is not firmly
  established in the sources reached (Finding and Estimates below).
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield is roughly in the high
  single digits of tonnes per hectare, with well-managed orchards
  reaching 20-30 t/ha and exceptional irrigated orchards reaching
  higher still; poorly managed orchards can fall under 5 t/ha
  (Finding 6).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient table
  exists for this crop; the best available figure is a measured
  seasonal water-use study from a Brazilian irrigated orchard, showing
  distinct water demand by growth phase (lowest during the dry
  flower-induction period, highest during fruit development). The crop
  needs good drainage; overwatering causes root rot and poor fruit
  quality.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by grafting (veneer, cleft, or chip
  budding, among other named techniques) for most commercial plantings,
  or by polyembryonic seedling where the cultivar group allows it;
  cuttings and air-layering are both impractical for this crop.
- **Labor:** The most detailed sourced labor data comes from the same
  Latin American agroeconomic study noted in Finding 6; other labor
  tiers are rough estimates scaled from that figure, since no other
  detailed labor-hour study was found.
- **Soil:** Deep (over roughly 1.2 m), well-drained soil from loam to
  sandy loam, tolerant of sand, gravel, and even oolitic limestone if
  drainage is adequate; young trees need high nitrogen while bearing
  trees need more potassium and phosphorus; excess nitrogen on sandy
  acid soils causes a specific fruit disorder (see below); classified
  as salt-sensitive in a standard international reference, without a
  specific numeric threshold given.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees typically bear first within three to
  five years, seedling trees around six years; the economic orchard
  cycle used in the detailed Latin American study runs about twenty
  years, though trees are documented to bear for centuries under
  favorable conditions. Alternate bearing becomes an issue once trees
  pass roughly a decade old.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Anthracnose and stem-end rot dominate
  postharvest losses; powdery mildew and a specific panicle-malformation
  disease (linked to a fungus plus mites) affect flowering; fruit flies
  are a major quarantine concern, along with a seed-boring weevil hidden
  inside the stone.
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named cultivars across the Indian and
  Southeast Asian groups plus a distinct Florida/US export set differ
  substantially in disease resistance, storage life, flower-type ratio,
  and intended use (fresh, processing, or storage) (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked with a pole and catching bag, or clipped with
  a short length of stem retained; picked at the mature-green stage and
  ripened off the tree.
- **Storage:** Mature-green fruit holds for two to four weeks under
  cool (not cold) refrigeration; fully ripe fruit tolerates colder
  storage; chilling injury is a real risk below the cultivar-specific
  threshold (Finding 3).

## Hazards

Sap, peel skin, and leaves contain urushiol-type compounds that cause
delayed contact dermatitis, with the most severe reactions documented
in people already sensitized to similar plant chemicals from other
families; peeled flesh is safe for most people. Flower volatiles can
also cause eye and breathing irritation in sensitive people. Wood smoke
from burning prunings is an irritant and should not be used for
cooking. Continuous feeding of mango leaves to cattle (a traditional
dye-making byproduct stream) has proven fatal in documented cases;
kernel material fed to livestock contains tannins that act as an
antinutrient unless soaked or boiled first. Export fruit is also
subject to fruit-fly and seed-weevil quarantine concerns.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the dermatitis/allergen hazard from sap, skin,
and leaves, and the livestock-toxicity hazard from continuous leaf

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Mature fruit is cleaned of stem sap, hot-water treated
  and graded, cool-stored, then ripened. Ripe fruit is pulped for
  leather or dried into slices. Green fruit becomes a sun-dried,
  ground tart seasoning powder, pickle, chutney, or brined product.
  Stones are processed into kernels, which are soaked and dried for
  flour or fat extraction. Culled fruit goes to pulp. Fresh stones also
  supply seedling rootstock for nursery propagation.
- Not covered by this research: canned slices, frozen mango, and
  aseptic pulp processing; jam, nectar, and sherbet production; the
  underlying flower-induction chemistry in detail; vapor-heat and
  irradiation quarantine treatments; bark dye and gum production.

In several South Asian cultural and religious traditions, mango leaves
and flowers play a symbolic role — for example in door garlands and
ceremonial water-pot displays for festivals and life events, and in
devotional iconography — though sourcing for these traditions is
limited to general web material rather than dedicated references.

## Open research questions

- Cold-resistance varies by source: one classic reference names a
  specific cultivar as notably cold-resistant, while a university
  extension study found no varietal difference in a major US growing
  region — not reconciled.
- Chilling-injury thresholds conflict somewhat between sources for at
  least one specific cultivar — not reconciled.
- No "dry-spell-before-bloom" weather-trigger modeling category
  currently exists in this project's general framework; this crop (like
  at least one other crop this project has researched) needs one.
- Induced flowering can shift or add off-season harvest windows, and
  some cultivars reportedly give a double crop, but no sourced
  second-window yield data was found, so only a single annual harvest
  window is modeled here for now.
- World-average yield is uncertain: standard international production
  statistics lump this crop together with two other tropical fruits in
  at least one widely cited source, so a clean world figure could not
  be derived; the regional figure used here comes from a single major
  producing country instead.
- Pulp and kernel yield fractions from whole fruit are rough estimates,
  not drawn from a dedicated processing-yield study.
- Labor figures for all tiers besides full mature bearing are scaled
  estimates rather than directly sourced figures.
- The detailed labor-share breakdown from the Latin American
  agroeconomic study came out partly garbled when the source document's
  chart was extracted as text; only the combined irrigation-plus-harvest
  share is reliably readable in prose, with other shares reconstructed
  as best as possible from the source chart.
- Whether malformation disease spreads through grafted scionwood was
  not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

Pollination-required is set to false as a baseline default, since
flower clusters are mostly male with modest hermaphrodite representation
and set fruit at a low rate even without reliable pollinators; bagging
experiments show insect pollination raises fruit set meaningfully, so
insects clearly help even though strict dependency is not established
in the sources reached.

Yield tiers are derived from a major South Asian producing country's
national production-over-area figure (world-average tier), a review
source's description of typical poorly managed yields (poor tier), and
two directly sourced figures from the detailed Latin American
agroeconomic study and an irrigated Brazilian orchard study respectively
(good and exceptional tiers).

Seasonal water-use figures and the resulting crop-coefficient-days
estimate are derived from a measured Brazilian irrigated-orchard study
reporting both an annual mean crop coefficient and a six-phase seasonal
breakdown; the two approaches agree reasonably closely.

Effective rooting depth and soil-moisture-depletion allowance are
estimated by analogy to two other tree-fruit crops this project has
researched with similarly deep taprooting (one a citrus crop, one
another grafted tropical tree fruit), cross-checked against the
documented soil depth used in the detailed Latin American study.
Drought tolerance is rated high, reflecting the deliberate grower
practice of withholding irrigation for two to three months before
flowering; the resulting "days without water before yield penalty"
figure is derived by dividing an estimated plant-available soil-water
reserve by the measured mean daily water-use rate from the Brazilian
study.

Peak carbon-uptake rate, growth-stage curve, and within-season stress
multipliers are estimated by analogy to two other evergreen tropical
tree-fruit crops this project has researched, reflecting broadly shared
C3 evergreen physiology.

Labor-hour figures for the full-bearing tier are derived directly from
the Latin American agroeconomic study's documented annual labor cost,
converted from local labor-day units into hours; other tiers are rough
estimates scaled from that figure, since no other detailed commercial
labor-hour study was found. The combined irrigation-and-harvest labor
share is directly stated in the source study's text; the individual
harvest and weeding shares are reconstructed from a partially garbled
source chart and should be treated as less reliable.

Soil nutrient-uptake rates are estimated as somewhat below a comparable
citrus crop this project has researched, since this crop receives
comparatively less nitrogen once bearing; the pH range is drawn directly
from the detailed Latin American study, with the upper bound widened
slightly to account for this crop's documented tolerance of highly
alkaline limestone-derived soils when iron is supplied as a chelate.

Typical cool-storage duration (about three weeks) is derived as the
midpoint of the two-to-four-week documented storage range for
mature-green fruit under cool refrigeration.

Pulp and kernel fractions of whole fruit are rough estimates derived
from documented peel and kernel percentage figures, treated as a
placeholder pending a dedicated processing-yield study.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (peak daily harvest rate, harvest
-plateau length, maximum days fruit can stand before quality decline,
and daily decay rate) are estimated from a documented multi-month
harvest season in one major US growing region, divided across an
assumed single-cultivar harvest window of about a month and a half,
with the maximum standing-days figure reflecting that this fruit drops
and ripens quickly once mature and is not well suited to prolonged
on-tree holding.
