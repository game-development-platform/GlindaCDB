## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus integer* (Thunb.) Merr., family Moraceae, 2n = 56 [1][3].
Common names include chempedak, cempedak, and campedak in Malay and
Indonesian, with several further local names across its range [1]. Two
varieties are recognized [1][3]: var. *integer*, the cultivated
champedak, grown from Peninsular Thailand and Malaysia through Sumatra,
Java, Borneo, Sulawesi, and the Moluccas; and var. *silvestris*, the wild
form native to Peninsular Malaysia and Thailand, with thinner, more acid,
odorless pulp — though one regional study found no consistent difference
between wild and cultivated trees.

A notable naming trap exists in the literature: the same Latin binomial
was long misapplied to a closely related crop in the same genus this
project has also researched, and at least one widely cited agroforestry
reference source still mixes data from the two species together (native
range, vernacular names, seed count, and fodder/timber use that actually
belong to the other species). This synthesis excludes that mixed-up
material and uses only the portions of that source consistent with other
champedak-specific sources.

Champedak is closely related to — and is grown and eaten similarly to —
that other crop (a large cauliflorous, monoecious, latex-bearing tree in
the same genus, whose young fruit is cooked as a vegetable and whose ripe
bulbs and seeds are eaten), but differs enough to merit its own crop
entry: champedak is strictly tropical with no tolerance for a dry season,
while the related crop can tolerate several months of drought and even
light frost; champedak prefers a high water table and tolerates periodic
flooding (with one conflicting source on this point, see Research
findings), while the related crop can die if its roots stand in water for
even a day or two; champedak's fruit is considerably smaller and far
more perishable (a 2-3 day shelf life, versus weeks for the related
crop); champedak has a documented fungus-mediated gall-midge pollination
system; and champedak has at least one distinctive fermented product (a
salted, fermented inner-rind product) not found in the related crop.
Natural and selected hybrids between the two species exist and are noted
here as a cultivar group rather than a separate crop entry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a tropical-rainforest relative of a related crop this
project has also researched, bearing a fruit with only a two-to-three-day
shelf life.** The tree is native to lowland rain forest, common in
secondary forest and locally abundant in primary lowland rain forest, a
long-lived sub-canopy species often found on wet hillsides up to roughly
500 m (occasionally to 1,300 m) [1]. Ripe fruit sounds dull and hollow
when tapped and changes from green toward yellow; other maturity
indicators noted in a compiled grower source include the size of surface
protuberances, smoother skin texture, and a yellowing fruit stalk
[3][4]. The fruit has a short shelf life of only 2-3 days once ripe;
fruit cut with its long stalk can be ripened at home or held briefly for
market, with cool-room storage extending life somewhat, though no
specific storage temperature was found in the sources reviewed [3][4].
In practice this means the fruit has a processing deadline of about
three days with no well-sourced cold-storage regime, and any surplus
harvest tends to be converted into shelf-stable products (Finding 5).

**Finding 2 — national agricultural statistics for a major producing
country give solidly sourced real-world yield figures.** Official
agricultural statistics for 2022 and 2023 both show a national average
yield of roughly 6.0 t/ha on harvested area, against a stated
"potential" yield of about 15.2 t/ha — implying that actual orchard
yields run well below what well-managed cultivation could achieve [6][7].
Only about 57-58% of planted area was actually harvested in either year,
reflecting a mix of young, non-bearing trees. Per-tree yield figures vary
widely across sources: one source gives 16-25 fruits per tree at the
productive stage, another gives 70-80 fruits (90% grade A) from a
10-year-old tree, and a third gives up to 100 fruits a year [10][17]. One
authoritative regional source notes plainly that "there are no yield
records, but champedak is considered a prolific bearer and yields may be
similar to" the related crop in the same genus [1] — the national
statistics now provide exactly the real yield records that source found
lacking.

**Finding 3 — a wet-site tree, with one source in direct conflict on
flooding tolerance.** One authoritative regional source states the
species prefers a shallow water table (roughly 0.5-2 m) and can survive
periodic flooding, even with acidic swamp water; a general reference
source similarly notes tolerance of temporary flooding at substantial
annual rainfall [1][3]. A conflicting source — a regional agricultural
extension blog — instead states the tree "cannot survive root
submersion exceeding 4 hours" and prefers well-drained sandy loam [8].
Separately, flowering itself is apparently triggered by a dry spell
("flowering occurs during dry seasons only"), with regions experiencing
two distinct dry seasons a year producing two annual crops; one
authoritative source describes the species' flowering as "more seasonal"
and "fickle" compared to the related crop in the same genus [1][10].
Taken together, this suggests a tree that cannot tolerate an extended dry
season but that specifically needs a short dry spell to trigger bloom —
a pattern also documented in some other tropical fruit trees this project
has researched. The flooding-tolerance conflict between sources is not
resolved here.

**Finding 4 — an unusual fungus-mediated gall-midge pollination system.**
In one forest study, a specific fungus species was found infecting the
male flower spikes; gall midges feed on the resulting fungal growth and
lay eggs there, and the combination of sticky pollen, the midges'
dominance among floral visitors, and pollen carried on both male and
female flower heads together point to the midges as the actual
pollinators — described by the study's authors as the first documented
pollination mutualism in which a fungus plays an essential role [11].
Insects visit only the scented male flower heads, which lack the nectar
found in female heads; most male heads form on peripheral shoots, while
female heads form only directly on the trunk and main branches
(cauliflory) [5]. Each fruiting head carries 1,400-5,000 individual
flowers, but only 0.8-7% of them actually set seed, and stigmas remain
receptive for 1-2 weeks [1]. Notably, the related crop in the same genus
that this project has also researched is itself pollinated by a
different gall midge species that likewise breeds in fungus-covered male
flower heads — a parallel pollination mechanism across the two species.

**Finding 5 — glut processing turns a highly perishable fruit into
shelf-stable products.** A traditional fermented product from one
regional cuisine (Kalimantan) is made from the inner rind: after the
edible bulbs are removed, the soft pith is separated from the hard outer
skin, cut into small pieces, salted, and fermented in jars or basins for
3-7 days (sometimes up to a month) until soft and yellowish; it then
keeps up to a year and is fried, cooked with rice, or made into a chili
condiment [12][13]. A separate seasonal sweet preserve from another
regional cuisine (Bangka and Lingga) is made by cooking the separated
flesh with brown sugar and a pinch of salt, stirring continuously for
about four hours — a product reportedly invented specifically because
the large harvest at peak season would otherwise spoil before it could
be used [14]. Battered, deep-fried bulbs are also a common regional
street food [4][16]. Seeds are roasted or boiled in salted water for
about 30 minutes and have a thin edible coating; young fruit is cooked in
coconut milk as a curry or soup, and young leaves are eaten as a
vegetable [1][4].

**Finding 6 — disease and fruit flies set much of the orchard labor.** A
bacterial dieback disease is described as "by far the most serious
disease" of this crop — it begins in growing shoots and spreads
downward, eventually killing the tree [1]. A separate fungal disease
(pink disease) is also documented [8]. Fruit flies are managed by
wrapping young fruit (at around 1.5 months of age) in newspaper, or by
bagging fruit in woven palm-leaf baskets (which leave a distinctive
lattice pattern on the ripened fruit), with insecticide sprays applied
roughly every 10 days until fruit is wrapped [1][10]. Monkeys can take up
to 10% of an orchard's crop [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A long-lived, cauliflorous, monoecious,
  latex-bearing tree reaching roughly 12-20 m under cultivation (one
  source describes the species as evergreen, another as deciduous — not
  resolved here); bears one to two crops a year depending on local
  dry-season pattern.
- **Climate:** Strictly tropical, requiring year-round moisture with no
  distinct dry season — markedly more climate-restricted than the
  related crop in the same genus this project has also researched
  (Finding 1, 3).
- **Food status / composition:** Ripe bulbs are raw-edible; young fruit
  is not raw-edible and is cooked as a vegetable (unripe fruit is
  astringent); seeds are likewise not raw-edible and are roasted or
  boiled. Composition figures conflict somewhat between a fresh-weight
  regional food-composition table and a dry-weight regional reference
  source once converted to a fresh basis (see Estimates below).
- **Pollination:** Required; pollinated by a specific gall midge that
  depends on a fungal infection of the male flowers for its own life
  cycle, an unusual fungus-mediated mutualism (Finding 4).
- **Yield:** A national average yield of roughly 6,000 kg/ha (harvested
  basis) is well sourced from official agricultural statistics for a
  major producing country, with documented potential yield more than
  double that under better management; per-tree yield figures vary
  widely by source and cultivar (Finding 2).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-coefficient figure exists for this
  genus in standard international references; water-use parameters here
  are rough estimates. The tree prefers a consistently high water table
  and (per most sources) tolerates periodic flooding, though this
  conflicts with one source describing a short flood-tolerance limit
  (Finding 3); drought tolerance is low.
- **Propagation:** Mainly by seedling, though budded or suckle-grafted
  clones on champedak, jackfruit-type, or other same-genus rootstock are
  also used commercially; seed is recalcitrant and must be sown almost
  immediately, spoiling within a few weeks of removal from the fruit.
- **Soil:** Prefers fertile, well-drained soil (sandy loam per one
  source); a moderately acid pH range is preferred. Nutrient-uptake
  figures are derived rather than directly sourced (see Estimates).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour budget exists for this crop; rough
  estimates suggest harvest, fruit wrapping/spraying against fruit flies,
  and general orchard maintenance (fertilizing, weeding, dieback
  monitoring) are the main labor components.
- **Storage:** Ripe fruit keeps only about 2-3 days at ambient
  temperature; cool-room storage is reported to extend life somewhat,
  though no specific temperature was sourced (Finding 1).
- **Stand life:** First fruiting is reported at 2-4 years for budded
  trees versus 3-6 years for seedlings by one source, though other
  sources give somewhat different figures for both routes; one source's
  "productive lifespan of 6-10 years" is ambiguous as to whether it means
  years-to-maturity or total productive life, and is not resolved here.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A serious bacterial dieback disease, a
  fungal pink disease, fruit flies (managed by wrapping or bagging young
  fruit), bark-boring beetles, and monkey predation on ripening fruit
  (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** A substantial number of officially registered clones
  exist in the main producing country (spanning decades of selection),
  with several officially recommended for commercial planting based on
  flavor, fruit size, and productivity; a wild variety with thinner,
  less flavorful pulp also exists as a genetic reservoir; hybrids with
  the related crop in the same genus are also grown commercially,
  producing notably larger fruit.
- **Harvest stages:** Two harvest stages — young fruit, cut while small,
  green, and firm, used as a cooked vegetable; and mature fruit, cut by
  hand with its stalk once fully ripe, over a harvest season of roughly
  six weeks per crop.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not researched in depth, but
  judged a poor fit for hydroponic or enclosed cultivation given its
  mature size as a 12-20 m forest tree.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a serious bacterial dieback
disease, a fungal pink disease, fruit flies, and bark-boring beetles;
monkeys are also a documented source of crop loss. Unripe fruit is
described as astringent, and ripe fruit is noted by one compiled source
as having a mild laxative effect.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — no allergy case reports specific to this crop were
found in a dedicated search, but a documented latex-fruit-syndrome
cross-allergy affecting the related crop in the same genus this project
has also researched may plausibly extend to this species as well, though
this remains unconfirmed by any source found. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** mature fruit cut with its stalk → ripened briefly (or
  cool-stored, though the regime is not well sourced) → opened by hand →
  bulbs (roughly 25-50% of fresh fruit weight) and rind separated; bulbs
  → eaten fresh, batter-fried as a street food, or cooked down with
  brown sugar into a sweet preserve; rind → inner pith separated from
  outer skin → salted and fermented 3 days to a month → a shelf-stable
  fermented product keeping up to a year; seeds → roasted or boiled in
  salted water; young fruit → peeled, sliced, and boiled into a curry or
  soup; young leaves eaten as a vegetable; seed → raised as a container
  seedling → optionally budded into a named clone.
- Not covered in detail by this research: the frying process for the
  battered-bulb street food; frozen pulp or spray/freeze-dried powder
  products (laboratory-scale only in the sources reviewed); fruit-
  wrapping technique in detail; timber, bark-rope, latex-based lime, and
  dye uses; traditional medicinal use of bark and roots.

## Open research questions

- Flooding tolerance is directly disputed between sources — most
  describe good tolerance of periodic or even swamp-water flooding,
  while one source gives a specific short submersion limit — not
  reconciled here.
- Whether the species is evergreen or deciduous is disputed between
  sources.
- Planting density/spacing figures vary considerably across sources.
- Time from flowering to fruit maturity is given differently across
  sources (a wider versus narrower month range).
- Bearing age (time to first fruit) for both seedling and budded
  propagation routes varies across sources.
- Fruit size ranges differ substantially across sources, and may partly
  reflect genuinely different named cultivars rather than contradictory
  reporting.
- Protein content conflicts between a fresh-weight regional food table
  and a dry-weight regional reference source once unit-converted — not
  reconciled (see Estimates).
- Seeds per fruit also differ across sources by a wide margin — one
  figure may have been carried over incorrectly from the related
  same-genus crop rather than independently sourced for this species.
- Cold-storage temperature, chilling sensitivity, and any frost/cold
  tolerance limit were not found in the sources reviewed.
- A standard crop-water-use coefficient for this genus does not exist in
  standard international references; the water-use figures used here are
  rough estimates pending a dedicated study.
- Current detailed agricultural practice in two of the largest producing
  states within the main producing country was not found in the sources
  reviewed.

## Estimates and derivations

Time to first harvest (roughly 1,095 days for budded clones) is derived
from a documented 2-4 year bearing-age range for budded trees, taking the
midpoint as representative of recommended commercial clones; seedling
trees are expected to take meaningfully longer (roughly 3-6 years per one
source).

Fresh-weight protein content is estimated by converting a dry-weight
regional reference figure (3.5-7 g protein per 100 g dry matter) to a
fresh basis using the source's own stated water content range, yielding
roughly 0.5-2.9 g per 100 g fresh — notably lower than a separate
regional fresh-weight food-composition table's figure of 3.0 g per 100
g, a conflict not resolved here; the fresh-weight table figure is
nonetheless adopted for its directly fresh-basis reporting.

A "poor" yield tier (roughly 3,500 kg/ha) is estimated as somewhat below
the weakest documented state-level average yields in the main producing
country's national statistics; a rough per-tree cross-check (using
documented per-tree fruit counts, assumed fruit weight, and planting
density) produces a wide range spanning from figures close to the
national average up to several times higher, depending on which sourced
per-tree productivity figure is used.

Water-use parameters (seasonal water-use index, root-zone depth,
drought-buffer days) are estimated by analogy to other ever-wet tropical
tree-fruit crops this project has researched, since no standard crop
coefficient exists for this genus; a crop coefficient somewhat higher
than that used for the related, more drought-tolerant same-genus crop is
assumed, reflecting this species' documented intolerance of any dry
season.

Nitrogen and potassium uptake per tonne of fruit are derived from the
adopted fresh-weight food-composition figures (protein and potassium
content); phosphorus uptake is carried over by analogy from the related
same-genus crop this project has also researched, since no direct figure
was found.

Soil pH optimum is estimated as the midpoint of a compiled grower
source's preferred range.

A cool-storage estimate of about 13°C holding for roughly a week is
carried over by analogy from the related same-genus crop's documented
storage behavior, adjusted toward a shorter duration since this crop
ripens and spoils distinctly faster at ambient temperature than that
crop does.

Harvest-season yield-rate shape (a roughly six-week peak harvest window,
rapid post-ripeness quality decline) is estimated from the sourced
harvest-season length and documented very short shelf life.
