## Naming and scope

*Citrus medica* L. var. *sarcodactylis* (Hoola van Nooten) Swingle, the
"fingered citron," known in Chinese as 佛手 (foshou, "Buddha's hand"), with
several named regional types: Guang foshou (Guangdong), Min foshou
(Fujian), Jin foshou (Jinhua, Zhejiang), and Chuan foshou (Sichuan) [3][4].
The fruit splits lengthwise into finger-like segments, ranging from
"open-hand" to "closed-hand" forms; it has only white pith, sometimes a
little acid pulp, and many forms are seedless [1][2].

This is botanically the same species as citron, a closely related citrus
crop this project has also researched, and the two share frost
tenderness, propagation by cuttings, and susceptibility to citrus
greening. They are nonetheless treated as separate crop entries here,
since they differ substantially in the things that matter for cultivation
and use: citron's main products are brined, candied peel and ritual
fruit, while Buddha's hand instead yields a fragrance/offering fruit, a
dried medicinal slice product recognized in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia,
roasted tea shreds, and — distinctively among the fruit crops researched
for this project — a fruiting potted-plant product in its own right
[1][2][3][6][7][8][9][10]. The two crops also have separate production
systems (Buddha's hand has a dedicated potted-plant industry centered on
Jinhua, with field and container cultivation on different cycles
[3][9][10][12]) and separate harvest-stage structures (a green medicinal
stage and a ripe fragrance stage, versus citron's green-candying and
ripe-etrog stages). The fruit also cannot be handled on a standard
packing line and must be cleaned and buffed by hand, since it moulds
quickly [2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — three distinct products from one tree: medicinal slices,
fragrance fruit, and potted plants.** For the medicinal product, fruit is
cut in autumn while still green or just turning yellow (one regional
account instead picks fruit in July, when the skin pales from deep to
light green), sliced thin lengthwise, and sun-dried or dried at low
temperature; the dried slices (a recognized "medicine-food" in the
Chinese Pharmacopoeia, with hesperidin as its quality marker) are stored
for years, with at least one region using a steam-processing step
[4][6][7]. For the fresh-fruit product, fruit is grown for fragrance
(rooms and clothing), as a temple offering, and as a New Year gift in
Chinese and Japanese tradition, and is also used for zest, candying, and
flavoring spirits; in a California growing region the fruit ripens
November-January and holds moderately on the tree [1][2]. For the potted
plant product, Jinhua (sometimes called the crop's "hometown") grows both
field and potted trees for New Year sales; potted plants stand 60-100 cm
tall against roughly 150 cm for field trees [3][8].

**Finding 2 — three flowering flushes a year, with heavy thinning.**
Plants in the Jinhua growing region flower and fruit three times a year
and first fruit in their third year. Growers keep large terminal flowers
that carry leaves and remove single-sex and weak flowers, thinning
50-60% of all flowers to reach roughly one fruit per 40 leaves. Average
fruit weight runs 400-500 g, with a documented maximum of 2,300 g.
Reported annual yields vary considerably: a well-managed mature tree is
reported at 20-25 kg of fresh fruit a year in one source, while a
separate review snippet reports around 40 kg and up to 100 kg [3][7][12].

**Finding 3 — frost-tender, and grown under plastic cover in at least one
cool-climate production region.** The plant is damaged below 0°C and
killed below -8°C, and is noted as having weaker cold and drought
tolerance than other citrus. In the Jinhua growing region, plants are
moved under plastic cover from mid-to-late November, with irrigation
given about 10 days before frost, a soil mulch layer at the base, and
wrapped trunks. A separate source describes the crop as "very
frost-sensitive," needing almost frost-free sites, and a general
reference source similarly describes sensitivity to frost, intense heat,
and drought. Optimal growth temperature is reported around 15-25°C
[1][2][3][10].

**Finding 4 — propagated mainly by summer cuttings, with citrus greening
as a clonal-stock risk.** Cuttings are typically taken from late June to
mid-July from strong mother plants, rooting in about a month and
producing shoots within two months before being planted out; longer
cuttings (50 cm, from 3-4-year-old wood) are also used, as are grafting
and air-layering ("high-pressure propagation") [1][3][11][12]. Standard
field planting is done in mid-to-late March, at roughly 2 × 2-2.5 m
spacing, with a few weeks of half-shade protection after transplanting
[3]. Like citron, the crop is susceptible to citrus greening disease; one
documented clonal line used in a California growing region was cleaned of
a separate viroid disease (exocortis) by shoot-tip grafting before
distribution through a certified-budwood program [1][2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A thorny, evergreen perennial shrub or small
  tree; potted plants reach 60-100 cm, field trees around 150 cm and over
  2 m wide. Flowers and fruits three times a year rather than on a single
  annual cycle (Finding 2).
- **Climate:** Subtropical (frost-free) to tropical-highland climates
  suit the crop without protection; warm-temperate cultivation is
  possible only with winter protection, as practiced in the Jinhua
  growing region (Finding 3).
- **Food status / composition:** Not a staple food; eaten mainly as pith
  and zest, with dried slices used medicinally and as tea. Caloric and
  protein composition were not sourced.
- **Pollination:** Not required by hand; flowers are thinned rather than
  hand-pollinated, and the species self-pollinates like citron.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not independently sourced for this
  species. By analogy with citrus-genus physiology, consistent with
  this project's findings for other citrus crops: flowering is likely
  triggered by cold and water-deficit stress rather than day length —
  citrus floral induction is described in the literature as
  "autoinductive," with no single indispensable photoperiodic
  stimulus. The three annual flowering flushes and heavy flower-
  thinning documented for this crop (Finding 2) are therefore treated
  as temperature/management-driven rather than day-length-driven [13].
  Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing — a woody citrus tree.
- **Yield:** No reliable single per-hectare figure could be derived with
  confidence; per-tree yield figures (20-100 kg/tree/year depending on
  source) and regional area-level figures diverge by a wide margin (see
  Estimates and Open research questions below).
- **Water use:** Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, root
  depth, drought-day tolerance) are carried over by direct analogy from
  the closely related citrus crop this project has also researched,
  since no dedicated water-use study was found for this crop specifically
  — adjusted downward slightly for drought-day tolerance, since multiple
  sources describe this crop as less drought-tolerant than other citrus.
  Potted plants are instead managed by a "do not water until dry, then
  water thoroughly" rule, with watering timing shifted seasonally, and
  pots tilted during the rainy season to shed excess water (Finding 3).
- **Propagation:** Vegetative only — summer cuttings, grafting, and air
  layering; many forms are seedless and there is no seed crop or hybrid
  seed system (Finding 4).
- **Soil:** Fertilized lightly and frequently (nitrogen during growth;
  phosphorus and potassium at flowering and fruiting), with organic
  fertilizer preferred; pH tolerance is assumed by analogy to citron
  (roughly 5.5-8.0, optimum 6.5), since no dedicated soil study was found
  for this crop. Salinity tolerance was not sourced for this species
  specifically either. By citrus-rootstock-level analogy only (strongly
  rootstock-dependent, as this project has also found for bitter
  orange): citrus yield generally suffers above roughly 2 dS/m soil
  salinity, with Alemow (*Citrus macrophylla*) and sour-orange-type
  rootstocks ranking among the more salt-tolerant options in trials,
  versus more sensitive stocks like Shekwasha and trifoliate-orange
  hybrids; one grower source lists this crop as commercially grafted
  onto Citrange rootstock, but gives no salinity data for that specific
  combination [14].
- **Storage:** Ripe fruit keeps roughly one to two weeks cool and dry, or
  two to four weeks refrigerated, but molds easily; green fruit intended
  for the medicinal product is sliced and dried immediately, and the
  dried slices then keep for years.
- **Labor:** No per-hectare labor study was found; rough estimates
  suggest harvesting, flower/fruit thinning, pruning, and post-harvest
  slicing or cleaning/buffing are all substantial labor components, with
  hand-cleaning required because the fruit cannot go through standard
  packing-line equipment. Per-pot labor in the potted-plant production
  system was not researched in detail.
- **Harvest stages:** Two harvest stages from the same tree — a green
  medicinal stage (full-size, green to turning, intended for slicing and
  drying) and a ripe fragrance stage (yellow, fully fragrant, hand-cut,
  cleaned, and buffed) — plus a third product, the fruiting potted plant
  itself, sold as a whole specimen rather than harvested as fruit.
- **Cultivars:** Several named regional types tied to particular uses
  (potted ornamentals and tea in the Jinhua type; medicinal slices in the
  Guangdong, Sichuan/Chongqing, and Fujian types), open-hand versus
  closed-hand fruit-shape forms, several small-fruited thornless clones
  bred specifically for miniature potted culture, and at least one
  documented California clonal introduction.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Notably good, for a tree fruit
  crop — the species has a real, documented container/substrate
  production system (perlite, coir, and peat substrates; miniature pots
  grown in greenhouse facilities) used commercially for the ornamental
  potted-plant product, which substantially shortens the production cycle
  compared with soil culture.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include anthracnose, canker, and scab, as
well as citrus greening disease; pest pressure includes leaf miners,
spider mites, swallowtail caterpillars, scale insects, and beetles.
Harvested fruit is also prone to mould if not handled promptly. The plant
itself is thorny.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other citrus, the peel oil is expected to be
phototoxic (by analogy with citron's bergapten/citropten content), though
this was not independently confirmed for this specific crop. Deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

In traditional Chinese medicine, this crop's dried fruit slices carry a
use caution that patients with a specific traditional diagnostic pattern
("yin deficiency and excess heat without qi stagnation") should avoid
prolonged use — a traditional-medicine caution rather than a
toxicological finding.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** green fruit → sliced lengthwise (roughly 1 cm thick) →
  sun- or kiln-dried → dried medicinal slices (steam-processed in at
  least one producing region, stored for years) or shredded and
  oven-roasted into tea shreds; ripe fruit → hand-cleaned and buffed →
  sold fresh for fragrance, offerings, and zest, or candied; prunings →
  summer cuttings → rooted plants → grown on as field trees or
  container-grown fruiting potted plants.
- Not covered in detail by this research: factory products made from the
  fruit (a fruit wine, crackers, and a yoghurt product reported in one
  source); volatile-oil yield; detailed per-pot labor and production
  cycle for the potted-plant system.

The fresh ripe fruit is used as a Buddhist temple offering and as a New
Year gift in both Chinese and Japanese tradition, valued for its shape and
fragrance. Separately, in Chinese folk practice, the fruiting potted
plant itself is displayed as a New Year decoration, since the crop's
Chinese name is a near-homophone for a phrase meaning "blessing and
longevity."

## Open research questions

- Reported yield figures conflict substantially: per-tree figures of
  20-25 kg/year in one source versus 40-100 kg/year in another, and
  regional area-level production figures that imply a per-hectare yield
  several times higher than either per-tree figure would suggest when
  converted using known planting density — not reconciled here.
- Reported production-area figures for the main producing region
  likewise conflict across sources by a wide margin.
- Harvest timing for the medicinal product is given differently across
  sources (autumn, green or turning, in one source; July, pale green, in
  another) — possibly reflecting genuine regional/cultivar differences
  rather than a contradiction.
- The cold-hardiness limit is given differently across sources (death
  below a specific low threshold in one source, versus a broader
  description of extreme frost-sensitivity in another) — the former may
  reflect protected, acclimated plants rather than the species' general
  tolerance.
- Per-pot labor and the detailed potted-plant production cycle were not
  researched in depth.
- Volatile-oil yield, productive lifespan, and a dedicated storage/
  chilling-sensitivity study for this specific crop (as opposed to
  citron) were not found.
- The dried-slice yield (fresh-to-dry mass ratio) was not found.
- Photoperiod sensitivity and salinity tolerance were not sourced for
  this species specifically; both are carried over as citrus-genus- or
  rootstock-level analogies only, flagged as such rather than treated
  as confirmed species-specific findings.

## Estimates and derivations

Time to first fruiting (roughly 730-1,095 days from planting) is derived
from the documented "third year after planting" rule, spanning the
earliest possible third-year crop through a full third year of growth;
potted plants are reported to fruit about a year later than field
plantings.

Yield tiers (roughly 2,000-20,000 kg/ha across poor-to-exceptional
production) are derived from per-tree yield figures combined with a
documented field planting density (roughly 2,200 plants/ha at 2 × 2.25 m
spacing), discounted somewhat for the smaller size of field plants
compared with the larger trees behind the higher per-tree yield figures.
Area-level production figures from the main producing region imply a much
higher per-hectare yield than this per-tree derivation, which may reflect
potted-plant sales or fruit sourced from a wider area being counted
together with field production; this is treated as an unresolved
conflict rather than averaged away.

Water-use parameters are carried over by direct analogy from the closely
related citrus crop this project has also researched, with the
drought-tolerance interval shortened somewhat to reflect this crop's
documented lower drought tolerance relative to other citrus.

Labor-hour estimates are derived from assumed per-fruit handling rates
(cutting, cleaning, slicing, and drying) applied to the estimated yield
tiers above, combined with a judgement-call harvest window length based
on the fruit's moderate on-tree holding ability; all such figures should
be treated as rough estimates pending a dedicated regional cost study.

Soil pH tolerance is carried over by analogy from the same closely
related citrus crop, since no dedicated soil study was found for this
species specifically.
