## Naming and scope

*Mangifera foetida* Lour. (Anacardiaceae); syn. *M. horsfieldii* Miq.
[1]. Further synonyms are *M. leschenaultii* and three varieties of *M.
foetida* [4]. Chromosome number 2n = 40 [1].
- **Names:** bachang, horse mango (English); mangue fétide (French) [1].
  - **Indonesia:** bacang or membacang (Malay, Minangkabau), limus
    (Sundanese, West Java), asem hambawang (Banjar, South Kalimantan)
    [1]; ambacang in West Sumatra [11].
  - **Malaysia:** machang, bacang, pahu (Sabah), macang (Sarawak) [1].
  - **Elsewhere:** la-môt (Burma), svaay sââ (Cambodia), ma chae or
    malamut (Thailand), xoài hôi (Vietnam) [1].
  - **Timber:** traded as machang [1][2].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Bambangan":** the agroforestry database lists it as an English
    name for this species [2]. Everywhere else it is *M. pajang*
    (`bambangan`) [1][6][7].
  - **"Kemantan":** the encyclopedia gives it as a Bornean name for this
    species [4]. A Malaysian survey uses it for a different, very rare
    species, *M. torquenda* [6].
- **Native range:** wild in the dipterocarp forests of Peninsular Malaysia,
  Peninsular Thailand, Sumatra and Borneo, and apparently wild in Java
  [1]. It is widely cultivated there [1].
  - **Introduced:** popular in southern Tenasserim (Burma). It is
    cultivated and sometimes naturalized in Burma, Thailand and the Lesser
    Sunda Islands, and is rare or almost unknown in Vietnam, Cambodia and
    the Philippines [1].
  - **Conflict:** the agroforestry database lists India as native and the
    Philippines and Vietnam as exotic [2]. No other source places it in
    India.
- **Existing group doc:** `wet-tropics-mangoes.md` (row
  `wet_tropics_mangoes`, `OUT-OF-DATE`) is an older, pre-migration doc
  covering this species together with kuini, binjai and bambangan [13].
  This doc supersedes its bachang material. See Open research questions
  for what to do with that row.

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from
`mango.md` [13]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a separate species, not a cultivar group of the common
  mango. As a scion it fails on mango rootstock (Finding 4) [5].
- **Climate:** a tree of ever-wet lowland forest, adapted to rain spread
  evenly through the year [1]. The common mango needs a dry spell to
  flower (`mango.md`, Finding 1) [13].
- **Harvest and handling:** the fruit's juice inflames the lips and mouth.
  Ripe fruit must be peeled thickly, and unripe fruit is washed in salt
  water or soaked in limewater first (Finding 1) [1].
- **Uses:** mainly a souring agent and pickle fruit, not a table fruit
  (Finding 2) [1].
- **Farming system:** a 30-35 m home-garden and forest tree, grown from
  seed and spaced 14-16 m apart [1].

**Relatives in this project:**
- mango (`mango`, `mango.md`): the same genus [1].
- kuini (`kuini`, proposed): *M. odorata* is described as a hybrid of
  the common mango and this species [11].
- bambangan (`bambangan`, `bambangan.md`): where both are grown
  together, natural cross-pollination gives hybrids with traits of both
  [1].
- binjai (`binjai`, `binjai.md`): the same wet-tropics group [13].
- wet-tropics mangoes (`wet_tropics_mangoes`, `wet-tropics-mangoes.md`):
  the older group doc [13].

**Proposed:**
- kemang (`kemang`, *M. kemanga*): a cultivated species; very rare in
  Malaysian home gardens [6], and studied among the Sumatran wild mangoes
  [11]. It has its own market name and no doc here.
- kasturi (`kasturi`, *M. casturi*): commonly cultivated in Kalimantan
  under the name kastooree. It grafts well on mango, and Fairchild uses it
  as an interstock for other wild mangoes [5]. Also host to the oriental
  fruit fly [12] (snippet). Since researched in `kasturi.md`.

Other species in the sources (*M. torquenda*, *M. laurina*, *M.
lalijiwa*, *M. griffithii* and more) are not proposed. No food use
beyond "edible" was found for them in this pass [5][6][11].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the juice burns until the fruit is fully ripe.**
- **Fruit:** the fresh fruit has an irritant juice that can inflame the
  lips and mouth [1][2][4].
- **Ripe fruit:** at full maturity the irritant is confined to the skin,
  so ripe fruit can be eaten fresh if peeled fairly thickly [1][2]. The
  skin is about 5 mm thick [1].
- **Unripe fruit:** it is washed in salted water, or kept for a while in
  limewater, before slicing [1].
- **Sap:** the bark holds a whitish, irritant sap that turns black in air
  [1]. A kernel study describes the latex as blistering the skin [8].
  An Indonesian report says the sap makes the skin itch [9].
- **Chemistry:** the irritant compound was not identified in any source
  read. Searches for bachang dermatitis returned only common-mango
  sources [12] (snippet). See Hazards.

**Finding 2 — a souring agent more than a table fruit.**
- **Taste:** "rather savoury", with a strong turpentine smell and a taste
  sometimes likened to durian. It is "not generally valued as a table
  fruit" [1].
- **Unripe uses:** salads (*rujak*), sour pickle (*asinan*), and preserved
  fruit in syrup (*manisan*) in West Java [1][7].
- **Sambal:** in Borneo, especially East Kalimantan, the fruit commonly
  replaces tamarind as the acid in sambal [1]. In South Sumatra and Jambi,
  ripe bacang is ground with chilli, salt, shrimp paste and palm sugar,
  and eaten with fish [9].
- **Seed sambal:** in West Java the grated seed goes into *pelok limus*,
  with fermented soybean cake, sugar, fermented fish and salt [3].
- **Malaysia:** chutneys and pickles [1]. A 2016 review names bacang as a
  fruit with potential for canned fruit, pickles and juice in Peninsular
  Malaysia [7].
- **Outlook:** PROSEA expects its traditional role "not likely to change
  much" [1].

**Finding 3 — a common home-garden tree with named local forms.**
- **Malaysia:** a survey of 25 districts in 11 states found it among the
  four *Mangifera* species grown in every state [6].
- **Local forms** [1]:
  - small, nearly round fruit (*limus piit*, West Java), told apart from
    the larger, oblong fruit sold in Malay markets;
  - a large, oblong, almost fibreless, fine-textured form (*limus
    tipung*, "flour", West Java);
  - a similar East Kalimantan form (*asem linggau*) with many seedless
    (abortive-seeded) fruit;
  - a Sarawak market form with deep yellow unripe fruit.
- **Sumatra:** wild-mango studies name three varieties: limus, manis
  ("sweet") and batu ("stone") [11].
- **Borneo:** two kinds, one very sweet and one sour [4].
- **Decline:** in South Sumatra, villagers report trees being felled for
  housing; in Jambi they are still common [9].

**Finding 4 — graft compatibility with mango is disputed.**
- **As a rootstock for mango:** PROSEA says it proved suitable for mango
  cultivars in a moist climate, but others doubt it because of swellings
  at the union [1]. The encyclopedia calls the seed a good,
  disease-resistant rootstock [4], with no supporting citation.
- **As a scion on mango:** at Fairchild (Florida), *M. foetida* grafted on
  'Turpentine' mango seedlings failed; scions lived up to 3 weeks [5].
  - **2007:** interstocks for this species were still being tried [5].
  - **2016:** an *M. lalijiwa* interstock on 'Turpentine' gave
    "acceptable success". The authors say this opens breeding and
    possibly field production in South Florida [5].
- **Own propagation:** by seed, which breeds true [1][3]. Selected forms
  are budded by the modified Forkert method, on one-year-old rootstocks
  in the dry season [1].

**Finding 5 — the composition figures are thin and partly conflicting.**
- **PROSEA, per 100 g edible portion:** water 72.5 g, protein 1.4 g,
  carbohydrate 25.4 g, calcium 21 mg, phosphorus 15 mg, thiamine 0.03 mg,
  β-carotene equivalent 0.218 mg, and vitamin C 56 mg [1][2].
- **West Java review:** protein 1.1 g (0.8-1.4), fibre 1.9 (1.8-2.0),
  calcium 26 mg (16-36), iron 0.25 mg and vitamin C 52 mg (47-56) per
  100 g [7]. Its table header gives fibre in mg; the companion RDA table
  (5.3% of 36 g) shows it must be grams.
- **Carotenoids:** 96.5-153.0 µg β-carotene equivalent per 100 g in a
  Malaysian study [7], below PROSEA's 218 µg.
- **Phenolics:** 122.8-199.8 mg GAE per 100 g edible portion; gallic,
  protocatechuic and vanillic acids are the main phenolic acids [7].
  Genistein 0.4-0.8 mg per 100 g [7].
- **Edible share:** 65% of fruit weight [1], or 56% in a later Malaysian
  study [7] (snippet).
- **Energy:** not given in any source; about 100-113 kcal per 100 g (see
  Estimates).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** perennial tree. One database calls it
  deciduous [3]; no other source gives its leaf habit. A tree up to 30-35 m, with a straight,
  unbuttressed bole [1]; specimens of 40 m are recorded [3].
  - **Flowering and fruiting** [1]:
    - **Sarawak:** flowers May-August, ripens August-November.
    - **East Kalimantan:** flowers April-September, ripens
      August-January.
    - **West Java:** harvested October-December.
  - Fruit is harvested in the rainy season [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing, one season a year in each region
  [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 6-8 years from planting [4].
- **Climate zones:** hot, wet lowland tropics, with abundant rain spread
  evenly through the year [1][2]. It can be grown in a monsoon climate
  [3].
  - **Elevation:** sources disagree. PROSEA gives above 1,000 m [1]; the
    databases give up to 1,000 m, occasionally 1,500 m [2][3]; an
    Indonesian report gives up to 1,800 m [9].
  - **Cold:** Not sourced. Fairchild's work suggests it can be grown in
    South Florida on an interstock [5].
- **Composition:** see Finding 5 [1][7].
- **Edibility:** ripe flesh raw, if thickly peeled; unripe fruit only
  after washing in salt water or soaking in limewater [1]. The seed is
  eaten grated in sambal [3].
- **Pollination:** insects [3]. Flowers are scentless, with only one or
  two fertile stamens [1]. Self-compatibility: Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. No per-tree or per-hectare figure was found
  [12] (snippet). Fruit is 100-200 g [4], or 9-14 (-16) cm × 7-12 cm [1].
  The edible flesh is about 56-130 g a fruit (see Estimates).
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Seedlings need much moisture [1].
- **Propagation:** by seed, which breeds true; seeds are monoembryonic
  [1][3]. Selected forms are budded [1]. See Finding 4 for grafting with
  mango.
  - **Clonal risk:** Not applicable in practice, since nearly all trees
    are seedlings [1].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced, beyond hillsides and ridges on sandy soils in the
  wild [3]. Salinity tolerance: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** seedlings need much moisture and light shade [1].
  Young plants tolerate deep shade and later grow well in full sun [1][3].
  Orchard spacing is 14-16 m [1][2], or about 39-51 trees/ha (see
  Estimates).
- **Cultivars:** no named cultivars; local forms only (Finding 3)
  [1][4][11].
- **Weather response:** frost, drought and waterlogging tolerance: Not
  sourced. Its range implies poor drought tolerance, but no source says
  so.
- **Water regime:** rain-fed; no flooded or paddy requirement [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** two stages are used.
  - **Unripe:** for pickles, salads and sambal [1][9].
  - **Ripe:** for fresh eating and sambal [1][9].
  - Ripening within one tree: Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Status:** native to western Malesia. Naturalized in places outside
    that range, such as Burma, Thailand and the Lesser Sundas [1]. IUCN
    Least Concern (2021) [3][4].
  - **Listings:** no invasive listing found in this pass.
  - **Pests carried:** it hosts the mango weevil, whose larvae feed in the
    flesh [1][2]. The United States restricts *Mangifera* seed imports
    because of mango seed borer, which is why Fairchild brought these
    species in as graftwood [5]. EPPO lists it as a host of the oriental
    fruit fly (*Bactrocera dorsalis*) [10].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. A 30-35 m tree with 14-16 m
  spacing is unlikely to suit protected cropping (our inference).

## Hazards

- **Irritant juice and sap:** the fruit juice inflames the lips and
  mouth until full maturity, and the bark sap and latex irritate or
  blister the skin [1][2][8][9]. Mitigation: thick peeling of ripe fruit,
  salt-water or limewater treatment of unripe fruit [1].
- **Mango allergy cross-reaction:** not sourced for this species. Common
  mango's sap allergens are resorcinols that cross-react with poison ivy
  [12] (snippet). Whether bachang carries the same compounds is unknown.
- **Pests:** a longhorn trunk borer (*Rhytidodera simulans*) can kill
  branches without killing the tree; *Arbela* bark damage is superficial;
  mango weevil larvae often damage the fruit [1][2]. EPPO records it, as
  *Mangifera*, as a host of ambrosia beetles (*Euwallacea fornicatus*
  s.l., *E. perbrevis*) and white peach scale [10].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for bachang.
- **Diseases:** Not sourced.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the irritant has no identified compound, no
  dose and no case series in any source read. Its link, if any, to the
  common mango's resorcinol allergens needs a dedicated look.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (unripe fruit):** green fruit → washed in salted water, or held
  in limewater → peeled and sliced → *rujak* salad, *asinan* pickle or
  *manisan* in syrup [1][7]; chutney and pickle in Malaysia [1].
- **Chain (sambal):** ripe fruit → peeled and chopped → ground with
  chilli, salt, shrimp paste and palm sugar → sambal bacang, eaten with
  grilled or fried fish [9]. In East Kalimantan the fruit replaces
  tamarind as the acid [1].
- **Chain (seed sambal):** seed → grated → mixed with fermented soybean
  cake, sugar, fermented fish and salt → *pelok limus* [3].
- **Chain (fresh):** ripe fruit → thick peel → eaten fresh, or in fruit
  cocktails [1]; juice or jam [4]. Edible share 56-65% [1][7].
- **Chain (kernel enzyme):** kernel → buffered extraction → crude
  protease; extraction has been optimized in the laboratory only [8].
- **Chain (timber):** trunk → machang timber (545-785 kg/m³ at 15%
  moisture) → light indoor and temporary construction and plywood;
  streaked heartwood for furniture [1]. Not durable [1].
- **Chain (dye):** fruit skin → dyebath with an alum mordant → colour on
  cotton for ecoprint; alum worked best for this material [11].
- **Medicinal:** leaves are said to reduce fever; seeds are used against
  ringworm-type fungal infection, scabies and eczema [1]. The sap is
  applied to ulcers [3]. Indonesian uses for diarrhoea, stomach ache and
  appetite [9]. Leaf and bark extracts are studied as antioxidants and
  mosquito larvicides [11].
- **Other:** Orang Asli in Peninsular Malaysia used the sap to deepen
  tattoo scars [1]. Ornamental in flower [1].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** the Malaysian commercial-product
  prospects (canned, pickles, juice) [7]; isoflavone content [7].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Energy, about 100-113 kcal per 100 g edible portion.** PROSEA gives
protein 1.4 g and carbohydrate 25.4 g, with water 72.5 g [1]. At 4 kcal/g
for each: 1.4 × 4 + 25.4 × 4 = 107 kcal. Fat and ash together can be at
most 100 − 72.5 − 1.4 − 25.4 = 0.7 g, adding up to 6 kcal at 9 kcal/g:
113 kcal at most. If the carbohydrate is by difference and includes about
1.9 g of fibre [7] at about 2 kcal/g instead of 4, the total drops by
about 4 kcal, to about 103 kcal. Range about 100-113 kcal. A food
composition table entry would replace it.

**Edible flesh per fruit, about 56-130 g.** Fruit weight 100-200 g [4] ×
edible share 56-65% [1][7]: 100 × 0.56 = 56 g; 200 × 0.65 = 130 g.

**Tree density, about 39-51 trees/ha.** Square spacing of 14-16 m [1]:
10,000 / (16 × 16) = 39.1; 10,000 / (14 × 14) = 51.0. No per-tree yield
was found, so no per-hectare yield follows.
