## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus odoratissimus* Blanco (Moraceae), described in 1837 [1][2].
PROSEA and Wikipedia list *A. tarap* Becc. and *A. mutabilis* Becc. as
synonyms [1][2]. The chromosome number is unknown [1].
- **Names:** marang (English; Sulu), madang (Lanao) and loloi (Tagalog)
  in the Philippines [1][3]. Terap or tarap (Malay), pingan (Iban),
  pi-ien (Bidayuh) and keiran (Kelabit) in Borneo [1][3]. Khanun
  sampalor in Thailand [3]. "Johey oak" is a less common English name
  [2][6].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Terap"** is also used in Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia for *A.
    elasticus* (tekalong). That species has longer leaf stalks, cylindrical
    male heads, and fruit with short and long rubbery spines [1].
  - **"Pingan"** is the Iban name for marang [1][3], but Wikipedia also
    gives pingan, or "mountain tarap," for *A. sarawakensis* [2].
  - **"Tarap bulu"** is *A. sericicarpus* [2].
- ***A. mutabilis* is distinct:** Gardner et al. (2022) confirmed with
  molecular data what the Iban and Dusun peoples already knew. *A.
  odoratissimus* and *A. mutabilis* are separate taxa, not one species,
  and community members clarified the features that tell them apart [4].
  Data in older sources under the combined name may mix the two.
- **Range:**
  - **Borneo:** native, and common in the wild in secondary forest up to
    1,000 m on sandy clay [1][3].
  - **Philippines:** found only in cultivation, in Mindoro, Mindanao,
    Basilan and the Sulu Archipelago. It was probably introduced from
    Borneo [1]. In Mindanao it is "widely grown," especially in Davao,
    North Cotabato and Agusan, and is seldom found in Luzon or the
    Visayas [6].
  - **Elsewhere:** cultivated in Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia, southern
    Thailand and Tripura in India [2]. Southern Thai villages grow it as
    a home-garden tree [3]. It is also grown in northern Queensland [1]
    and was introduced to South America [10].

**Gate check (2026-11-23): passes.** The row was proposed from
`jackfruit.md`, which named marang as an important fruit tree of
Southeast Asia [12]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every
count:
- **Taxonomy:** a separate *Artocarpus* species [1][2]. It is not a form
  of jackfruit (`jackfruit.md`), champedak (`champedak.md`), breadfruit
  (`breadfruit.md`) or lakoocha (`lakoocha.md`) [2][12].
- **Range:** Bornean, with a Philippine cultivated range centred on
  Mindanao [1][6]. It is strictly a wet-tropics tree that wants evenly
  spread rain [1][3].
- **Farming system:** a seed-grown tree that bears in 4-6 years [1].
  Philippine plantings totalled 1,700 ha in 1987 [1]. In Mindanao,
  though, it is mostly a volunteer tree on marginal and hilly land, with
  no identified plantation [6]. In Borneo it is gathered from the wild as
  well as grown [1][10].
- **Harvest:** the heavy fruit hangs at the ends of long, slender
  branches. Most of it falls to the ground despite the use of pole
  knives, and the ripe fruit keeps only briefly (Findings 2-3) [1].
- **Uses:** eaten fresh, in cakes and ice cream. The seeds are boiled or
  roasted, and young fruit is curried in coconut milk [1][3]. Its peel and
  core are being studied as adsorbents [2].

**Relatives in this project:**
- **Jackfruit, champedak, breadfruit, lakoocha:** the other *Artocarpus*
  rows [12]. Its flesh is usually softer than jackfruit's [2]. FAO
  compares it unfavourably with jackfruit on yield and shelf life [3].
- **Breadfruit rootstock:** grafting breadfruit onto marang dwarfs it,
  with about 60% less height and 80% shorter internodes (Finding 4) [5].
  See `breadfruit.md`.
- **Bambangan** (`bambangan.md`): Sabah's other native fruit in the same
  antioxidant study [8][12].

**Proposed:**
- **`peluntan`** (*A. sericicarpus*; also gumihan, pedalai, tarap bulu):
  a hairy, bright-orange fruit from the same areas, often confused with
  marang [2]. **Now researched (passed its gate check 2026-11-27; see
  `peluntan.md`):** a validly distinct species — a ~15 cm bright-orange,
  densely hairy "giant rambutan" with sweet creamy pulp and edible roasted
  seeds, less smelly and often rated better-flavoured than marang (though with
  thinner pulp by one account); it is the minor Bornean *Artocarpus* most seen
  in Sarawak markets, but only at local scale.
- **`pingan`** (*A. sarawakensis*, "mountain tarap"): orange, shaped like
  marang, with smaller sections [2]. **Now researched (passed its gate check
  2026-11-27; see `pingan.md`):** a rare Sarawak-endemic wild forest fruit,
  gathered not grown, of good flavour but little-studied. Its research
  **resolved the three-way "pingan" name clash**: following Gardner et al.
  (2022), "pingan" belongs to *A. mutabilis* (`artocarpus_mutabilis`), so the
  *A. sarawakensis* doc uses **"mountain tarap"** and recommends the cropID be
  renamed off `pingan`.
- **`artocarpus_mutabilis`** (*A. mutabilis*): recognized by the Iban and
  Dusun as distinct, and confirmed as distinct in 2022 [4]. **Now researched
  (passed its gate check 2026-11-25; see `artocarpus-mutabilis.md`):** it is
  the *wild* sister of marang — pingan (Iban) / tonggom-tonggom (Dusun), with
  smaller, less-sweet, thinner-pulped fruit — and is not cultivated. Its
  research also found that the Iban name "pingan" is assigned by Gardner et
  al. (2022) to *A. mutabilis*, conflicting with the `pingan` (*A.
  sarawakensis*) proposed row below.
- **Not proposed:** *A. elasticus* is named only as a confusable species
  and a rootstock [1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a prized fruit that is mostly not planted.**
- **Reputation:** many describe it as finer and more delicately flavoured
  than jackfruit [1][2]. In Sarawak, cultivated marang is among the most
  esteemed fruits, with a ready local market for the small supply [1].
- **PROSEA's puzzle:** "it is puzzling why marang is not widely grown."
  Low yield and short shelf life explain it only partly, since neither
  would deter home gardeners [1].
- **Philippines, 1987:** 1,700 ha produced about 7,900 t worth about 15
  million pesos (US$750,000), at a mean yield of 4.6 t/ha [1].
- **Mindanao now:** it grows as a volunteer on marginal hilly land, with
  no identified plantation, and is undervalued and under-used [6].
  - **Trade study (2026):** profit varies strongly with season.
    Soccsksargen farmers and agents earn the highest margins, and Davao
    wholesaler-retailers lead among traders. The study recommends
    postharvest infrastructure [7] (abstract only).
- **Thailand:** FAO saw no prospect for orchards. Thais find the very
  sweet taste and strong smell unfamiliar, and yields and shelf life are
  poorer than jackfruit's. Processing might change that [3].

**Finding 2 — dangerous, wasteful harvest.**
- **Fruit position:** fruit is borne at the ends of long, flexible
  branches, so ripe fruit is heavy, fragile and hard to reach [1].
- **Method:** a curved knife on a long bamboo pole [1][3]. Reaching
  fruit at the end of slender twigs "is hazardous" [1].
- **Losses:** the delicate fruit should be caught, but most of it drops
  [1]. Tree-ripened fruit turns brownish, falls and splits easily [2].
- **Picking stage:** it can be cut full-sized but still firm and left to
  soften [2]. Wikipedia says it is best not fully ripened on the tree
  [2].
- **Breeding aim:** shorter, more branched trees that are easier to
  harvest [1].

**Finding 3 — a very short shelf life.**
- **After picking:** fruit is graded and sent to market in bamboo baskets
  or sacks as soon as possible. The ripe fruit's shelf life is "very
  short" [1].
- **After opening:** the flesh oxidizes and loses flavour quickly, so it
  must be eaten at once [2][6].
- **Scent:** the strong smell is in the rind, not the flesh. Wikipedia
  likens it to a milder durian [2].
- **Cold storage (snippet):** a 2023 *Horticulturae* study found low
  temperature reduced peel browning and deterioration for up to 16 days
  [11]. Temperatures were not seen.
- **Consequence:** it is grown largely for local consumption, because the
  short shelf life limits wider use [2].

**Finding 4 — cultivated trees are larger-fruited, and marang dwarfs
breadfruit.**
- **Wild versus cultivated (Sarawak):**
  - Cultivated trees have larger inflorescences with twice as many
    flowers [1].
  - Their fruit is 3-6 times as heavy as wild fruit, up to 2.5 kg [1].
  - The cultivated form might be a tetraploid derived from a diploid
    wild ancestor [1].
  - Wide variation in plant and fruit characters has been observed [1].
- **Wild bearing is erratic:** one Sarawak forest tree failed to flower
  in 5 of 8 years and fruited only twice in that period [1].
- **Rootstock effect:** breadfruit grafted on marang rootstocks grew
  about 60% shorter, with about 80% shorter internodes [5]. A shortage of
  gibberellin in the scion is the proposed mechanism [5]. This is a
  marang trait with a use in another crop.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen tree up to 25 m, 40 cm across,
  sometimes with low buttresses [1][2][3]. Juvenile leaves are deeply
  lobed, and many trees lose the lobing at maturity [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial; one main season a year.
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 4-6 years from seed [1]. Seedlings are
  planted out at about 1 year old [1].
- **Climate zones:** humid tropical lowland and foothill.
  - **Latitude:** between 15°N and 15°S [2].
  - **Temperature:** coastal areas where temperatures never stay below 7
    °C. It is not cold-tolerant [2].
  - **Rain:** abundant and evenly spread [1][3].
  - **Elevation:** sea level to 800 m in the Philippines, in part shade
    [1][3]; wild up to 1,000 m in Sarawak [1][3].
  - **Chill:** none.
- **Composition (per 100 g edible portion; Galang 1955 via PROSEA and
  FAO)** [1][3]:
  - **Energy and proximates:** water 65.7-84.2 g; protein 0.8-1.47 g; fat
    0.2-0.3 g; carbohydrate 32.4 g; ash 0.5-0.8 g; fibre 0.6-0.77 g.
    Energy 265-510 kJ, or 63-122 kcal [1][3].
  - **Minerals:** calcium 17 mg, phosphorus 35 mg, iron 2.1 mg.
  - **Vitamins:** vitamin C 30 mg.
  - **Edible share:** 24-33% of fruit weight [1][3].
  - **Seeds:** 50-147 per fruit, each 0.5-1 g fresh, with 38-44% water.
    By dry weight they hold 11-15% protein, about 20% fat and 54-72%
    carbohydrate [1].
  - **Internal conflict:** the carbohydrate figure cannot fit with the
    highest water figure (see Estimates).
  - **USDA:** no FoodData Central entry found [9].
  - **Phytochemicals:** seed and flesh were analysed for polyphenols and
    antioxidant activity in Sabah [8].
- **Edibility:** the white flesh is raw-edible [1][2]. Seeds are edible
  boiled (30 minutes in salty water) or roasted [1][2]. Young fruit is
  cooked as a vegetable [1].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced as mechanism. Male and female flowers are
  in separate heads [1]. Stigmas stay receptive for 1-2 weeks after the
  female head emerges, and flowering follows about 3 weeks after emergence
  [1]. Fruit is lumpy where not all seeds were pollinated [2], so
  pollination is needed.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Philippines:** 4.6 t/ha mean in 1987, "not very different from
    other years" [1].
  - **Per tree:** Not sourced (see Estimates for about 67-90 kg).
  - **Wild trees:** erratic (Finding 4) [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Irrigation is advised during the first 2-3
  dry seasons [1].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** seeds lose viability quickly. They are cleaned and sown at
    once in sandy loam, or straight into containers [1][3]. Germination
    takes about 4 weeks and is hypogeal [1].
  - **Vegetative:** marcotting failed, since branches callused in 33-34
    days but did not root [1]. It can be budded or grafted onto *A.
    elasticus* and inarched with breadfruit [1]. FAO reports "some
    degrees of success" with budding, grafting and inarching [3].
  - **Clonal risk:** not applicable, being seed-grown. No viruses
    sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. Pruning is limited to dead branches
  [1].
- **Soil:** rich, well-drained loam in the Philippines; sandy clay in the
  wild in Sarawak [1][3]. pH: Not sourced. Salinity: Not sourced.
  - **Fertilizer:** 100-200 g ammonium sulphate after planting and toward
    the end of the rains. Bearing trees get 0.5-1 kg complete fertilizer
    twice a year [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** very short shelf life (Finding 3) [1].
  Temperature regimes: Not sourced beyond the snippet in Finding 3 [11].
- **Establishment:** 12-14 m apart, at the start of the rains [1]. Weed
  regularly and irrigate through the first 2-3 dry seasons [1].
- **Cultivars:** none named. Philippine and Sarawak cultivated trees vary
  widely [1].
- **Weather response:** not cold-tolerant, below 7 °C (Climate zones) [2].
  Drought, flood and wind responses: Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** rain-fed once established, with early irrigation
  [1]. No flooded culture.
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Seasons:** May-July in Luzon and August-December in Mindanao [1].
    Mindanao's two main seasons are September-October and occasionally
    December [6]. Sarawak October-January [1]. Northern Queensland over 2
    months, peaking in February after October-November flowering [1].
  - **Stage:** full size and firm, for softening off the tree; the skin
    turns greenish yellow when ripe [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** no invasive listing found in this
  pass. IUCN Near Threatened [2].
  - **Pests:** oriental fruit fly maggots (recorded as *Dacus umbrosus*)
    in the fruit; no other serious pests or diseases [1][3].
  - **Movement rules:** Not sourced.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. Tree size and the long,
  flexible fruiting branches argue against it.

## Hazards

- **Harvest injury:** reaching fruit on slender twigs is "hazardous" [1].
  Falling fruit of about 1 kg, up to 2.5 kg cultivated, comes from a tree
  up to 25 m (Finding 2) [1][2]. No injury data were found.
- **Plant toxins:** none known [10]. Seeds are eaten after cooking [1].
- **Allergy:** Not sourced for marang. Whether jackfruit's allergen risk
  (`jackfruit.md`) applies is unknown [12].
- **Food safety:** the opened flesh oxidizes fast and should be eaten
  at once (Finding 3) [2].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced.
- **Medicinal claims:** local medicinal uses are mentioned without
  detail [10].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — nothing for this crop beyond the harvest-injury
  note, which needs a physical-hazard field shared with other tall
  fruit trees.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** pole knife, or fallen fruit [1] → graded → bamboo
  baskets or sacks to market at once [1] → softened if picked firm [2] →
  rind cut round, twisted and pulled apart [2] → flesh eaten at once
  [2][6].
- **Chain (cooking):** flesh → cakes [1] and ice-cream flavouring [3].
- **Chain (vegetable):** young fruit → cooked in coconut milk as a
  curried vegetable [1].
- **Chain (seed):** seeds → boiled 30 minutes in salty water, or roasted
  → eaten, with a nutty flavour [1].
- **Chain (processing):** under study in the Philippines [1]. Processing
  is FAO's suggested route to a Thai market [3].
- **Byproducts:** peel, core and seeds have been tested as adsorbents for
  dyes, and for cadmium and copper (Brunei) [2]. These are laboratory
  uses.
- **Rootstock:** for dwarfing breadfruit (Finding 4) [5].
- **Timber:** sometimes traded as "terap" timber [10].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** the medicinal uses [10].

## Estimates and derivations

**Philippine yield check, 4.6 t/ha.** 7,900 t ÷ 1,700 ha = 4.65 t/ha
[1], matching PROSEA's stated mean.

**Yield per tree, about 67-90 kg; about 67-90 fruits.**
- **Trees per hectare:** at 12 m spacing, 10,000 ÷ 144 = 69 trees/ha. At
  14 m spacing, 10,000 ÷ 196 = 51 trees/ha [1].
- **Per tree:** 4.6 t/ha [1] ÷ 69 = 67 kg; ÷ 51 = 90 kg.
- **Fruit count:** at about 1 kg a fruit [2][3], that is 67-90 fruits.
- **Caveat:** it assumes all 1987 hectares were at full spacing and
  bearing. A per-tree survey would replace it.

**Edible flesh per fruit, about 240-330 g.** 24-33% [1] × about 1,000
g [3] = 240-330 g. A 2.5 kg cultivated fruit [1] would give 600-825 g.

**Seed weight per fruit, about 25-150 g.** 50-147 seeds [1] × 0.5-1 g
[1] = 25-147 g. This is a rough range, since the extremes need not
coincide.

**Composition check.**
- **Mass balance:** at the highest water figure, 84.2 g water + 32.4 g
  carbohydrate = 116.6 g, which is more than 100 g. The carbohydrate
  value can only belong to the drier samples. At 65.7 g water, 65.7 +
  32.4 + 1.47 + 0.3 + 0.8 = 100.7 g, which is about consistent [1].
- **Energy:** 265-510 kJ ÷ 4.184 = 63-122 kcal [1]. Atwater on the
  carbohydrate figure: 1.47 × 4 + 0.3 × 9 + 32.4 × 4 = 5.9 + 2.7 + 129.6
  = 138 kcal. That is above the stated maximum, so the ranges likely
  come from different analyses.
