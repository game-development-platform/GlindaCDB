## Naming and scope

*Melicoccus bijugatus* Jacq. (Sapindaceae); synonyms *Melicocca bijuga*
L. and *Melicocca carpopodea* Juss. [1][2]. Browne described the genus
in 1756 from trees cultivated in Puerto Rico. Linnaeus's spelling
*Melicocca* was used for two centuries, until a 1994 proposal to
conserve it was rejected [2]. The epithet refers to the leaves, which
have two pairs of leaflets [2].
- **Names:** mamoncillo; Spanish lime (Florida) [1].
  - **Caribbean:** genip, ginep, guinep (Jamaica, Bahamas, Trinidad and
    elsewhere) [1]; quenepa (Puerto Rico, Dominican Republic) [1][2];
    quenepe, kenèp (Haiti) [1][2]; limoncillo (Dominican Republic) [1];
    ackee or "Bajan ackee" (Barbados) [1][2].
  - **South and Central America:** mamón (Colombia to Argentina), maco,
    muco, macao (Colombia, Venezuela), honeyberry and marmalade box
    (Guyana), knippa (Suriname), quenett (French Guiana), guayo and
    grosella de miel (Mexico), tapaljocote (El Salvador) [1].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Ackee"** in Barbados means this fruit, not *Blighia sapida*
    (`ackee.md`) [1][6].
  - **"Mamoncillo chino"** in Cuba is longan (`longan.md`) [6].
  - **"Quenepe chinois"** in Haiti is lychee (`lychee.md`) [6].
- **Range:** native to Colombia, Venezuela and Margarita Island, the
  Guianas and Suriname [1][2]. It is cultivated and grows wild in those
  countries, and also in coastal Ecuador, lowland Central America, the
  West Indies and the Bahamas [1]. It probably reached the Caribbean
  before Columbus [2].
  - **Florida:** occasional as far north as Fort Myers and Palm Beach.
    It is most plentiful in Key West, especially as a street tree [1].
  - **Elsewhere:** a few trees in California, Hawaii, Israel, the
    Philippines and Zanzibar [1].

**Gate check (2026-11-23): passes.** The row was proposed from
`longan.md` [6]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a Neotropical genus in its own tribe (Melicocceae) [2].
  It is not close to the Asian longan, lychee or rambutan, and not to
  the West African ackee [2][6].
- **Range:** northern South America, Central America and the Caribbean
  [1][2]. No other researched Sapindaceae fruit in this project is
  American.
- **Farming system:** trees are usually male or female, so female trees
  need a male tree nearby (Finding 1) [1][2]. It is a hardy, drought-
  tolerant dooryard and street tree on limestone and poor soils, given
  almost no care (Finding 2) [1].
- **Harvest:** whole clusters are cut from tall trees with poles. The
  season runs from June to October [1][3].
- **Uses:** lime-sized fruit eaten out of hand by sucking the thin pulp
  off a large seed. Also juice, rum infusions, and roasted seeds eaten
  in place of cassava (Finding 3) [1][3].

**Relatives in this project:**
- longan (`longan`, `longan.md`), the proposing doc, and lychee
  (`lychee`, `lychee.md`): the same family. Their seeds were assayed
  for toxins alongside mamoncillo seeds [4][6].
- ackee (`ackee`, `ackee.md`): the same family; it shares the name
  "ackee" in Barbados [1][6].

**Proposed:** none new. *Melicoccus oliviformis* (huaya india) is a
related edible species [5], but no farming or market information was
found for it in this pass.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — mostly separate male and female trees.**
- **Sexes:** male and female flowers are usually on separate trees,
  though some trees are partly polygamous [1]. Wikipedia describes the
  species as typically dioecious, with occasional self-fertile trees
  [2].
- **Pollination:** predominantly female trees, or hermaphrodite trees
  acting as females, generally need a male tree nearby. In Cuba, some
  trees have enough flowers of both sexes to crop heavily on their own
  [1].
- **Genetics:** a 2019 marker study of 25 accessions in Yucatán found
  low genetic diversity, similar in both sexes [5].
- **Flowers:** fragrant, white, 4 petals, 8 stamens, in racemes 6-10 cm
  long. They are rich in nectar and attract hummingbirds and honeybees
  [1].

**Finding 2 — a hardy, dry-country tree that is given little care.**
- **Climate:** up to 1,000 m in South America. It stands several degrees
  of frost in Florida, but is too tender to fruit in California [1].
- **Drought:** well adapted to low rainfall (75-125 cm a year at Key
  West), and tolerates long droughts [1].
- **Soil:**
  - **Cuba:** nearly all soils, best on deep, rich calcareous soil [1].
  - **Florida:** thrives on the oolitic limestone of south Florida and
    the Keys [1].
  - **Colombia:** grows on soils poor enough that it is planted for
    soil reclamation [1].
  - **Habitat:** it grows wild especially in dry coastal districts [1].
- **Care:** "Ordinarily... given no care except for watering and
  fertilizing when first planted" [1]. Trees grow wild in backyards and
  along roads and trails [3].
- **Scale:** cultivated "mainly, in orchards for self-consumption or
  local sale" [5].

**Finding 3 — a large seed with a thin layer of tart pulp.**
- **Fruit:** nearly round, green, like a small unripe lime, with a thin,
  leathery, brittle skin [1]. It is 2-4 cm across [2].
- **Pulp:** the salmon or yellowish aril is translucent, juicy, scant,
  somewhat fibrous, and clings to the seed. It is acid-sweet when fully
  ripe [1].
- **Seed:** usually one large hard-shelled seed, sometimes two. The
  kernel is white, crisp, starchy and astringent [1].
- **Edible share:** 46.6-48.6% of fruit weight in Puerto Rico selections
  [1], and 48.2-55.6% in Florida selections [1].
- **Eating:** the skin is torn at the stem end, and the pulp-coated
  seed is squeezed into the mouth and sucked [1].
- **Market:**
  - **Street sale:** children often sell the fruit to tourists and
    locals as a summer refreshment [3].
  - **Export:** it reaches markets in New York, Boston and Philadelphia
    in limited seasonal quantities [3].
  - **Ponce:** in Puerto Rico it grows especially abundantly around
    Ponce, which holds a yearly Festival Nacional de la Quenepa [2].

**Finding 4 — some selection, little breeding.**
- **Puerto Rico (1960s):** George Jackson rated 54 trees, discarding
  fruit with less than 45% pulp or 20% sugars. Four selections were
  kept, with 22.7-26.0% total sugars and 60-108 fruits per kg [1].
- **Florida (1976):** Carl Campbell compared selections at Homestead
  [1]:
  - **'Montgomery':** large, 51.5% pulp, good flavour, heavy cropping
    most years. Up to 18% of fruit had two seeds.
  - **'No. 3' (Key West):** 48.2% pulp, good flavour, heavy cropping.
  - **'Queen':** large, 55.6% pulp, only fair flavour.
- **Today:** Puerto Rico and Florida are working to raise the
  flesh-to-seed ratio [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** slow-growing tree, up to 25 m, with a trunk up
  to 1.7 m thick [1]. Leaves are briefly deciduous [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial; one season a year [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced for seedlings in the
  native range. In Israel, no tree had flowered before 10 years of age
  [1]. Vegetatively propagated trees bear earlier than seedlings [1].
- **Climate zones:** tropical and frost-light subtropical lowlands, up to
  1,000 m (Finding 2) [1]. Chill requirement: Not sourced.
- **Composition (per 100 g edible portion)**, ranges from analyses in
  Cuba, Central America and Colombia [1]:
  - **Energy and proximates:** 58-73 kcal; moisture 68.8-82.5 g; protein
    0.50-1.0 g; fat 0.08-0.2 g; carbohydrate 13.5-19.2 g; fibre
    0.07-2.60 g; ash 0.34-0.74 g.
  - **Minerals:** calcium 3.4-15 mg, phosphorus 9.8-23.9 mg, iron
    0.47-1.19 mg.
  - **Vitamins:** carotene 0.02-0.44 mg, thiamine 0.03-0.21 mg,
    riboflavin 0.01-0.20 mg, niacin 0.15-0.90 mg, ascorbic acid 0.8-10
    mg.
  - **Other:** tannin 1.88 g.
  - **USDA:** no FoodData Central entry was found [7].
- **Edibility:** the aril is raw-edible. The seed is eaten only after
  roasting or cooking [1][2].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced as pollinator. The flowers are visited by
  honeybees and hummingbirds [1]. A male tree is generally needed
  (Finding 1) [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced as weight. The Florida selections are described
  only as "heavy in most years" [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. It tolerates long drought on 75-125 cm of
  rain a year [1].
- **Propagation:** usually from seed. Superior types should be propagated
  vegetatively [1].
  - **Air-layering:** works in summer on branches at least 5 cm across,
    rooting in 5-6 weeks [1].
  - **Approach grafting:** feasible if rootstocks are raised in
    lightweight medium in plastic bags [1].
  - **Veneer grafting and chip budding:** have generally failed [1].
  - **Clonal risk:** Not sourced. Genetic diversity in Yucatán
    accessions is low [5].
- **Labor:** Not sourced. Picking needs ladders and poles with cutters
  [1].
- **Soil:** calcareous, limestone and poor soils (Finding 2) [1].
  Salinity: Not sourced, though it grows wild in dry coastal districts
  [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** the leathery skin keeps the fruit fresh for
  a long time, so "it ships and markets well" [1]. Temperatures and
  shelf life are Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Not sourced (spacing). Young trees are watered and
  fertilized at planting only [1].
- **Cultivars:** the Puerto Rico #1-#4 selections; 'Montgomery', 'Queen'
  and the Key West 'No. 3' in Florida (Finding 4) [1].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** rain-fed once established [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Season:** Florida June-September; Bahamas July-October [1].
    Usually July-September in its main range [3].
  - **Ripeness:** the rind turns brittle but does not change colour.
    Fruit picked too early turns blackish [1].
  - **Method:** whole clusters are clipped once a sample shows they are
    ripe [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** no invasive listing found in this
  pass. IUCN Least Concern [2].
  - **Pests:** host of the citrus blackfly (*Aleurocanthus woglumi*),
    which is controlled by parasitoids [1].
  - **Diseases (Florida):** mushroom root rot (*Armillariella
    tabescens*), *Fusarium* and *Phyllosticta* leaf spot, and algal leaf
    spot (*Cephaleuros virescens*) [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Choking:** the pulp-coated seed is slippery and can be swallowed
  by accident. It can lodge in a child's throat and cause suffocation
  [1][2]. Morton reports that a Jamaican government chemist attributed
  child deaths to choking, and rejected the claim that the pulp fibres
  coat the stomach [1].
- **Hypoglycin A in the seed:** six dried seeds held 90.5-970 µg/g HGA
  and from below the reporting limit to 5.20 µg/g MCPG [4]. HGA is the
  toxin behind ackee poisoning, and MCPG is a related toxin. This was
  the first report of either toxin in mamoncillo [4].
  - **Context:** the longan seed range in the same study reached 3,540
    µg/g [4]. The US ackee import limit is 100 µg/g (see `ackee.md`)
    [6].
  - **Roasted seeds:** they are eaten, and Orinoco peoples use the
    cooked seeds as a cassava substitute [1][2]. No source says whether
    roasting or cooking lowers HGA.
- **Pulp:** HGA is "sometimes detectable" in the fruit, possibly varying
  with ripeness as in ackee [2]. The 2016 soapberry aril study tested no
  mamoncillo [8]. Interviews in the Dominican Republic and
  Cuba reported possible toxic effects in adolescents, and throat
  irritation after eating large amounts [3]. These are anecdotal
  reports.
- **Food safety:** none sourced beyond the above.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for mamoncillo.
- **Medicinal claims:** roasted kernels with honey for diarrhoea, and
  leaf decoction enemas (Venezuela) [1]. Seeds ground into horchata as
  an antidiarrhoeal (Nicaragua) [2]. Pulp for hypertension, asthma,
  diarrhoea and constipation [3]. These are reported uses, not tested
  treatments.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — seed hypoglycin A and the choking risk. The
  project's hazard model needs a toxin field (shared with ackee, longan
  and lychee) and a physical-hazard field for choking.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** tree → cluster clipped when ripe → sold in bunches
  by street vendors [1][3] → skin torn open, pulp sucked from the seed
  [1].
- **Chain (juice):** peeled fruit → boiled → juice for cold drinks [1].
  The juice is canned commercially in Colombia [1].
- **Chain (preserves):** loose pulp scraped from the seed → pie filling,
  jam, marmalade or jelly. This is laborious for the yield [1].
- **Chain (spirits):** fruit → aged in rum → "bili" (Vieques, Puerto
  Rico) [3]. Soft drinks and alcoholic drinks generally [2].
- **Chain (seed):** seed → roasted → eaten like chestnuts [1][2]; or
  cooked → cassava substitute (Orinoco peoples) [1]; or ground →
  horchata (Nicaragua) [2]. See Hazards for HGA.
- **Honey:** the flowers give a darkish honey of good flavour, valued by
  Jamaican beekeepers despite the short March-April bloom [1].
- **Dye:** raw juice makes an indelible stain, tried experimentally [1].
- **Leaves:** scattered in houses in Panama to rid them of fleas [1].
- **Timber:** the heartwood is yellow, hard, heavy and fine-grained. It
  decays outdoors, but is used for rafters, indoor framing and
  cabinetwork [1][2].
- **Ornamental:** planted along roads and as a street tree [1][2].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. Puerto Rico's Festival Nacional
  de la Quenepa is a civic festival [2].

## Estimates and derivations

**Fruit weight, about 9-17 g; pulp about 4-8 g per fruit.** The Puerto
Rico selections ran 60-108 fruits per kg [1]:
- **Fruit:** 1,000 g / 108 = 9.3 g; 1,000 g / 60 = 16.7 g.
- **Pulp:** × 46.6-48.6% edible [1] = 9.3 × 0.466 = 4.3 g; 16.7 × 0.486
  = 8.1 g.
These are selected trees; ordinary seedlings are likely smaller and less
pulpy [1].

**Seed HGA against the ackee import limit, about 0.9-9.7 times.** 90.5 /
100 = 0.9; 970 / 100 = 9.7 [4][6]. The seed values are per gram of dried
seed. The ackee limit applies to the ackee fruit, so this is a scale
comparison, not a legal test.

**Energy check.** Morton gives 58-73 kcal [1]. Atwater at the high ends
of the ranges: protein 1.0 × 4 + fat 0.2 × 9 + carbohydrate 19.2 × 4 =
4.0 + 1.8 + 76.8 = 82.6 kcal. At the low ends: 0.5 × 4 + 0.08 × 9 + 13.5
× 4 = 2.0 + 0.72 + 54.0 = 56.7 kcal. Morton's range lies inside these
bounds.
