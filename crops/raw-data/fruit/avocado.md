## Naming and scope

The cultivated avocado, treated here as one crop encompassing its three
horticultural races and their hybrids [2]:

- **Mexican race** — the hardiest race; small, thin-skinned, high-oil
  fruit; anise-scented leaves [1][2].
- **Guatemalan race** — subtropical; round, thick-skinned fruit [2].
- **West Indian race** — tropical; large, low-oil fruit [2][1].

Commercial cultivars are mostly interracial hybrids; the dominant
modern commercial cultivar is a Guatemalan × Mexican cross. The races
differ meaningfully in frost tolerance and salt tolerance, and are
treated here as cultivar groups within this one crop entry rather than
separate crops.

Not included here: a wild and semi-cultivated close relative species,
which has its own crop entry elsewhere in this project. Comparisons for
water-use and general evergreen subtropical grafted-tree practice are
drawn, for calibration purposes only, from this project's research on
citrus crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the tree itself serves as cold storage, since fruit will
not ripen while still attached.** A ripening inhibitor produced at the
fruit stem actively prevents ripening on the tree, so mature fruit can
be held on the tree for many months after reaching maturity [1][2].
Growers exploit this by picking selectively over a long season — one
documented California growing region harvests a single major cultivar
across roughly seven months [7]. Maturity itself is judged by dry
matter content (which tracks oil content), with specific minimum
thresholds set by cultivar and by export market [2][4]. Counter-
intuitively, the longer fruit hangs on the tree past maturity, the
faster it ripens once finally picked [2]. Immature fruit, picked before
reaching the minimum maturity threshold, never ripens properly at all —
it instead turns rubbery and shriveled [1].

**Finding 2 — two distinct flower types exist, and growers must plant
both together.** Each individual flower opens twice on two separate
occasions — first functioning as female, then as male roughly a day
later — and cultivars split into two types based on the specific timing
of this two-stage opening [4]. Isolated trees of a single type commonly
fail to fruit from lack of effective pollination, so commercial growers
deliberately interplant both flower types together; the species overall
is described as only partly self-pollinating [1][4]. At least one major
commercial cultivar also tends to bear heavily only in alternate years
[4].

**Finding 3 — the crop is extremely sensitive to both drought stress
and waterlogging simultaneously, an unusually narrow tolerance band.**
The roots require an unusually high proportion of soil pore space to be
filled with air compared to most other crops [5]. A specific water-mold
root-rot pathogen thrives in wet soils and is the single most
significant disease in most growing regions, actively killing affected
trees [1][5]. Water stress causes fruit to drop from fruit-set through
roughly half of final fruit size, and more broadly reduces final fruit
size, quality, and oil content; a standard international crop-water-
response reference specifically advises against regulated-deficit
irrigation for this crop, unlike many other tree fruits [5]. Average
water use is remarkably stable year-round (reflecting the crop's
evergreen habit), and documented regional gross irrigation volumes are
substantial [5]. Separately, the crop is salinity-sensitive, with
chloride specifically causing damage; sensitivity varies meaningfully
by race, with the Mexican race most sensitive and the West Indian race
least sensitive, and rootstock choice also significantly affects
salinity tolerance [1][5][8].

**Finding 4 — grafted clonal cultivars on selected rootstocks, with an
unusually long productive stand life.** Seedling trees bear fruit after
several years and are highly variable in fruit quality, so commercial
trees are instead grafted cultivars on either seedling or clonal
rootstocks [1][4]. Clonal rootstocks are specifically selected for
tolerance of the major root-rot pathogen, poor soil aeration, or
salinity [4][8]. Trees can bear fruit productively for 50 years or
more, with some documented specimens over a century old [1]. The
dominant modern commercial cultivar traces back to a single original
tree, historically patented and eventually lost to root-rot disease
decades later [4].

**Finding 5 — storage life is limited by two distinct chilling-related
disorders affecting flesh and skin separately.** Unripe fruit is stored
at a moderately cool (not cold) temperature and high humidity for
three to four weeks, with storage beyond roughly six weeks described as
genuinely challenging [2]. Two distinct chilling injuries occur: an
internal disorder (grey-brown flesh discoloration around the seed)
after about four weeks at a specific, somewhat warmer-than-expected
storage temperature — the main practical limit on long-term storage —
and a separate external disorder (skin blackening) below a lower
specific temperature threshold [2]. At least one major exporting
region uses a gradual step-down cooling regime (lowering temperature by
one to two degrees per week, never below a specific floor) to manage
this trade-off [2]. A deliberate ethylene "preconditioning" treatment
at moderate temperature produces ripe fruit within less than a week
[2].

**Finding 6 — documented hazards fall mainly on animals rather than
people.** A specific plant toxin present in leaves (most concentrated
there), bark, skin, and seed causes serious illness and death in
multiple animal species, including livestock, horses, rabbits, and
birds [1][3][4]. In people, the primary documented concern is allergy,
including a specific cross-reactivity syndrome linking this fruit to
natural rubber latex allergy [4].

**Finding 7 — photoperiod's role in flowering is genuinely disputed
rather than settled, elevation is directly tied to the race system's
origins, and frost remains a pure hazard with no quality upside.** Unlike
most of the other tree fruits this project has researched, avocado's
flowering trigger is not clearly temperature-only: one university source
says day length "does not seem to be" a factor and temperature is the
main driver, but a separate study on two major cultivars found short
days (≤10 hours) associated with successful flowering while 12-hour days
under warm conditions blocked it, and a Florida source ties floral
initiation to both low temperature and, it says, short day length more
importantly, with initiation starting near the shortest days of the
year — these sources genuinely conflict and are not reconciled here
[14]. Elevation is tied directly to the three-race system's origins
(Guatemalan race from upland Guatemala, Mexican race from highland
Mexico, West Indian race from lowland coastal Mesoamerica) and remains
load-bearing in modern growing regions: Kenyan commercial production
guidance sets a roughly 1,000-2,500 m band, and a Colombian study across
seven localities at 1,340-2,420 m found the flowering-to-harvest interval
lengthens at higher elevation, though no source found a clean elevation-
versus-disease-pressure relationship specifically [15]. Frost is
confirmed as purely a hazard, consistent with the race-dependent frost-
tolerance differences already noted (Finding 4/Agronomic data) — no
documented case of frost improving fruit quality was found [17].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial tree, reaching
  substantial height if unpruned [1][4].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; mature fruit is held on the
  tree and picked selectively over several months per cultivar, with at
  least one major commercial cultivar showing pronounced alternate-year
  bearing [2][4][7].
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean, and tropical-highland
  climates for the Mexican and Guatemalan races; tropical lowland
  climates for the West Indian race [1][2][4]. Elevation is load-bearing
  rather than incidental in modern tropical growing regions: Kenyan
  commercial guidance sets a roughly 1,000-2,500 m growing band, and a
  Colombian multi-site study found the flowering-to-harvest interval
  itself lengthens at higher elevation within that range (Finding 7)
  [15].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; unusually calorie-dense and fat-rich for
  a fruit, with meaningful protein content; flesh turns bitter if
  cooked too long [1][4][6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Genuinely disputed rather than settled —
  sources disagree on whether day length drives flowering at all, with
  at least one study finding short days favor flowering and long days
  under warm conditions block it, against another source describing
  flowering as temperature-driven with day length not a factor (Finding
  7) [14]. Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing, since avocado has no
  such organ.
- **Pollination:** Partially required — insect- (primarily bee-)
  pollinated, with interplanting of both flower-opening types needed
  for reliable fruit set; unpollinated flowers can still produce small,
  seedless fruit as a minor specialty product [1][4].
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield sits in the high
  thousands of kg/ha, with well-managed commercial orchards reaching
  considerably higher and documented very-good-year yields for the
  dominant commercial cultivar reaching roughly 25 t/ha (see Estimates
  below).
- **Propagation:** Exclusively grafted nursery trees (scion cultivar on
  seedling or clonal rootstock); clonal rootstocks are specifically
  chosen for root-rot tolerance, poor-aeration tolerance, or salinity
  tolerance. Seed is recalcitrant (loses viability rapidly on drying)
  and used only for rootstock production, never for fruiting-cultivar
  propagation. Grafted propagation carries a confirmed clonal/viral
  degeneration risk from Avocado sunblotch viroid, the scarred-fruit/
  crippled-tree disease already noted under Disease/pest profile:
  graft transmission via infected budwood or rootstock seedlings is
  described as its most prevalent spread route, but it is also seed-
  transmitted — a "recovery" phenomenon in previously symptomatic trees
  raises seed-transmission rates sharply, so symptomless carrier trees
  can be just as risky a seed source as visibly affected ones. Control
  in at least one major growing region relies on registered disease-free
  scion/seed sources confirmed by viroid indexing, with infected trees
  removed and stumps killed; at least one other major growing region is
  reported to lack an equivalent clean-budwood certification program
  [16].
- **Water use:** Root depth is comparatively shallow (roughly 0.5-1.0
  m); the crop needs an unusually high proportion of air-filled soil
  pore space and is best planted on raised berms or ridges with a deep
  water table, specifically to manage its dual sensitivity to both
  drought and waterlogging (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** Selective, multi-pass hand harvest over several months is
  a major and distinctive labor demand; establishment, general
  cultural care (irrigation checks, weeding, rodent control, root-rot
  treatment), and pruning/topping are also documented labor
  categories, derived from a detailed regional cost study.
- **Soil:** Light, deep, well-drained soil (reflecting the crop's
  volcanic-soil origins) is strongly preferred, with a near-neutral to
  slightly acidic pH optimum; the Mexican and Guatemalan races become
  chlorotic on high-pH calcareous soils, though the crop is
  successfully grown on such soils in at least one major region with
  appropriate micronutrient management.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees reach first harvest around the third
  year after planting; trees can remain productive for 50 years or
  more, with some specimens documented at over a century old.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific water-mold root-rot pathogen is
  the dominant and most serious disease across most growing regions,
  with rootstock choice offering tolerance but no true resistance;
  Avocado sunblotch viroid causes scarred fruit and crippled trees and
  spreads through propagation material, both by grafting and by seed
  (see Propagation, above, for its clonal/viral degeneration risk in
  detail) [16].
- **Cultivars:** A considerable number of named cultivars exist across
  all three races and their hybrids, differing in flower-opening type,
  oil content, cold hardiness, fruit size, skin character, and season.

## Hazards

A specific water-mold root-rot pathogen is the dominant disease
affecting this crop across most growing regions; Avocado sunblotch
viroid causes scarred fruit and crippled trees, spreading through both
grafting and seed from infected (including symptomless carrier) trees.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — a specific plant toxin present throughout the
tree (most concentrated in leaves, also present in bark, skin, and
seed) causes mastitis, heart damage, and death in multiple animal
species including livestock, horses, rabbits, and birds; ripe fruit is
specifically noted as toxic to caged birds. Deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — in people, the primary documented health concern
is allergy, including a cross-reactivity syndrome linking this fruit's
proteins to natural rubber latex allergy, which can in rare cases be
life-threatening. Deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Harvest (picked hard, unripe) → precooling, stem-scar
  treatment, and grading → cool storage (step-down or controlled-
  atmosphere regime for long shipping) → ethylene-triggered ripening at
  moderate temperature → fresh market. Culls and lower-grade fruit are
  processed into flesh paste, from which oil is extracted either by
  mechanical pressing/centrifuging of malaxed fresh paste or by solvent
  extraction of dried flesh, with pomace as a byproduct; at least one
  major historical producing country processed a meaningful share of
  its crop specifically for oil, soap, and cosmetic use. Separately,
  rootstock seed combined with cultivar budwood produces new grafted
  nursery trees.
- **Other outputs:** Pruning and grove-thinning wood for fuel and small
  woodworking use; seed specifically for rootstock production.
- Not covered by this research in detail: frozen processed products
  (frozen guacamole and frozen fruit halves), pickled slices, seed-
  derived ink, the girdling technique used to raise yield in shy-
  bearing trees, and honey production from the crop's flowers.

## Open research questions

- The appropriate soil-moisture-depletion allowance for irrigation
  scheduling conflicts between a standard international reference
  figure (a notably conservative allowance, for root-aeration reasons)
  and documented regional practice showing a much higher depletion
  level without yield loss — not reconciled.
- Several named cultivars of regional commercial importance were not
  covered in cultivar-level detail here.
- Packing-house labor hours (as opposed to field labor, which is well
  documented from a regional cost study) are an estimate rather than a
  directly sourced figure.
- Poor-yield and several water-use-related "placeholder" figures (dry-
  day tolerance, yield-penalty-per-dry-day, and related stress
  multipliers) are estimates rather than directly sourced figures.
- Nitrogen-uptake figures reflect fertilizer application rates in one
  documented regional system rather than a true nutrient-removal
  figure.
- Soil phosphorus and potassium uptake were not researched.
- Photoperiod's role in flowering is not resolved: sources genuinely
  conflict on whether day length matters at all, let alone in which
  direction, and this pass did not reconcile them.
- Oil-content-by-race figures conflict between two sources by a wide
  margin and were not reconciled to a single set of figures.
- Whether elevation itself drives disease pressure (as opposed to
  drainage/aeration, which is already well documented) was specifically
  looked for and not found.

## Estimates and derivations

A poor-yield tier (roughly 3,000 kg/ha) is estimated from documented
young-orchard and alternate-bearing-off-year yield figures, reflecting
the real yield variability this crop shows under water stress, root-
rot pressure, or off-year bearing.

Growth-cycle length to first harvest (roughly 730-1,825 days from
planting) is derived from a documented "bears in the third year"
figure for grafted trees, extended to a longer seedling-tree range
where seedlings are used directly (a practice reserved for rootstock
production, not fruiting cultivars).

A seasonal water-use-days index (roughly 259 days-equivalent per year)
is derived from a documented year-round average crop coefficient
multiplied through the full year, reflecting this crop's evergreen,
non-seasonal water-demand pattern; regional seasonal water totals in
millimeters are derived by unit-converting a documented regional
irrigation-volume figure (cross-checked against an independently
derived per-tree daily water-use figure, which closely matches).

Tree density per hectare is derived by unit-converting documented
per-acre planting-density figures at several different standard tree
spacings.

Labor-hour figures by category are derived from a detailed regional
cost study's per-acre hours (establishment, cultural care, pruning),
unit-converted to a per-hectare basis; harvest labor specifically is
derived by converting a documented piece-rate picking cost into an
hours-equivalent figure using the same study's documented general
manual-labor wage rate, since harvest itself is paid by the unit
picked rather than by the hour.

A seed-to-planted-tree multiplication ratio (roughly 110) is derived
from a mature orchard's yield per tree divided by an estimated typical
fruit weight, since fruiting cultivars are propagated by grafting
rather than seed and only rootstock seedlings are grown from seed.

A nitrogen-application figure (roughly 17 kg per tonne of fruit) is
derived from a documented regional per-tree annual fertilizer
application rate, divided by documented mature-orchard yield — this
represents fertilizer applied, not a true nutrient-removal figure.

A representative storage-life figure (roughly 25 days) is derived as
the midpoint of a documented three-to-four-week optimal storage window.

A peak-yield-per-day figure is derived by dividing a good-yield figure
by a documented harvest-window length in days for one major growing
region; a maximum standing-time-on-tree figure is derived from the
documented practice of holding mature fruit on the tree for months
without it spoiling, though no precise sourced limit was found.
