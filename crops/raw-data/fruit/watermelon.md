## Naming and scope

This entry covers the genus *Citrullus* as three related crop profiles
within one document, since they share enough underlying physiology (warm-
season cucurbit growth habit, water-use pattern, frost sensitivity, and
monoecious insect pollination) to document together, while differing
enough in product, yield, and cultivar range to warrant separate
treatment. Only general vine residue handling is shared across all three.

- **Dessert watermelon** is *C. lanatus* (the common sweet eating melon),
  domesticated in northeastern Africa; a specific wild melon species is
  believed to be its likely progenitor [1].
- **Citron melon** (*C. amarus*, formerly classified as a botanical
  variety of *C. lanatus*) is now recognized as a separate species: the
  preserving, fodder, or "pie" melon, and the wild tsamma melon of the
  Kalahari [1][11].
- **Seed watermelon** covers two distinct species groups grown
  specifically for their large, nutritious seeds rather than for flesh.
  The first is a Chinese edible-seed group within *C. lanatus* itself
  (the roasted-seed "guazi" melon, also called seed melon or 籽瓜), grown
  mainly in the dry northwest [27]. The second is West African egusi
  watermelon, *Citrullus mucosospermus* — the closest wild relative of
  cultivated watermelon [26], though its naming is unsettled (older
  sources use *Colocynthis citrullus* L. or *C. lanatus* subsp.
  *mucosospermus*) [23][26]. A further seed cucurbit sold under the same
  "egusi" name in West Africa, *Cucumeropsis mannii* (white-seeded
  egusi), is a different genus entirely and is excluded from this entry;
  Benin varietal trials that lump all three "egusi" species together are
  read here only for the *Citrullus* data [23].

This entry draws comparisons against several other warm-season cucurbit
crops this project has researched (for shared plasticulture, multi-pick
harvest, and grafting/cucurbitacin patterns), without naming those
specific crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — seedless watermelon requires a dedicated pollinizer crop
and more bees.** Seedless watermelons are sterile-pollen triploid hybrids
produced by crossing a tetraploid line with a diploid line. Because their
pollen cannot fertilize their own flowers, up to roughly 30% of a
seedless field is planted to ordinary seeded pollinizer plants, laid out
either as one dedicated pollinizer row per two to three seedless rows, or
mixed directly within rows at various ratios; one regional trial found a
specific ratio best for fruit quality, with a slightly higher ratio
giving similar results, while a separate source notes that dedicated
pollinizer rows still leave the large majority of harvested fruit
triploid (seedless). Large commercial operations often use non-fruiting
"super pollinizer" varieties bred purely to supply pollen without taking
up marketable growing space. National guidance recommends at least one
beehive per acre for seeded fields and three per acre for seedless
fields, while a regional extension source instead recommends one to two
colonies per acre, rising as the seedless share of a planting increases.
Triploid seed itself is notably costly and germinates poorly, so
seedless plants are nearly always set out as nursery-raised transplants
rather than direct-seeded. Seedless watermelon made up the large majority
of U.S. retail sales by the mid-2010s.

**Finding 2 — heat-loving, frost-tender, and non-climacteric.** This crop
needs sustained warmth well above typical cool-season thresholds; seed
germinates markedly faster at higher soil temperatures, and soil must
warm past a specific minimum threshold before planting. The plant is
frost-sensitive, with growth slowing markedly below a specific cool
threshold and cool snaps delaying fruit set. Fruit reaches maturity
within a documented range of roughly 80-110 days from sowing (or
somewhat more from transplanting), and critically, it does not continue
to ripen after being picked, so growers rely on a specific set of
external ripeness indicators (a ground-contact spot changing from white
to pale yellow, the nearest tendril drying out, and a dull rather than
sharp sound when the fruit is tapped). Standard cold storage is a
moderate cool temperature and high humidity for two to three weeks.
Chilling-injury guidance conflicts between two sources: one documents
injury appearing within a few days below a specific threshold, while
another places the injury threshold at a notably colder temperature over
a longer exposure — not resolved. The fruit is also extremely sensitive
to ethylene exposure, with even a very low concentration causing
softening, rind thinning, and flesh-color fading.

**Finding 3 — large-scale production systems and documented yield
ranges.** A major U.S. production region reports yields reaching a high
figure under ideal conditions, with multi-year regional averages running
considerably lower. Fields in that region are planted across an extended
window for a long harvest season and picked at least twice as fruit
matures unevenly. A world average yield figure derived from recent global
production and harvested-area statistics sits well below that region's
average, with one country responsible for a majority of global
production. A single plant seldom produces more than two or three
harvestable fruit. Water stress during early fruit growth produces small,
misshapen fruit and a specific calcium-related blossom-end disorder,
while heavy water application (irrigation or rain) shortly before harvest
causes fruit splitting.

**Finding 4 — in the seed-type species groups, the seed itself is the
marketable product, and the Chinese group is graded by seed width.** In
the Chinese edible-seed group (northwest China — Gansu, Xinjiang, Inner
Mongolia, Ningxia and Anhui), the low-sugar flesh (soluble-solids content
only about 4-5%) is not eaten; the plant is grown for its large flat
seeds, which are washed, dried, salted and roasted (sometimes glazed with
a licorice flavouring) as the "guazi" snack [27][28]. Named seed varieties
carry roughly 250-320 seeds in a 2.5-3.5 kg fruit, with a 1000-seed weight
of about 210-260 g (breeding lines reaching 340 g) [27] — higher seed
counts than an earlier snippet-level figure of 100-200 suggested [9]. The
commercial grade is set by **seed width**: before about 1989 the standard
was under 10 mm and seed yields under 1,500 kg/ha, while varieties around
11 mm wide, grown with plastic mulch and phosphorus, lifted yields to
about 2,250 kg/ha [27]. Red-seeded varieties (about 8 mm wide, a separate
market) yield only 50-75% of black-seeded ones, and the large black-seeded
cultivars are closely related and notably susceptible to anthracnose and
powdery mildew [27]. A few dual-purpose "Lanzhou Daban" types give both
edible flesh (SSC 6-7%, storable into November-December) and a ~10 mm seed
[27].

In the West African egusi group (*C. mucosospermus*), the flesh is bitter
and discarded; harvested fruit is deliberately left to rot in the field or
in bags for several days (sources differ on duration) until the pulp
softens enough to release the seeds, which are then washed out (washing
efficiency improving markedly with longer fermentation), dried in two
stages, and shelled by hand (commonly by women) or machine [4][10]. The
kernels — roughly 30% protein and 50% oil on a dry-weight basis, the oil
chiefly linoleic acid — are ground into a soup-thickening paste ("egusi"),
pressed for oil, or fermented into a traditional seasoning ("ogiri")
[4][10][24]. It is a rain-fed smallholder crop; reported dry-seed yields
run from roughly 240 to 520 kg/ha under experimental fertility (up to
about 1,000 kg/ha in the best trials), and a national figure for Cameroon
fell from about 414 to 373 kg/ha between 2015 and 2019 [23]. Yield is set
mainly by fruit number and fruit weight, and falls sharply when planting
is delayed past the early rains — late plantings can set no seed at all
[23].

**Finding 5 — citron melon: a preserving, fodder, and emergency water
melon.** This species has hard white flesh, rarely eaten raw, that is
notably high in pectin, making it valued for preserves (jams and a
traditional cooked fruit preserve), pickles, and cattle feed. It grows
wild across the Kalahari region, where it serves as a documented dry-
season water source for both wildlife and people; it was cultivated in
ancient Egypt several millennia ago and is documented as invasive in
parts of western Mexico today [11]. Certain accessions of this species
show resistance to a specific fungal wilt disease and to root-knot
nematodes, making them useful as a watermelon rootstock option [8].
Pectin content and preserve use are also supported by [12].

**Finding 6 — "camel melon": a widespread introduced weed in Australia,
and a rootstock that out-yields ungrafted plants but not commercial
stocks.** Australia's Plant Census treats the name *C. lanatus* as
misapplied there to *C. amarus*, the camel melon; the name is still
contested, with some authors using *C. lanatus* var. *citroides* [19].
It is widely distributed across Australia, probably introduced in the
1800s and thought to have come with Afghan camel-train operators in
the 1860s, and is grouped with other summer-growing invasive cucurbit
weeds; an older secondary source lists it as a noxious weed in Victoria
and Western Australia, which was not verified against current state
declarations [19]. A 2017 *Crop Protection* review of its biology and
management exists but was not read [19]. As a rootstock, a 2025
two-season trial in Adana, Türkiye, grafted watermelon onto 11 *C.
amarus* genotypes: total yield beat ungrafted controls but fell short of
commercial rootstocks, and fruit sugar rose while lycopene and
carotenoids did not [20].

**Finding 7 — egusi seed is a documented aflatoxin risk, and the fix is
drying and storage rather than the field.** Aspergillus is the dominant
mould on stored egusi kernels, and aflatoxin B1 contamination is a
post-harvest, not a field, problem: one Nigerian survey detected aflatoxin
B1 in 27% of 137 farm-store samples (higher in the humid forest zone than
the drier savanna), and a storage study found no aflatoxin for the first
few months but rising contamination from about the fourth week in poorly
dried or poorly stored seed, worst in jute sacks [25]. Prompt two-stage
drying and dry storage are the controls; a botanical (lemon-grass) seed
treatment reduced infestation in one trial [25]. Fermentation into the
"ogiri" condiment itself destroys much of the toxin — one study lost about
65% of aflatoxin B1 and 83% of aflatoxin G1 by the third day and reached
full detoxification by the fourth — but this is a salvage pathway, not a
safeguard, since the mould must still be kept down in storage [25]. This
is the ergot/fungal-contamination hazard class the current checklist calls
out, and it is specific to the seed groups (the dessert- and citron-melon
profiles carry their own, different hazards).

## Agronomic data (dessert watermelon, *C. lanatus*)

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual trailing vine, grown as a
  fruit crop though botanically closer to a vegetable-type cucurbit; can
  reach several meters in vine length.
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous/multi-pick harvest within a single
  season, since fruit sets at different times across the planting; at
  least two picks, sometimes three to four, across one harvest window.
  Harvested at a single ripeness stage, since the fruit does not continue
  to ripen once cut.
- **Climate:** Irrigated arid and semiarid climates, Mediterranean,
  subtropical, tropical, and warm-temperate summer production.
- **Composition:** A low calorie density and very high water content,
  with modest protein content, typical of a watery dessert melon.
- **Pollination:** Required, insect-pollinated (monoecious flowers);
  seedless (triploid) production specifically requires a dedicated
  pollinizer planting and elevated hive density, since triploid pollen is
  sterile (Finding 1). Poor pollination generally produces misshapen,
  pear-shaped fruit.
- **Yield:** A wide range from a world average up through a major
  production region's multi-year average and an ideal-conditions ceiling
  figure, down to a considerably lower estimated figure for low-input,
  rainfed production (Finding 3; see Estimates below for adopted tier
  values).
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve exists
  for this species, with documented stage lengths differing between a
  warm desert-climate season and a cooler Mediterranean-climate season.
  This is a deep-rooted, moderately drought-tolerant but waterlogging-
  sensitive crop; clay soils are avoided unless carefully irrigated, to
  prevent prolonged saturation and associated root rot and sudden wilt.
  Water stress during establishment is sometimes deliberately applied to
  encourage deep rooting, but stress during fruit set and sizing causes
  small or misshapen fruit and calcium-related disorders, while large
  water swings near harvest cause splitting (Finding 3).
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding or by transplant; seedless
  (triploid) plantings are always raised as transplants, started warm and
  comparatively dry, given costly and poor-germinating triploid seed.
  Diploid F1 hybrid seed is also common for seeded cultivars. Grafting
  onto resistant cucurbit rootstocks (a bottle-gourd species, a squash
  interspecific hybrid, or resistant citron-melon accessions) is an
  optional disease-management option, primarily against a specific
  fungal wilt disease.
- **Labor:** Partially documented via regional small-acreage guides and
  enterprise budgets, with harvest and post-harvest handling identified
  as particularly labor-intensive stages; other labor tiers are estimated
  by scaling from this documented baseline.
- **Soil:** A moderately wide pH tolerance; non-saline sandy loam or silt
  loam soils are preferred, since light soils warm earlier in spring; a
  high-yield crop holds a documented nitrogen quantity in its biomass,
  with phosphorus and potassium maintenance rates also documented for
  depleted soils.
- **Stand life:** A single-season annual crop.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal wilt disease and a soil-
  borne fungal root-rot pathogen, anthracnose, a bacterial fruit-blotch
  disease (not spread after harvest), charcoal rot and a specific vine-
  decline disease under heat stress, and a virus that can move
  symptomlessly in infected transplants.
- **Cultivars:** A very large number of named cultivars (reportedly over
  a thousand) spanning seeded diploid hybrids and open-pollinated types,
  seedless triploid hybrids, miniature "personal" seedless types, small
  "icebox" types, disease-resistant heritage open-pollinated lines, a
  record-setting giant type, and a luxury black-rind regional specialty
  type.
- **Harvest:** Hand-cut at a single ripeness stage determined by external
  indicators (Finding 2), since the fruit does not ripen further once
  picked; windrowed and hand-to-hand loaded onto transport in large
  commercial operations.
- **Storage:** A moderate cool temperature and high humidity for roughly
  two to three weeks; extremely ethylene-sensitive, and chilling-injury
  guidance conflicts between sources on the exact threshold (Finding 2).

## Agronomic data (seed watermelon: Chinese edible-seed and West African egusi groups)

*(Re-researched 2026-11-25 against the full current checklist in
`skills/crop-research/SKILL.md` and `templates/research-doc.md`. "Chinese
group" is the *C. lanatus* edible-seed/guazi type of northwest China;
"egusi group" is West African *C. mucosospermus*. Genus-level traits
shared with the dessert-watermelon profile above are cited there.)*

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season frost-tender annual trailing vine,
  as the dessert-watermelon profile, but grown to physiological seed
  maturity and harvested once at the end of the season rather than picked
  for fresh fruit [27].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single end-of-season harvest. Chinese-group growers
  "sow after the last frost and wait until senescence to harvest," about
  55 days from pollination to seed-fruit ripening and over 120 days total
  crop development [27]. This is the opposite of the dessert type's
  asynchronous multi-pick harvest — the whole planting is taken at once
  for seed.
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Annual; first (and only) harvest in the
  planting year, ~120 days from sowing for the Chinese group [27]; not
  separately sourced for egusi.
- **Climate zones:** Chinese group concentrated in the hot, intensely
  sunny, extremely dry summer climate of northwest China (Gansu, Xinjiang,
  Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Anhui), where dry sunny conditions are credited
  with heavier seed [27][28]. Egusi group grown across the forest and
  savanna zones of West Africa (Benin, Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal, Guinea,
  Cameroon) as a rain-fed crop [23][26]. No chill or vernalization
  requirement (warm-season annual); no notable elevation band sourced for
  either group.
- **Composition:** Flesh not the product (Chinese-group flesh SSC ~4-5%
  and not eaten; egusi flesh bitter) [27][23]. Egusi kernels ~30% protein
  and ~50% oil on a dry basis (reported fat 50-59 g/100 g, protein 22-36
  g/100 g), the oil chiefly linoleic acid; defatted egusi flour reaches
  ~58-60% protein [24]. Chinese-group seed is protein- and oil-rich but no
  measured profile was found.
- **Edibility:** The seed is the food. Chinese-group seed roasted and eaten
  as a snack; egusi kernel ground into soup paste, pressed for oil, or
  fermented into a condiment (see Uses) [27][4][10]. Flesh discarded in
  both.
- **Pollination:** Genus-level, insect-pollinated monoecious flowers (see
  dessert-watermelon profile). Both seed groups are grown as
  open-pollinated landraces or OP cultivars, not triploid hybrids, so the
  dedicated-pollinizer and elevated-hive requirements of seedless dessert
  production do not apply [27].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not independently sourced for either seed
  group; genus-level evidence for *Citrullus* is conflicting (day-neutral
  vs. long-day, see citron-melon Photoperiod bullet and [18]). Egusi's
  strong planting-date effect on yield appears driven by rainfall and
  season length rather than photoperiod, but this was not tested directly
  [23]. Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing (a vine annual).
- **Yield:** Chinese group: dry-seed yields under about 1,500 kg/ha for the
  old narrow-seeded varieties, about 2,250 kg/ha for improved ~11 mm
  varieties with plastic mulch and phosphorus, and about 202 kg/mu (~3,000
  kg/ha) in a high-input trial; a national figure of ~200,000 t over
  ~140,000 ha (~1,430 kg/ha) is internally inconsistent with the 2,250
  figure and is treated as a rough national average [27]. Egusi group:
  ~240-520 kg/ha under experimental fertility, up to ~1,000 kg/ha at best,
  with a Cameroon national yield of ~373-414 kg/ha (2015-2019, declining)
  [23]. Red-seeded Chinese varieties yield 50-75% of black-seeded [27].
- **Water use:** No seed-group-specific crop-coefficient figure; carried
  over by analogy from dessert watermelon (see Estimates). Chinese group is
  irrigated in the arid northwest; egusi is rain-fed [27][23].
- **Propagation:** Direct-seeded in both groups; harvested seed doubles
  informally as planting seed. Both are open-pollinated landraces/OP
  cultivars, not F1 hybrids, so no triploid-transplant or clonal/viral
  degeneration issue arises; the main genetic concern is the narrow base
  and disease susceptibility of the large black-seeded Chinese cultivars
  [27]. Seed colour in the Chinese group segregates on a small set of genes
  (red *r*, tan *t*, white *w*; black-dotted dominant over all) [27].
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours for either group; egusi shelling is noted
  as a hand operation commonly done by women, and machine shellers exist
  [4][10].
- **Soil:** Chinese group on irrigated northwest soils; egusi on rain-fed
  West African soils, responsive to phosphorus (optimum ~20 kg P/ha) and to
  poultry manure, with best fruit/yield at ~150 kg/ha NPK and 1 × 1 m
  spacing [23]. Salinity tolerance not sourced for either seed group;
  genus-level dessert watermelon is only rated "moderately sensitive
  (estimate)" (see citron-melon Salinity bullet and [18]).
- **Storage (postharvest):** The stored product is dry seed, not fresh
  fruit. The key risk is mould and aflatoxin in poorly dried or humidly
  stored egusi seed (Finding 7); prompt two-stage drying, dry storage, and
  avoiding jute sacks are the controls [25]. Dual-purpose Chinese "Lanzhou
  Daban" fruit keeps into November-December [27]. Storage insects in stored
  egusi are a documented concern [10][25].
- **Establishment:** Direct sowing after the last frost (Chinese group) or
  with the early rains (egusi); Chinese high-yield practice uses plastic
  mulch and 3,000-4,000 plants/mu (~45,000-60,000 plants/ha) [27]; egusi
  trials best at 1 × 1 m spacing [23].
- **Cultivars:** Chinese black-seeded large-seed types 'GN-1', 'Jingyuan
  Daban', and the named 'Hetaopi', 'Green Skin', 'Dark Skin', 'Striped
  Skin' (fruit 2.5-3.5 kg, 250-320 seeds, 1000-seed weight 210-260 g);
  red-seeded 'Ningxia Red', 'QJ-25', 'Xingfeng' (narrower ~8 mm seed, lower
  yield); dual-purpose 'Lanzhou Daban' [27]. Egusi is grown as landraces;
  germplasm collections (e.g. a Côte d'Ivoire university collection) show
  wide morphological diversity [26].
- **Weather response:** Frost-tender (sown after frost, harvested before
  it); no sweetening-by-frost benefit (the product is seed). Chinese group
  suited to hot, dry, sunny climates; egusi drought/heat tolerance not
  separately quantified [27]. Heavy rain near egusi harvest would raise the
  fermentation/drying mould risk (Finding 7).
- **Water regime:** Ordinary irrigation (Chinese northwest) or rain-fed
  (egusi); no flooded/paddy requirement [27][23].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One mature-seed stage, taken as a single
  end-of-season harvest of the whole planting (not asynchronous) [27].
- **Soil biology:** Chinese black-seeded cultivars susceptible to Fusarium
  wilt, anthracnose, powdery mildew and gummy stem blight; no mycorrhizal
  or allelopathy finding sourced [27].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Egusi (*C. mucosospermus*) is native to
  West Africa and grown within its native range (native Benin and Ghana;
  cultivated Ghana, Guinea, Nigeria, Senegal); no invasive-species listing
  was found in this pass, in contrast to the citron-melon profile's camel
  melon [26]. The Chinese edible-seed group is a cultivated *C. lanatus*
  form with no invasive listing found; seed moving into China is subject to
  ordinary phytosanitary import inspection [28]. *Legal restrictions:* none
  specific found.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced for either seed group.

## Agronomic data (citron melon, *C. amarus*)

*(This profile re-reviewed 2026-11-09 against the full current checklist in
`skills/crop-research/SKILL.md` and `templates/research-doc.md` — see the new
Photoperiod and Salinity bullets below. The dessert-watermelon and
seed-watermelon profiles above are unaffected by this pass.)*

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual trailing vine, harvested as
  a single end-of-season crop; notably drought-hardy given its origin in
  one of the driest inhabited regions on Earth [11].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single end-of-season harvest of hard fruit, which
  can then be held into the dry season (see Storage) [11][12].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced (see Estimates below for
  the dessert-watermelon analogy).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded (see Propagation); plant spacing:
  Not sourced.
- **Climate:** Arid and semiarid climates; native to the Kalahari region
  of southern Africa, and historically naturalized as a "pie melon" or
  preserving melon in dry regions of the southern United States [11][12].
- **Edibility:** The flesh is hard and white and rarely eaten raw, though
  at least one documented indigenous culinary tradition eats it both raw
  and cooked [11]. In South Africa and Botswana it is cooked in water and
  mixed with maize meal into a porridge ("kgodu"), and ripe seeds are
  sun-dried and roasted as a snack [21].
- **Composition:** Fruit composition not sourced; high pectin content
  [11][12]. A University of KwaZulu-Natal thesis analysed seed nutrient
  composition, noting little is known about the food-quality attributes
  of citron watermelon; its figures were not seen [21].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this species (genus-level: see the
  dessert-watermelon profile).
- **Yield:** No dedicated yield data were found; figures here are rough
  estimates scaled down from dessert watermelon, reflecting this crop's
  typically low-input, low-care cultivation in dry regions (see Estimates
  below). A 2025 South African thesis reports wide yield variation among
  genotypes, without a figure seen [21].
- **Water use:** Estimated by analogy to dessert watermelon, but with
  notably higher drought tolerance reflecting this species' Kalahari
  origin and documented ability for harvested fruit biomass to remain
  viable unrooted for many months [11][12] (see Estimates below).
- **Photoperiod:** Not independently researched for this species.
  Genus-level sources on dessert watermelon conflict (one database
  calling it day-neutral/"indifferent to photoperiodism," another
  calling it long-day), and no citron-melon-specific study was found;
  a wild *Citrullus colocynthis* desert trial found flowering timing
  insensitive to sowing date, loosely consistent with day-neutral
  behavior, but this is analogy, not direct evidence [18]. Not
  applicable to tuberization/bulbing — a vine annual with no storage
  organ.
- **Salinity:** Not sourced for citron melon specifically — despite its
  use as a disease-resistant watermelon rootstock (see Uses), no study
  testing *C. amarus* for salt tolerance was found. For comparison,
  dessert watermelon's own FAO salinity rating (citation [13]) is only
  a non-numeric estimate — "Moderately Sensitive" — so no firm
  genus-level numeric benchmark exists either; citron melon's desert/
  Kalahari origin does not by itself establish salt tolerance [18].
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding; red- and green-seeded types exist,
  and the species readily persists as a volunteer or weedy plant outside
  cultivation [11][12].
- **Storage:** Hard fruit can be stored for an extended period into the
  dry season, consistent with its documented ability to persist unrooted
  for many months; a specific dry-storage duration figure here is a rough
  estimate [12] (see Estimates below).
- **Uses:** Preserves and a traditional cooked fruit preserve (reflecting
  high pectin content), pickles, cattle fodder, roasted seeds in at least
  one named traditional type, and use as a disease-resistant watermelon
  rootstock [8][11][12][20].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced for this species.
- **Weather response:** Drought: highly tolerant, the defining trait
  (Kalahari origin; a wild water source in the dry season) [11]; a USDA-
  ARS project (2025-2028) plans to map drought-tolerance loci from
  desert and citron watermelon, and a 2022 drought-screening study of
  accessions exists (findings not seen) [22]. Frost, heat and
  waterlogging: Not sourced for this species.
- **Water regime:** Rain-fed or low-input in dry regions; no flooded
  requirement [11].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One mature-fruit stage for preserves,
  fodder and seed [11][12]; no separate green stage found.
- **Soil biology:** Some accessions resist a fungal wilt (Fusarium) and
  root-knot nematodes, the basis of rootstock use [8]. Mycorrhizal or
  allelopathy findings: Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to the Kalahari; documented as
  invasive in part of western Mexico [11]; a widespread introduced weed
  ("camel melon") in Australia, with an unverified noxious-weed listing
  in Victoria and Western Australia (Finding 6) [19]. *Legal
  restrictions:* the possible Victorian and Western Australian noxious
  listings are the only ones found [19].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced as a fruit crop; as a rootstock
  it is used in grafted watermelon transplant production (Finding 6)
  [20].
- **Cultivars:** Red- and green-seeded types and a named red-seeded
  heritage type [12]; 11 genotypes were trialled as rootstocks [20].

## Hazards

- Fresh-cut dessert-watermelon flesh carries ordinary cut-produce
  microbial contamination risk (specific foodborne bacteria); standard
  food-safety handling practice applies. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller
  write-up of fresh-cut melon pathogen risk, shared across the melon-type
  crops this project has researched, is deferred until this project has
  a dedicated place for this kind of shared health/safety data.
- A bitter plant-toxin class is documented in wild and some semi-wild
  *Citrullus* forms (including some citron-melon types) and in material
  carried over from grafted rootstocks; this is the same general toxin
  class documented for other bitter wild cucurbit fruit this project has
  researched. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — flagged for the same shared write-up
  noted above.
- A specific small, bumpy wild citron-melon form is documented to have
  poisoned livestock (sheep) in at least one historical account; this is
  a fodder-safety consideration rather than a human food-safety concern,
  relevant specifically to citron melon's use as cattle feed.
- **Aflatoxin in stored egusi seed (fungal-contamination hazard).**
  Aspergillus is the dominant storage mould on egusi kernels and aflatoxin
  B1 is a documented post-harvest contaminant — found in 27% of farm-store
  samples in one Nigerian survey, rising from about the fourth week in
  poorly dried or humidly stored seed, worst in jute sacks (Finding 7)
  [25]. Prompt two-stage drying and dry storage are the controls;
  fermentation into "ogiri" destroys most of the toxin (~65% of aflatoxin
  B1 by day 3, full detox by day 4) but is a salvage path, not a safeguard
  [25]. This is a distinct fungal-toxin hazard, separate from the bitter
  plant-toxin class noted above for wild/semi-wild *Citrullus*.

## Uses and processing

- **Dessert watermelon chains:** fruit → graded → packed into bins or
  cartons → stored at a moderate cool temperature. Culls → juiced, or
  fermented into wine. Rind → pickled. Pure-stand diploid fruit → seed
  saved for further planting. Tetraploid × diploid crossing → triploid
  seedless seed production. Transplants optionally grafted onto resistant
  cucurbit rootstocks.
- **Seed watermelon chains:** Chinese-group fruit → split → seeds washed
  and sun-dried → salted and roasted (sometimes licorice-glazed) as the
  "guazi" snack; graded by seed width, with wider black seeds the premium
  grade [27][28]. Egusi (*C. mucosospermus*) fruit → field-fermented (fruit
  left to rot several days) → seeds washed out (efficiency rises with
  fermentation time) → two-stage dried → shelled (by hand, commonly by
  women, or by machine) → ground into "egusi" soup paste, pressed for oil
  (~half linoleic acid), or fermented into "ogiri" seasoning [4][10][24].
  Dual-purpose Chinese "Lanzhou Daban" fruit → edible flesh plus seed [27].
- **Citron melon chains:** fruit → cubed and cooked into preserves or
  pickled; fruit fed directly to cattle as fodder; seeds roasted in at
  least one named traditional type; disease-resistant accessions used as
  rootstock material for dessert watermelon.
- Not covered in detail by this research: fresh-cut cube and slice
  processing for dessert watermelon; novelty molded/shaped fruit
  production; detailed traditional soup preparation using West African
  seed-group kernels; a further cucurbit genus sometimes sold under a
  shared common name with the West African seed-type group, explicitly
  excluded from this entry.

## Open research questions

- Chilling-injury temperature thresholds for dessert watermelon conflict
  between two sources (a notably colder threshold over a longer exposure
  in one, versus a milder threshold over a shorter exposure in the
  other) — not resolved.
- World growing-area figures for dessert watermelon differ by roughly 5%
  between two statistical-aggregator sources for similar recent years —
  not resolved.
- The naming of the West African egusi group is unsettled (*Citrullus
  mucosospermus* vs. *Colocynthis citrullus* vs. *C. lanatus* subsp.
  *mucosospermus*), and it is often conflated with the different-genus
  white egusi *Cucumeropsis mannii*; varietal trials that lump the two make
  species-specific figures hard to separate [23][26] — not resolved.
- Fermentation duration for egusi seed extraction is reported very
  differently between sources (a few days versus up to two weeks) — not
  resolved.
- Per-hectare seed yields are now sourced for both groups (Chinese

- No measured composition, water-use, labour-hour or salinity figures were

- No dedicated yield, storage, or detailed composition data were found

- 2026-11-17 pass caveats (citron melon): Finding 6 and the new lines

## Estimates and derivations

Dessert-watermelon water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy
seasonal water slots, drought-sensitivity days) are derived by applying
the standard international crop-coefficient reference's documented stage
lengths for a Mediterranean-climate season, combined with an assumed
total-available-water capacity and the reference's documented depletion
fraction and yield-response factor for this species.

Dessert-watermelon yield tiers are derived from: a world-average figure
calculated from recent global production and harvested-area statistics;
a "good" tier from a major production region's multi-year average yield
(converted to metric units); an "exceptional" tier from that same
region's documented ideal-conditions ceiling figure; and a "poor" tier
estimated for rainfed, low-input production at roughly half the world
average, since no direct source was found for this tier.

Atmospheric/growth-curve parameters and stress multipliers for dessert
watermelon are carried over by analogy from another warm-season trailing
cucurbit crop this project has researched, given the shared family and
general growth habit, adjusted slightly upward to reflect this species'
somewhat more vigorous vine growth.

Dessert-watermelon labor-hour figures are derived from regional small-
acreage guides and enterprise-budget figures documenting harvest and
transplanting/plastic-mulch labor, converted to a per-hectare basis;
other labor tiers and operations are estimated by scaling from this
documented baseline, with a fairly wide uncertainty range.

Soil nitrogen-uptake figures are derived from a documented maximum
nitrogen quantity held in the biomass of a high-yield crop, divided by
that same high-yield figure to give a per-tonne uptake rate; phosphorus
and potassium figures are estimated by scaling from documented soil
maintenance-fertilization rates.

The dessert-watermelon yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived
by dividing the adopted "good" yield tier across a documented two-to-
three-pick harvest window; on-tree standing-days and quality-decay-rate
figures are estimates, since no source specifically documents how long
ripe fruit can safely remain on the vine before quality declines.

Seed-watermelon growing-season length, pollination requirements, and
water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from dessert watermelon,
given the shared genus (Chinese group) or closely related species (egusi)
and similar general growth habit.

Seed-watermelon seed-yield tiers are now taken directly from sourced field
figures rather than built up from per-plant counts: Chinese group about
1,500 kg/ha (old narrow-seeded), 2,250 kg/ha (improved ~11 mm varieties
with plastic mulch and phosphorus), up to ~3,000 kg/ha (high-input trial,
202 kg/mu); egusi group about 240-520 kg/ha (experimental fertility) up to
~1,000 kg/ha (best trials), with a Cameroon national average near 373-414
kg/ha [23][27]. These replace the earlier estimate chain (per-plant fruit ×
seeds-per-fruit × per-seed weight × density), which is no longer needed.
The 1 mu = 1/15 ha conversion is used for the Chinese figures (the 1990
report's "6 mu = 1 acre," i.e. 15 mu/ha, giving 202.3 kg/mu ≈ 3,034
kg/ha).

Citron-melon yield tiers are estimated at roughly half of dessert
watermelon's corresponding tiers, reflecting this crop's typically low-
input, low-care cultivation in dry regions, since no dedicated yield
source was found; its drought-tolerance rating is derived directly from
its documented Kalahari origin and demonstrated ability to persist as an
emergency water source in that environment; its storage-duration
estimate follows directly from documented accounts of harvested fruit
remaining viable for months.
