# Watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus*), Seed Watermelon (Zigua, Egusi) and Citron Melon (*C. amarus*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers the genus *Citrullus* as three related crop profiles
within one document, since they share enough underlying physiology (warm-
season cucurbit growth habit, water-use pattern, frost sensitivity, and
monoecious insect pollination) to document together, while differing
enough in product, yield, and cultivar range to warrant separate
treatment. Only general vine residue handling is shared across all three.

- **Dessert watermelon** is *C. lanatus* (the common sweet eating melon),
  domesticated in northeastern Africa; a specific wild melon species is
  believed to be its likely progenitor [1].
- **Citron melon** (*C. amarus*, formerly classified as a botanical
  variety of *C. lanatus*) is now recognized as a separate species: the
  preserving, fodder, or "pie" melon, and the wild tsamma melon of the
  Kalahari [1][11].
- **Seed watermelon** covers two distinct species groups grown
  specifically for their large, nutritious seeds rather than for flesh: a
  Chinese seed-type group within *C. lanatus* itself, and a separate West
  African species, the closest known wild relative of dessert watermelon.
  A further distinct cucurbit genus sometimes sold under one of the same
  common names in West Africa is explicitly excluded from this entry, as
  it is a different genus entirely.

This entry draws comparisons against several other warm-season cucurbit
crops this project has researched (for shared plasticulture, multi-pick
harvest, and grafting/cucurbitacin patterns), without naming those
specific crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — seedless watermelon requires a dedicated pollinizer crop
and more bees.** Seedless watermelons are sterile-pollen triploid hybrids
produced by crossing a tetraploid line with a diploid line. Because their
pollen cannot fertilize their own flowers, up to roughly 30% of a
seedless field is planted to ordinary seeded pollinizer plants, laid out
either as one dedicated pollinizer row per two to three seedless rows, or
mixed directly within rows at various ratios; one regional trial found a
specific ratio best for fruit quality, with a slightly higher ratio
giving similar results, while a separate source notes that dedicated
pollinizer rows still leave the large majority of harvested fruit
triploid (seedless). Large commercial operations often use non-fruiting
"super pollinizer" varieties bred purely to supply pollen without taking
up marketable growing space. National guidance recommends at least one
beehive per acre for seeded fields and three per acre for seedless
fields, while a regional extension source instead recommends one to two
colonies per acre, rising as the seedless share of a planting increases.
Triploid seed itself is notably costly and germinates poorly, so
seedless plants are nearly always set out as nursery-raised transplants
rather than direct-seeded. Seedless watermelon made up the large majority
of U.S. retail sales by the mid-2010s.

**Finding 2 — heat-loving, frost-tender, and non-climacteric.** This crop
needs sustained warmth well above typical cool-season thresholds; seed
germinates markedly faster at higher soil temperatures, and soil must
warm past a specific minimum threshold before planting. The plant is
frost-sensitive, with growth slowing markedly below a specific cool
threshold and cool snaps delaying fruit set. Fruit reaches maturity
within a documented range of roughly 80-110 days from sowing (or
somewhat more from transplanting), and critically, it does not continue
to ripen after being picked, so growers rely on a specific set of
external ripeness indicators (a ground-contact spot changing from white
to pale yellow, the nearest tendril drying out, and a dull rather than
sharp sound when the fruit is tapped). Standard cold storage is a
moderate cool temperature and high humidity for two to three weeks.
Chilling-injury guidance conflicts between two sources: one documents
injury appearing within a few days below a specific threshold, while
another places the injury threshold at a notably colder temperature over
a longer exposure — not resolved. The fruit is also extremely sensitive
to ethylene exposure, with even a very low concentration causing
softening, rind thinning, and flesh-color fading.

**Finding 3 — large-scale production systems and documented yield
ranges.** A major U.S. production region reports yields reaching a high
figure under ideal conditions, with multi-year regional averages running
considerably lower. Fields in that region are planted across an extended
window for a long harvest season and picked at least twice as fruit
matures unevenly. A world average yield figure derived from recent global
production and harvested-area statistics sits well below that region's
average, with one country responsible for a majority of global
production. A single plant seldom produces more than two or three
harvestable fruit. Water stress during early fruit growth produces small,
misshapen fruit and a specific calcium-related blossom-end disorder,
while heavy water application (irrigation or rain) shortly before harvest
causes fruit splitting.

**Finding 4 — in the seed-type species group, the seed itself is the
marketable product, not the flesh.** In the Chinese seed-type group,
national growing area expanded severalfold between the mid-1990s and the
mid-2010s; each plant typically bears a small number of modest-sized
fruit, each containing a documented range of 100-200 seeds, which are
salted and roasted as a popular snack food. In the West African seed-type
species group, harvested fruit is deliberately left to rot in the field
for several days (sources differ on the exact duration) until the pulp
softens enough to release the seeds; the seeds are then washed out (with
documented washing efficiency improving considerably as fermentation
time increases), dried in a two-step process, and shelled by hand or
machine. The kernels are ground into a traditional soup-thickening
ingredient, or fermented into a separate traditional seasoning product;
the seed oil is documented as roughly half linoleic acid by composition.

**Finding 5 — citron melon: a preserving, fodder, and emergency water
melon.** This species has hard white flesh, rarely eaten raw, that is
notably high in pectin, making it valued for preserves (jams and a
traditional cooked fruit preserve), pickles, and cattle feed. It grows
wild across the Kalahari region, where it serves as a documented dry-
season water source for both wildlife and people; it was cultivated in
ancient Egypt several millennia ago and is documented as invasive in
parts of western Mexico today. Certain accessions of this species show
resistance to a specific fungal wilt disease and to root-knot nematodes,
making them useful as a watermelon rootstock option.

## Agronomic data (dessert watermelon, *C. lanatus*)

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual trailing vine, grown as a
  fruit crop though botanically closer to a vegetable-type cucurbit; can
  reach several meters in vine length.
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous/multi-pick harvest within a single
  season, since fruit sets at different times across the planting; at
  least two picks, sometimes three to four, across one harvest window.
  Harvested at a single ripeness stage, since the fruit does not continue
  to ripen once cut.
- **Climate:** Irrigated arid and semiarid climates, Mediterranean,
  subtropical, tropical, and warm-temperate summer production.
- **Composition:** A low calorie density and very high water content,
  with modest protein content, typical of a watery dessert melon.
- **Pollination:** Required, insect-pollinated (monoecious flowers);
  seedless (triploid) production specifically requires a dedicated
  pollinizer planting and elevated hive density, since triploid pollen is
  sterile (Finding 1). Poor pollination generally produces misshapen,
  pear-shaped fruit.
- **Yield:** A wide range from a world average up through a major
  production region's multi-year average and an ideal-conditions ceiling
  figure, down to a considerably lower estimated figure for low-input,
  rainfed production (Finding 3; see Estimates below for adopted tier
  values).
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve exists
  for this species, with documented stage lengths differing between a
  warm desert-climate season and a cooler Mediterranean-climate season.
  This is a deep-rooted, moderately drought-tolerant but waterlogging-
  sensitive crop; clay soils are avoided unless carefully irrigated, to
  prevent prolonged saturation and associated root rot and sudden wilt.
  Water stress during establishment is sometimes deliberately applied to
  encourage deep rooting, but stress during fruit set and sizing causes
  small or misshapen fruit and calcium-related disorders, while large
  water swings near harvest cause splitting (Finding 3).
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding or by transplant; seedless
  (triploid) plantings are always raised as transplants, started warm and
  comparatively dry, given costly and poor-germinating triploid seed.
  Diploid F1 hybrid seed is also common for seeded cultivars. Grafting
  onto resistant cucurbit rootstocks (a bottle-gourd species, a squash
  interspecific hybrid, or resistant citron-melon accessions) is an
  optional disease-management option, primarily against a specific
  fungal wilt disease.
- **Labor:** Partially documented via regional small-acreage guides and
  enterprise budgets, with harvest and post-harvest handling identified
  as particularly labor-intensive stages; other labor tiers are estimated
  by scaling from this documented baseline.
- **Soil:** A moderately wide pH tolerance; non-saline sandy loam or silt
  loam soils are preferred, since light soils warm earlier in spring; a
  high-yield crop holds a documented nitrogen quantity in its biomass,
  with phosphorus and potassium maintenance rates also documented for
  depleted soils.
- **Stand life:** A single-season annual crop.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal wilt disease and a soil-
  borne fungal root-rot pathogen, anthracnose, a bacterial fruit-blotch
  disease (not spread after harvest), charcoal rot and a specific vine-
  decline disease under heat stress, and a virus that can move
  symptomlessly in infected transplants.
- **Cultivars:** A very large number of named cultivars (reportedly over
  a thousand) spanning seeded diploid hybrids and open-pollinated types,
  seedless triploid hybrids, miniature "personal" seedless types, small
  "icebox" types, disease-resistant heritage open-pollinated lines, a
  record-setting giant type, and a luxury black-rind regional specialty
  type.
- **Harvest:** Hand-cut at a single ripeness stage determined by external
  indicators (Finding 2), since the fruit does not ripen further once
  picked; windrowed and hand-to-hand loaded onto transport in large
  commercial operations.
- **Storage:** A moderate cool temperature and high humidity for roughly
  two to three weeks; extremely ethylene-sensitive, and chilling-injury
  guidance conflicts between sources on the exact threshold (Finding 2).

## Agronomic data (seed watermelon: zigua and egusi groups)

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual trailing vine, essentially
  as the dessert-watermelon profile above, but harvested as a single
  end-of-season crop for seed rather than picked continuously for fresh
  fruit.
- **Climate:** The Chinese seed-type group is grown across several
  semiarid and temperate-summer northwestern growing regions; the West
  African seed-type species group is grown across a broad tropical-
  savanna belt in West Africa.
- **Composition:** The flesh is not the marketable product (reported as
  notably bitter in the West African species group); the harvested seeds
  are protein- and oil-rich, with the West African group's seed oil
  documented as roughly half linoleic acid and close to a fifth oleic
  acid by composition.
- **Yield:** No reliable per-hectare seed-yield figures were found for
  either group; documented per-plant fruit and seed counts for the
  Chinese group are used to derive a rough estimated per-hectare seed-
  yield range (see Estimates below). National growing area for the
  Chinese group more than doubled over roughly two decades, concentrated
  in a small number of named northwestern growing regions.
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding; harvested seed doubles informally
  as planting seed in both groups, though this was not researched in
  detail. Both groups are grown as traditional landraces or open-
  pollinated cultivars rather than modern F1 hybrids.
- **Harvest / processing:** In the Chinese group, fruits are split open
  and seeds washed and sun-dried, then lightly salted and roasted (and
  sometimes coated in a licorice-flavored glaze) as a popular snack food.
  In the West African group, fruits are left to rot in the field or in
  bags for several days until the pulp softens; seeds are then washed
  out (washing efficiency improves markedly with longer fermentation),
  dried in two stages, and shelled by hand (commonly by women) or
  machine; kernels are ground into a traditional soup-thickening
  ingredient, pressed for oil, or further processed into a traditional
  fermented seasoning product.
- **Quality risks:** Microbial and fungal contamination during the
  fermentation and drying stages, and insect damage during storage, are
  documented quality concerns in formal processing-standards guidance for
  the West African group.

## Agronomic data (citron melon, *C. amarus*)

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual trailing vine, harvested as
  a single end-of-season crop; notably drought-hardy given its origin in
  one of the driest inhabited regions on Earth.
- **Climate:** Arid and semiarid climates; native to the Kalahari region
  of southern Africa, and historically naturalized as a "pie melon" or
  preserving melon in dry regions of the southern United States.
- **Composition:** Not independently researched; the flesh is hard and
  white and rarely eaten raw, though at least one documented indigenous
  culinary tradition eats it both raw and cooked.
- **Yield:** No dedicated yield data were found; figures here are rough
  estimates scaled down from dessert watermelon, reflecting this crop's
  typically low-input, low-care cultivation in dry regions (see Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** Estimated by analogy to dessert watermelon, but with
  notably higher drought tolerance reflecting this species' Kalahari
  origin and documented ability for harvested fruit biomass to remain
  viable unrooted for many months.
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding; red- and green-seeded types exist,
  and the species readily persists as a volunteer or weedy plant outside
  cultivation.
- **Storage:** Hard fruit can be stored for an extended period into the
  dry season, consistent with its documented ability to persist unrooted
  for many months; a specific dry-storage duration figure here is a rough
  estimate.
- **Uses:** Preserves and a traditional cooked fruit preserve (reflecting
  high pectin content), pickles, cattle fodder, roasted seeds in at least
  one named traditional type, and use as a disease-resistant watermelon
  rootstock.

## Hazards

- Fresh-cut dessert-watermelon flesh carries ordinary cut-produce
  microbial contamination risk (specific foodborne bacteria); standard
  food-safety handling practice applies. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller
  write-up of fresh-cut melon pathogen risk, shared across the melon-type
  crops this project has researched, is deferred until this project has
  a dedicated place for this kind of shared health/safety data.
- A bitter plant-toxin class is documented in wild and some semi-wild
  *Citrullus* forms (including some citron-melon types) and in material
  carried over from grafted rootstocks; this is the same general toxin
  class documented for other bitter wild cucurbit fruit this project has
  researched. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — flagged for the same shared write-up
  noted above.
- A specific small, bumpy wild citron-melon form is documented to have
  poisoned livestock (sheep) in at least one historical account; this is
  a fodder-safety consideration rather than a human food-safety concern,
  relevant specifically to citron melon's use as cattle feed.
- Fermenting and drying seed (in the West African seed-type group) carries
  a documented fungal-contamination risk during processing; specific
  mycotoxin data were not researched.

## Uses and processing

- **Dessert watermelon chains:** fruit → graded → packed into bins or
  cartons → stored at a moderate cool temperature. Culls → juiced, or
  fermented into wine. Rind → pickled. Pure-stand diploid fruit → seed
  saved for further planting. Tetraploid × diploid crossing → triploid
  seedless seed production. Transplants optionally grafted onto resistant
  cucurbit rootstocks.
- **Seed watermelon chains:** Chinese-group fruit → split → seeds washed
  and dried → salted and roasted. West African-group fruit → field-
  fermented → seeds washed → dried → shelled → ground into soup
  thickener, pressed for oil, or fermented into a traditional seasoning
  product.
- **Citron melon chains:** fruit → cubed and cooked into preserves or
  pickled; fruit fed directly to cattle as fodder; seeds roasted in at
  least one named traditional type; disease-resistant accessions used as
  rootstock material for dessert watermelon.
- Not covered in detail by this research: fresh-cut cube and slice
  processing for dessert watermelon; novelty molded/shaped fruit
  production; detailed traditional soup preparation using West African
  seed-group kernels; a further cucurbit genus sometimes sold under a
  shared common name with the West African seed-type group, explicitly
  excluded from this entry.

## Open research questions

- Chilling-injury temperature thresholds for dessert watermelon conflict
  between two sources (a notably colder threshold over a longer exposure
  in one, versus a milder threshold over a shorter exposure in the
  other) — not resolved.
- World growing-area figures for dessert watermelon differ by roughly 5%
  between two statistical-aggregator sources for similar recent years —
  not resolved.
- The precise taxonomic identity of the West African seed-type group is
  disputed between sources, with at least one treating it as a different
  genus entirely from a second commonly-used common name — not resolved.
- Fermentation duration for West African seed-group processing is
  reported very differently between sources (a few days versus up to two
  weeks) — not resolved.
- No reliable per-hectare seed-yield data were found for either seed-type
  species group; the estimates provided here rest on a chain of
  assumptions about planting density and per-seed weight.
- No dedicated yield, storage, or detailed composition data were found
  for citron melon; figures here are rough estimates scaled from dessert
  watermelon.
- Isolation distance and diploid seed-yield figures for dessert-
  watermelon seed production were not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

Dessert-watermelon water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy
seasonal water slots, drought-sensitivity days) are derived by applying
the standard international crop-coefficient reference's documented stage
lengths for a Mediterranean-climate season, combined with an assumed
total-available-water capacity and the reference's documented depletion
fraction and yield-response factor for this species.

Dessert-watermelon yield tiers are derived from: a world-average figure
calculated from recent global production and harvested-area statistics;
a "good" tier from a major production region's multi-year average yield
(converted to metric units); an "exceptional" tier from that same
region's documented ideal-conditions ceiling figure; and a "poor" tier
estimated for rainfed, low-input production at roughly half the world
average, since no direct source was found for this tier.

Atmospheric/growth-curve parameters and stress multipliers for dessert
watermelon are carried over by analogy from another warm-season trailing
cucurbit crop this project has researched, given the shared family and
general growth habit, adjusted slightly upward to reflect this species'
somewhat more vigorous vine growth.

Dessert-watermelon labor-hour figures are derived from regional small-
acreage guides and enterprise-budget figures documenting harvest and
transplanting/plastic-mulch labor, converted to a per-hectare basis;
other labor tiers and operations are estimated by scaling from this
documented baseline, with a fairly wide uncertainty range.

Soil nitrogen-uptake figures are derived from a documented maximum
nitrogen quantity held in the biomass of a high-yield crop, divided by
that same high-yield figure to give a per-tonne uptake rate; phosphorus
and potassium figures are estimated by scaling from documented soil
maintenance-fertilization rates.

The dessert-watermelon yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived
by dividing the adopted "good" yield tier across a documented two-to-
three-pick harvest window; on-tree standing-days and quality-decay-rate
figures are estimates, since no source specifically documents how long
ripe fruit can safely remain on the vine before quality declines.

Seed-watermelon growing-season length, pollination requirements, and
water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from dessert watermelon,
given the shared genus (for the Chinese group) or closely related genus
(for the West African group) and similar general growth habit.

Seed-watermelon seed-yield figures are built from a chain of estimates:
a documented per-plant fruit count and seeds-per-fruit figure for the
Chinese group, combined with an assumed per-seed dry weight (since no
direct weight figure was found) and an assumed planting density, to
derive a rough per-hectare dry-seed-yield estimate; this is among the
weakest estimates in this entry and would be considerably improved by a
dedicated regional seed-yield trial.

Citron-melon yield tiers are estimated at roughly half of dessert
watermelon's corresponding tiers, reflecting this crop's typically low-
input, low-care cultivation in dry regions, since no dedicated yield
source was found; its drought-tolerance rating is derived directly from
its documented Kalahari origin and demonstrated ability to persist as an
emergency water source in that environment; its storage-duration
estimate follows directly from documented accounts of harvested fruit
remaining viable for months.
