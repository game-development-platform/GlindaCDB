## Naming and scope

*Blighia sapida* K.D. Koenig (Sapindaceae); syn. *Cupania sapida* [1][3].
- **Names:** ackee, akee, achee, akee apple, vegetable brain (*seso
  vegetal*) [1][2]. From the Akan *akye fufo* [3]. In West Africa, *akye*,
  *akyen* or *ishin*; *finzan* in Côte d'Ivoire [1]. The timber is sold as
  *achin* [1].
- **Name clash:** in Barbados and several Eastern Caribbean islands,
  "akee" means the mamoncillo (*Melicoccus bijugatus*), not this tree
  [1][3].
- **Origin:** the forests of West tropical Africa (the old Ivory Coast and
  Gold Coast) [1][2].
- **Introduction to Jamaica:** sources disagree.
  - **1793:** brought by Captain Bligh, who also took it to Kew [1][2][7].
  - **Before 1773:** the encyclopedia's date, with Bligh's role being the
    Kew introduction [3].
  - Both agree it was in Jamaica in the 18th century [10][11].
- **Genus:** three species, all from tropical Africa [9]. A related
  species, *B. unijugata*, is used as a fish poison in Ghana [14]. Not
  proposed as a row, since no food or cultivated use was found.

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from the
longan and lychee docs, where ackee's import limit serves as the
yardstick for Sapindaceae toxins. It is distinct enough for its own row
on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** its own genus, not a cultivar group of any researched crop.
- **Range and farming:** African in origin, grown commercially only in
  the Caribbean, mostly Jamaica [2][4].
- **Harvest:** the fruit must split open on the tree before it can be
  eaten, a harvest rule no other crop here has (Finding 1).
- **Uses:** the eaten part is a fatty aril, cooked as a vegetable, and
  most of it is canned in brine (Finding 4).
- **Regulation:** a dedicated US import alert with a per-factory approval
  list (Finding 2).

**Relatives in this project:**
- lychee (`lychee`, `lychee.md`) and longan (`longan`, `longan.md`): the
  same family [3]. Their docs use ackee's 100 ppm hypoglycin A limit as
  the yardstick for their own toxins.
- mamoncillo (`mamoncillo`, proposed): shares the name "akee" in
  Barbados [1].

**Proposed:** none new.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the fruit is safe only after it opens on the tree.**
- **Toxin:** hypoglycin A (HGA) is high in every part of the unripe fruit,
  at roughly 1,000 ppm [4].
- **What happens at opening:** when the capsule splits, HGA in the arils
  and the raphe (the pink membrane joining aril to seed) falls
  dramatically and fast. Seeds and rind stay toxic [4].
  - **Light:** Morton says the toxicity is "largely dispelled by light as
    the jacket opens" [1].
  - **Mechanism:** in 'Cheese' ackees, aril HGA fell from about 8,000
    mg/kg green to 271 mg/kg ripe. Over the same stages seed hypoglycin B
    (HGB) rose from 1,629 to 11,774 mg/kg. The authors conclude that the
    seed acts as a sink for the aril's HGA [5].
- **How much remains:** sources disagree.
  - **Morton:** ripe arils keep one twelfth of the unripe level [1].
  - **Bowen-Forbes and Minott:** about one thirtieth (271 of about
    8,000) [5].
  - **FDA:** "negligible levels" in fully ripe arils [4].
  - **UWI Mona lecture page:** levels peak at maturity, then "rapidly
    diminish to non-detectable levels" once the fruit opens [11].
  - The 271 mg/kg figure is almost three times the 100 ppm US limit (see
    Estimates). The abstract doesn't state whether it is on a dry or a
    fresh basis.
- **Cooking doesn't help:** HGA is heat-stable, so cooking an unripe
  fruit doesn't make it safe [4].
- **The cultural rule:** in Jamaican tradition the fruit must "smile" or
  "laugh" (open on the tree) before it is picked [10]. Morton says it
  must have "yawned" [1].
- **Overripe fruit:** the UF/IFAS guide says fruit "split too long (i.e.,
  over ripe)" is also poisonous [2]. The FDA and the other sources don't
  mention this.

**Finding 2 — the US allows imports only from approved factories.**
- **History:** Morton (1987) says US import "has been banned" [1].
  Exports to the US began in 2000 [7].
- **Now:** FDA Import Alert 21-11 detains all ackee products without
  examination unless the factory is on the alert's Green List [4]. This
  covers raw, frozen, canned and dried products.
  - **Yellow List:** a factory petitions with its food-safety controls,
    such as a HACCP plan, supplier specifications and an export
    certificate from the Bureau of Standards Jamaica. It can then export,
    with a private laboratory analysis for each shipment [4].
  - **Green List:** after at least five consecutive shipments released
    on laboratory results, it can petition to skip detention [4].
  - **Who is on it:** in the current alert, mostly Jamaican processors,
    plus firms in Belize and Haiti [4].
- **Limit:** product with more than 100 ppm HGA is treated as adulterated
  [4]. Processors that fail it usually made one of these mistakes [4]:
  - mis-sorting ripe from unripe fruit;
  - heat-shocking fruit to make it split faster;
  - buying fruit that a supplier forced open.
- **Elsewhere:** Trinidad outlawed the tree in 1900 after deaths [1].
  Rashford says trees were destroyed there soon after Scott's 1916-1917
  papers [10].
- **Jamaica:** processed ackee has its own national standard, DJS
  276:2023, which replaces JS 276:2016 [14].

**Finding 3 — Jamaican vomiting sickness: seasonal, and long disputed.**
- **Symptoms:** acute vomiting without diarrhoea, then drowsiness,
  convulsions, coma and often death [1]. Profound hypoglycaemia is the
  key sign, and sugar solutions help [1][4].
- **Mechanism:** HGA and its metabolite MCPA block fatty-acid
  oxidation, which drains glucose stores [3]. In a volunteer study a
  second dose 24 h after the first gave much higher metabolite levels
  than a single dose. The authors suggest this may help explain why
  deaths cluster in the harvest season [6].
- **Scale in Jamaica:**
  - **CDC and the Jamaican Ministry of Health:** 38 cases of toxic
    hypoglycaemic syndrome in one investigation, 28 of them ackee
    poisoning including six deaths, and 271 cases reported since 1980
    [14] (snippet).
  - **2010-2013:** a Jamaican review lists suspected cases, for example
    320 in 2011 [14] (snippet).
  - **Under-reporting:** sources agree the toxicity is under-reported
    [14].
- **Elsewhere:**
  - **Nigeria:** eight siblings poisoned after eating roasted seeds and
    arils [6].
  - **United States:** a fatal case in South Florida in 2026, with a
    21-day decline despite intensive care [6].
  - **Burkina Faso:** an epidemic that killed every victim, all of them
    children [14] (snippet).
  - **Haiti, French Guiana and Suriname:** cases are also recorded there
    [14] (snippet).
- **Seasonality dispute:**
  - **Morton:** most cases fall in the Jamaican winter, when 30-50% of
    arils have small, undeveloped seeds [1].
  - **Rashford:** ackee is available year-round, with its main harvest
    in summer. That undercuts Scott's 1916 claim that winter is "the main
    ackee season" [10].
  - **Evans and Arnold (1938):** winter fruit has less fat and is
    "apparently" more toxic. Rashford says this is unproven [10].
  - **UWI Mona:** patients usually show chronic malnutrition, so the
    consumer's nutritional state matters [11].
- **Why the seed matters:** Morton's undeveloped seeds fit the finding
  that the seed draws HGA out of the aril [1][5]. This link is our
  inference; no source draws it.

**Finding 4 — Jamaica's ackee is mostly canned in brine for export.**
- **Scale:**
  - **Production:** about 30,744 t in 2009 (Ministry of Agriculture data
    bank), with 2,124 t exported [14] (snippet).
  - **Export earnings:** US$26 million for preserved ackee in 2023 at
    US$7.79 per kg, a 4% rise on 2022 [13]. Earnings fell to US$18.1
    million in 2024 after hurricane disruption [13].
  - **Rank:** the Ministry calls it Jamaica's third most valuable
    agricultural export [14] (snippet).
- **Employment:** about 3,000 direct and 6,000 indirect jobs [7].
- **Packaging:** the standard package is the A-2 can, about 540 g (19
  oz) [13].
  - **2026 can shortage:** a shortage of can lids halted processing in
    the June-October season, with one processor holding "about 20 million
    ackees" [13].
- **Markets:** the United Kingdom, United States and Canada, with the UK
  largest for fresh exports in 2020 [7].
- **Fresh exports:** a much smaller trade. In 2020, 139,216 kg of fresh
  ackee were worth US$718,889 [7].
- **Supply:** most fruit comes from scattered trees, many of them feral
  [2]. The Ministry is promoting about 1,000 acres of new orchards [7].
- **Data conflict:** FAO's 2005 export table gives 9,236 kg worth US$9.6
  million. That would be about US$1,040 per kg, so one of the two figures
  is probably wrong (see Estimates) [8].

**Finding 5 — a lowland tree with unresolved sex expression.**
- **Climate:**
  - **Elevation:** sea level to 900 m in Jamaica [1][2], or 1,000 m in
    the Jamaican investment profile [7].
  - **Temperature:** best at 24-27 °C, with 20-34 °C tolerated
    (Useful Tropical Plants, via PIER) [9]. The Jamaican profile gives
    21-27 °C [7].
  - **Rainfall:** best at 2,000-4,000 mm a year, with 700-6,000 mm
    tolerated [9].
- **Cold:**
  - **Mature trees:** in southern Florida they survived brief spells at
    -3.3 °C (26 °F) [1][2]. Plants can be killed at -3 °C or lower [9].
  - **Young trees:** killed by winter cold in southern Florida [1], and
    intolerant of any frost [9].
  - **Failed sites:** it doesn't fruit in Guatemala City, and has never
    survived in Guyana or Malaya [1].
- **Flowers:** sources disagree.
  - **Andromonoecious:** bisexual and male flowers on the same raceme
    [1][2]. The encyclopedia calls the flowers unisexual [3].
  - **Dioecious:** one source says the species is dioecious, so both
    sexes must be planted [9].
  - **Reconciliation:** the Hawaii risk assessment quotes a source saying
    that trees in the native range "seem to be dioecious", while those in
    Jamaica are andromonoecious [9].
- **Pollination:** by insects [2]; bees visit the flowers [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen (rarely deciduous) tree, 10-12 m
  [1]. 9-23 m in the UF/IFAS guide [2]; 80 ft in the garden flora PIER
  quotes [9].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing, with fruit ripening unevenly on
  the tree. Pickers must take only open fruit, so each tree is picked
  repeatedly [2][8]. Season by region:
  - **Jamaica:**
    - **Rashford:** some fruit all year, with peaks in the winter and
      summer dry seasons; the main harvest is in summer [10].
    - **UWI Mona:** January-March and June-August [11].
    - **Encyclopedia:** January-March and October-November [3].
    - **The 2026 processing season:** June-October [13].
    - **Investment profile:** "two and a half" crop cycles a year [7].
  - **Bahamas:** February-April and July-October [1].
  - **Florida:** spring bloom, summer fruit, sometimes a light autumn
    bloom with winter fruit [1][2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Seedlings:** 3-4 years [3][9], 3-6 years [2], or 2-3 years after
    planting, with full bearing at 7 years [7].
  - **Cuttings:** 1-2 years [3].
- **Climate zones:** tropical to warm subtropical; see Finding 5 for the
  elevation, temperature, rainfall and frost limits [1][2][7][9].
  - **Flower induction:** a dry spell followed by rain in the tropics, and
    cool weather in the subtropics [2]. The guide says "probably".
  - **Chill requirement:** none sourced, and not expected for a tropical
    tree.
- **Composition:** sources disagree (see Estimates).
  - **Raw arils** (Morton's Mexican analysis, per 100 g) [1]:
    - **Proximates:** moisture 57.6 g, protein 8.75 g, fat 18.78 g,
      fibre 3.45 g, carbohydrate 9.55 g.
    - **Minerals:** calcium 83 mg, iron 5.52 mg.
    - **Vitamin C:** 65 mg.
  - **Canned, drained** (CFNI Caribbean tables, 1998, per 100 g) [11]:
    - **Proximates:** water 76.7 g, energy 151 kcal, protein 2.9 g, fat
      15.2 g, carbohydrate 0.8 g, fibre 2.7 g.
    - **Minerals:** calcium 35 mg, iron 0.7 mg, potassium 270 mg, sodium
      240 mg.
  - **A US-branded can** (label data, per 100 g) [12]: 151 kcal, fat
    13.21 g, protein 3.77 g, sodium 377 mg, vitamin C 28.3 mg.
  - **Fat:** lipids are 51-58% of aril dry weight, and linoleic acid is
    over 55% of the fatty acids [11].
  - **The UF/IFAS table** mixes the raw and canned figures in one "fresh"
    column, for example 76.7% water with 18.78 g fat [2].
- **Edibility:** only the aril of a naturally opened fruit, with seed and
  raphe removed [2][4].
  - **Cooking:** usually parboiled, in salted water or milk, then fried
    or stewed [1].
  - **Raw:** eaten raw or in soup in parts of Africa [1].
  - **Never edible:** seeds and rind [4].
- **Pollination:** insect [2]. Sex expression is disputed, as either
  andromonoecious or dioecious (Finding 5) [1][2][9]. Self-compatibility
  is unknown [9].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Per tree, by weight:** well-cared-for mature trees give 45-68 kg
    (100-150 lb) a year [2][7]. The guide adds that real production data
    "is not available" [2].
  - **Per tree, by count:** JAMPRO gives 400-600 fruits a tree [14]
    (snippet).
  - **Per hectare:** about 4.4-8.4 t/ha (see Estimates).
  - **Fruit weight:** typically 100-200 g [3].
- **Water use:** not sourced as a crop coefficient or seasonal figure. The
  UF/IFAS guide says mature-tree water needs "have not been determined",
  and that drought stress should be avoided from bloom through fruit
  development [2]. Established trees tolerate drought [2][9], though
  fruiting may suffer [2].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** the usual route [1][7].
    - **Storage:** seeds are recalcitrant and short-lived. Sow within
      days, or hold up to 3 months in moist storage at 21 °C [2][9].
    - **Germination:** 2-4 weeks at 80% [9], or 2-3 months [2].
  - **Vegetative:** shield or patch budding, side-veneer or cleft
    grafting, air layering, and stem cuttings under mist [1][2][9].
    Vegetative propagation "is now encouraged" in Jamaica [9].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** none sourced. Stem canker of grafted ackee
    seedlings is listed among Jamaica's major fruit-tree diseases [14]
    (snippet).
  - **Variation:** Morton says seedlings show "very little variation"
    [1], yet named types exist (Cultivars).
- **Labor:** no labour hours sourced. In the Jamaican 20-acre orchard
  budget, harvesting is 96% of labour cost by year 10 (see Estimates) [7].
  - **Pickers:** fruit is picked with tools so it doesn't fall to the
    ground [7][8].
  - **Buying unit:** farmgate fruit is sold by the box of 20-22 dozen, at
    JM$3,360 a box in 2021 [7].
- **Soil:**
  - **Range:** grows on deep fertile soils and on poor ones, such as sand
    and oolitic limestone [1][2]. Also on infertile, rocky soils [9].
  - **pH:** 5.5-7.5 [7]; best at 5.5-6.5, tolerating 4.3-8 [9].
  - **Iron deficiency:** trees suffer it on alkaline soils, and iron
    chelate drenches correct it [2].
  - **Salinity:** not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - **Field handling:** keep picked fruit cool and shaded, in ventilated
    crates no more than three feet deep, and move it during the cooler
    part of the day [8].
  - **Storage:** only temporary, in a cool, dry, ventilated place [8]. No
    temperature or shelf life is given. FAO's Jamaican storage table has
    no ackee row [8].
  - **Ripening class:** non-climacteric [8].
  - **Factory ripening:** processors buy fruit that is unopened or just
    splitting and spread it on racks. Fruit that hasn't split fully
    within 3-4 days is discarded [4]. FAO gives three days on racks for
    brine-pack fruit [8].
  - **Processed:** the arils are canned in brine or frozen [4].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** at least 25-30 ft from other trees and structures [2];
    the guide's metric figures are wrong (see Estimates).
  - **Density:** 40-50 trees per acre in Jamaica [14] (snippet).
  - **Intercropping:** possible in the first 2-3 years, with vegetables,
    pineapple, pumpkin or sweet potato [7].
  - **Nursery trees:** grown in 3-gallon (11 L) containers [2].
  - **Pruning:** tops cut back to 3.6-4.6 m after several years of
    cropping [2].
- **Cultivars:** none are formally named.
  - **Jamaica:**
    - **Butter:** soft, deeper yellow, and loses its shape when cooked
      [3][7].
    - **Cheese:** firmer, cream to pale yellow, keeps its shape, and is
      preferred for canning [3][7].
    - **Range:** up to 48 cultivars in these two groups [3].
  - **UF/IFAS (Florida):** regular, hard, soft ("butter") and "cheese"
    types. The "cheese" types have more flesh and are considered superior
    [2].
  - **Toxin difference:** Bowen-Forbes and Minott tracked both groups
    [5]. The abstract gives figures only for 'Cheese'.
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** -3.3 °C briefly for mature trees; young trees are
    frost-tender (Finding 5) [1][2][9]. Frost is a hazard only, with no
    quality benefit sourced.
  - **Drought:** established trees tolerate it [2][9].
  - **Flooding:** not flood-tolerant, and may decline in flooded soil
    [2][7]. Sprinklers on timers can cause root rot [2].
  - **Wind:** pruned trees can withstand hurricane-force winds without
    toppling [2].
  - **Hurricanes:** Hurricane Beryl (2024) cut Jamaican supply [13][14].
- **Water regime:** ordinary rain-fed or irrigated orchard; no flooded
  requirement. The Jamaican orchard budget includes irrigation equipment
  [7].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one safe stage, naturally opened on the
  tree [2][4].
  - **For brine-packing:** fruit may be taken mature and just splitting,
    then opened on racks [4][8].
  - **Rejects:** diseased or damaged fruit, deep-red raphe that would
    stain the aril, seeds not black or brown, and arils or raphe that
    don't come away easily [8].
  - **Asynchronous ripening:** fruit opens at different times within one
    tree, so each tree is picked several times [8][10].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** West tropical Africa [1][9].
  - **Naturalized:** in Jamaica and across the Caribbean and Central
    America [1][2][9]. Holm and colleagues (1979) list it as a weed in
    Jamaica [9].
  - **Escapes:** it can escape cultivation on sandy soils [9].
  - **Hawaii-Pacific Weed Risk Assessment:** score 1, "Evaluate"; the
    questionnaire is dated 2009 [9].
    - **Weed status:** no garden, agricultural or environmental weed
      record [9].
    - **Seed:** no persistent seed bank [9].
    - **Dispersal:** by people and probably large birds and monkeys,
      which most Pacific islands lack [9].
    - **Where present:** Tahiti, Guam, Kauaʻi and Oʻahu [9].
  - **Legal:**
    - **United States:** fruit and products are controlled through FDA
      Import Alert 21-11 (Finding 2) [4].
    - **Trinidad:** the tree was outlawed in 1900 [1].
  - **Conservation:** IUCN Least Concern (2019) [3].
  - **Plant quarantine:** no restriction on moving plants or propagation
    material was found.
- **Controlled environment:** in European greenhouses, cuttings of ripe
  shoots are rooted in sand and grown on in peat and loam [1]. No
  fruiting under glass was sourced.

## Hazards

- **Hypoglycin poisoning (Jamaican vomiting sickness):**
  - **Toxins:** HGA in unripe arils, raphe, seeds and rind, and HGB in the
    seeds [1][4][5]. Morton calls HGB half as toxic as HGA [1].
  - **Who is affected:** see Finding 3 for symptoms, scale and
    seasonality. Children and malnourished people are at most risk
    [11][14].
  - **Mitigation:** eat only naturally opened fruit, remove seeds and
    raphe, and never cook unripe fruit expecting it to be safe [2][4].
- **Rat toxicity data:** fed within ackee, HGA's acute toxic dose was
  about 216-231 mg/kg body weight. The 30-day maximum tolerated dose was
  1.50 mg/kg/day [6]. For what that would mean in portions, see
  Estimates.
- **Breastfeeding:** a 2026 case report describes an infant in metabolic
  crisis from HGA passed in breast milk, in a child with a fatty-acid
  disorder [6] (title only).
- **Animals:** unripe arils readily killed rabbits in feeding trials, and
  rats had to be force-fed [1]. Squirrels eat unripe arils but leave the
  seeds [1]. PIER advises against planting it where children or pets can
  reach the fruit [9].
- **Fish poison:** crushed fruit is used to stun fish [1]. That makes it
  a hazard to waterways if the fruit is used this way.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for ackee.
- **Diseases:** leaf spots, and stem canker of grafted seedlings, are
  among Jamaica's major fruit-tree diseases [14] (snippet). No major pests
  or diseases have been reported in Florida [2].
- **Theft:** praedial larceny (theft of produce from farms) is a named
  threat to Jamaican orchards [7].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the safe-intake question has no home in this
  project yet. Three things would need combining, and none of the sources
  does it:
  - the residual HGA in ripe arils (Finding 1);
  - the rat dose figures;
  - the 100 ppm regulatory limit.
  The same applies to the HGA levels in canned product, and to the
  reported link between repeated meals and harvest-season deaths [6].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (home cooking):** opened fruit on the tree → pick → remove
  seeds and raphe → parboil arils in salted water or milk → fry in
  butter, or cook with salt cod, onion and tomato, or add to stews and
  curries [1][3].
  - **Byproducts:** seeds and rind, which are toxic [4].
  - **National dish:** ackee and saltfish [3].
- **Chain (canning):** mature, just-splitting fruit → racks for about 3
  days to open → discard any not fully open after 3-4 days → remove seeds
  and raphe → arils into brine → can and seal [4][8][14].
  - **Product:** about 540 g in the standard A-2 can [13].
  - **Control point:** HGA must be at most 100 ppm for the US market [4].
- **Chain (freezing):** opened arils → frozen or vacuum-packed product,
  sold to hotels and for export [4][7][13].
- **Chain (soap):** green fruit lathers in water and is used for
  laundering in West Africa [1].
  - **Ash soap:** seeds (for their oil) and rind (for potash) are burned,
    and the ashes are made into soap [1].
  - **Who makes it:** in Benin, mainly women, who keep the trees for soap
    and sale [14] (snippet).
- **Chain (fish poison):** crushed fruit → into water → stunned fish
  [1].
- **Wood:** the heartwood is hard and durable, and reported immune to
  termites [1]. It is used for:
  - construction and pilings;
  - oars, paddles and casks;
  - railway sleepers (recommended) [1].
- **Flowers:** a flower extract is used as cologne in Cuba [1].
- **Medicinal:** ethnomedical uses only, none validated.
  - **Brazil:** a seed extract was used against parasites [1].
  - **Cuba:** ripe arils with sugar and cinnamon for fever and dysentery
    [1].
  - **Côte d'Ivoire:** bark ointments for pain, crushed leaves for
    headache and ulcers, and leaf juice as eye drops [1].
  - **Recent reviews:** many more ailments are listed [14].
- **New products:** ackee oil, facial products, ackee wine and the ackee
  patty [7].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:**
  - **Seed oil:** reported pesticidal properties [7].
  - **Benin uses:** food (81% of 863 households), cosmetic (10%),
    medicinal (7%), plus small shares for sale and fertilizer [14]
    (snippet).
  - **Shade and ornament:** shade tree in Ghanaian villages and along
    streets [1].
- **Ritual and religious:**
  - **Gold weights:** the seeds were once used as standard weights for
    gold dust. The Gold Coast "ackey" currency was named after them [3].
  - **Culture:** in Jamaican tradition, the opened fruit's "smile" stands
    for well-being. The theme runs through Jamaican folk knowledge and
    art [10].
  - **Song:** ackee features in the mento folk song "Linstead Market"
    [3].
  - **Benin:** ackee trees carry a "myth" value [14] (snippet; not
    described further).

## Open research questions

- **Residual HGA in ripe arils:** sources disagree. Morton gives one
  twelfth of the unripe level, Bowen-Forbes and Minott 271 mg/kg, the FDA
  "negligible", and UWI Mona "non-detectable" [1][4][5][11]. The basis
  (dry or fresh weight) and the 'Butter' figures need the full paper.
- **Season of poisoning:** Morton puts most cases in winter, while
  Rashford puts the main harvest in summer and finds the winter link
  unexplained [1][10].
- **Sex expression:** andromonoecious in Jamaica against dioecious in the
  native range; this needs a primary botanical source [1][2][9].
- **Introduction date:** 1793 by Bligh [1][2][7], or before 1773 [3].
- **Production statistics:** the latest national tonnage found is the
  2009 snippet [14]. FAO's 2005 export row doesn't add up [8].
- **Yield and labour:** no per-hectare yield and no labour hours were
  sourced; both are estimated or reported as cost shares (see Estimates).
- **Postharvest:** no storage temperature or shelf life for fresh fruit
  or arils was sourced.
- **Overripe fruit:** the UF/IFAS guide says overripe fruit is poisonous
  [2]; no other source tested this.

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield per hectare, about 4.4-8.4 t/ha.** Inputs: 45-68 kg per mature
tree a year [2][7], and 40-50 trees per acre [14]. 40-50 trees/acre ×
2.471 = 99-124 trees/ha. Low: 99 × 45 = 4,450 kg; high: 124 × 68 = 8,430
kg. Assumes the per-tree figure is whole fruit, which the sources don't
state, and that every tree is mature. A measured orchard yield would
replace it.

**Per-tree yield cross-check, 40-120 kg.** 400-600 fruits a tree [14] ×
100-200 g a fruit [3] = 40-120 kg, which brackets the 45-68 kg figure
[2][7]. The two sources roughly agree.

**Box weight and price per fruit.** A box holds 20-22 dozen = 240-264
fruits [7]. At 100-200 g each [3], that is 24-53 kg. At JM$3,360 a box
[7], each fruit fetches JM$12.7-14.0 at the farm gate (3,360 ÷ 264 =
12.7; 3,360 ÷ 240 = 14.0).

**Harvest share of orchard labour cost, 73% to 96%.** From the 20-acre
orchard budget [7]:
- **Year 4:** harvesting JM$741,309 of JM$1,017,625 total labour = 72.8%.
- **Year 10:** JM$7,413,088 of JM$7,689,403 = 96.4%.
Hours can't be derived, because the budget gives no wage rate.

**Canned-export volume, about 3.3 million kg (6.2 million cans).**
US$26 million ÷ US$7.79 per kg = 3.34 million kg for 2023 [13]. Divided
by 0.54 kg per A-2 can [13], that is about 6.2 million cans. This
assumes every export is in A-2 cans and the price is per kg of packed
product; the source doesn't say which.

**FAO 2005 export row, implausible.** US$9,604,000 ÷ 9,236 kg = US$1,040
per kg [8], against US$7.79 per kg in 2023 [13]. The kg figure is
probably missing digits, possibly a thousandfold, since 9,236 t would be
about US$1.04 per kg. We can't tell which is wrong.

**Ripe-aril HGA against the US limit, about 2.7 times.** 271 mg/kg [5]
÷ 100 ppm (= 100 mg/kg) [4] = 2.7. The basis of the 271 figure (dry or
fresh) isn't in the abstract. On a dry basis the fresh figure would be
lower, and an aril is about 58% water [1]. As a rough fresh equivalent,
271 × (1 − 0.576) = 115 mg/kg, still just over the limit. That is
illustrative only.

**Residual fraction disagreement.** Morton: one twelfth (8.3%) of the
unripe level [1]. Bowen-Forbes and Minott: 271 ÷ 8,000 = 3.4% [5].

**What the rat maximum tolerated dose would mean in arils (illustrative
only).** Rat 30-day maximum tolerated dose: 1.50 mg HGA/kg/day [6]. For a
20 kg child: 30 mg a day. At the 100 mg/kg limit [4], that is 300 g of
arils a day; for a 70 kg adult, 1.05 kg. This scales a rat figure to
humans by body weight alone, which no source supports. It is shown only
to give the limit a sense of scale; it is not a safe-intake figure (see
Hazards, TO BE ADDRESSED).

**Spacing conversion error in the UF/IFAS guide.** 25-30 ft is printed
as "6.7-7.6 m" [2], but 25 ft = 7.6 m and 30 ft = 9.1 m. On a square
grid, 7.6-9.1 m gives 10,000 ÷ 7.6² = 173 down to 10,000 ÷ 9.1² = 121
trees/ha. That overlaps the Jamaican 99-124 trees/ha [14].

**Raw-aril energy, about 242 kcal per 100 g.** From Morton's proximates
[1], using Atwater factors: protein 8.75 × 4 = 35.0, fat 18.78 × 9 =
169.0, carbohydrate 9.55 × 4 = 38.2. Total 242 kcal, against 151 kcal
for canned product [11][12]. The difference is mostly water (57.6 g vs
76.7 g) and the higher protein and carbohydrate of Morton's analysis. The
UF/IFAS table pairs 151 kcal with 18.78 g fat, which mixes the two
sources [2].
