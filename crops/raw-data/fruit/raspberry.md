## Naming and scope

This covers the red raspberry, *Rubus idaeus*, including both its
European and American subspecies and their hybrids, which make up most
modern commercial cultivars [1][2][3]. Yellow raspberries are the same
species, differing only in fruit color, and are treated here as a
cultivar note rather than a separate crop [2]. Black raspberry (a
different *Rubus* species, native to eastern North America, with its own
distinct farming system) and purple raspberry (a black-×-red hybrid) are
explicitly excluded from this entry — black raspberry passes this
project's distinctiveness check and is treated as its own separate crop,
while purple raspberry remains a cultivar note.

This crop's closest researched sibling is a different perennial soft-
fruit crop this project has also researched, sharing hand-and-machine
harvest, near-freezing storage, and bee pollination; a separate cane
fruit in the same genus is a further close relative this project has
also researched (or is researching in parallel).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — biennial canes on perennial roots give rise to two distinct
cropping systems.** The root system and crown live for many years, but
each individual cane lives only two growing seasons: in its first year it
grows vegetatively (a "primocane"), and in its second year it fruits on
lateral shoots as a "floricane" before dying [2]. Summer-bearing
(floricane-type) cultivars fruit only on these second-year canes, in a
single several-week midsummer harvest window [2]. Primocane-fruiting
(fall-bearing) cultivars instead flower at the tips of first-year canes
once the cane reaches a specific node count, independent of day length or
temperature, giving a harvest window running from mid-to-late summer
through autumn [2]. Some growers specifically choose to mow entire
primocane-fruiting fields to the ground each winter and take only the
single fall crop, forgoing a second summer crop entirely — a genuinely
distinctive dual-harvest-system management choice.

**Finding 2 — a crop that is machine-harvested for processing but hand-
picked for the fresh market.** Over 90% of raspberries in a major Pacific
Northwest growing region are machine-harvested [2]; one over-the-row
mechanical harvester, with a single operator plus several field graders,
replaces roughly 80-85 hand pickers and can operate at night [2]. Hand
harvest can account for up to two-thirds of total production labor cost
[2]. Critically, machine-harvested fruit — while uniformly ripe — is not
suited to fresh-market sale, meaning fresh and processing production are
genuinely separate harvest systems requiring different cultivars
(specifically ones with easily released fruit) and trellis designs
(a narrow single-wire trellis for machines versus a wider trellis for
hand picking) [2].

**Finding 3 — among the most perishable fruit researched for this
project.** Raspberries are described in reference material as one of the
most fragile and perishable of all fruit [1]. Recommended handling
involves forced-air cooling to just above freezing within half a day of
harvest, followed by storage at a specific near-freezing temperature and
very high humidity for no more than a few days [1]. A single day at warm
room temperature is enough to initiate gray-mold decay, though the fruit
itself is not chilling-sensitive [1]. Ethylene exposure darkens red fruit
and promotes gray-mold growth [1].

**Finding 4 — a specific soil-borne water-mold disease sets the crop's
fundamental site requirements.** Wet or heavy soils bring on a
Phytophthora root-rot disease, described as one of the most serious pest
problems of this crop [2][3]. While roots can reach a considerable depth
in well-drained soils, the large majority of the root system — and most
water uptake — is concentrated in the topsoil [2]. A fairly narrow,
moderately acidic soil-pH range is considered optimal, with alkaline
soils above a certain threshold needing active acidification [2][3].
Commercial stand life runs under a couple of decades in favorable Pacific
Northwest growing conditions, and considerably shorter in harsher
climates or where virus pressure is high [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial root system and crown with biennial
  canes; two distinct harvest-pattern variants exist within the species
  (summer-bearing floricane and fall-bearing primocane types, Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Broadly temperate, spanning a wide hardiness-zone range
  across different cultivar types; summer-bearing types have a
  substantial winter chill requirement, while primocane-fruiting types
  can succeed in climates with insufficient chill for floricane types.
- **Composition:** A moderate calorie content with meaningfully higher
  protein content per unit weight than many other soft fruit.
- **Pollination:** Commercial cultivars are self-fertile, so a single-
  cultivar planting is feasible, but insect pollinators (bees, which do
  the large majority of pollination) are still required to actually
  transfer pollen; a documented minimum hive density is recommended, and
  pollination quality is sensitive to cool or wet weather, which both
  slows pollen-tube growth and reduces bee activity. A pollen-borne virus
  causes a distinctive "crumbly fruit" disorder.
- **Yield:** A wide range from an older European regional average, through
  a documented Pacific Northwest state average, up to an estimated
  exceptional figure for well-managed, intensively cropped fields
  (see Estimates below for adopted tier values). Yield is understood as
  the product of several compounding components (canes per area, nodes
  per cane, bud-break percentage, laterals per node, fruit per lateral,
  and drupelet count and size per berry), with the plant able to partly
  compensate when one component underperforms.
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient reference
  provides a crop-coefficient curve for berry bushes generally, though
  without matching stage-length figures, which are estimated here for a
  representative summer-bearing growing season. Effective rooting depth
  is shallow relative to the plant's maximum root depth, since the large
  majority of roots and water uptake are concentrated in the topsoil.
  Water stress specifically reduces individual drupelet size and overall
  berry weight, while adequate irrigation increases fruit size, yield,
  and cane vigor; however, overwatering (and particularly wetting the
  crown area) directly promotes the root-rot disease described in Finding
  4 — drip irrigation that keeps the row wet but the alley and crown dry
  is the preferred practice in at least one major growing region.
- **Propagation:** Exclusively vegetative — certified, virus-tested
  dormant nursery canes with fibrous roots, root cuttings, or tissue-
  culture plugs; true seed is used only in breeding. The plant also
  spreads naturally by root suckers, which can become invasive if not
  managed.
- **Labor:** Hand harvest is extremely labor-intensive relative to
  mechanical harvest (Finding 2); essentially all non-harvest labor-hour
  figures are estimates, since no detailed regional cost study was
  directly accessible in the sources consulted.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained soil with a moderately acidic pH
  preference is essential, given the crop's strong susceptibility to
  root rot in wet or heavy soils (Finding 4); replanting into fields
  previously used for certain nightshade-family vegetable crops is
  specifically discouraged due to a shared soil-borne wilt disease unless
  the field is fumigated first.
- **Stand life:** Commercially productive for under a couple of decades
  in favorable growing regions, considerably less in harsher climates or
  under high virus pressure (Finding 4); cane tissue and flower buds
  differ in winter-hardiness, with named cultivars varying considerably
  in both absolute cold hardiness and the timing of seasonal cold
  acclimation.
- **Disease/pest profile:** The root-rot disease described in Finding 4
  is the single most serious site-level constraint; additional documented
  diseases include a soil-borne wilt disease (shared with certain
  nightshade vegetable crops), a pollen-borne virus causing crumbly
  fruit, and further viral disease complexes.
- **Cultivars:** A substantial number of named Pacific Northwest and
  European summer-bearing and primocane-fruiting cultivars, differing in
  chill requirement, machine-harvestability, fruit firmness and color
  stability after picking, cold hardiness, and intended market (fresh
  versus processing).
- **Harvest:** Either hand-picked every few days (fresh market) or
  machine-harvested, often at night, with uniformly ripe fruit suited
  only to processing (Finding 2).
- **Storage:** An extremely short shelf life — only a few days under
  ideal cold, humid conditions — making this one of the most perishable
  fruit crops this project has researched (Finding 3).

## Hazards

- No hazards beyond ordinary food-safety handling were found. A popular
  herbal-tea preparation made from the plant's leaves has no proven
  medicinal effect according to reference material, and a popular dietary
  supplement marketed under a related name derived from a compound found
  in the fruit is not actually derived from real fruit and has no
  supporting clinical evidence for its marketed effects.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** hand-picked fruit → cooled to near-freezing within half a
  day → packed into retail containers → cold-stored for a few days.
  Machine-harvested fruit and culls → made into jam, or infused into a
  spirit-based liqueur. Leaves → dried for herbal tea. Spent floricanes
  (after fruiting) → chopped and left in the orchard alleys as a
  byproduct.
- Not covered in detail by this research: individually-quick-frozen
  product, frozen puree, and juice-concentrate processing; long-cane
  production systems; primocane-suppression technique; hive-placement
  practice; a specific raspberry-based distilled spirit.

## Open research questions

- The two distinct harvest systems (summer-bearing floricane versus
  fall-bearing primocane) differ enough in cycle, pruning, and timing
  that treating them as two separate cultivation paths rather than a
  single unified harvest pattern may be warranted; this is not resolved
  here.
- Winter chill-hour requirements are documented well for summer-bearing
  types but less precisely for the relationship between chill
  requirement and primocane-fruiting cultivars' ability to succeed in
  lower-chill climates.
- Essentially all non-harvest labor-hour figures are estimates, since a
  detailed regional cost study referenced in source material could not be
  directly accessed; only a qualitative labor-cost ratio (hand harvest as
  up to two-thirds of total labor cost) and a machine-versus-hand-picker
  substitution ratio were directly sourced.
- No formal salinity threshold was found, only a qualitative
  "sensitive" rating from a standard international reference table.
- Nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium removal-per-tonne figures were not found
  in any source consulted.
- World-average yield is derived from an older, European-only production
  figure; a more recent global production total exists but without a
  matching growing-area figure, so a current global per-hectare yield
  could not be derived.
- Planting-year yield figures (how much a newly established planting
  yields before reaching full production) were not found in the portions
  of source material consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-timeline figures (days from planting to first meaningful harvest)
are estimated separately for each harvest system: primocane-fruiting
cultivars can crop in their first autumn after a spring planting, while
summer-bearing cultivars need a full extra year before their first crop
on second-year canes, since they fruit only on canes that have already
overwintered once.

Yield tiers are derived from an older European regional production
figure (converted from an original area-and-tonnage table to a per-
hectare basis) for the world-average tier, a documented Pacific Northwest
state average for the "good" tier, a documented lower national average
for the "poor" tier, and a judgment-based estimate above the regional
state average for the "exceptional" tier, reflecting well-managed
intensive production.

Water-use parameters (stage lengths, crop-coefficient-days, seasonal
water totals) are estimated using a standard international reference's
general berry-bush crop-coefficient curve combined with an assumed
representative stage-length breakdown for a summer-bearing growing
season, since the reference source provides no matching stage-length
table specific to this crop.

Effective rooting depth for irrigation-scheduling purposes is set below
the plant's documented maximum root depth, reflecting the fact that the
large majority of the root system and water uptake are concentrated in
the topsoil.

Plants-per-hectare planting density is derived from two documented
commercial row-and-in-row spacing conventions, converted from an acre
basis to a hectare basis.

Labor-hour figures are derived partly from a documented machine-to-hand-
picker substitution ratio (used to estimate mechanical-harvest labor from
an assumed hand-harvest picking rate) and are otherwise largely estimated,
since a detailed regional cost study referenced in source material could
not be directly accessed.

The yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived from the adopted
"good" yield tier divided by an assumed harvest-window length; on-tree
standing-quality decay rate and the very short maximum standing-days
figure both reflect the fruit's extreme perishability as described in
Finding 3, though neither is a directly sourced numeric figure.
