## Naming and scope

A hybrid citrus crop known by a Chinese name referencing Beijing, where
it originated as a potted ornamental. Its exact parentage is disputed
across sources: a genomic study frames it as a cross between citron and
a mandarin-pomelo hybrid, while older sources describe it simply as a
cross between lemon and mandarin, or lemon and orange — a conflict not
resolved here [1][2][3][4][5][6]. A US agricultural explorer introduced
it to American cultivation in 1908 from a potted specimen found near
Beijing [1][2]. This entry covers both the original clone and its
later virus-free selection, "Improved," which is the only form that may
be legally planted today in regions where a serious citrus virus
disease is regulated [1][3].

This crop clearly passes this project's distinctiveness test against a
closely related citrus crop this project has also researched: it is
grown and used quite differently in several respects covered in detail
below, including cold tolerance, propagation method, fruit keeping
quality, growth habit, and a distinct disease-control history that sets
real planting restrictions. Sourced values from that closely related
citrus crop are reused here as explicitly labeled analogies wherever
this crop's own research base runs thin.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a hardy, own-rooted citrus that does not keep.** This
crop is typically grown from rooted cuttings rather than grafted onto
a rootstock, and is described as more cold-tolerant than any true
lemon, reportedly able to grow wherever oranges thrive; mature trees
tolerate a notably harder freeze than the least-hardy true lemon
cultivars. The fruit itself, however, is tender and has "proved
unsatisfactory commercially due to fruit tenderness and poor shipping
and storage qualities" in at least one detailed horticultural
reference. Documented storage data are thin: one study found certain
phytochemicals held steady for up to four weeks under refrigerated
market-simulation storage, while home-kitchen guidance gives a much
shorter five-to-ten-day refrigerator life. This is a sharp contrast
with a closely related citrus crop this project has also researched,
whose fruit can be green-picked, cured, and kept for up to six months.

**Finding 2 — a symptomless viral carrier turned into a planting
rule.** The original introductions of this crop into cultivation
carried a serious citrus virus disease without showing any symptoms
themselves. By the mid-20th century, most infected trees in one major
US growing state were destroyed once the connection was understood. One
detailed source specifically notes that this crop is not grafted onto a
particular sour-orange rootstock because of this risk, since the
combination of a virus-carrying scion on a virus-sensitive rootstock is
especially dangerous. In the 1960s, a state agricultural department
screened existing nursery stock and found that one grower's trees were
the only known virus-free source; this virus-free line became the
budwood source for the "Improved" selection released by a university
breeding program in 1975. In modeling terms, this crop is a case where
a plant can act as a disease reservoir for other crops while remaining
outwardly healthy itself, with a resulting legal rule restricting which
planting stock may be used.

**Finding 3 — a significant export crop in one country, but not that
country's primary commercial citrus.** This crop is documented as a
major citrus export for one Southern Hemisphere country, trailing only
a specific orange type in export value; one major growing district
there produces the large majority of that country's citrus, with a
documented average yield figure for lemons generally in that district.
One major packer in a mid-2010s season exported roughly a thousand
tonnes of this crop a year, plus several hundred tonnes sold
domestically, drawing from a few dozen growers averaging a couple of
hectares each, with the peak season running from autumn to spring in
that hemisphere. A separate source states that a different, true-lemon
cultivar is actually that country's main commercial lemon, and
describes this crop specifically as not well received in one major
export market and as not shipping or storing particularly well — a
direct conflict with the packer-sourced account describing that same
market as central to its exports, left unresolved here. A rough,
unverified estimate for total planted area in a major US growing state
was also found but could not be traced to its original source.

**Finding 4 — sweeter juice, but flavor-deficient as a concentrate.**
Documented juice composition shows meaningfully higher sugar and lower
acid content than a true lemon's roughly 5-6% acid level. At least one
US growing state historically processed this crop's juice
commercially into concentrate, but the result lacked flavor and had to
be fortified with true-lemon peel oil to be commercially usable. A
juice-yield percentage figure was found but could not be confirmed
against its original source.

**Finding 5 — a container and dooryard tree.** Mature trees are
compact, reaching only a modest height, and the crop is widely
described as an ideal container plant or houseplant, though it is
unlikely to fruit reliably when kept purely indoors. In its country of
origin it was traditionally kept as a potted ornamental rather than
grown as an orchard crop. This makes it a strong candidate for
protected or non-traditional growing settings, though the specific
light requirements for reliable indoor fruiting were not researched.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen, shrubby, compact perennial
  tree; bearing tends toward everbearing, with documented main-crop
  windows varying by growing region (winter in at least two Northern
  Hemisphere sources, autumn through spring in a Southern Hemisphere
  export market).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest stage — fruit is picked ripe,
  with no documented green-picking-and-curing path as used for a
  closely related citrus crop this project has also researched.
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean, and warm-temperate climates
  outdoors, tolerating meaningfully colder conditions than true lemon;
  also well suited to container growing in a much wider range of
  climates.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, including the thin, fragrant rind, which
  is commonly eaten along with the flesh.
- **Pollination:** Self-pollinating; no pollinator is strictly required,
  though the fruit is seedy enough that fertilization evidently does
  normally occur.
- **Yield / composition:** No dedicated yield survey specific to this
  crop was found; yield tiers here are derived from a mix of
  documented export volumes, a regional average for lemons generally,
  and estimates based on an assumed planting density (see Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** No water-use study specific to this crop was found;
  figures here are drawn entirely by analogy from a closely related
  citrus crop this project has also researched. Overwatering is
  documented to cause tree decline, and well-drained soil is
  specifically recommended.
- **Establishment:** Most commonly grown from rooted stem cuttings on
  its own roots, which bears a few years sooner than grafted trees;
  grafting onto various rootstocks is also practiced, though a
  particular sour-orange rootstock is specifically avoided for the
  original (non-virus-free) clone given the disease risk in Finding 2.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour study specific to this crop was found;
  figures here are estimated by analogy to a closely related citrus
  crop, adjusted upward for harvest and post-harvest handling to
  reflect the extra care needed with this crop's notably tender fruit
  and thin, easily marked rind.
- **Soil:** Sandy loam, moist but well-drained; nutrient-uptake and pH
  figures are drawn by analogy from a closely related citrus crop, since
  no crop-specific data was found.
- **Stand life:** Own-rooted cuttings can begin fruiting within as
  little as two years, while grafted plants typically take around four
  years to begin fruiting — a conflict in reported first-fruiting age
  that likely reflects the genuine difference between the two
  propagation methods rather than a true contradiction. Own-rooted
  trees can remain productive for thirty years or more.
- **Disease/pest profile:** The serious virus disease in Finding 2 is
  the crop's standout plant-health concern; otherwise it shares the
  general disease and pest profile of citrus generally, including a
  bacterial greening disease, citrus canker, and various other fungal
  and insect pests documented for citrus crops broadly.
- **Cultivars:** Essentially a single clone historically, now mostly
  represented by its virus-free "Improved" selection, plus at least one
  documented dwarfing-rootstock combination for container use and a
  distinct commercial strain grown for a Southern Hemisphere export
  market.
- **Harvest:** Picked ripe (deep yellow with an orange tint) and handled
  gently given the fruit's tender, thin skin.
- **Storage:** Considerably shorter-lived than true lemon — refrigerated
  storage extends shelf life to several weeks at most, while home
  refrigeration typically keeps fruit for only one to two weeks.

## Hazards

The original (non-virus-free) form of this crop is a symptomless
carrier of a serious citrus virus disease that can threaten other
citrus plantings; only certified virus-free "Improved" budwood should
be planted where this disease is regulated. Citrus peel oil generally
contains phototoxic compounds that can cause skin reactions in
sensitive people when the zested peel contacts skin in sunlight; this
has not been specifically analyzed for this crop but is assumed by
analogy to citrus generally, given this crop's rind is commonly eaten
and zested. A possible drug-interaction effect similar to the
well-documented grapefruit-drug interaction, linked to this crop's
mandarin and pomelo ancestry, has not been specifically tested for this
crop.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the viral plant-health/regulatory hazard, the
phototoxic peel-oil hazard, and the possible drug-interaction hazard
are all deferred in fuller detail until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety and plant-health data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit is picked ripe, gently graded and packed, and held
  under refrigeration for up to several weeks. Juice is sometimes
  processed into concentrate, historically fortified with true-lemon
  peel oil to correct for this crop's comparatively weak flavor as a
  concentrate. Prunings are rooted as cuttings to propagate new trees.
  The whole fruit, rind and all, is used in baking, dressings, and
  sauces, and in a lower-sugar variant of traditional lemonade; it also
  sees use as a seafood seasoning and is widely grown as a container or
  dooryard ornamental.
- Not covered by this research: marmalade and other preserved-fruit
  products; peel-oil extraction in detail; canned or frozen product
  streams.

## Open research questions

- The crop's exact parentage is genuinely disputed across sources
  (citron × mandarin/pomelo hybrid versus lemon × mandarin versus lemon
  × orange) — not reconciled.
- Whether a particular export market is central to or poorly receptive
  toward this crop is directly contradicted between two sources — not
  reconciled.
- Documented cold-hardiness thresholds for this crop and for true
  lemons generally are nearly identical across the sources reached,
  despite every source agreeing that this crop is distinctly hardier —
  a conflict not reconciled.
- First-fruiting age is reported as either about two years (own-rooted
  cuttings) or about four years (grafted plants) — likely reflects a
  genuine propagation-method difference rather than a true conflict, but
  is not fully confirmed.
- No dedicated per-hectare yield survey for this specific crop was
  found in any growing region; all yield tiers here are derived from
  export-volume data, a regional average for lemons generally, and
  assumed planting density, and should be treated as weak estimates.
- No crop-specific water-use, labor-hour, or nutrient-uptake study was
  found; figures throughout this entry are drawn by analogy from a
  closely related citrus crop.
- Peel-oil phototoxin content, chilling-injury threshold, and indoor-
  fruiting light requirements were not specifically researched for this
  crop.
- A planted-area estimate for one major US growing state could not be
  traced to its original source and is treated as unverified.
- A documented juice-yield percentage figure could not be confirmed
  against its original source and is treated as unverified.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from bloom to harvest (roughly 120-365 days) are estimated by
analogy to a documented four-to-twelve-month bloom-to-harvest range for
true lemons in one major US growing state, reflecting this crop's
similarly intermittent flowering pattern with a main winter crop
following a spring bloom.

Climate-zone suitability is derived from documented statements that
this crop grows wherever oranges thrive and survives outdoors into a
specific moderately warm hardiness-zone range, combined with its
evergreen, no-chill-requirement physiology shared with citrus
generally.

Calorie and protein content are estimated by analogy to true lemon
composition data, adjusted slightly upward to reflect this crop's
documented higher sugar and lower acid content relative to true lemon.

Yield tiers are derived from a documented export-plus-domestic sales
volume for one Southern Hemisphere packer divided by an estimate of
that packer's total growing area (world-average tier); a documented
regional yield average for lemons generally in the same growing
district (good tier, used as a stand-in since this crop's own
smaller-canopy trees may yield somewhat less); an estimate combining an
assumed planting density with a documented mature-tree yield figure for
true lemons generally (exceptional tier); and a documented third-year
tree yield for true lemons generally combined with the same assumed
planting density (poor tier).

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days and the resulting water-use "legacy"
figures are drawn directly by analogy from a closely related citrus
crop's standard-reference citrus crop-coefficient data, with a note
that this crop's typically smaller canopy may sit closer to a lower
documented canopy-cover coefficient tier than the default used.

Drought tolerance is rated medium and waterlogging tolerance is rated
low, reflecting documented tree decline under overwatering; other water
-stress parameters are copied directly by analogy from the same closely
related citrus crop.

Atmosphere physiology, growth-stage curve, and within-season stress
multipliers are copied directly by analogy from the same closely
related citrus crop, reflecting shared evergreen citrus physiology.

An assumed planting density of roughly 625 trees per hectare is
estimated from this crop's documented mature tree size and an assumed
close planting spacing, cross-checked against a wider documented home
-garden spacing recommendation that would imply a considerably lower
density.

Labor-hour figures are drawn by analogy from the same closely related
citrus crop for land preparation and pruning, with harvest and post
-harvest handling figures raised meaningfully above that analogy crop's
values to reflect the extra care needed in picking and packing this
crop's notably tender, thin-skinned fruit.

Soil nutrient-uptake rates, optimal pH range, and salinity sensitivity
are all copied directly by analogy from the same closely related citrus
crop, since no crop-specific data was found.

Typical cool-storage duration (about four weeks) is derived directly
from a documented four-week refrigerated market-simulation storage
study; a shorter ambient shelf-life estimate (about a week) is derived
from documented home-refrigerator shelf-life guidance, shortened
somewhat to reflect uncontrolled room-temperature conditions.

Years to first harvest and the multi-year ramp to full bearing are
estimated from the documented two-year (own-rooted) to four-year
(grafted) first-fruiting range, with a straight-line yield ramp assumed
to reach full bearing by around the sixth year in the absence of a more
detailed documented bearing curve.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (peak daily harvest rate,
maximum days fruit can stand before quality decline, and daily decay
rate) are derived from the adopted good-yield-tier figure divided
across a documented roughly four-month main-harvest window, with the
maximum standing-days and decay-rate figures treated as placeholder
estimates reflecting the fruit's documented tenderness and comparatively
short keeping quality relative to true lemon.

The pollination-required field is set to false as a baseline default,
consistent with this crop's documented self-pollinating habit, following
the same modeling convention used for the closely related citrus crop
this project has also researched, even though fruit set specifically
without insect activity is not directly documented for this crop.
