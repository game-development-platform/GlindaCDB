## Naming and scope

*Citrus × meyeri* Yu. Tanaka (Rutaceae) [1][3]. The Chinese name is
*běijīng níngméng*, "Beijing lemon" [1]. This doc covers the original
clone and its virus-free selection, 'Improved Meyer' (UC Riverside CRC
3737, PI 539447) [3].
- **Parentage:**
  - **Older views:** "possibly lemon X mandarin orange" [2]; "lemon and
    orange parentage" [3]; lemon × mandarin or tangerine [8].
  - **Genomic data:** it is a hybrid of a pummelo-mandarin admixed parent
    with citron, written as [(*C. maxima* × *C. reticulata*) × *C.
    medica*]. Citron is a single dose across the genome, and is assumed
    to be the male parent [4]. The maternal cytoplasm matches sweet
    orange and pummelo, but the exact mother "remains to be determined"
    [4]. The encyclopedia gives the same structure [1].
  - **Reading:** sweet orange is itself a pummelo-mandarin admixture, so
    the older "lemon × orange" description fits the genomic result better
    than "lemon × mandarin". The citron is the lemon-like parent, not a
    true lemon (see Open research questions).
- **Introduction:** Frank N. Meyer of the USDA found it as a potted
  ornamental near Beijing and introduced it in 1908 as S.P.I. #23028
  [1][2][3]. It was "likely established thousands of years" before then
  [1].
- **Gate check (2026-10-03, re-confirmed 2026-11-23): passes** against
  lemon (`lemon.md`):
  - **Taxonomy:** a different hybrid. Lemon is sour orange × citron, while
    Meyer carries a pummelo-mandarin parent with citron [4].
  - **Climate:** cold and heat resistance comparable to sweet orange,
    giving "a much wider range of climatic adaptation" than lemon [3].
  - **Propagation:** commonly grown from cuttings on its own roots [2].
  - **Postharvest:** too tender to ship or store well, and "does not cure
    or color well" [3]. It is also highly chill-sensitive [7].
  - **Plant health:** it carries viruses without symptoms (Finding 2)
    [3][5].

**Relatives in this project:**
- lemon (`lemon`, `lemon.md`): the commercial lemon, and the source of
  every analogy flagged below [14];
- citron (`citron`, `citron.md`): the paternal ancestor [4];
- sweet lemon and Palestine sweet lime (`sweet_lemon`, `sweet-lemon.md`):
  Palestine sweet lime has the same three-ancestor structure as Meyer
  [4].

**Proposed:**
- **Ponderosa lemon** (`ponderosa_lemon`): a "presumed lemon X citron
  hybrid" that Morton lists among the fruits treated as lemons [2]. It
  is another lemon-like hybrid with its own row candidacy.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a hardy, own-rooted citrus whose fruit will not travel.**
- **Cold:**
  - It "compares favorably with the sweet orange" for cold and heat
    resistance [3].
  - As a dooryard tree it grows "wherever oranges thrive" in Florida,
    even at Pensacola [2].
  - Trees withstand brief dips to 20 °F (about −6.7 °C; see Estimates)
    [8]. It is the most dependable citrus in Sonoma County, given
    protection in very cold winters [8].
- **Own roots:**
  - "easily and commonly grown from cuttings" [2];
  - large rooted cuttings planted straight into the grove fruit 2-3 years
    sooner than budded trees, and stay in full production for over 30
    years [2];
  - grafted plants usually begin fruiting in about four years [1].
- **Not a fresh-trade fruit:**
  - "too tender and juicy to withstand handling, shipping, and storage
    without excessive waste" [3];
  - it does not cure or colour well in storage, and has failed to become
    a commercial variety "of more than local importance anywhere" (1967
    text) [3].
- **Chilling injury:**
  - in Uruguayan fruit stored at 1 °C, Meyer peel showed brown depressed
    areas after 14 days [7];
  - after 58 days its chilling-injury index was 2.5, against 0.28 for
    'Lisbon' lemon (see Estimates) [7];
  - covering fruit on the tree (shading) delayed and reduced chilling
    injury in Meyer [7].

**Finding 2 — a symptomless carrier of two citrus viruses.**
- **Tristeza:**
  - the original introductions were "symptom less carriers of the
    tristeza virus" [3];
  - Morton calls it a carrier of "a virulent strain", which is why it is
    not grafted onto sour orange [2];
  - by the mid-1940s it was widely grown in California; after the virus
    link was found, "most of the Meyer lemon trees in the United States
    were destroyed" [1];
  - its use was banned in some citrus areas [3].
- **Citrus tatter leaf virus (CTLV):**
  - CTLV was first found in latently infected Meyer trees at Chico,
    California [6];
  - isolates from Meyer trees in California and Florida cause bud-union
    incompatibility on the widely used trifoliate and trifoliate-hybrid
    rootstocks [5];
  - four of the 12 quarantine-collection isolates sequenced came from
    Meyer trees: Texas (1958), California (1958, 1969) and Florida
    (1964) [6].
- **The fix:** a virus-free selection was found in the 1950s and released
  by the University of California in 1975 as 'Improved Meyer' [1].
  Improved trees "now available are virus-free" [3]. Since the 1970s it
  has been the only form sold in Sonoma County [8]. Budwood is supplied
  in California through the Citrus Clonal Protection Program [3].
- **In modelling terms:** an outwardly healthy plant that can act as a
  disease reservoir for other citrus, which led to rules on which
  planting stock may be used.

**Finding 3 — New Zealand's export lemon.**
- **Export status:** Meyer lemons are New Zealand's "major citrus export
  crop", followed by navel oranges [9]. Most citrus is sold
  domestically [9].
- **Value:** New Zealand exported more than NZ$7 million of lemons in
  2018 [9].
- **Markets:** Japan, the USA, the Pacific Islands and China [9]. Part of
  the Meyer crop goes to Japan and the USA [11].
- **Where it grows:**
  - most of the Meyer crop is grown around Gisborne [11];
  - Gisborne grows over 65% of New Zealand's citrus, and Northland about
    20% [9];
  - Northland's most productive citrus are Meyer and 'Yen Ben' lemons [9].
- **Area:** all lemons covered 363.2 ha in June 2022, of which 170.5 ha
  was in Gisborne [10]. That makes Gisborne about 47% of the national
  lemon area (see Estimates). No Meyer-only figure exists. In 1966 the
  Meyer area was 58 ha, against 278 ha of 'Lisbon' and 'Eureka' [13].
- **A recent Gisborne season:** a smaller Meyer crop but larger fruit;
  sales of larger fruit in Japan were up 24% [11].
- **Density:** Meyer and mandarin "are planted more intensively" than
  oranges, which are spaced 5 × 3 m (about 660 trees/ha) [9].
- **Conflict:** the 1967 judgement that it fails commercially [3] against
  New Zealand's export trade [9][11]. This is probably a later shift in
  market and handling, not a contradiction of fact (Open research
  questions).

**Finding 4 — a sweet lemon whose oil falls short.**
- **Flavour:** sweeter and less acidic than 'Lisbon' or 'Eureka' [1], but
  still "moderately acid" [2] or "acidic" [3]. The skin is edible [1].
- **Processing:** Florida made it into concentrate, but the product had
  to be enhanced with true-lemon peel oil, because Meyer peel oil "is
  deficient in flavoring properties" [2].
- **Juice chemistry:** no sugar or acid figure was sourced this pass.

**Finding 5 — a container and dooryard tree.**
- **Size:** 6-10 ft (about 1.8-3 m) [1]; "small, with few thorns" [2];
  shrubby and relatively small [3].
- **Containers:** outdoor containers 16 in (about 40 cm) wide support a
  tree, and 8-12 in pots work indoors [8]. It needs at least 4 hours of
  direct sun a day [8]. It is "especially adapted" to tubs [3], and
  traditionally potted in China [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen, small, nearly thornless tree or
  shrub [2][3]. New shoots are thorny, and the thorns become branches
  with age [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** everbearing, with flowering intermittent all year
  but mainly in spring [3]. Fruit comes "mostly from December to April"
  in Florida [2], and "mainly in winter" at Riverside [3]. Sonoma's crop
  is heaviest in fall and winter, with flowering and fruiting together
  even on young trees [8].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** about 4 years for grafted trees [1].
  Cuttings fruit 2-3 years sooner than budded trees [2], so at about 1-2
  years (see Estimates).
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Range:** comparable to sweet orange, and wider than lemon or lime
    [3]; grows "wherever oranges thrive" [2].
  - **Cold:** brief dips to −6.7 °C [8].
  - **Climate effects:** the fruit is "remarkably affected" by climate
    and looks different in different regions [3].
  - **Wind:** lemon fruits are scarred and trees defoliated by wind, so
    windbreaks help (a lemon-wide statement) [2].
  - **Chill requirement:** none (Not sourced as a figure). Elevation: Not
    sourced.
- **Composition:** Not sourced for this cultivar; there is no USDA entry
  [12]. Lemon's figures are in `lemon.md` [14]. Fruit is 5.7-7.5 cm wide
  and 6.25-9 cm long, with a peel 3-6 mm thick, about 10 segments and
  8-12 small seeds [2]; up to 10 seeds [1].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible, including the thin, fragrant skin [1].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this cultivar. It is "moderately
  seedy" [3], so it sets seed.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Not sourced as a figure. The tree is "prolific" [2] and
  "productive" [3]. New Zealand's lemon operating cost is NZ$15,500/ha
  "based on average yields", but no yield is given [9].
- **Water use:** Not sourced as a figure. Irrigate young trees in the dry
  season, letting the surface dry between waterings [8]. Well-drained
  soil is crucial [1]. FAO-56 citrus coefficients are in `lemon.md` [14].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Cuttings:** the usual route [2].
  - **Grafting:** "does well" on sweet orange and rough lemon, but is not
    grafted onto sour orange because of tristeza [2]. The Riverside tree
    is on 'Yuma Ponderosa' lemon [3].
  - **Trifoliate stocks:** they risk bud-union incompatibility if CTLV is
    present [5].
  - **Clean stock:** certified virus-free 'Improved' budwood [3].
  - **Grafted trees:** suckers from below the graft should be removed [8].
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. New Zealand lemon operating costs are
  NZ$15,500/ha a year, mostly thinning, pruning, fertiliser and harvest,
  at NZ$20 an hour [9] (see Estimates). Meyer needs "little or no
  pruning" [8].
- **Soil:** well-drained [1]; a light, porous mix in containers [8]. No
  pH or salinity data for this cultivar. Lemon's salinity threshold is in
  `lemon.md` [14].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - **Chilling injury:** begins at 14 days at 1 °C [7].
  - **Commercial storage:** handling and storage cause "excessive waste",
    and the fruit does not cure [3].
  - **Storage life and best temperature:** Not sourced for Meyer. The
    standard lemon regime is not a safe analogy, given the cold
    sensitivity [7][14].
- **Establishment:** denser than New Zealand's 5 × 3 m orange spacing [9].
  Mulch the root zone, keeping mulch 4-6 in from the trunk [8].
- **Cultivars:** one historical clone and its virus-free 'Improved'
  selection [1][3]. In Sonoma the 'Improved Meyer' has been the only
  variety available since the 1970s [8].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** brief −6.7 °C dips survived; cover the canopy in severe
    cold [8].
  - **Wind:** see Climate zones [2].
  - **Drought and waterlogging:** Not sourced as thresholds.
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation [8].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one ripe stage, picked deep yellow with a
  slight orange tint [1]. Lemon's green-pick and cure route does not work
  for Meyer [3]. Ripening is spread through the year, with a winter peak
  [2][3][8].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Origin:** a Chinese cultigen [1]. No invasive listing was found in
    this pass.
  - **Viruses:** the main biosecurity issue is the old clones (Finding 2)
    [1][3][5][6].
  - **Rules:** "currently banned in some citrus areas" (1967) [3], and
    certified budwood now comes through the California clonal programme
    [3].
  - **Other citrus rules:** general citrus quarantines (for example on
    huanglongbing) were not checked in this pass.
- **Controlled environment:** suits containers and grows as a houseplant
  [3][8]. Indoor trees need at least 4 hours of direct sun a day and as
  much time outdoors as possible in warm weather [8].

## Hazards

- **Plant-health reservoir:** old clones carry tristeza and CTLV without
  symptoms [3][5][6]. Plant only certified virus-free 'Improved' stock
  [3].
- **Peel-oil phototoxicity (by analogy):** lemon peel oil is phototoxic
  (`lemon.md`) [14], and Meyer's edible peel is commonly zested [1]. No
  measurement for Meyer was sourced.
- **Drug interaction:** Not sourced. The pummelo and mandarin ancestry
  [4] raises the question but proves nothing.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the virus-reservoir rules, peel-oil phototoxicity
and the possible drug interaction are deferred until this project has a
place for plant-health and health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** picked ripe → handled gently → sold locally or
  exported (New Zealand to Japan and the USA) [3][9][11].
- **Chain 2 (concentrate):** juice → concentrate, fortified with
  true-lemon peel oil [2].
- **Culinary:** whole fruit including skin [1]; seasoning fish and
  seafood [1]. Its rise in US cooking came from Californian chefs from
  the 1970s [1].
- **Ornamental:** potted in China [1]; the classic dooryard and tub
  citrus [3].
- **Propagation material:** cuttings [2]; certified budwood [3].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** marmalade, peel-oil extraction and
  frozen products.
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. It is listed in Slow Food's Ark
  of Taste [1].

## Open research questions

- **Parentage wording:** "lemon × mandarin" [2][8] and "lemon × orange"
  [3], against the genomic (pummelo-mandarin) × citron [4]. The mother
  is undetermined [4]. The 2016 phylogeny that first placed its
  cytoplasm (Curk et al.) was identified but not read.
- **Commercial fit:** a "failed" commercial variety (1967) [3] against New
  Zealand's leading citrus export [9]. What changed (handling, cold
  chain, markets) is not sourced.
- **Cold hardiness:** like sweet orange [3] and −6.7 °C dips [8], but no
  measured killing temperature.
- **Years to bearing:** about 4 years grafted [1], and 2-3 years sooner
  from cuttings [2]. No direct cutting figure.
- **Storage:** no safe storage temperature or storage-life figure, given
  the chill sensitivity [7].
- **Not sourced:**
  - yield, juice sugar and acid, and composition;
  - water use, labour hours, pH and salinity;
  - photoperiod, soil biology and drug interaction.
- **Works identified, not yet read:**
  - Curk et al. (2016), "Phylogenetic origin of limes and lemons revealed
    by cytoplasmic and nuclear markers," *Annals of Botany* 117(4)
    (PMC4817432; the OAI service would not return it).
  - *The Citrus Industry*, Vol. 1 (1967), the
    Meyer description quoted by UC Riverside [3].
  - Citrus New Zealand industry statistics: Meyer area, production and
    exports.

## Estimates and derivations

**Cold dip in Celsius** [8]: (20 °F − 32) × 5/9 = −6.7 °C.

**Gisborne share of New Zealand lemon area** [10]: 170.5 ÷ 363.2 = 46.9%.

**Meyer share of New Zealand lemon area, 1966** [13]: 58 ÷ (58 + 278) =
17%.

**Years to bearing from cuttings** [1][2]: grafted trees take about 4
years [1], and cuttings fruit 2-3 years sooner than budded trees [2]. So
4 − 3 = 1 to 4 − 2 = 2 years. The two inputs come from different
sources and settings. Range 1-2 years, widened to 1-3 for safety.

**Chilling-injury ratio** [7]: 2.5 ÷ 0.28 = about 9 times the 'Lisbon'
index after 58 days at 1 °C.

**New Zealand lemon cost as hours** [9]: NZ$15,500 ÷ NZ$20 an hour =
775 hours/ha a year. This is an upper bound, because the cost also
includes fertiliser and other non-labour items. It applies to lemons in
general, not Meyer.

**Orange density check** [9]: 10,000 m² ÷ (5 × 3 m) = 667 trees/ha,
matching "around 660". Meyer is planted denser than this.
