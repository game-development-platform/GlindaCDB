## Naming and scope

*Mangifera caesia* Jack (Anacardiaceae); 2n = 40 [1]. Synonyms: *M.
verticillata*, *M. caesia* var. *verticillata* and var. *wanji* [1]. The
species name was neotypified in 2019 [10].
- **Names:** binjai (English, French; Malay in Brunei, Sumatra, South
  Kalimantan, Peninsular Malaysia and Sarawak) [1]; jack or white mango
  [4].
  - **Borneo:** belunu (Brunei), beluno (Sabah Malay), bundo or bundu
    (Sabah Dusun) [1][6].
  - **Indonesia:** binglu (Sundanese), palong, wani (Balinese) [1].
  - **Philippines:** baluno (Manobo), bauno, bayuno, balunut (Sulu, Cebu
    Bisaya) [1]; mangga wani, gwani (Subanen) [4].
  - **Thailand:** bin-ya, lam-yai (peninsular) [1]; yaa-lam [4].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Wani"** is both a local name for the species (Bali) [1] and the
    name of its sweet cultivated form (Finding 2) [1][3].
  - **"Machang"** is a trade name for the timber of several *Mangifera*
    species, not only this one [2][3].
- **Range:** native to Sumatra, Borneo and Peninsular Malaysia, the same
  natural range as kemang [1]. Cultivated in Bali, peninsular Thailand
  and rarely western Java. Carried by the Sulu people from northern Borneo
  to the Sulu Archipelago and Mindanao, where it abounds around Zamboanga
  and near Butuan; once found in Papua New Guinea [1].
  - **Conflict:** a Brunei market survey lists it as exotic to Borneo,
    while saying it is widely grown and treated as a local fruit [5].
    The agroforestry database lists it as native only to Malaysia [2].
    PROSEA [1] and Wikipedia [4] place its origin in Borneo or include
    Borneo.
- **Existing group doc:** `wet-tropics-mangoes.md` (row
  `wet_tropics_mangoes`, `OUT-OF-DATE`) covers this species with bachang,
  kuini and bambangan [11]. This doc supersedes its binjai material.

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from
`mango.md` [11]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a separate species. Grafting on mango has failed,
  probably because the two are not closely related [1].
- **Farming system:** commonly cultivated on periodically flooded
  riverbanks in East Kalimantan, a flood tolerance none of the other
  researched mangoes is credited with (Finding 1) [1].
- **Fruit and market:** a thin-skinned, soft, white-fleshed fruit with a
  sweet, fibreless, high-priced form, "wani" (Finding 2) [1].
- **Hazard:** the white juice of the immature fruit is extremely
  irritant, on the skin and when swallowed (Finding 3) [1].
- **Against kemang:** the two share a range and hybridize in East
  Kalimantan orchards [1]. Binjai has a longer petiole and a smooth,
  yellowish or whitish-green fruit [1]. Kemang stays a separate
  `PROPOSED` row.

**Relatives in this project:**
- mango (`mango`, `mango.md`): the same genus [1].
- kemang (`kemang`, proposed): natural hybrids with binjai [1].
- bachang (`bachang`, `bachang.md`) and bambangan (`bambangan`,
  `bambangan.md`): the same wet-tropics group, with the same irritant sap
  [11].
- kuini (`kuini`, proposed): the same group [11].
- wet-tropics mangoes (`wet_tropics_mangoes`, `wet-tropics-mangoes.md`):
  the older group doc [11].

**Proposed:** none new.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a lowland fruit tree that stands flooding.**
- **Habitat:** rather rare in forest, more frequent in periodically
  inundated areas and marshes [1].
- **Cultivation:** it "stands inundation well" and is commonly grown on
  periodically inundated riverbanks in East Kalimantan [1].
- **Climate:** wet tropical lowlands, generally below 400 m and rarely to
  800 m, with rain spread evenly through the year [1][2]. Other sources
  give 450 m [4] and 500 m [3]; one report says it also succeeds in a
  monsoon climate [3].
- **Not sourced:** flood duration or depth it tolerates.

**Finding 2 — a sour wild fruit with one prized sweet form.**
- **Wild fruit:** sweetish-sour, juicy, fibrous, with a "peculiar" sour
  taste and a strong smell at maturity [1].
- **"Wani":** mainly in Bali, also East Kalimantan. The fruit is sweet,
  juicy, almost fibreless, without the rank smell, and "fetches a high
  price in local markets" [1]. Smaller and rounder than the wild type,
  9-11 × 6.5-7 cm, glossy pale green, with a thin-walled, monoembryonic
  stone [1].
- **Sabah:** "bundu" sells at RM5-10 for a bunch of four to ten fruits,
  and sellers call it sweet [6].
- **Prospects:** PROSEA suggests the best wani forms from Bali and East
  Kalimantan merit wider cultivation, if they can be propagated
  vegetatively for earlier bearing and smaller trees [1].

**Finding 3 — irritant juice and sap.**
- **Immature fruit:** the white juice is extremely irritant on the skin
  and when ingested; it "has been used to injure enemies" [1].
- **Bark:** the sap is irritant [1], and described as poisonous, causing
  serious skin and eye irritation [3].
- **Ripe fruit:** in the Philippines, the fruit may cause allergic contact
  dermatitis on the lips, face or neck [8]. In Sabah, Kadazandusun people
  call the rash that can follow cutting the gummy seed "bunduon" [6].

**Finding 4 — eaten fresh, as juice and in sambal.**
- **Fresh and juice:** ripe fruit eaten fresh; wani makes "creamy
  juices" [1]. Eaten dipped in chilli and dark soy sauce [4].
- **Sambal:** a chilli sambal eaten with river fish. Ripe flesh is salted
  in jars to make it when there is no fresh fruit [1].
- **Brunei:** a dipping sauce (*cacah*) for *ambuyat*, the sago dish
  considered the national dish [4].
- **Other parts:** young leaves raw or as a condiment; fresh or dried
  seeds grated with spices and dried fish as a side dish with rice [3].
- **Medicinal (Sabah, Dusun):** listed as medicinal, with no use given
  in the review read [9].

**Finding 5 — a little-studied crop.**
- **Agronomy:** it is propagated from seed, "an indication that the
  agronomy of the crop has received little attention" [1].
- **No data:** no particulars about husbandry, pests, diseases or yield
  levels have been published [1][2].
- **Recent studies:** chemistry only. A 70% ethanol fruit extract had
  the strongest radical scavenging of eight neglected Malaysian fruits
  [7]. Leaf extracts were tested as mosquito larvicides [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** a large tree, 30 (-45) m tall, bole 50-80
  (-120) cm, without buttresses; dome-shaped crown [1]. Deciduous [3][4].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial; one fruiting season a year [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** wet tropical lowlands; see Finding 1 [1][2][3][4].
  Chill requirement: Not sourced (tropical).
- **Composition (per 100 g edible portion)** [1]: water 86.5 g, protein
  1 g, fat 0.2 g, carbohydrate including fibre 11.9 g, ash 0.4 g,
  thiamine 0.08 mg, β-carotene equivalent 0.005 mg, vitamin C 58 mg;
  energy 200 kJ. About 65% of the fruit is edible [1].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible when ripe; the immature fruit's juice is
  irritant (Finding 3) [1].
- **Pollination:** insects [3]. Flowers pale lilac and fragrant, in
  dense panicles 15-25 (-40) cm long [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced as weight. Trees bloom profusely, and old trees
  can produce thousands of fruits [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Needs rain spread evenly through the year
  [1].
- **Propagation:** from seed [1]; also by marcotting [4]. Inarching
  potted, decapitated seedling rootstocks onto mother-tree twigs is
  presumed possible [1]. Grafting on mango has failed [1].
  - **Clonal risk:** Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced; tolerates periodic flooding [1]. Salinity: Not
  sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced as temperature or shelf life.
  Ripe fruit is soft and juicy and must be handled carefully [1].
- **Establishment:** mature trees need 12-16 m each way [1][2].
- **Cultivars:** the "wani" form (Finding 2) [1][3].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** rain-fed; grown on seasonally flooded riverbanks [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** fruit ripens 3 months after flowering
  [1].
  - **East Kalimantan:** flowering October-December, fruit November-March
    in the rainy season [1].
  - **Sabah:** flowering February-April, fruit August-October [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** No invasive listing found in this pass.
  IUCN Least Concern (2013 listing [3]; a 2021 assessment exists [4]).
  Pests: none published [1][2].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Irritant juice and sap:** see Finding 3 [1][3][6][8].
- **Food safety:** none sourced.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for binjai.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the irritant juice of the immature fruit, the
  bark sap and the seed-cutting rash are handling hazards [1][3][6]. The
  project's hazard model has no field for crop handling hazards yet
  (the same gap as bachang and bambangan).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** tree → ripe fruit picked or gathered → handled
  carefully (soft) → market, often in bunches [1][6] → eaten fresh or
  dipped in chilli and soy sauce [1][4].
- **Chain (juice):** ripe wani fruit → creamy juice [1].
- **Chain (sambal):** ripe flesh → chilli sambal with river fish [1];
  or flesh → salted in jars → sambal out of season [1].
- **Chain (Brunei):** fruit → *cacah* dipping sauce → eaten with
  *ambuyat* [4].
- **Chain (seed):** fresh or dried seeds → grated with spices and dried
  fish → side dish with rice [3].
- **Leaves:** young leaves raw or as a condiment [3].
- **Timber:** wood density 410-570 kg/m³ at 15% moisture, used for light
  construction [1][2]; sold as machang [3].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. It was shown on a 1999
  Malaysian rare-fruits stamp [4].

## Estimates and derivations

**Energy, about 48 kcal per 100 g.** 200 kJ [1] / 4.184 = 47.8 kcal.
Atwater check: protein 1 × 4 + fat 0.2 × 9 + carbohydrate 11.9 × 4 =
53.4 kcal; the difference is fibre counted in the carbohydrate.

**Edible flesh per fruit, about 150-250 g (wild type) and 130-180 g
(wani).** Treating the fruit as an ellipsoid of density 1 g/cm³:
- Wild type 12-15 × 6-7 cm [1]: π/6 × 12 × 6 × 6 = 226 g; π/6 × 15 × 7
  × 7 = 385 g. × 65% edible [1] = 147-250 g.
- Wani 9-11 × 6.5-7 cm [1]: π/6 × 9 × 6.5 × 6.5 = 199 g; π/6 × 11 × 7 ×
  7 = 282 g. × 65% = 129-183 g.
The wild fruit is obovate and necked, so the ellipsoid overstates its
volume. A measured fruit weight would replace this.

**Trees per hectare, about 39-69.** 10,000 m² / (16 m × 16 m) = 39;
10,000 / (12 × 12) = 69 [1].

**Sabah price per fruit, about RM0.50-2.50.** RM5-10 a bunch / 4-10
fruits [6]: 5 / 10 = 0.50; 10 / 4 = 2.50. This assumes the price range
and bunch-size range pair up freely.
