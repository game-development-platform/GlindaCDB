# Nectarine (*Prunus persica*, smooth-skinned form)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as *Prunus persica* (L.) Batsch carrying the recessive nectarine
(glabrous-skin) allele at the G locus — a "fuzzless peach," genetically
the same species, tree, leaves and flowers as the fuzzy-skinned form
[5][7]. Two cultivar groups are recognized: **high-chill** (California
and the temperate US; Fantasia, Arctic Glo, Harko, Redgold) and
**low-chill** (Florida; Sunbest, Sunraycer, UFRoyal, UFQueen, Sunmist,
Suncoast) [2][4][10].

The nectarine is, botanically and in cultivation, essentially identical
to a closely related stone-fruit crop this project has also researched,
differing chiefly in product identity and a handful of measurable
traits: its own market name and eating experience (no fuzz, a
redder-looking skin, on average slightly smaller and sweeter fruit)
[7]; faster postharvest water loss, with visible shriveling at only
6-8% weight loss [1]; generally lower per-tree and per-hectare yield
(in one Mississippi trial, nectarines averaged 4.8 kg per tree a year
against 8.4 kg for the fuzzy-skinned form planted alongside them, and
California nectarines yield roughly 10 t/acre against 15-20 t/acre for
the fuzzy form) [2][3]; somewhat lower susceptibility to chilling
injury in modern cultivars [1]; and the single-gene genetic basis of
the trait itself [5]. Claimed differences in disease and insect
susceptibility were not confirmed in the one trial that directly
compared adjacent plantings (see Research findings, below). California
grows about 94% of US nectarines, almost all sold fresh [3].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one recessive gene produces a different fruit.** Fuzz is
dominant; the nectarine phenotype is homozygous recessive at the G
locus on chromosome 5 [5][7]. A candidate gene (*PpeMYB25*) controls
fruit trichome formation, with a retroelement insertion in one of its
exons cosegregating with the nectarine trait, and a corresponding
genetic marker has been validated across known nectarine source
material [5]. A 2025 study found at least one peach-to-nectarine mutant
with a structurally intact copy of this gene but no detectable
transcript, implying additional causal mechanisms beyond the known
retroelement insertion [5]. Nectarines have been known in China for
over 2,000 years, and the English word dates to 1611 [5][7]. Beyond
fuzz, documented differences include fruit size, shape, firmness, skin
colour, aroma, flavour and disease resistance; one recorded nectarine
bud sport bore smaller fruit that ripened about four days later, with
finer-grained yellow freestone flesh, and the nectarine allele is
separately linked to smaller fruit size in general [2][6]. A seedling
from a cross between the two forms segregates according to simple
dominance, with the fuzzy phenotype dominant.

**Finding 2 — storage is somewhat better than for the fuzzy-skinned
form, but still well above typical refrigeration temperatures.**
Recommended storage is −0.5 to 0°C at 90-95% relative humidity,
achieving 4-6 weeks of life depending on cultivar, with a flesh
freezing point of about −0.9°C [1]. Fruit should be precooled quickly
below about 2.2°C, by forced-air cooling or in some regions
hydrocooling [1]. Modern cultivars keep at least six weeks at 0°C;
chilling-injury-resistant cultivars keep at least three weeks at 5°C,
but susceptible ones turn mealy and brown within one to two weeks at
that temperature. Moving fruit from 0°C to 5°C after one to two weeks
injures it as severely as holding it at 5°C the whole time. No chilling
injury occurs at 10°C or above, but the fruit then softens, loses water
and decays quickly; injury symptoms only become visible once fruit is
subsequently ripened at 18.3°C or above. The fastest-injury temperature
band is roughly 2.2-7.8°C [1][8]. Visible shriveling appears once
weight loss reaches 6-8%; remedies include picking earlier in the day,
cooling quickly with minimal delay, waxing, and tight control of
temperature, airflow and humidity [1]. Controlled-atmosphere storage at
1-2% O2 plus 5% CO2 preserves firmness and colour and gives limited
protection against chilling injury; a richer 10% O2 plus 10% CO2
atmosphere is sometimes used specifically against chilling injury.
Oxygen below 1% or CO2 above 15% should be avoided, since both produce
off-flavours and skin browning [1]. Brown rot is the most significant
postharvest disease, followed by a soft rot that does not develop below
7°C [1].

**Finding 3 — yields are markedly lower than for the fuzzy-skinned
form, and the disease-susceptibility question is unresolved.** A
multi-year Mississippi trial planted 21 cultivars at a northern site
and nine at a southern site, on standard peach rootstocks at close
spacing, drip-irrigated and sprayed on the same program as the fuzzy
form [2]. Two severe spring freezes during the trial period destroyed
every cultivar's crop in those years, since the trees were already past
bloom [2]. The best-producing cultivars gave over 90 kg per tree
cumulative over the trial; the weakest gave under 5 kg per tree as a
four-year average. The overall four-year average at the northern site
was 19.1 kg per tree, somewhat below a comparable Alabama figure; at
the southern site it was only about 4.3 kg per tree, since winter chill
there was too low for most cultivars to flower well. Averaged across
the trial, the fuzzy-skinned form yielded nearly twice as much per tree
as the nectarine form planted in the same conditions [2]. Separately,
the lack of fuzz has been claimed in the literature to predispose
nectarines to brown rot and insect damage and to limit where the crop
can be grown, but the Mississippi trial's own authors found disease and
insect pressure no greater for nectarines than for the fuzzy form grown
in an adjacent orchard — a conflict the available sources do not
resolve. In California, modern nectarine yields run around 10 t/acre
against 15-20 t/acre for the fuzzy form; the commercial nectarine
industry there was described as essentially nonexistent until the
1950s, with the great majority of production now concentrated in two
counties. Production fell below 100,000 tons in the 2022/23 season for
the first time since the early 1970s, following rain, hail and
near-freezing weather. California growers apply somewhat less nitrogen
fertilizer per acre to nectarines than to the fuzzy form, per a 2009
survey [3].

**Finding 4 — chill requirement varies widely by cultivar and cultivar
group.** Within the high-chill group, most Mississippi trial cultivars
needed over 800 chill-hours, with two cultivars doing best at the
southern site needing substantially less (about 700 and 525 hours
respectively) [2]. One widely grown high-chill cultivar is reported at
500 chill-hours by one source and 750 by another — an unresolved
conflict — while a different early cultivar is reported at 450 hours
[10]. Within the low-chill group, named University of Florida cultivars
range from about 225 to 375 chill-units, with fruit development periods
of 77-95 days, spanning melting and non-melting flesh types, freestone
and clingstone types, and varying bacterial-spot resistance [4]. At the
Mississippi trial's southern site, insufficient and erratic flowering
attributed to inadequate chilling was explicitly noted [2].

**Finding 5 — fruit cracking is reported but only thinly evidenced.**
Wet weather near harvest is anecdotally reported by growers to crack
nectarine fruit [11]. One Japanese study linked earlier skin-cell
damage at the stomata to cracking and rough skin in a susceptible
cultivar, and a breeding program has selected specifically for smooth
skin free of russeting or flecking and for crack resistance [11]. This
remains a low-confidence finding, resting on anecdotal grower reports
plus a single study.

## Agronomic data

Nectarine shares its tree biology, rootstocks, nursery practice, cull
processing (pitting, drying, jam, purée), kernel and foliage hazards,
bloom-frost sensitivity table, and pruning and thinning practice with
the closely related fuzzy-skinned form (see Naming and scope); what
follows focuses on where nectarine itself differs.

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous tree, grown as for the
  fuzzy-skinned form.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, with several picks as skin
  ground colour changes; fruit must not be allowed to over-ripen on the
  tree [2]. Growing season from bloom to harvest runs roughly 77-150
  days depending on cultivar group and chill satisfaction.
- **Climate:** Temperate and irrigated Mediterranean climates for the
  high-chill group; subtropical climates (central and northern Florida)
  for the low-chill group; commercial production is concentrated in
  dry, irrigated California valleys [2][3][4].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; climacteric (continues ripening off the
  tree) [4].
- **Pollination:** Not required — self-fertile, as for the fuzzy-skinned
  form, with bees as the typical pollinator [9].
- **Yield:** A world-average figure is difficult to source precisely
  on its own, since international statistics commonly report this crop
  combined with the fuzzy-skinned form (a combined figure of roughly
  13 t/ha is available); nectarines specifically are documented at
  roughly half to two-thirds of the fuzzy form's yield. California's
  modern average of about 10 t/acre (roughly 22.4 t/ha) is a reasonable
  "good" yield benchmark; the Mississippi trial's poorest, chill-short
  site averaged only about 4.3 kg per tree per year.
- **Water use:** Water needs track the fuzzy-skinned form's own crop
  coefficient curve closely, since both are the same species and tree;
  the one genuine nectarine-specific water trait is faster postharvest
  water loss, with visible shriveling at only 6-8% weight loss, versus
  the fuzzy form's greater tolerance [1]. As with the fuzzy form, water
  stress in the final fruit swell before harvest, and in the
  post-harvest period (which sets next year's crop), are the most
  sensitive stages.
- **Establishment:** Budded nursery trees on peach seedling rootstocks,
  planted at close hedgerow or palmette spacing in the trials reviewed
  here [2].
- **Labor:** Broadly tracks the fuzzy-skinned form's own labor profile,
  scaled down somewhat at harvest for the lower yield, and raised
  somewhat at post-harvest handling for the extra care against
  shriveling.
- **Soil:** No nectarine-specific soil data beyond the fuzzy form's own
  figures; trial soils in the sources reviewed here ran mildly to
  moderately acidic.
- **Stand life:** First commercial production around year three from
  planting in the trial reviewed here; productive lifespan in the
  range of roughly 7-15 years by region, broadly similar to the fuzzy
  form [2][7].
- **Propagation:** Vegetative, via budding onto peach seedling
  rootstocks — not grown true from seed commercially. True nectarine
  seed (from a cross between two nectarine parents) breeds true for the
  smooth-skin trait, since it is a simple recessive; any cross
  involving a fuzzy-skinned parent gives fuzzy-skinned offspring in the
  first generation [5][7]. Occasionally, a fuzzy-skinned tree will bear
  a nectarine branch as a spontaneous bud sport, and the reverse has
  also been documented.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Brown rot (the most significant postharvest
  disease), a soft rot inactive below 7°C, bacterial spot (with
  resistance present in some low-chill cultivars), leaf curl, scab, an
  internal fruit-boring moth, and wood-boring insects are documented
  [1][4][7].
- **Cultivars:** High-chill cultivars include early, mid- and
  late-season types spanning white- and yellow-fleshed, freestone and
  semi-clingstone forms, with chill requirements from roughly 450 to
  over 800 hours; low-chill University of Florida cultivars span
  roughly 225-375 chill-units, with varying flesh texture, stone
  adhesion, size, shipping quality and bacterial-spot resistance.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include brown rot, a soft rot inactive
below 7°C, bacterial spot, leaf curl, scab, an internal fruit-boring
moth, and wood-boring insects.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other stone fruits in this genus, the seed
kernel contains cyanogenic glycosides (amygdalin), and wilted foliage
and prunings are a documented cyanide poisoning hazard to livestock.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit is sorted (culls removed), precooled below about
  2.2°C, then held at −0.5 to 0°C for up to 4-6 weeks before sale;
  culls are pitted and dried, made into jam, or puréed, following the
  same processing chain as the closely related fuzzy-skinned stone
  fruit; nursery rootstock seedlings are budded with nectarine
  cultivar wood.
- Not covered by this research: canned and frozen nectarine products
  (almost all production is sold fresh); waxing and fungicide
  treatment in detail; the genetics and economics of spontaneous bud
  sports between nectarine and the fuzzy-skinned form beyond what is
  noted above.

## Open research questions

- Whether disease and insect susceptibility genuinely differs between
  nectarine and the closely related fuzzy-skinned form is disputed: one
  source claims higher susceptibility for the smooth-skinned form, but
  a direct trial comparison found no such difference.
- Chill-hour requirement for at least one widely grown high-chill
  cultivar is reported inconsistently across sources (roughly 500 vs.
  750 hours).
- Whether a reported four-year average per-tree yield figure from one
  trial is a true yearly average or a cumulative total across the
  trial period is unclear from the source material.
- Energy content is reported somewhat differently between two sources
  (about 55 vs. 46 kcal per 100 g for the related fuzzy-skinned form),
  a gap possibly attributable to how dietary fibre is counted.
- Nectarine-specific water-use figures (a dedicated crop-coefficient
  curve) were not found; figures used here are carried over from the
  closely related fuzzy-skinned crop as an analogy.
- World-average and exceptional yield figures, and most labor figures
  beyond harvest, rest on estimates or analogy to the fuzzy-skinned
  form rather than nectarine-specific measurement.
- Nectarine-specific postharvest handling costs and detailed economics
  (beyond the Mississippi and California studies cited) were not
  located.
- Whether low-chill nectarine cultivars share the fuzzy-skinned form's
  documented warm-night fruit-set failure and retained-leaf behavior was
  not separately checked for nectarine cultivars.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 77-150 days from bloom to harvest) is
derived by combining the low-chill group's documented fruit-development
period (77-95 days) with the high-chill Mississippi trial's bloom-to-harvest
window (roughly 70-150 days).

Energy and protein content (roughly 550 kcal/kg and 9.4 g protein/kg)
are derived from a detailed commodity composition analysis (moisture,
protein, fat and carbohydrate percentages), converted to standard
energy units; a separate source's figure for the closely related
fuzzy-skinned form's flesh (about 460 kcal/kg) is lower, consistent with
nectarines being described as slightly sweeter, though the gap may
partly reflect differing treatment of dietary fibre in the two
analyses.

Yield tiers are derived/estimated as follows: a "good" yield of about
22,400 kg/ha is derived directly from a reported California average
tonnage-per-acre figure; a world-average figure of about 9,000 kg/ha is
estimated from a combined (nectarine-plus-fuzzy-form) international
yield statistic, discounted using the documented nectarine-to-fuzzy-form
yield ratio from the sources above; an exceptional yield of about
30,000 kg/ha is estimated above the California average, consistent with
what intensive, high-density fresh-market orchards elsewhere are known
to achieve, though no direct source was found; a poor yield of about
1,000 kg/ha is estimated from the trial's chill-short, low-yielding site
and from the freeze-loss years recorded there.

Water-use parameters are carried over from the closely related
fuzzy-skinned crop's own crop-coefficient curve and stage-length
figures by direct analogy, since no nectarine-specific water-use study
was found; the only genuine nectarine-specific water trait identified
is its faster postharvest water loss (Finding 2).

Labor-hour estimates by operation are derived from the fuzzy-skinned
form's own figures, with harvest-labor scaled down in proportion to
nectarine's lower average yield, and post-harvest handling labor raised
somewhat to account for the additional care needed against shriveling.

A simple one-gene segregation model is derived from the documented
recessive inheritance of the smooth-skin trait: a cross between two
nectarine parents breeds entirely true; a cross between two fuzzy-skinned
parents (where neither carries the recessive allele) breeds entirely
fuzzy-skinned offspring; and a cross between one fuzzy-skinned carrier
parent and one nectarine parent splits roughly evenly between the two
fruit types.
