## Naming and scope

*Dimocarpus longan* Lour. (Sapindaceae), known as the "dragon's eye" of
southern China [1][2]. This entry covers the commercial longan
subspecies and variety grown throughout Southeast Asia and southern
China. The species also includes a fully tropical subspecies (locally
known by several regional names including "cat's eye," a closely
related crop this project has also researched separately) that flowers
without needing any cool season at all, unlike the commercial longan
covered here, which specifically will not flower without one [3].

This crop is grounded against two other tropical/subtropical Sapindaceae
fruit crops this project has also researched, which share its
distinctive flowering-trigger challenge, cluster-style harvest, air-
layer propagation, and (for one of them) a similar seed-toxin profile.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — flowering can be switched on with a chemical.** Naturally,
this crop needs a short (two- to three-month) cool winter period with
moderate mean temperatures; sources differ somewhat on the exact
threshold temperature [3][4][6]. Since the late 1990s, growers in a
major Southeast Asian producing country have adopted a practice of
drenching a specific inorganic oxidizing salt (potassium chlorate) into
the soil under dormant trees, at rates that vary by cultivar, or
alternatively applying it as a trunk injection or foliar spray [3][6]
[11]. Treated trees flower within a few weeks regardless of season,
meaning fresh fruit is available on that country's domestic market
essentially year-round [6][11]. The chemical's effectiveness varies
sharply by season, working far better in cool or hot seasons than
during the rainy season [6]. This flower-induction chemical is a
genuinely hazardous oxidizer: a documented case describes orchard
workers dying after mixing it with nitrogen fertilizer, which can create
an explosive combination [7].

**Finding 2 — strong biennial bearing.** Production in an "off" year
typically runs only a fraction of an "on" year's output [3]. National
yield statistics from a major producing country swung substantially
between alternating higher and lower years across a nearly decade-long
historical record [3]. Mature trees are described as typically giving
"one poor crop, one fair crop, and one excellent crop" over a three-year
cycle [3]. Growers actively manage this tendency by thinning a
substantial fraction of both flower spikes and young developing fruit
[3].

**Finding 3 — dried fruit is a major product in its own right.** Whole
fruit is briefly boiled, then dried at a controlled, gradually rising
temperature over roughly a day, down to a specific target moisture and
sugar-concentration level [3]. At least one specific cultivar gives a
documented dried-fruit yield of roughly 35-38% of fresh fruit weight by
mass — a sourced processing yield ratio [3]. Peeled flesh dried this way
turns black and leathery, and is brewed as a well-known traditional
drink and used in herbal medicine [3].

**Finding 4 — seeds carry a serious toxin load; the flesh does not.**
Testing found a specific toxic amino acid (hypoglycin A, also found in
unripe ackee fruit and documented for a related tropical fruit crop
elsewhere in this project's research) at low levels in the edible flesh
but at dramatically higher levels in the seed — the highest level found
of any soapberry-family seed tested in one study, and far above a
regulatory import limit set for a related fruit known to cause the same
toxic syndrome [8][9]. Seeds are used traditionally as a shampoo
ingredient rather than eaten [3].

**Finding 5 — short shelf life; sulfur dioxide treatment roughly
doubles it.** The rind browns within a few days at warm ambient
temperatures [3]. Cold storage at a specific refrigerated temperature
range extends shelf life to a few weeks, though storage colder than a
specific threshold causes an off-flavor and a dark-brown chilled-peel
disorder [5]. Fumigating fruit with sulfur dioxide gas roughly doubles
documented storage life compared to untreated fruit at the same
temperature; this fumigation is standard commercial practice in at
least one major producing country but is not approved for fresh-market
sale in at least one major importing country [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial tree reaching
  substantial height, with brittle wood and corky bark (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Normally one crop a year, picked in two passes
  about a week to ten days apart; strongly biennial-bearing; off-season
  crops can be forced on demand using the chemical flower-induction
  practice described in Finding 1.
- **Climate:** Subtropical regions with a cool, dry winter, or tropical
  highland/monsoon-tropical regions that still experience a cool season
  — the commercial form of this crop will not flower in truly
  aseasonal tropical climates.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (the aril/flesh).
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (mainly bees, most
  effective during a specific daytime window), with flowers opening in
  a distinctive staggered sequence (male, then female, then
  hermaphrodite, then male again) across a period of several weeks.
- **Yield / composition:** National yield averages from a major
  producing country are substantially diluted by alternating
  biennial-bearing cycles and young unproductive orchards; well-managed,
  mature, on-year orchards yield considerably more (Finding 2; see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure was
  found specific to this crop; available regional water-use research
  gives conflicting figures. Roots can reach several meters deep to
  reach a water table on some alluvial soils. A deliberate dry period is
  used to help trigger natural flowering, but ample soil moisture is
  then needed from flowering through fruit maturity, with drought during
  flowering and fruit set reducing yield, while excess rain at flowering
  causes poor pollination and flower drop.
- **Establishment:** Propagated almost entirely by air-layering
  (marcotting), occasionally supplemented by grafting onto seedling
  rootstocks; marcotted trees lack a taproot and need staking, mounding,
  or later root-grafting support against wind.
- **Labor:** No dedicated commercial labor-hour study was found; labor
  figures here are estimated largely by analogy to a related Sapindaceae
  fruit crop's own harvest-productivity rate, given the similarity of
  cutting whole fruit clusters from large evergreen trees.
- **Soil:** Rich sandy loam to oolitic-limestone or calcareous rocky
  soils, or heavy alluvium with access to a shallow water table; the
  crop is marginal on strongly acidic sand and flowers poorly on muck
  soils.
- **Stand life:** First crop roughly three to four years after planting
  air-layered trees (considerably longer from seedlings); full canopy
  by around six to seven years; peak per-tree yield from roughly year 15
  to 25, with decline after about 35 years; biennial bearing is severe
  throughout productive life (Finding 2).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A seed- and graft-transmissible virus
  disease causing a distinctive "witches' broom" rosette-shoot symptom
  affects a meaningful fraction of trees in at least one major producing
  country, with losses considerably worse in severe cases; a stink bug
  affecting flower panicles, an erosive mite, fruit flies, fruit-piercing
  moths, and fruit bats are also documented pests (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named cultivars from several countries differ
  in season (early, mid, or late), fruit and seed size, aril recovery
  percentage, sweetness, shelf life, ease of chemical flower induction,
  and suitability for fresh eating, canning, or drying (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Whole fruit clusters (panicles) are clipped with a leaf
  or two attached, in two passes about a week to ten days apart.
- **Storage:** A few weeks under proper refrigeration; markedly extended
  by sulfur dioxide fumigation, though this treatment is not approved
  for fresh sale in at least one major importing country (Finding 5).

## Hazards

Seeds of this fruit carry a dramatically higher concentration of a
toxic amino acid (hypoglycin A) than the edible flesh, which carries
only a low level; seeds are not meant to be eaten and are instead used
traditionally as a shampoo ingredient. The chemical compound used for
artificial flower induction (potassium chlorate) is a genuinely
hazardous worker-safety concern as a strong oxidizer, particularly when
mixed with nitrogen fertilizer or organic matter, which can create an
explosion risk; a documented case describes fatal consequences from
improper mixing. Sulfur dioxide fumigation residue is a consideration
for fumigated fresh fruit. The fruit is a known host for fruit-fly
quarantine pests, requiring heat or irradiation treatment for some
export markets. A seed- and graft-transmissible viral disease causes
significant crop loss in affected trees in at least one major producing
country.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed hypoglycin-A toxin hazard, the potassium-
chlorate worker-safety hazard, and the sulfur-dioxide fumigation-residue
hazard are each deferred in fuller detail until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit clusters are cut with leaves attached, sorted, and
  sold in bunches, or clipped to individual fruit and cooled for
  refrigerated storage. Smaller or surplus fruit is boiled briefly and
  dried whole, yielding roughly a third to two-fifths of fresh weight as
  a dried product (a sourced processing ratio for at least one
  cultivar). Peeled flesh is also dried into a distinctive black,
  leathery product used for a traditional drink and in herbal medicine,
  or made into nectar or a spirit-based liqueur. Dried seeds are
  processed for use as a shampoo ingredient (exploiting their natural
  saponin content). Prunings are used as fuel.
- Not covered by this research: canning in syrup and freezing in
  processing detail; sulfur dioxide fumigation technique; the chemical
  flower-induction compound as a tracked input commodity; the specific
  construction of wind-mounding, bamboo props, or bat-exclusion netting;
  dried leaves and flowers used in herbal medicine; honey production;
  timber use.

  clear reconciliation — the estimate used here sits between them,
  informed by a related tropical fruit crop's own documented figure.

  sources regarding bloom reliability (reliable versus erratic bearing)
  — not reconciled.

  estimates rely on analogy to a related fruit crop's harvest rate.

  numeric figures and are treated as rough estimates here.
- Salinity tolerance was not found in the standard international
  reference checked, and is simply not established in the sources
  reached.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers are derived primarily from a multi-year national average
for a major producing country (averaging roughly 5,000 kg/ha across a
decade that included both "on" and "off" biennial-bearing years, cross-
checked against a more recent national-level estimate that rounds to a
similar figure), with a good-yield tier (roughly 7,500 kg/ha) derived
from documented on-year national peaks and better-performing provinces,
an exceptional tier (roughly 19,500 kg/ha) taken from a documented
"maximum observed" commercial figure, and a poor tier (roughly 1,000
kg/ha) derived from documented low-end regional figures, likely
reflecting unirrigated hill orchards or severe off-year effects.

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days (and the resulting water-use "legacy"
figures) are estimated by setting an effective crop coefficient roughly
midway between two conflicting regional research figures — one from a
likely water-limited site, one from a cluster of irrigated subtropical
tree-fruit orchards — landing close to a related tropical/subtropical
fruit crop's own documented figure. Root-zone depth used for scheduling
purposes is set shallower than the maximum documented rooting depth
(which can reach several meters to a water table on some soils), since
only a fraction of that depth is typically relevant for irrigation
scheduling. Drought tolerance is rated medium and waterlogging tolerance
low-to-medium, reflecting documented advice that wet lowlands and heavy
clay are best avoided, balanced against the crop's genuinely deep root
system on suitable soils.

A growth-stage curve is estimated judgementally as a nearly flat,
evergreen pattern with a deliberate dip during the dry rest period used
to help trigger natural flowering.

Labor-hour figures across harvesting, pruning/thinning, and post-harvest
handling operations are estimated primarily by analogy to a related
Sapindaceae fruit crop's documented hand-harvest productivity rate
(cutting whole fruit clusters), adjusted to account for this crop's
two-pass harvest pattern; no direct labor-hour study was found for this
crop in either of its two major producing-region source documents (one
reports costs only in currency without a stated wage rate, so it could
not be converted to hours).

Days from planting to first harvest and the ramp-up period to peak
per-tree yield are derived directly from documented timelines (roughly
three to four years to first crop from air-layered planting material,
and continuing yield increases up to around year 15). Peak daily
harvest rate within a season is derived by dividing the adopted
good-yield tier across a roughly two-week two-pass harvest window
implied by the documented pass interval. Maximum days fruit can stand
before quality decline, and the daily decay rate, are derived from
documented guidance that the harvest passes are spaced roughly a week to
ten days apart and that flavor falls noticeably if harvest is delayed
beyond that window.

Soil pH optimum and range (roughly 6.0 optimum, with tolerance from
roughly 5.0 to 8.0) are rough estimates, informed by the crop's
documented good performance on calcareous, limestone-type soils at the
high end and its documented marginal performance on strongly acidic
sandy soils at the low end; no precise sourced numeric range was found.
