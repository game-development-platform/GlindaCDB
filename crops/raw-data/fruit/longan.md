## Naming and scope

*Dimocarpus longan* Lour. (Sapindaceae); syns. *Euphoria longana* Lam.,
*Nephelium longana* Cambess. [1][2]. The name means "dragon's eye" in
Chinese [1].
- **Local names:** *lam-yai* (Thailand), *leng-keng* (Malaysia and
  Indonesia), *nhãn* (Vietnam), *mamoncillo chino* (Cuba) [1][2][3].
- **Covered here:** the commercial longan, *D. longan* subsp. *longan*
  var. *longan* [1].
- **Not covered:**
  - **Mata kucing:** subsp. *malesianus*, "fully adapted to the
    unchanging heat, humidity, daylength" of the equatorial zone. It is
    its own row, `mata_kucing` (`mata-kucing.md`) [1].
  - **Wild varieties:** var. *longepetiolulatus*, *obtusus* and
    *magnifolius* grow wild in China [1]; the encyclopedia also lists
    var. *echinatus*, a spiny Bornean form [11].
- **Thai types:** three edible types are distinguished within var.
  *longan* [1]:
  - a large forest tree with small fruit;
  - the native *lamyai kradook*, with small fruit and large seeds, used
    as a rootstock;
  - the commercial *lamyai kraloke*, with large fruit and small seeds.

**Relatives in this project:**
- lychee (`lychee`, `lychee.md`);
- rambutan (`rambutan`, `rambutan.md`);
- pulasan (`pulasan`, `pulasan.md`);
- mata kucing (`mata_kucing`).

**Proposed:**
- **Ackee** (`ackee`, *Blighia sapida*): the Sapindaceae fruit whose
  hypoglycin A import limit is the yardstick for longan seed toxin
  (Finding 4) [6]. It is Jamaica's commercial fruit, since researched
  in `ackee.md`.
- **Mamoncillo** (`mamoncillo`, *Melicoccus bijugatus*): a Caribbean
  Sapindaceae fruit tested alongside longan, with seed HGA of 90.5-970
  µg/g [6]. Since researched in `mamoncillo.md`.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a chemical switch for flowering.**
- **Natural trigger:**
  - **Cool winter:** a short (2-3 month), cool (mean 15-22 °C) winter
    gives a prolific bloom [1]. One review puts the threshold at "usually
    ≤ 18 °C" [5], and the Florida guide at 15 °C or less with a dry
    October-February [3].
  - **Warm winters:** blooming is poor after a warm winter [2].
- **Potassium chlorate (KClO3):**
  - **Who uses it:** Thai growers discovered they could induce flowering
    with it [1]. Thai markets now have fresh longan "all year round," and
    chlorate induction has "only been found to be effective for longan"
    [5].
  - **Soil drench:** 8 g/m² for 'Daw' and 1-4 g/m² for 'Chompoo' gave
    100% flowering [1]. A review gives 0.5-2 kg per tree in a ditch
    15-20 cm deep at the drip line, with the soil kept slightly wet for
    15 days [5].
  - **Other methods:** foliar spray at about 1,000 ppm [1] or 0.5-3 g/L
    [5]; trunk injection at 0.25 g per cm of stem diameter, for stems
    under 10-15 cm [1].
  - **Season:** flowering reached only 11.9-50.9% in the rainy season,
    against 77.5-88.6% in the cool and hot seasons [5]. October-February
    is best [1].
  - **Overdose:** "excessive KClO3 concentrations may lead to less
    flowering, no flowering or leaf burn" [5].
  - **Thai field trial:** 75 g per metre of canopy diameter gave 93.5%
    flowering in August on 6-year-old 'Daw' trees [12].
  - **Expansion:** chlorate plus easy-flowering cultivars such as
    'Phetsakon' spread longan into central Thailand and to Chanthaburi
    and Trat [1].
  - **Florida:** growers can now improve on-season flowering and produce
    off-season fruit [9].

**Finding 2 — severe biennial bearing, and prices that swing against it.**
- **Off years:** production in an off year is usually 20-40% of an on
  year [1].
- **Thai national yields, 1989-1998:** they alternated 2,512 / 7,325 /
  3,756 / 6,137 / 3,375 / 6,269 / 4,137 / 6,175 / 5,500 / 5,730 kg/ha
  [1].
- **Taiwan:** 26 / 36 / 14 / 32 / 14 kg per tree in 1994-1998 [1].
- **Mature trees:** over three years, 12-15-year-old trees average "one
  poor crop, one fair crop and one excellent crop" [1].
- **Prices move the other way:**
  - **Thailand:** 28.00 baht/kg in 1989 on 44,661 t, against 7.46
    baht/kg in 1990 on 145,869 t [1].
  - **Taiwan:** NT$74.40/kg in the 1996 off year, against NT$17.34 in
    1995 [1].
- **Management:**
  - **China:** growers remove about 40% of flower spikes and 30% of young
    fruit [1].
  - **Thailand:** growers halve the flowers and then remove 10% of the
    fruit [1].
  - **Morton:** describes removing three-quarters of the flower spikes
    [2].
  - **Florida:** panicles are thinned to 50-75 fruit [9], or by about 50%
    [3].

**Finding 3 — drying makes it a storable commodity.**
- **Method:** fruit is boiled for 5 minutes, dried at 55 °C, then at 70
  °C, for 19-20 hours in total. The product has 18-19% moisture and
  60-65 °Brix [1].
- **Yield ('Chuliang'):** dried whole fruit is 35-38% of fresh weight,
  and dried flesh 13-16% [1].
- **Exports:** Thailand exported 38,075 t of dried longan worth US$85.7
  million in 1997, against 81,632 t fresh worth US$84.8 million [1].
- **Uses:** the dried aril is "black, leathery and smoky in flavor" and
  is brewed as a refreshing drink [1][2].

**Finding 4 — the seed carries a serious toxin; the flesh carries
traces.**
- **Seeds:** hypoglycin A (HGA) at 45.3-3,540 µg/g dried seed (n = 5)
  [6]. That top value was the highest of any species tested, above
  mamoncillo (90.5-970) and litchi (up to 267 in commercial groups) [6].
- **Yardstick:** US imports of ackee are restricted to fruit with less
  than 100 µg/g HGA [6].
- **Child dose:** a child could reach the rat maximum tolerated dose by
  eating "only half of a five gram seed (~3000 μg/g HGA)" [6].
- **Arils:** HGA below the 1 µg/g detection limit to 2.45 µg/g dried
  fruit (n = 5). MCPG, the related toxin, was below the limit in all
  five [7].
- **Seed use:** seeds are used like soapberries for shampoo, because
  they contain saponin [1][2].

**Finding 5 — sulfur dioxide doubles storage life but is barred for US
fresh sale.**
- **At ambient temperature:** at 25-31 °C the rind browns within 3-4
  days and the aril rots within a week [1].
- **Recommended cold storage:** 4-7 °C and 90-95% RH for 2-3 weeks [4].
- **Untreated against SO2-treated storage life** [4]:

  | Temperature | Untreated | SO2-treated |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0 °C | 14-28 days | 21-42 days |
  | 4 °C | 14 days | 14-28 days |
  | 10 °C | 7-14 days | 14 days |
  | 20 °C | 3-5 days | 7 days |
  | 30 °C | 1-2 days | 3-5 days |

- **Trial results:** 20 minutes of SO2 then 4 °C gave 100% good 'Shixia'
  fruit after 28 days [1]. SO2, PE bags and 3 °C held fruit for 46 days
  [1].
- **Rules and limits:** SO2 on fresh fruit "is not approved in the United
  States" [4]. Hydrocooled fruit should not be fumigated, because it
  takes brown spots and higher residue [4].

**Finding 6 — a cheap tree crop with production now measured in
millions of tonnes.**
- **1997-1998:**
  - **China:** 432,400 ha and 232,000 t in 1997 [1].
  - **Thailand:** 41,504 ha and 238,000 t in 1998 [1].
  - **Taiwan:** about 12,000 ha and 53,000-151,000 t in 1994-1998 [1].
  - **Vietnam:** Mekong Delta 30,030 ha and 280,000 t in 1999 [1].
- **Recent:**
  - **China:** about 1.91 million t in 2023 [12].
  - **Thailand:** a projected 1,438,137 t for 2024 [12].
  - **China, Thailand and Vietnam together:** about 95% of world output
    [11].
- **Florida:** about 1,600 acres. Its "estimated" 28.9 million lb
  appears to come from the budget's own per-acre assumption (see
  Estimates) [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree to 20 m [1]; 9-12 m tall and 14
  m wide [2]; up to 31 m, but 9.1-12.2 m in south Florida [3]. Brittle
  wood and corky bark [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial; one main crop a year, picked in two
  passes 7-10 days apart [1]. Off-season crops can be forced with
  chlorate (Finding 1) [1][5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Air layers:** bear in the third or fourth year [1]; 2-3 years
    after planting [2][3]; marcotted Thai trees in the fourth year [1].
  - **Seedlings:** juvenile for about 7 years [1]; 7-8 years [1]; up to
    6 years [3].
  - **Ramp-up (Thailand):** 2-5 kg per tree in year 5; 20 kg in year 7;
    60 kg in year 10; 100-190 kg by year 15; then 150-200 kg for ten
    years, declining after 35 years [1].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** subtropical; grows in the tropics but needs a marked
    change of seasons to flower [1].
  - **Elevation:** native at 150-450 m in southern China [2]; sea level
    to about 460 m [3].
  - **Rainfall:** about 1,500 mm suits it [1]; 1,200-1,400 mm over
    100-150 rainy days is ideal [1].
  - **Heat:** night temperatures above 25 °C after fruit set harm fruit,
    and above 40 °C fruit is damaged and drops [1]. Flowering and fruit
    set do best at 20-25 °C in Thailand [1].
  - **Cold:** small trees are damaged at -0.56 to -1.11 °C. Larger trees
    suffer leaf injury at -2.2 to -2.8 °C, small-branch injury at -3.3
    to -3.9 °C, and trunk damage at -4.4 °C [2]. Young trees are severely
    damaged at -1 to -0.5 °C and killed at -2 to -3 °C [3]. Slightly less
    cold-tolerant than lychee [3], though Morton says it endures more
    frost [2].
  - **Chill:** no chill-hour figure; the cool-winter requirement is
    Finding 1 [1][3][5].
- **Composition (per 100 g fresh aril):**
  - **USDA SR Legacy 169089:** 60 kcal (251 kJ); water 82.8 g; protein
    1.31 g; fat 0.1 g; carbohydrate 15.1 g; fibre 1.1 g; potassium 266
    mg; magnesium 10 mg; phosphorus 21 mg; calcium 1 mg; iron 0.13 mg;
    vitamin C 84.0 mg; riboflavin 0.14 mg [8].
  - **Morton's table, fresh:** 61 kcal; moisture 82.4 g; protein 1.0 g;
    carbohydrate 15.8 g; calcium 10 mg; phosphorus 42 mg; iron 1.2 mg;
    ascorbic acid "6 mg (possibly)" [2].
  - **Morton's table, dried:** 286 kcal; moisture 17.6 g; carbohydrate
    74.0 g [2].
  - **FAO table (Wong and Saichol 1991):** 109 calories; moisture 72.4 g;
    carbohydrate 25.2 g; vitamin C 8.0 mg [1]. It also gives "458
    kJ/100g" [1].
  - **Fruit structure:** edible portion 67-78% of export-grade fruit [1];
    aril 60-75% of weight, with 15-25% TSS [1]; 12-21% soluble solids in
    Florida [3]. Fruit weighs 5-20 g [1] or 6-19 g [3].
- **Edibility:** Raw-edible aril; also frozen, canned or dried [1]. The
  seed is not eaten (Finding 4).
- **Pollination:** Cross-pollinated, with duodichogamy: staminate, then
  pistillate, then hermaphrodite, then staminate flowers again. Male and
  female phases overlap for 4-6 weeks [1]. Mainly insect-pollinated, most
  effective 08:00-14:00 [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Thailand, national:** 2,512-7,325 kg/ha in 1989-1998 [1]. By
    province in 1993, from 2,625 kg/ha (Chanthaburi) to 8,400 kg/ha
    (Nan) [1].
  - **Taiwan:** 4,506-12,701 kg/ha at 300-600 trees/ha [1].
  - **China:** 634-2,506 kg/ha by province in 1987-1997 [1]; 18-36 kg
    per tree [1]. Trees are on poor hill sites with no irrigation [1].
  - **Dwarf high-density 'Shixia' (China):** 2,250 kg per mu at 8 years
    (see Estimates) [1].
  - **Florida, per tree:** 22.5-45 kg (light), 68-113 kg (medium) and
    135-225 kg (heavy) [1][2]; 23 to over 227 kg [3].
  - **Florida budget:** 250 lb per tree at 87 trees per acre with 83%
    pack-out, about 18,053 lb per acre marketable [9] (see Estimates).
  - **Encyclopedia:** 2-5 t/ha typical, with up to 19.5 t/ha reported in
    Israel [11].
- **Water use:**
  - **Rooting:** roots reach 2-4 m to the water table on Thai alluvium
    [1].
  - **Irrigation timing:** from panicle emergence through fruit
    development and the post-harvest flush, then withdrawn before the
    next flowering [1].
  - **Thai yields:** "irrigation overrides other factors" and yields are
    higher along rivers [1].
  - **Florida:** water from flowering through harvest; hold it back from
    late summer through winter [3].
  - **Crop coefficient:** one Indonesian thesis reports ETo 5.76 and ETa
    7.90 mm/day but a Kc of 0.30 [12] (Open research questions).
- **Propagation:**
  - **Air layering (marcotting):** the main method. 80-90% success is
    commonly claimed. Roots form in about a month in the Thai wet season
    [1], or in 10-12 weeks in Florida [3]. Marcotted trees have no
    taproot and need props, mounding or inarched seedlings against wind
    [1].
  - **Grafting:** approach grafting onto 8-12-month 'Wuyuan' seedlings
    [1]; patch budding with over 85% success [1]; whip grafting with 80%
    in Taiwan [2].
  - **Graft incompatibility:** 'Chuliang' reached 36% incompatibility in
    Guangdong. Grafting onto seedlings of the same cultivar is advised
    [1].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** witches' broom is a filamentous virus. It
    spreads through infected seed and budwood, dodder, the litchi stink
    bug, the longan psylla and a gall mite [1]. Shoot-tip culture gives
    virus-free plantlets [1].
- **Labor:**
  - **Florida budget:** $779 per acre per year for cultural labour, and
    $5,662 for picking, packing and marketing, which is 59.63% of the
    total cost [9]. Thinning is "labor intensive" [9].
  - **Hours:** no labour-hour figure was found.
- **Soil:** Rich sandy loam and oolitic limestone; marginal on moderately
  acid sand; flowering deficient on muck [1][2]. Heavy alluvium with
  access to the water table in Thailand [1]. Avoid wet lowlands and heavy
  clay [1]. Not tolerant of saline soil or water [3]. No pH figure was
  sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Finding 5. Respiration is 3.5-11.3 mg
  CO2/kg/h at 5 °C and 30-53 at 20 °C; ethylene production is under 1
  nL/kg/h [4]. Below 5 °C an off-flavour develops after about a week,
  and 0 °C peel browning is chilling injury [4]. Desiccation dulls the
  skin [4].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** 6 × 6 to 12 × 12 m (70-300 trees/ha) [1].
  - **By region:** about 50 trees/ha in mature Thai orchards; 300-600 in
    Taiwan; 195-300 in Fujian [1]; 87 per acre (20 × 25 ft) in Florida
    [9].
  - **Planting holes:** 60 × 60 × 60 cm [1].
  - **Training:** Thai trees are cut to a 1.2 m trunk and trained to
    24-32 sub-branches by year 3-4 [1].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **China:** over 400 cultivars, 14% early, 68% mid and 18% late [1].
    - **'Chuliang':** 12.0-16.5 g, 69-74% edible, TSS 20-23%; over
      100,000 ha [1].
    - **'Shixia':** small, crisp, best for fresh eating [1].
    - **'Wuyuan':** used as a rootstock [1][2].
    - **'Fuyan':** over 90% of Fujian plantings; used for canning [1].
  - **Thailand:**
    - **'Daw':** early; about 73% of Thai area; a consistent bearer and
      relatively free of witches' broom [1]. Florida calls its bearing
      "Regular?" [3] (Open research questions).
    - **'Chompoo':** pink aril, TSS 21-22%, irregular cropping [1].
    - **'Biew Khiew':** late, crisp, susceptible to witches' broom [1].
    - **'Haew':** heavy but alternate bearing [1].
  - **Taiwan:** over 98% 'Fengko' [1].
  - **Florida:** 99% 'Kohala', introduced 1954 [2][3][9]; 'Diamond River'
    (Petch Sakorn) is precocious but of fair quality [3].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** see Climate zones [2][3].
  - **Wind:** sensitive to branch splitting [1], although young trees
    establish on windy sites [3].
  - **Rain:** excessive rain at flowering causes flower drop [1][3];
    overcast weather before harvest causes fruit shedding [1].
  - **Drought:** drought at flowering cuts yield [1].
  - **Flooding:** young trees can die after 5-10 days of flooding [3],
    though Morton says it can stand brief flooding [2].
- **Water regime:** Irrigated or rainfed, with a deliberate dry spell
  before flowering [1][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Bloom to harvest:** 5-7 months [1]; about 5 months [1]; 140-190
    days [3].
  - **Ripening:** non-climacteric; pick when ripe, in two passes [1].
  - **Seasons:** Thailand late June-late August; China late July-late
    September; Taiwan July-October; Queensland January-April [1];
    Florida mid-July to early September [3].
  - **Grades (Thailand):** A is 55-75 fruit/kg (14-18 g), B is 76-80
    fruit/kg, and C is over 80 fruit/kg [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Weed risk:** the Hawaii Weed Risk Assessment scores it -2, low
    risk. It is highly domesticated, reported naturalized where grown,
    and listed as a weed, but with no impacts found [10].
  - **Quarantine:** a fruit-fly host; hot-air, vapour-heat or
    irradiation treatment for quarantine [4].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Seed hypoglycin A:** up to 3,540 µg/g dried seed, 35 times the US
  ackee import limit (Finding 4; see Estimates) [6]. The aril carries
  only traces [7].
- **Potassium chlorate (flower inducer):**
  - **Chemistry:** "a type of strong oxidant that is used as a
    herbicide" [5].
  - **Accidents (snippet level):**
    - **Chiang Mai, 2023:** three workers were killed while mixing 150
      kg of chlorate with 50 kg of urea and heat-sealing it into bags
      [12].
    - **Chanthaburi, 2019:** one death after chlorate and sodium
      chlorate were left in the sun and mixed [12].
    - **Chiang Mai, 1999:** a blast at San Pa Tong killed 35 workers at
      a plant illegally storing chlorate sold to longan farmers [12].
- **Sulfur dioxide residue:** improper fumigation leaves high residue
  and spoils flavour [1]; not approved for US fresh fruit [4].
- **Witches' broom virus:** in Fujian, 20-100% of trees are affected,
  with crop losses averaging 10-20% and over 50% in severe cases [1].
  There is no cure [1].
- **Longan decline:** affects up to 40% of trees in Thailand [11].
- **Pests:**
  - **Longan stink bug** (*Tessaratoma javanica*): epidemic in Thailand
    in March-April [1].
  - **Others:** erinose mite, fruit flies, fruit-piercing moths, scales
    and stem borers [1]; lychee webworm, scales and root weevils in
    Florida [3].
  - **Fruit bats:** cause serious damage in Thailand and Australia;
    netting is the only effective control [1].
- **Postharvest fungi:** *Lasiodiplodia theobromae*, *Pestalotiopsis*,
  *Cladosporium*, *Fusarium* and *Aspergillus niger* [4].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** panicle cut with one or two leaves → sorted →
  packed as bunches (21-22 kg Thai bamboo baskets) or clipped single
  fruit in 2.25-4.5 kg crates → precooled → SO2-fumigated (not for the
  US) → 4-7 °C [1][4]. Asian buyers prefer bunches [4].
- **Chain 2 (dried):** boiled 5 minutes → dried at 55-70 °C for 19-20
  hours → 35-38% of fresh weight as whole dried fruit, or 13-16% as
  dried flesh → brewed drink or herbal medicine [1].
- **Chain 3 (canned):** large-fruited cultivars ('Biew Khiew', 'Daw',
  'Fuyan') canned in their own juice with little added sugar [1].
- **Chain 4 (frozen):** frozen whole in the skin; quality is unchanged
  on thawing [1][3].
- **Chain 5 (other):** nectar [1]; liqueur from flesh macerated in
  alcohol [1][2]; fresh-cut aril [4].
- **Seed:** shampoo (saponin) [1][2]; a styptic [2].
- **Leaves and flowers:** sold in Chinese herb markets; the leaves
  contain quercetin and quercitrin [1][2].
- **Wood:** red, hard heartwood for posts, tools and furniture [2].
- **Fuel:** seeds and rind are burned [2].
- **Medicinal:** dried flesh is used for stomach ache and insomnia, and
  as an antidote for poison [1][2].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.

## Open research questions

- **Composition conflicts:**
  - **Vitamin C:** 84 mg [8] against 8 mg [1] and "6 mg (possibly)" [2].
  - **Energy and moisture:** 60 kcal and 82.8% water [8] against 109
    calories and 72.4% water [1]. The FAO's 458 kJ does not match its
    own 109 calories (see Estimates).
- **Cold tolerance:** slightly less hardy than lychee [3] against
  enduring more frost [2].
- **Flooding:** dies after 5-10 days [3] against tolerating brief
  flooding [2].
- **Years to bearing:** 2-3 [2][3] against 3-4 [1] for air layers.
- **'Daw' bearing:** a consistent bearer [1] against "Regular?" [3].
- **Crop coefficient:** 0.30 against an ETa/ETo of 1.37 in the same
  thesis [12].
- **Thailand's area:** the FAO text gives 411,504 ha for 1998, but its
  tables give 41,504 ha. The table value fits the yields (see
  Estimates) [1].
- **Recent statistics:** these are trade-news snippets; no FAOSTAT
  series was found [12].
- **Not sourced:** soil pH range, salinity threshold, labour hours,
  soil biology and controlled-environment use.

## Estimates and derivations

**Thai 10-year mean yield** [1]: (2,512 + 7,325 + 3,756 + 6,137 + 3,375 +
6,269 + 4,137 + 6,175 + 5,500 + 5,730) = 50,916 kg ÷ 10 = 5,092 kg/ha.

**Thailand 1998 area check** [1]: 238,000 t ÷ 41,504 ha = 5.73 t/ha,
which matches the 5,730 kg/ha table entry. At 411,504 ha it would be
0.58 t/ha, so the text figure is a typo.

**Other national yields** [1]:
- China 1997: 232,000 t ÷ 432,400 ha = 0.54 t/ha. This is below every
  provincial figure in the same report, which suggests much of the area
  was young, non-bearing plantings.
- Taiwan 1995: 151,388 t ÷ 12,192 ha = 12.4 t/ha (table: 12,701 kg/ha).
- Vietnam Mekong 1999: 280,000 t ÷ 30,030 ha = 9.3 t/ha.

**Mature Thai orchard ceiling** (an estimate): 150-200 kg per tree [1] ×
about 50 trees/ha [1] = 7.5-10 t/ha.

**Dwarf 'Shixia'** [1]: 2,250 kg per mu × 15 mu/ha = 33.75 t/ha. At 120
trees per mu (1,800/ha), that is 18.75 kg per tree.

**Florida budget yield** [9]:
- 87 trees × 250 lb × 0.83 = 18,053 lb per acre.
- 18,053 × 0.4536 kg ÷ 0.4047 ha = 20.2 t/ha marketable.
- Density: 87 × 2.471 = 215 trees/ha.

**Florida production check** [9]: 28.9 million lb ÷ 1,600 acres = 18,063
lb per acre. That is the budget's own assumption, so the state figure
looks derived, not measured.

**Florida cost** [9]: $9,496 per acre ÷ 0.4047 = $23,465/ha. Harvest
and marketing are 5,662 ÷ 9,496 = 59.6% of that.

**Fresh fruit per kg dried** [1]: 1 ÷ 0.38 to 1 ÷ 0.35 = 2.6-2.9 kg of
fresh fruit per kg of dried whole fruit; 1 ÷ 0.16 to 1 ÷ 0.13 = 6.3-7.7 kg
per kg of dried flesh.

**Seed HGA against the ackee limit** [6]: 3,540 ÷ 100 = 35 times. The
lowest seed value, 45.3 µg/g, is below the limit.

**FAO energy mismatch** [1]: 109 kcal × 4.184 = 456 kJ, close to its 458
kJ. Both are nearly twice USDA's 251 kJ [8], which fits the FAO table's
lower moisture (72.4 against 82.8 g).

**Crop-coefficient check** [12]: 7.90 ÷ 5.76 = 1.37, not the 0.30 the
thesis summary states.

**Chlorate rate units** [1][5]: a foliar spray of 1,000 ppm = 1 g/L,
inside the review's 0.5-3 g/L range.

**Bloom to harvest** [1][3]: 5-7 months ≈ 150-210 days, overlapping
Florida's 140-190 days.
