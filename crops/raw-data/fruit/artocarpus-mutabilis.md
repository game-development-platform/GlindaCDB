## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus mutabilis* Becc. (Moraceae), the **wild** sister species of marang
(*A. odoratissimus*, `marang.md`). For over a century it was treated merely as a
synonym of *A. odoratissimus*: Wikipedia and PROSEA both list "*A. mutabilis*
Becc." (and "*A. tarap* Becc.") as synonyms of the lumped species [5]. Gardner
et al. (2022) then showed with molecular data what the Iban and Dusun peoples of
Sarawak had long maintained in the field — that the lumped tree is in fact **two
species**, and resurrected *A. mutabilis* for the wild one [1][3].

- **Names:** **pingan** is the Iban name for this species, and **tonggom-tonggom**
  (also spelt tonggom-onggom) the Dusun name [1][3]. The cultivated sister species
  (marang) carries the separate names **lumok** (Iban) and **timadang** (Dusun)
  [3]. English usage has no distinct common name; this doc uses "pingan."
- **The split (de-anonymised).** During 2016 fieldwork in Sarawak, Iban and Dusun
  collaborators insisted the "one species" of Western botany was two; Gardner et
  al. (2022) confirmed it with DNA and morphology, and a 2026 leaf-anatomy study
  (Ruslan, Gardner & Ahmad Puad, *Telopea*) confirmed it again on anatomical
  characters [1][2][3][4]. A practical consequence noted in `marang.md`: an
  unknown share of older "marang"/"*A. odoratissimus*" data in the literature
  actually belongs to this species [1][6].
- **Name clash — "pingan".** The name is contested across three taxa. The most
  recent authority (Gardner et al. 2022) assigns **pingan** to *A. mutabilis*
  [1][3]; older PROSEA material gives pingan as an Iban name for the lumped marang
  [5][6]; and Wikipedia's marang entry gives "pingan"/"mountain tarap" to yet
  another species, *A. sarawakensis* (this project's separate `PROPOSED` `pingan`
  row) [5][6]. **Resolved (2026-11-27):** the `pingan` row (*A. sarawakensis*)
  has now been researched (`pingan.md`), and it adopts **"mountain tarap"** as
  its common name, leaving "pingan" to *A. mutabilis* per Gardner et al. (2022);
  that doc also recommends renaming its cropID off `pingan`. This doc's reading,
  "pingan = *A. mutabilis*", therefore stands as the current best one [3][5].
- **Range:** Borneo — documented wild in primary and secondary forest in central
  and southern Sarawak [2]. The Philippine and other cultivated populations once
  filed under "*A. odoratissimus*" are the cultivated sister species (lumok), not
  this wild one, so this species' range should not be assumed to include them
  [1][2][6].

**Gate check (2026-11-25): passes.** Proposed from `marang.md`, which flagged *A.
mutabilis* as "recognized by the Iban and Dusun as distinct, and confirmed as
distinct in 2022" [6]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a validly distinct *Artocarpus* species, long synonymised under
  marang but resurrected on molecular and anatomical evidence [1][2][5]. Not a
  form of marang, jackfruit, champedak, breadfruit or lakoocha [1][6].
- **Range:** Bornean wild-forest (Sarawak documented) [2], versus marang's
  cultivated range centred on the Philippines/Mindanao [6].
- **Farming system — the sharpest distinction:** this is the **wild** sibling,
  with no documented cultivation; marang (lumok) is the **cultivated** one. The
  two were split precisely along the wild/cultivated line [1][3].
- **Harvest / uses:** a wild-gathered fruit, smaller and less sweet than marang,
  with thinner pulp [1][3].

**Relatives in this project:** `marang.md` (the cultivated sister species and the
proposing doc), and the other *Artocarpus* rows `jackfruit.md`, `champedak.md`,
`breadfruit.md`, `lakoocha.md` [6]. No new relatives were surfaced for proposing
beyond the already-`PROPOSED` `peluntan`, `pingan` and this row; the `pingan`
(*A. sarawakensis*) row's name conflict with this species is flagged above.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the wild half of a species that indigenous knowledge split before
science did.** Iban and Dusun field botanists distinguished pingan
(*A. mutabilis*) from lumok (*A. odoratissimus*) long before Western taxonomy,
which had lumped them since Beccari's names were synonymised; molecular work
(Gardner et al. 2022) and a later leaf-anatomy study (2026) vindicated the local
distinction [1][2][3]. The case is cited as an example of indigenous knowledge
preserving both local knowledge and biodiversity [1].

**Finding 2 — a wild tree, not a cultivated one.** Pingan is consistently
described as the **wild** form: smaller leaves, more slender twigs, and smaller,
**less-sweet** fruit with **thinner pulp** than cultivated lumok; its vegetative
parts (buds, young twigs) **often — but not always — bear long hairs**, where
lumok's are short [1][3]. No cultivation, plantation, planting density, yield or
labour figure was found for this species specifically; what exists under
"marang"/"*A. odoratissimus*" is the cultivated sister (`marang.md`) and cannot
be assumed to transfer [1][6].

**Finding 3 — the diagnostic characters are in the fruit, not the leaves.**
Gardner et al. found that **fruit size and the length and shape of the fruit
spines** are better characters for telling the two species apart than the length
of the stipule indumentum (hairiness), which overlaps [1]. Externally the two are
otherwise similar; they also differ in stipule and twig characters [2]. The 2026
*Telopea* study adds internal leaf-anatomy differences — epidermal-wall type,
number of cells in the glandular trichomes, length of the non-glandular
trichomes, number of vascular bundles in the midrib and petiole, and number of
collenchyma layers — from specimens in primary and secondary forest of central
and southern Sarawak [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial evergreen forest tree, as its sister species
  (a tree to ~25 m in marang); pingan has smaller leaves and more slender twigs
  than lumok [1][2]. Precise stature `Not sourced.` for this species alone.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial; wild fruiting. Season `Not sourced.` for this
  species specifically (marang's Bornean season is roughly October-January, but
  that figure may itself mix the two species) [6].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced. (Marang bears in 4-6 years from
  seed, but this is cultivated-lumok data and may not transfer) [6].
- **Climate zones:** Humid tropical Borneo, lowland and foothill forest; found
  wild in primary and secondary forest in central and southern Sarawak [2]. By
  habitat it shares marang's wet-tropics, evenly-watered, frost-free niche, but
  no species-specific temperature, latitude or elevation band was sourced; chill:
  none (equatorial) [2][6].
- **Composition:** Not sourced for this species. The fruit is reported **smaller
  and less sweet with thinner pulp** than marang, so marang's composition figures
  (`marang.md`) would, if borrowed, likely **overstate** sugar/edible-flesh share
  here — so they are deliberately not carried over [1][3].
- **Edibility:** The fruit is edible and eaten, but less palatable than marang —
  less sweet, thinner pulp [1][3]. Preparation detail (raw vs cooked, seed use)
  `Not sourced.` for this species specifically.
- **Pollination:** Not sourced. Monoecious with male and female flowers in
  separate heads is the genus/sister pattern, implying insect pollination, but
  this was not confirmed for *A. mutabilis* [6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to any storage organ
  (a tree fruit).
- **Yield:** Not sourced. No cultivated stands exist to measure; wild bearing in
  the genus can be erratic (marang's wild trees fruit irregularly) [6].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. A wet-tropics forest tree of consistently humid
  Borneo [2].
- **Propagation:** Not sourced for this species. As a wild tree it regenerates by
  seed in situ; the genus has recalcitrant, quickly-perishing seed and poor
  vegetative-propagation success (marang) [6].
- **Labor:** Not sourced. No cultivation system to cost; fruit is wild-gathered.
- **Soil:** Not sourced for this species. Marang grows wild on sandy clay in
  Sarawak; pingan shares the same Sarawak forest habitat [2][6]. pH / salinity:
  Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for this species. Marang (the sister)
  has a very short shelf life and fast flesh oxidation; whether pingan behaves the
  same is unknown [6].
- **Establishment:** Not applicable / not sourced — a wild forest tree with no
  documented planting practice.
- **Cultivars:** None. Not cultivated, so no named cultivars; it is itself the
  wild counterpart of cultivated marang [1][3].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced. Frost-intolerant by habitat (equatorial
  lowland forest); drought/flood/wind responses not sourced [2].
- **Water regime:** Rain-fed wild forest tree; no flooded/paddy requirement
  (none known for the genus) [6].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not sourced for this species. Wild-gathered when
  ripe; the fruit-spine length/shape that distinguishes it from marang is also the
  main field cue to the species [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to Borneo; no invasive listing found, and
  as a slow wild forest tree it has no weedy reputation [1][2]. **No IUCN
  assessment was found for *A. mutabilis* specifically** — marang's "Near
  Threatened" listing predates the split and applies to the lumped concept, so
  this newly-resurrected wild species' conservation status is effectively
  unassessed, and the 2026 anatomy study frames the distinction as mattering
  *for conservation* [2][6]. Movement rules / legal restrictions: Not sourced.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced; not a protected crop (a wild forest
  tree).

## Hazards

- **Plant toxins:** None sourced; none are known for the sister species, whose
  seeds are eaten after cooking [6].
- **Harvest / physical:** As a tall forest tree it would share marang's
  heavy-falling-fruit and high-reach hazards, but no species-specific harvest
  practice was found [6].
- **Allergy, food safety, ergot/mycotoxin:** Not sourced.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — nothing specific for this crop beyond the shared tall-tree
  physical-hazard note already flagged for `marang.md`.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** wild tree → fruit gathered when ripe → eaten fresh; less
  sweet and thinner-pulped than marang, so less esteemed [1][3].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** any cooked, seed or by-product uses (marang's
  seeds are boiled/roasted and its young fruit curried, but those are the sister
  species' documented uses, not confirmed for pingan) [6].

## Open research questions

- **Is pingan harvested/traded as a crop at all, or only incidentally gathered?**
  No market, yield, season or harvest-practice data were found for *A. mutabilis*
  specifically; if it turns out to be a managed wild-harvest resource this row
  should gain a `crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/` file.
- **How much "marang" data is really pingan?** The two were lumped for a century,
  so composition, phenology and range figures in older sources may belong to
  either [1][6].
- **Conservation status:** no IUCN assessment for *A. mutabilis* was found; the
  split leaves this wild species unassessed [2].
- **The "pingan" name clash** between *A. mutabilis* (Gardner et al. 2022) and
  *A. sarawakensis* was **resolved (2026-11-27)** when the `pingan` row was
  researched (`pingan.md`): *A. sarawakensis* takes "mountain tarap", "pingan"
  stays with *A. mutabilis*, and a cropID rename off `pingan` is recommended to
  the maintainer [1][5].
- **Works identified, not yet read (full text):**
  - Gardner, E.M. and others (2022), *Current Biology* 32(11):R511-R512 — full
    text, for the complete diagnostic character set [1].
  - Ruslan, Gardner & Ahmad Puad (2026), "Comparative leaf anatomy between two
    sister species: *Artocarpus odoratissimus* (Lumok) and *A. mutabilis*
    (Pingan) in Sarawak," *Telopea* 30:25-34 — full text, for the anatomical
    measurements and any range/ecology notes [2].

## Estimates and derivations

No quantitative estimates are made for this species: no yield, composition,
spacing, bearing-age or labour figure was found for *A. mutabilis* specifically,
and the sister species' figures are deliberately **not** borrowed because pingan's
fruit is documented as smaller, less sweet and thinner-pulped, which would bias
any such transfer (Composition; Finding 2) [1][3].
