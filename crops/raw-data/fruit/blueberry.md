## Naming and scope

Cultivated highbush blueberry, covering two cultivar groups: northern
highbush (a higher-chill, winter-hardy type) and southern highbush
(hybrids with two heat-tolerant wild relatives, bred for low chill and
heat tolerance). A third cultivar group, rabbiteye, is included within
this same crop entry — it differs from highbush in chill requirement,
mature height, and storage life, but is treated as a cultivar group
rather than a separate crop, consistent with how this project handles
other within-species cultivar races.

Not covered by this entry: lowbush ("wild") blueberry, which represents
a genuinely different farming system entirely — managed native stands
that are periodically burned or pruned and then raked rather than ever
planted or bred — and is treated by this project as its own separate
crop entry. No other researched crop in this project is a close
agronomic sibling to this one (it is the first bush fruit and the first
member of its genus researched); a related acid-loving genus-mate (a
well-known tart processing berry) is covered as a separate, later
entry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — fundamentally an acid-soil specialist with shallow,
root-hair-free roots.** This crop performs best in a narrow, distinctly
acidic soil pH range, with a minimum organic-matter requirement well
above most fruit crops [3][6]. Most planting sites need elemental
sulfur applied a year or more before planting to reach this target pH,
with application rate depending on soil type and starting pH [3]. Roots
stay almost entirely within the top 20-30 cm of soil, with the large
majority of root mass within the canopy dripline, and the plant
entirely lacks root hairs — making it unusually sensitive to drought and
to any change in soil-water conditions [3]. This root biology is why
sawdust mulch and raised beds are standard commercial practice, with at
least one detailed grower budget renewing the sawdust mulch every three
years [3][6]. Soil pH above the target range causes a documented iron-
deficiency chlorosis [3].

**Finding 2 — bee pollination essentially makes the crop.** Pollen is
heavy and sticky, so wind pollination contributes little; insect
pollination is required [3]. Bumblebees (and other sonicating bees)
vibrate the flower's anthers to release pollen, a more efficient
mechanism than honeybee visitation, though honeybees are still widely
used in large numbers for commercial pollination [3]. A roughly 80%
fruit-set rate is considered a full commercial crop; each flower
contains multiple ovaries, so the more that are successfully
pollinated, the larger the resulting berry [3]. Recommended hive
density has risen considerably over time — an older guideline figure
is well below what at least one modern detailed grower budget actually
uses [3][6].

**Finding 3 — frost tolerance is tightly tied to flower development
stage, and the crop has a real winter-chill requirement.** Northern
highbush cultivars require several hundred hours of winter chill below
a specific cool threshold temperature, with one commercial-storage
reference giving a somewhat broader range; rabbiteye requires
considerably less chill [1][3]. Critical spring frost-damage
temperatures fall steadily as flower buds progress through developmental
stages, from quite cold-tolerant at the earliest bud-swell stage to
acutely frost-sensitive at petal fall (the single most vulnerable
stage, damaged even at temperatures right at freezing) [3]. Fully
dormant flower buds of northern highbush cultivars can survive extreme
winter cold, far below the threshold that would damage the same buds
once they begin breaking dormancy in spring [3].

**Finding 4 — fruit must be picked fully ripe, cooled fast, and is not
chilling-sensitive.** Berries continue gaining sugar content and
measurable size for some time after turning fully blue in color, but
explicitly will not improve in quality after harvest, so correct pick
timing matters enormously [3]. Standard commercial storage is just
above freezing at high humidity for up to about two weeks for highbush
types, with rabbiteye storing notably longer [1]. Standard practice is
forced-air cooling to a moderate temperature followed promptly by
further cooling to near-freezing, ideally within about an hour of
harvest [1]. The crop is documented as not known to be chilling-
sensitive [1]. Machine harvesting is documented to roughly halve
storage life compared to hand-picked fruit [1].

**Finding 5 — hand versus machine harvest splits the crop into two
distinct economic systems.** At least one detailed modern grower budget
runs the large majority of its crop as hand-picked fresh fruit at a
documented per-pound labor cost roughly five times higher than its
machine-harvested fruit, which a small crew can cover at a documented
multi-acre-per-day rate [6]. Full mature production in that same budget
reaches a documented high per-hectare yield from around the seventh
year onward [6]. Certain cultivars bred for concentrated (rather than
staggered) ripening are specifically suited to machine harvest [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous woody perennial shrub, maturing
  over five to seven years to full production.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; a two-to-five-week harvest
  window per cultivar, covered by several hand-picking passes or
  repeated machine passes.
- **Climate:** Temperate (northern highbush); warm-temperate to
  subtropical (southern highbush and rabbiteye).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Required, insect-pollinated (bumblebees and other
  sonicating bees most efficient, honeybees also widely used, solitary
  bees contributing); some cultivars are self-unfruitful, and cross-
  pollination generally raises both fruit set and berry size.
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield sits in the mid
  thousands of kg/ha, with wide regional variation; one South American
  producing country reported the world's highest national average
  yield in a recent year (see Estimates below).
- **Propagation:** Entirely vegetative — hardwood cuttings from dormant
  one-year shoots (the standard method), with softwood cuttings used
  for cultivars that root poorly from hardwood material; true seed is
  used only in breeding programs, since seedling plants do not come
  true to type.
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve for
  berry bushes applies broadly, though an extension-guide rooting depth
  is notably shallower than the standard reference's typical range for
  berry bushes generally — the shallower, crop-specific figure is used
  here. A documented supplemental irrigation rate applies especially
  from fruit expansion through the end of harvest; the plant is notably
  intolerant of both drought stress (reduced fruit size and reduced
  next-year bud set) and waterlogging (standing water for as little as
  24 hours after rain is a documented concern, alongside a water-mold
  root-rot risk).
- **Labor:** A detailed modern grower cost budget documents labor hours
  by individual operation (planting, pruning, spraying, irrigation,
  pest scouting, bird control, trellis work) and harvest cost per pound
  for both hand and machine systems.
- **Soil:** A narrow, distinctly acidic pH target considerably lower
  than most fruit crops; porous sandy or sandy-loam soil with
  substantial organic matter and excellent drainage is required, since
  roots cannot penetrate compacted soil.
- **Stand life:** Bushes mature over five to seven years to full
  production; at least one detailed modern grower budget assumes a
  total field life of roughly 25 years including establishment.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal disease causing fruit
  mummification, a fungal stem-blight disease, a water-mold root rot,
  anthracnose, and several viruses are documented; bird predation is a
  significant enough loss driver that at least one modern grower budget
  includes active bird-control measures (noise deterrents and alarm
  systems).
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named cultivars across the northern highbush,
  southern highbush, and rabbiteye groups differ in chill requirement,
  cold hardiness, ripening season and concentration (affecting
  suitability for machine harvest), yield, fruit size and flavor,
  storage life, and susceptibility to cracking, overbearing, or soil-pH
  sensitivity.

## Hazards

No dietary toxin, drug interaction, or unusual food-safety hazard was
found in the sources consulted beyond ordinary postharvest disorders
and decay organisms (see Agronomic data above). A documented quarantine
restriction exists in some regions limiting unfumigated shipment of
fruit due to a specific fruit-fly pest, with a particular fumigant
treatment historically the only approved method in at least one
jurisdiction.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Hand-picked fruit → forced-air cooled, graded, packed into
  retail containers → cold storage just above freezing → fresh market.
  Machine-harvested fruit and culls → jam, sauce, and pie filling.
- **Propagation chain:** Hardwood cuttings (from dormant pruned wood) →
  rooted nursery plants.
- Not covered by this research in detail: industrial freezing (a
  substantial share of at least one major growing region's crop is
  processed rather than sold fresh), juice and wine production, and
  dried or infused berry products.

## Open research questions

- Rooting depth conflicts between the standard international crop-
  coefficient reference's typical range for berry bushes generally and
  a crop-specific extension guide's considerably shallower figure; the
  shallower, crop-specific figure is used here as more directly
  applicable.
- Salinity-tolerance thresholds are available only at the search-
  snippet level, without a standard international reference entry for
  this crop specifically.
- Several cultivars within each group (additional rabbiteye and
  southern highbush cultivars, and low-chill evergreen types grown in
  some South American and Central American production regions) were
  not covered by the cultivar tables consulted.
- Detailed labor-hour figures for post-harvest handling (cooling,
  grading, packing) were not available in the main grower-budget
  source and are estimated rather than sourced.
- Water-use stage lengths are estimated rather than drawn from a
  dedicated crop-specific field study; a lysimeter-based crop-water-use
  study for this crop would replace these estimates.
- A hand-harvest labor-hour conversion assumes picker piece-rate
  earnings are roughly equivalent to the grower budget's general wage
  rate — an unverified assumption.

## Estimates and derivations

A growth-cycle length to first harvest is derived from a detailed
modern grower budget's documented planting timing and first commercial
crop in year two, with a longer timeline (to year three) noted from a
separate regional extension guide for slower-establishing sites.

A world-average yield figure is derived from a recent global production
total divided by total harvested area (from trade-press reporting of
official international statistics); good and exceptional tiers are
derived respectively from a detailed modern grower budget's documented
full-production yield and a documented upper reported yield figure from
a regional extension guide; a poor tier is estimated from the low end
of a documented regional yield range, cross-checked against the same
grower budget's early-production-year yield figure.

Water-use stage lengths and a seasonal water-use-days index are
estimated from a documented fruit-ripening timeline (days from petal
fall to harvest) combined with the standard international berry-bush
crop-coefficient curve; the resulting estimated seasonal water total is
cross-checked against a documented weekly supplemental-irrigation rate
over the fruiting period and found broadly consistent in scale.

Drought and waterlogging tolerance labels follow directly from the
crop's documented root biology (shallow, hairless roots sensitive to
soil-moisture change, and documented sensitivity to standing water and
root rot); associated drought-buffer figures are placeholder estimates
pending a dedicated deficit-irrigation study for this crop.

A planting-density figure per hectare is derived by unit-converting a
documented commercial spacing figure from a detailed modern grower
budget.

Detailed labor-hour figures across establishment, cultural work,
harvesting, and post-harvest handling are derived primarily from a
single detailed modern grower budget's per-acre hour and cost figures;
hand-harvest hours specifically are derived by converting the budget's
documented piece-rate harvest cost per pound into an hours-per-hectare
figure using the budget's general hourly wage rate, an assumption
flagged as unverified; post-harvest handling hours are an estimate,
since the source budget did not itemize this operation separately.

A peak-yield-per-day figure is derived by dividing the good yield tier
by a documented typical harvest-season length in days (toward the
shorter end of a documented two-to-five-week range); maximum post-
harvest standing time and quality-decay rate are both estimated
placeholders, informed by a documented note that certain cultivars hold
their ripe fruit on the bush notably better than others.
