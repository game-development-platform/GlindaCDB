## Naming and scope

The coconut palm, *Cocos nucifera* (Arecaceae), the only living species
of its genus [1]. Two genetic subpopulations exist across its range —
one centered on island Southeast Asia and the Pacific, and one centered
on southern India [1]. A wild-type fruit form is thick-husked, angular,
and buoyant (adapted for ocean dispersal), while the domesticated fruit
form is thin-husked, rounder, and carries more meat and water [1]. Tall
and Dwarf botanical varieties (cross-pollinating and self-pollinating
respectively) and their hybrids are treated here as cultivar groups
within a single crop entry, since this is a single-species genus and all
of its several distinct products (nuts, water, fermented sap, fiber) come
from harvest-stage and processing choices on the same palm rather than
from genuinely different crops.

This crop shares general tropical-perennial growing biology (year-round
bearing, leaf and fiber byproducts, labor-intensive hand harvest) with
several other tropical tree-fruit crops this project has also
researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one palm, three harvest stages, and effectively three
separate economies.** A fruit bunch can be taken at three distinct
stages, and the choice of stage fundamentally changes the product: a
"tender" stage (roughly 6-9 months after flowering) is harvested for
coconut water; a "mature" stage (roughly 11-13 months) is harvested for
meat, copra, oil, and fiber; or, alternatively, the unopened flower
spadix itself can be tapped for sweet sap (used fresh, fermented into
toddy, boiled into coconut sugar, or distilled into a local spirit) — a
tapped spadix produces no nuts at all [1][8]. Mature palms flower
continuously year-round, producing up to about 15 flower clusters a
year, and bunches are typically harvested every 30-45 days, roughly
eight times annually [1][4].

**Finding 2 — well-quantified copra-processing ratios.** A documented
industry conversion figure states that 1,000 nuts yield roughly 180 kg
of dried coconut meat (copra), 110 kg of extracted oil, and 55 kg of
copra meal (a byproduct used as animal feed) [11]. Copra is dried down
to about 5-6% moisture, either by sun-drying (about 8 days), solar
dryers (3-4 days), or kiln drying (24-36 hours, in kilns handling
hundreds to thousands of nuts at a time) [4]. Well-dried copra yields
roughly 63-71% oil by weight; poorly dried copra is vulnerable to a
specific mold and its associated toxin [4][10]. Properly dried copra
keeps for up to six months in treated storage bags, after which insect
losses rise sharply; alternatively, whole unhusked nuts can be stored
10-12 months until the kernel loosens naturally inside the shell, a
traditional storage method in its own right [4].

**Finding 3 — slow to bear, very long-lived, strictly tropical, and
unusually salt-tolerant.** Tall-variety palms first bear fruit at 6-10
years and can live 60-100 years; Dwarf-variety palms bear sooner but
have shorter total lifespans [1]. Per-palm annual yield figures vary
considerably by type (roughly 80-100 nuts for Tall varieties, 70-80 for
Dwarf, and 100-130 for hybrid crosses), though one general reference
source notes that "fewer than 30 is more typical" in less intensive
conditions — a real tension between best-practice and typical-practice
figures [1][4]. Site requirements are specific: year-round warmth,
substantial annual rainfall (roughly 1,500-2,500 mm) with humidity above
60%, elevation from sea level to about 600 m, tolerance of at most a
single month of dry season on sandy soil (or up to three months on
heavier soil), a fairly wide pH tolerance, and — notably — a very high
salt tolerance, to the point that the species can be irrigated with
seawater; it is intolerant of both cold and shade [1].

**Finding 4 — sap tapping for toddy and sugar is a well-documented
parallel production system.** Sap begins flowing roughly 12-14 days
after the flower spadix is specially prepared for tapping, peaking some
2-5 weeks later [8]. Daily sap yield per palm scales with tapping
frequency: roughly 0.6-1.2 liters with one daily tapping, 0.6-3.0 liters
with two, and 1.1-4.7 liters with three [8]. Over a full tapping season
this totals somewhere around 200-400 liters per palm per year, depending
on source and region [1][8]. Fresh sap ferments into toddy within hours
if left untreated [1]. When boiled down instead, sap yields coconut
sugar; one regional study of Dwarf-variety palms found daily sugar
output in proportion to daily sap volume, and one major producing
country makes on the order of 300,000 tonnes of coconut sugar annually
[9].

**Finding 5 — harvest remains a dangerous, climbing-dependent,
labor-constrained task.** Most nuts worldwide are still harvested by
human climbers: a documented productivity figure from one major growing
region gives roughly 75 palms a day climbed with a simple rope, versus
roughly 150 a day using a mechanical climbing aid [12]. Other harvest
methods documented regionally include bladed poles worked from the
ground, trained monkeys (in parts of Thailand and Malaysia), and simply
gathering nuts that have fallen naturally (documented in Papua New
Guinea) [1]. Climbing is a genuinely dangerous occupation, and at least
one major growing region faces a chronic, worsening shortage of workers
willing to do it [1][12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A single-stemmed palm reaching roughly 30 m;
  evergreen, flowering continuously year-round with no dormant season.
- **Climate:** Strictly tropical, humid lowlands up to about 600 m
  elevation; intolerant of cold and shade (Finding 3).
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible. A detailed composition
  source gives roughly 3,500 kcal/kg and 33 g protein/kg for raw
  coconut meat specifically (not the whole nut including husk and
  shell).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not directly confirmed day-neutral by a
  species-specific induction study; the documented year-round
  continuous flowering pattern strongly implies day-neutral induction,
  but one Sri Lankan study found photoperiod instead governs the
  *speed* of spadix primordia development (faster during long-day
  months) rather than flower induction itself, and separate studies
  attribute seasonal yield bunching to temperature-driven fruit-set and
  fruit-abortion effects rather than to photoperiod-controlled
  flowering [15]. Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing — a palm with
  no storage organ.
- **Pollination:** Not strictly required at plantation scale — Dwarf
  varieties are self-pollinating, and Tall-variety plantations commonly
  set fruit without any managed pollination system; insect and wind
  pollination are both presumed to play a role, though this was not
  directly researched.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 6,000 kg/ha of dehusked
  nuts) is derived from a major producing country's documented
  per-hectare nut count and an estimated per-nut dehusked weight; good,
  exceptional, and poor tiers are derived similarly from documented
  per-palm nut-count figures at different planting densities (see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Adopted from a standard international crop-
  evapotranspiration reference's figures for palm trees generally
  (near-constant, high crop coefficient year-round, consistent with
  continuous evergreen growth); documented site requirements specify a
  maximum dry-season length that varies by soil type, and sap yield is
  known to fall sharply during drought.
- **Propagation:** Entirely by seed nut raised in nursery beds; the
  species has a single growing point and no sourced method of clonal or
  grafted propagation. Tall-variety seed must be sown within a few
  months of harvest; Dwarf and hybrid seed nuts are sown much sooner
  (within about two weeks). Nursery seedlings are typically
  transplanted at around 9-12 months of age.
- **Soil:** A notably wide pH tolerance (roughly 4.5-8, best around
  5.5-7); very high salt tolerance, including tolerance of seawater
  irrigation — a genuinely unusual trait among crops this project has
  researched.
- **Labor:** Harvest is dominated by manual climbing, a documented
  dangerous and increasingly labor-constrained task in at least one
  major growing region; other labor components (land preparation,
  weeding, post-harvest dehusking and copra-making) are rough estimates,
  since no comprehensive cost study covering every operation was found.
- **Soil nutrients:** Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake per
  tonne of yield are derived from a documented regional per-palm
  fertilizer-application guideline rather than directly measured crop
  removal (see Estimates below).
- **Storage:** Mature nuts in the husk keep roughly 3-5 months at
  ambient shaded conditions; dehusked mature nuts keep about two months
  refrigerated; tender (immature) nuts keep only three to four weeks
  even refrigerated. A sudden temperature change of about 8°C or more
  can crack stored nuts.
- **Stand life:** Tall-variety palms first bear at 6-10 years and live
  60-100 years; Dwarf varieties bear sooner but have shorter total
  lifespans (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A lethal insect-vectored phytoplasma disease,
  a mite causing yield losses up to 60% in affected plantings, a leaf
  beetle, and armyworms.
- **Cultivars:** Tall (outcrossing, longer-lived, more nuts per palm at
  maturity), Dwarf (self-pollinating, earlier-bearing, shorter-lived,
  often preferred for tender-nut/water production), and Tall × Dwarf
  hybrids (combining higher nut counts with somewhat earlier bearing);
  the wild-type and domesticated fruit forms are also recognized as a
  further cultivar-level distinction.
- **Harvest stages:** Three distinct harvest stages from the same
  palm — sap (tapped from an unopened flower spadix, which then bears
  no nuts), tender (harvested 6-9 months after flowering, for coconut
  water), and mature (harvested 11-13 months after flowering, for meat,
  copra, oil, and fiber products).
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable as a hydroponic or
  enclosed crop, given its need for full sun and very large mature
  size.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a lethal insect-vectored
phytoplasma disease, a mite causing severe yield losses where present,
a leaf beetle, and armyworms.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — harvest by climbing carries genuine occupational
fall risk, and falling nuts themselves are also a physical hazard;
poorly dried copra is vulnerable to a specific mold and its associated
toxin, a food-safety concern for both human consumption and use as
animal feed; a traditional distilled coconut-sap spirit carries the
same general methanol-related processing-residue considerations as
other traditional fruit spirits; and coconut oil's high saturated-fat
content is noted by health authorities as a dietary consideration.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** tender nuts → shaped and treated with a mild preservative
  dip, or opened directly for coconut water; mature nuts → dehusked →
  split → dried into copra → pressed into oil and copra meal (animal
  feed byproduct); alternatively, mature nuts stored whole and unhusked
  for 10-12 months as "ball copra" until the kernel loosens; fresh meat
  → coconut milk or desiccated coconut; husks → fiber and pith products;
  shells → charcoal; flower spadix → tapped for sap → used fresh, boiled
  into sugar the same day, fermented into toddy, or further distilled
  into a traditional spirit; fronds → thatch and weaving material; seed
  nuts → nursery seedlings.
- Not covered in detail by this research: coconut vinegar and a
  fermented coconut-water food product; heart-of-palm harvest; canned
  coconut milk/cream and frozen grated coconut in detail; virgin versus
  refined oil processing detail; coconut flour.

A long-standing Hindu ritual tradition treats the whole mature nut as an
offering, ritually broken before a deity as a symbolic act, and the nut
is a required element at many weddings and festival observances across
its range; a separate, broad Austronesian cultural tradition across the
Pacific and parts of Southeast Asia treats the palm as central to
mythology and oral tradition, sometimes described as a "tree of life."

## Open research questions

- Per-palm nut-count figures conflict notably between a detailed
  regional agricultural-extension source (80-100 nuts/palm for Tall
  varieties) and a general reference source's lead summary ("fewer than
  30 is more typical") — likely reflecting a real gap between
  best-practice and typical smallholder conditions rather than a
  genuine contradiction, but not reconciled here.
- Oil yield per hectare likewise conflicts between a detailed regional
  source's figure and a lower figure derived independently from a major
  producing country's average nut count — not reconciled here.
- A detailed cost study covering non-harvest labor operations (land
  preparation, weeding, copra-making) was not found; those labor-hour
  figures here are rough estimates.
- Soil nutrient uptake per tonne of yield is derived from a documented
  per-palm fertilizer-application guideline rather than a direct
  crop-removal study, and should be treated as an approximation.
- Fiber and charcoal product yields, detailed coconut-milk and
  desiccated-coconut conversion ratios, and smallholder labor practice
  in several major producing countries were not found in the sources
  reviewed.
- Hybrid-seed production practice (parent-line selection, assisted
  pollination method, seed-garden design) was not researched in depth.
- World-average nut-count-to-yield conversion rests on an estimated
  per-nut dehusked weight rather than a directly measured figure for
  every producing region.
- No species-specific study directly confirms coconut flower induction
  as day-neutral/photoperiod-insensitive; this is currently an
  inference from the documented continuous year-round flowering
  pattern. No cross-latitude comparison study was found either — the
  sources reviewed all covered near-equatorial growing regions.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers are derived from documented per-palm nut-count figures at
different planting densities (for example, 175 palms/ha at a standard
spacing combined with 100-150 nuts/palm for good and exceptional tiers,
and a lower per-palm count at a different documented regional spacing
for the poor tier), each multiplied by an estimated per-nut dehusked
weight derived from a documented weight-per-count figure in a
postharvest handling reference.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index,
drought-buffer days) are derived from a standard international crop-
evapotranspiration reference's year-round "palm trees" crop-coefficient
figures and documented rooting-depth and soil-moisture-depletion
allowances; drought tolerance is judged moderate (reflecting the
documented one-to-three-month dry-season tolerance depending on soil
type) and waterlogging tolerance is estimated as moderate by analogy to
other tropical coastal palms, since no direct flooding-tolerance study
was found.

Growth-stage curve and photosynthesis-related parameters are estimated
by analogy to other evergreen tropical tree-fruit crops this project has
researched, since no species-specific canopy-photosynthesis study was
found; the curve is shaped to reflect the multi-year juvenile period
before first bearing.

Planting density for hybrid plantings (roughly 138 palms/ha) is derived
directly from a documented standard hybrid spacing guideline.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest are derived directly from documented
regional climbing-productivity figures (palms climbed per day by rope
versus by mechanical climbing aid) applied to a standard planting
density and harvest frequency; other labor operations (land preparation,
weeding, copra-making) are rough estimates, since no comprehensive
regional cost study covering every operation was found.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake per tonne of yield are
derived from a documented regional per-palm annual fertilizer-
application guideline, converted to a per-hectare and then per-tonne
basis using the good-yield-tier figure; this is an application-rate-
based approximation rather than a direct measured crop-removal study.

Storage duration (about 120 days) is derived as roughly the midpoint of
a documented 3-5 month ambient in-husk storage range for mature nuts.

Time to first harvest (roughly 2,555 days, about 7 years) and the
multi-year ramp to full bearing are derived from the documented 6-10
year bearing-age range for Tall-variety palms, taking a representative
midpoint and assuming several further years to reach full plateau
production.
