## Naming and scope

*Mangifera odorata* Griff. (Anacardiaceae); 2n = 40 [1]. Synonyms: *M.
foetida* var. *bombom*, var. *kawini* and var. *mollis* Blume; *M.
oblongifolia* Hook.f.; *M. foetida* var. *odorata* (Griff.) Pierre [1].
- **Names:** kuwini (English) [1]; kwini, kuini, kuweni, huani, Saipan
  mango [3]; manguier odorant (French) [1].
  - **Indonesia:** bembem, kaweni (Sundanese); kuweni or kweni (Malay,
    Javanese) [1].
  - **Malaysia:** kuini; huani or wani (Sabah) [1].
  - **Philippines:** huani, uani (Cebu, Bisaya), juani (Jolo) [1].
  - **Thailand:** kinning (Narathiwat); mamuang chingrit or mamuang pa
    (central) [1].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Wani"** is a Sabah name for kuini [1], and also the name of the
    sweet form of binjai (`binjai.md`) [10].
  - **"Machang"** is the timber trade name used for kuini wood as well as
    for bachang and binjai [1][10].
- **Range:** never found in the wild; its origin is unknown [1].
  - **Main areas:** commonly cultivated in Borneo, Sumatra and Java [1].
    In Borneo it is mostly near coastal towns and along travel routes,
    which suggests a fairly recent arrival [1].
  - **Elsewhere:** peninsular Thailand, South Sulawesi, and the south
    coast of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago [1]. Occasionally grown in
    southern Vietnam, on Christmas Island and Guam [1], and in the
    Mariana Islands [3].
  - **Malaysia:** one of four *Mangifera* species grown in all 11 states
    surveyed [6].
- **Existing group doc:** `wet-tropics-mangoes.md` (row
  `wet_tropics_mangoes`, `OUT-OF-DATE`) covers this species with
  bachang, binjai and bambangan [10]. This doc supersedes its kuini
  material. All four species now have their own docs.

**Gate check (2026-11-23): passes.** The row was proposed from
`mango.md` [10]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** an accepted species of hybrid origin. AFLP markers show
  it inherits bands from both mango and bachang, and that it is closer to
  bachang (Finding 1) [4]. It is neither a mango cultivar nor a form of
  bachang.
- **Range:** a cultivated tree of Indonesia, Malaysia, the southern
  Philippines and peninsular Thailand [1][6]. Its centre lies in wetter
  places than mango's.
- **Farming system:** a home-garden and village-orchard tree that
  stands in for mango where the climate is too wet (Finding 2) [1].
  Some West Sumatran villages specialize in it [1].
- **Harvest:** two crops a year where there are two dry seasons. The
  main West Java season is August-November [1].
- **Uses:** a table fruit that must be peeled thickly, plus chutney and
  salt pickles. Its seed kernel is made into a flour for Javanese
  confections (Finding 3) [1].

**Relatives in this project:**
- mango (`mango`, `mango.md`): a proposed parent [1][4].
- bachang (`bachang`, `bachang.md`): the other proposed parent, and the
  closer relative [4].
- bambangan (`bambangan`, `bambangan.md`) and binjai (`binjai`,
  `binjai.md`): the same wet-tropics group [10].
- wet-tropics mangoes (`wet_tropics_mangoes`, `wet-tropics-mangoes.md`):
  the older group doc [10].
- kasturi (`kasturi`, `kasturi.md`): another cultivated *Mangifera* of
  hybrid origin, with mango as a parent [10].

**Proposed:** none new.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a mango × bachang hybrid, closer to bachang.**
- **Evidence:** kuini's intermediate form had led to the suggestion that
  it is a hybrid of *M. indica* and *M. foetida* [4].
  - **Markers:** in AFLP profiles, kuini had no unique bands. Every
    sample carried bands specific to both mango and bachang [4].
  - **Backcrossing:** kuini clustered closer to bachang, so it may have
    backcrossed with it [4].
- **Genomes:** a Philippine whole-genome study of huani found more
  variants against the mango reference genomes than for *M. altissima*
  or 'Carabao' mango [7].
- **Seed:** often polyembryonic [1]. In mango, such seed carries
  vegetative embryos that reproduce the parent, so it comes true from
  seed (`mango.md`) [10]. Whether kuini seedlings behave the same way is
  not sourced.

**Finding 2 — a mango substitute for climates too wet for mango.**
- **Role:** a popular fruit with local economic importance "in areas
  where *Mangifera indica* L. cannot be grown satisfactorily because the
  climate is very wet" [1].
- **Climate:** below 1,000 m, with fairly heavy rain spread through the
  year. It also grows with moderate rain (1,200 mm) if there are no long
  dry spells, as on the dry western islands of the Sulu Archipelago [1].
- **Farming:** mostly mixed with other trees in home gardens and village
  orchards, spaced 12-14 m apart [1].
- **Specialized villages:** near Solok, West Sumatra, it is the dominant
  fruit tree. Vegetables or bananas grow under the light shade of old
  trees [1].
- **Statistics:** kuini is not reported separately in Malaysia's 2023
  agriculture census [9]. No area or production figures were found.

**Finding 3 — thick-peeled table fruit, pickles and kernel flour.**
- **Fresh:** the less fibrous, milder forms are prized as table fruit
  [1]. The skin holds an acrid juice, so the fruit is peeled thickly, or
  steeped in diluted limewater before eating [1].
- **Young fruit:** made into chutney and pickled with salt [1].
- **Seed kernels:** in Java, ground into flour for *dodol* (a
  glutinous-rice confection) and *jenang pelok* (a thick preparation
  made from *Curcuma* rhizomes) [1].
- **Kernel composition:** a Malaysian study of market fruit found the
  seed kernel richer in fat, protein, carbohydrate and ash than the peel.
  The peel was richer in fibre, minerals, β-carotene and ascorbic acid
  [5]. Peel and kernel extracts inhibited α-amylase and α-glucosidase in
  vitro [5].

**Finding 4 — named forms that differ mainly in turpentine taste.**
- **West Java** [1]:
  - **"bembem":** an inferior form with a strong turpentine smell and
    taste, like bachang;
  - **"kaweni":** less fibrous, with a mild turpentine taste; "the best
    forms are very palatable";
  - **"gandarassa":** rare, from Banten; said to be better than kaweni,
    less sweet but juicier and less strongly scented.
- **Philippines:** "sangay" from Jolo ripens yellow, against the greenish
  "huani" [1].
- **Collections:** kuini is in most fruit-tree collections in the region.
  However, the collections do not hold its full range of forms [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** medium-sized tree, 10-15 m, rarely
  over 20 m. The crown is round or broadly ovoid, and the bole is up to
  80 cm across [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial. Two crops a year where there are two
  dry seasons [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** humid tropical lowlands below 1,000 m (Finding 2)
  [1]. Chill requirement: Not sourced (tropical).
- **Composition (per 100 g edible portion)** [1]:
  - **Proximates:** water 80 g, protein 0.9 g, fat 0.1 g, carbohydrate
    including fibre 18.5 g, ash 0.6 g.
  - **Vitamins:** β-carotene equivalent 0.36 mg, thiamine 0.04 mg,
    riboflavin 0.06 mg, niacin 0.7 mg, vitamin C 13 mg.
  - **Energy:** 290 kJ.
  - **Edible share:** about 70% of the fruit [1].
  - **By-products:** fresh peel has 75.9% moisture and 94.5 kcal per
    100 g; seed kernel has 50.0% moisture and 208.5 kcal per 100 g [5].
  - **Ranking:** a five-species comparison ranked *M. odorata* pulp
    second for nutritional quality, after kasturi [8].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible when ripe, peeled thickly (Finding 3) [1].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced as pollinator. Fragrant 5(-6)-merous
  flowers with one fertile stamen, in pyramidal panicles 15-50 cm long
  [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced.
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Grows with about 1,200 mm of rain if there
  are no long dry spells [1].
- **Propagation:** usually from seed (often polyembryonic); grafted only
  rarely [1].
  - **Marcotting:** possible but difficult [1].
  - **Budding on mango:** it succeeds, but when mango is the scion,
    incompatibility shows after two years [1].
  - **Clonal risk:** Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced. Salinity: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** 12-14 m planting distance [1].
- **Cultivars:** the bembem, kaweni and gandarassa forms of West Java,
  and the sangay and huani forms of the Philippines (Finding 4) [1].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced, beyond the two-crop pattern under
  two dry seasons [1].
- **Water regime:** rain-fed [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Young fruit:** for chutney and pickles [1].
  - **Ripe fruit:** for eating fresh [1].
  - **West Java:** main season August-November [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** no invasive listing found in this
  pass. IUCN status: data deficient [3].
  - **Pests:** mango weevil larvae (*Cryptorrhynchus gravis*) feed on the
    flesh and sometimes the seed. Caterpillars of *Philotroctis
    eutraphera* and *Noorda albizonalis* bore into the fruit [1].
  - **Other hosts:** Wikipedia also lists oriental fruit fly (*Bactrocera
    dorsalis*), termites (*Coptotermes*) and two fungi (*Marasmiellus
    scandens*, *Marasmius crinis-equi*) [3].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Acrid skin juice:** the fruit must be peeled thickly or steeped in
  limewater (Finding 3) [1].
- **Bark sap:** the bark "contains irritant sap" [1][2].
- **Food safety:** none sourced.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for kuini.
- **Folk medicine:** the bark is recommended for external use against
  "hystero-epilepsy", in a cosmetic-like mixture [1]. This is a recorded
  folk use, not a tested treatment.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the acrid skin juice and irritant bark sap are
  handling hazards. The project's hazard model has no field for crop
  handling hazards yet (the same gap as bachang, bambangan and binjai).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** garden tree → ripe fruit → peeled thickly, or
  steeped in diluted limewater → eaten fresh [1].
- **Chain (young fruit):** young fruit → chutney, or pickled with salt
  [1].
- **Chain (kernel flour, Java):** stone → seed kernel → flour → *dodol*
  or *jenang pelok* [1].
- **Chain (by-products):** peel and seed kernel → dried → powder,
  studied as a functional food ingredient [5].
- **Timber:** used locally as machang, but reportedly of poor quality
  [1]. Similar to mango wood [1].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Energy, about 69 kcal per 100 g.** 290 kJ [1] / 4.184 = 69.3 kcal.
Atwater check: protein 0.9 × 4 + fat 0.1 × 9 + carbohydrate 18.5 × 4 =
3.6 + 0.9 + 74.0 = 78.5 kcal. The difference is fibre counted in the
carbohydrate.

**Fruit weight, about 190-550 g; edible flesh about 130-390 g.** Treating
the fruit as an ellipsoid of density 1 g/cm³, using the 10-13 × 6-9 cm
size [1]:
- **Small fruit:** π/6 × 10 × 6 × 6 = 188 g.
- **Large fruit:** π/6 × 13 × 9 × 9 = 551 g.
- **Edible flesh:** × 70% edible [1] = 132-386 g.
The fruit is oblique and "hardly flattened" [1], so the ellipsoid is a
fair shape. A measured fruit weight would replace this.

**Trees per hectare, about 51-69.** 10,000 m² / (14 m × 14 m) = 51;
10,000 / (12 × 12) = 69 [1].
