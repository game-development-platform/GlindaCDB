# Garden Strawberry (*Fragaria × ananassa*) and Woodland Strawberry (*Fragaria vesca*, with musk strawberry *F. moschata*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-10

## Naming and scope

This entry covers three species, treated as two distinct crop profiles
within one document, since they share enough underlying biology
(postharvest handling, a shared frost-at-bloom critical-temperature table,
and shared water-use data) to document together while differing enough in
products, propagation, yield, and cultivars to warrant separate profiles.

- **Garden strawberry**: *Fragaria × ananassa*, an octoploid hybrid
  between a North American and a South American wild strawberry species,
  which arose in 18th-century France, with its hybrid origin formally
  identified several decades later [1]. Its cultivars fall into three
  flowering-habit groups: short-day (June-bearing), everbearing, and
  day-neutral [1][4].
- **Woodland strawberry**: *F. vesca*, diploid, wild across the Northern
  Hemisphere, cultivated mostly as seed-grown alpine (ever-fruiting,
  runnerless) forms [7].
- **Musk strawberry**: *F. moschata*, usually hexaploid, documented here
  as a species group within the woodland profile [8].

Only plant residue/compost handling is shared across the two profiles;
fruit, planting material, and finished products differ enough between
garden and woodland/musk strawberries to be documented separately within
this file.

This entry draws on this project's prior research into other soft fruits
it has studied (for comparable hand-harvest, rapid-cooling, and fresh-
versus-processing split patterns) and into a warm-season plasticulture
melon crop it has also researched (for comparable annual-system,
cost-study-sourced labor patterns), without naming those specific crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the flowering habit decides the harvest pattern.**
Short-day (June-bearing) cultivars form flower buds when day length falls
below a cultivar-dependent threshold in the 12-15 hour range; moderate
temperatures favor this initiation, while heat above roughly 24 °C
prevents it and temperatures below about 9 °C sharply reduce it [10].
These cultivars give one concentrated 3-6 week harvest the year after
planting, with regional timing varying by several weeks across different
temperate growing regions [4][11]. Everbearing cultivars give a crop in
the planting year itself, followed by two smaller crops the next year, in
late spring and early autumn [4]. Day-neutral cultivars flower regardless
of day length [1], and are picked every 1-3 days across a long season
that varies by region from early-to-mid summer through autumn in colder
climates, or from spring through early autumn in milder coastal climates
[6][11][3]. Day-neutral flowering is itself stopped by heat: flower
formation is inhibited at daytime temperatures around 30 °C and nighttime
temperatures around 26 °C, with runners forming instead of flowers [10];
general vegetative growth for the species occurs across a documented
temperature range of roughly 7-29 °C [6].

**Finding 2 — one of the most perishable fruits grown, requiring cooling
within the hour.** This fruit does not continue to gain sugar after
harvest, so it must be picked fully ripe, with documented minimum
ripeness-color standards varying slightly between grading systems [2].
Cooling must begin within an hour of harvest [2]. Standard cold storage
is near 0 °C at high humidity for up to about a week; the fruit is not
chilling-sensitive and does not respond to ethylene treatment [2]. Gray
mold is the dominant postharvest loss, continuing to grow slowly even at
0 °C, while a separate fungal rot does not grow below about 5 °C [2]. No
postharvest fungicides are used commercially; shipping instead relies on
elevated-CO2 pallet covers to suppress decay, though CO2 levels above
roughly 15% cause the fruit's skin to discolor and develop off-flavors
[2]. Pickers grade and pack fruit directly in the field [2][3].

**Finding 3 — an intensive annual plasticulture system achieves very
high yields at a correspondingly high labor and capital cost.** A
detailed regional cost-study from a major U.S. coastal growing region
documents a dense double-row planting on fumigated, plastic-mulched,
drip-irrigated beds, with a total seasonal water application in the
range of 700 mm [3]. Field labor in that system includes substantial
hand-weeding and a notably large hand runner-removal labor input [3].
Harvest runs from spring through early autumn with two clear monthly
peaks, hand-picked at a documented rate of several trays per person-hour
by piece-rate crews; documented yields in this system range widely, with
the cost study's own assumption converting to roughly 80 t/ha of fruit,
and total cash production costs running into six figures per acre [3].
Separate national statistics show a wide regional yield gap between two
major U.S. producing states, with the large majority of national
production sold fresh rather than processed [5].

**Finding 4 — the traditional cold-climate "matted row" perennial
system.** In colder growing regions, spring-planted short-day cultivars
have their first-year flowers deliberately removed so the plants instead
fill the growing rows with runners [1][4]. Straw mulch is applied after
several hard autumn freezes, since plants are otherwise injured at or
below a specific sub-freezing threshold [4][6]. After each harvest, the
bed undergoes a renovation process: mowing the foliage back hard,
narrowing the rows, thinning the remaining plants, and fertilizing [4].
Yields decline after the second and third harvest years, so beds are
typically replanted after three to four growing seasons; documented
matted-row yields run somewhat lower than documented day-neutral system
yields [11].

**Finding 5 — a detailed frost-kill threshold table by bloom stage, with
some source disagreement.** Documented critical temperatures vary by
flower-development stage: tightly closed buds tolerate the coldest
temperatures (roughly −5.6 °C), a slightly more advanced bud stage
tolerates somewhat less cold, fully open blossoms tolerate only mild
frost (around −1 °C), and already-set green fruit sits at an
intermediate tolerance level [15]. Overhead sprinkler irrigation, run
continuously at a minimum application rate, can protect blossoms during
still radiation-frost nights but is considered risky during wind-driven
freezes [15]. In at least one region, straw kept in the row alleys is
specifically pulled over blossoms by hand when frost is forecast [4]; a
separate source notes that early-season straw placed between rows can
actually raise frost risk at bloom time by insulating the soil from the
air.

**Finding 6 — woodland and musk strawberries are a genuinely distinct
small-scale crop profile.** Wild woodland-strawberry fruit persists on
the plant for only slightly over a day, possibly the shortest fruit
persistence of any fleshy fruit native to its home region [7]. At least
one country harvests several hundred tonnes of wild fruit annually,
mainly for export [7][12]. Several named alpine (ever-fruiting,
runnerless) cultivars are grown from seed, rarely produce runners, fruit
over a long season, and lose vigor after only a few years [7][12]. Musk
strawberry specifically requires separate male plants (or a second
clone) for fruit to set, since its female and hermaphrodite plants are
not self-fertile; it is grown on a small scale in at least one European
country [8]. Documented uses across these species include jam, sauces,
liqueur (fruit macerated in spirits), and dried-leaf herbal tea [7][12].

## Agronomic data (garden strawberry, *Fragaria × ananassa*)

- **Life form / season:** Perennial crown-forming herb with runners,
  grown either as a short-lived perennial (matted row) or as a true
  annual (plasticulture systems).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single 3-6 week window (short-day/June-bearing) or
  continuous picking every 1-3 days across a long season (day-neutral),
  per Finding 1; harvested fully ripe in a single quality stage.
- **Climate:** Temperate and cool-temperate (matted-row systems);
  Mediterranean and subtropical (winter-annual plasticulture systems);
  tropical highlands are plausible but not researched in this pass.
- **Composition:** A modest calorie content and a relatively low protein
  content typical of soft fruit; these figures are estimated from well-
  known general food-composition values rather than independently sourced
  in this pass.
- **Pollination:** Not required — flowers are hermaphrodite and
  commercial fields are typically single-cultivar blocks, so fruit sets
  without a dedicated pollinizer; insect visitation likely improves fruit
  shape (since the fleshy receptacle's growth follows fertilization of
  the many small surface seeds) but this effect is not independently
  sourced here.
- **Yield:** A wide range from a world average (derived from global
  production-area statistics) up to considerably higher figures in
  intensive coastal plasticulture systems, and down to lower figures in
  traditional matted-row systems (see Estimates below for adopted tier
  values).
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve exists
  specifically for this species, mapped onto a documented coastal-system
  seasonal calendar; this is the single most water-stress-sensitive fruit
  crop in that standard reference's full crop list, reflecting the
  species' very shallow root system. Root-rot diseases are documented
  risks of waterlogged soils, though not quantified.
- **Propagation:** Vegetatively, by runner daughter plants (bare-root or
  plug transplants) from certified, virus-tested nursery mother plants;
  seed-propagated F1 hybrid lines are an emerging but still minor
  technology, bred from inbred parent lines self-pollinated over several
  generations, since vigor is lost after a few generations of further
  selfing. A specific chilling duration applied to nursery mother plants
  affects both the number and size of resulting daughter plants.
- **Labor:** Reasonably well documented for an intensive coastal
  plasticulture system via a detailed regional cost study (planting,
  hand-weeding, and especially runner-removal labor are all substantial);
  harvest labor is derived from a documented piece-rate picking speed
  rather than directly reported hours. Labor figures for traditional
  matted-row and other systems are estimated by scaling from this
  coastal-system baseline.
- **Soil:** A narrow near-neutral to mildly acidic pH preference; avoid
  fields recently planted to certain unrelated vegetable or ground crops,
  due to shared soil-disease risk. Soil nutrient-uptake figures are
  estimated by analogy to other soft fruit crops, since no dedicated
  nutrient-removal study was found.
- **Stand life:** A single season (annual plasticulture) or roughly three
  harvest seasons with renovation between them (matted row), with yields
  declining after the second and third harvest years in the latter
  system.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Gray mold is the dominant postharvest loss
  organism (Finding 2); two named root-rot diseases affect wet soils; a
  specific aphid-vectored virus is documented; runner-borne virus and
  pathogen transmission is a recognized risk requiring certified clean
  nursery stock.
- **Cultivars:** A substantial number of named cultivars spanning short-
  day, everbearing, and day-neutral groups, sourced from several regional
  university extension programs and a major coastal-growing-region
  research program; a specific low-allergen white-fruited cultivar group
  is documented (see Hazards below).
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked fully ripe, graded and packed directly in the
  field (Finding 2); harvest frequency and season length depend heavily
  on cultivar group (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Near 0 °C at high humidity for up to about a week, with
  cooling required to begin within an hour of harvest; ambient shelf life
  at room temperature is roughly a single day (Finding 2).

## Agronomic data (woodland / musk strawberry, *F. vesca* / *F. moschata*)

- **Life form / season:** Small perennial herb; alpine cultivated forms
  are clump-forming and runnerless, while wild forms spread by runners.
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous picking over a long season (alpine
  cultivated forms) or a shorter early-summer season (wild-gathered
  forms).
- **Climate:** Temperate and cool-temperate, native to woodland edges
  across much of the Northern Hemisphere, with one country's wild
  populations documented across a wide elevation range in its northern
  highlands.
- **Composition:** Estimated by analogy to garden strawberry (same genus
  and similarly shallow-rooted habit); fruit is documented at roughly
  84% water by weight, similar to garden strawberry.
- **Pollination:** Not required for woodland strawberry itself. Musk
  strawberry specifically requires separate male plants or a second
  clone, since its flowers are not self-fertile (Finding 6).
- **Yield:** No reliable per-area yield data were found for either
  species; documented figures instead describe output per plant (a
  handful of berries per plant per week for alpine cultivars) and
  national wild-harvest tonnage for one exporting country (see Estimates
  below for the rough per-hectare figures derived from these).
- **Water use:** Estimated by analogy to garden strawberry, since no
  species-specific data were found.
- **Propagation:** Alpine cultivated forms from seed or division; wild
  forms by runners and seed; musk strawberry by clonal propagation,
  always including separate male plants for pollination. A documented
  related natural hybrid between woodland and garden strawberry exists,
  though it was not researched in detail here.
- **Labor:** Picking the very small berries is understood to be slow per
  unit weight, though this was not independently sourced; estimated by
  analogy to garden strawberry.
- **Soil / site:** Trail and roadside edges, meadows, young woodland, and
  clearings; often found in sites too shaded to fruit well.
- **Stand life:** Alpine cultivated plantings lose vigor after only a
  few years.
- **Harvest / storage:** Fruit persists on the plant for only about a day
  (Finding 6); postharvest cold-storage behavior was not researched for
  this species, and ambient shelf life is essentially a single day.

## Hazards

- A specific allergenic protein associated with this fruit's red pigment
  is linked to oral allergy syndrome, hives, and in rare cases severe
  breathing difficulty in sensitized individuals. White-fruited cultivars
  lack this specific allergenic protein, and at least one named cultivar
  is documented as nearly allergen-free as a result. **TO BE ADDRESSED**
  — a fuller cross-referenced write-up of this allergen, shared with
  other fruits in the same broader allergy family this project has
  researched, is deferred until this project has a dedicated place for
  this kind of shared health/safety data.
- Commercial production relies on a regular fungicide, miticide, and
  insecticide spray program, plus pre-plant soil fumigation, raising
  ordinary agrichemical-residue and worker re-entry-interval
  considerations; this was not researched further in this pass.
- Fumigation with a specific regulated fumigant gas is used for export to
  certain markets with strict quarantine requirements targeting two named
  insect/mite pests.

## Uses and processing

- **Garden strawberry chains:** field-packed fruit → forced-air cooled to
  near 0 °C within an hour of harvest. Culls → pureed or made into jam.
  Fruit → dried. Certified virus-tested mother plants → runner daughter
  plants → transplants (bare-root, in matted rows, or as rooted plugs);
  alternatively, F1 hybrid seed → seedling plugs.
- **Woodland / musk strawberry chains:** alpine seed → plants → berries.
  Berries → jam, or liqueur (macerated in spirits). Leaves → dried herbal
  tea. Musk strawberry requires co-planted male plants for fruit set.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-berry and aseptic-puree
  processing; strawberry wine; detailed fumigation, plastic-mulch,
  tunnel, straw-mulch, and sprinkler frost-protection system engineering.

## Open research questions

- Bloom-stage frost-kill temperatures are reported somewhat differently
  across several regional extension sources (a roughly 2-5 °F spread for
  open blossoms and tight buds alike) — not resolved.
- World production-tonnage figures differ slightly between two sourced
  reference years and datasets — not resolved.
- Everbearing-cultivar yield shares between the spring and autumn crops
  were not found in any source consulted.
- Day-length and temperature thresholds governing flower initiation are
  reported only at snippet level and conflict somewhat between sources
  (a roughly 5-10 °C spread in some cases) — not resolved.
- Woodland-strawberry per-hectare yields have no direct source and are
  derived here from a rough per-plant-per-week estimate combined with an
  assumed planting density — among the weakest estimates in this entry.
- Non-harvest labor figures for garden strawberry, other than the
  directly sourced coastal plasticulture system, are estimates scaled
  from that one system rather than independently measured.
- Soil nutrient-uptake figures for garden strawberry were not found in
  any dedicated source and are estimated by analogy to other soft fruit.
- The current commercial trade share of F1 hybrid seed-propagated
  strawberries, and detailed seed-production methodology (pollination
  control, seed yield, isolation distance), were not researched in this
  pass.
- Whether woodland strawberry's wild populations harvested for export in
  one country are genuinely this species or a related wild species found
  regionally is not fully settled in the sources consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

Garden-strawberry pollination requirement (not required) follows from
documented hermaphrodite flower structure and standard single-cultivar
commercial field layout; a dedicated pollination study measuring bee
effects on fruit weight and shape would improve confidence in this
estimate.

Water-use stage lengths and total crop-coefficient-days are derived by
mapping a documented coastal-system seasonal calendar onto a standard
international crop-coefficient curve for this species; the resulting
total sits close to, but somewhat below, the directly measured seasonal
water application reported by that same coastal-system cost study,
providing a useful cross-check.

Drought-tolerance and days-without-water-before-penalty figures are
derived from the standard reference's total-available-water and
depletion-fraction values for this species' very shallow root zone,
combined with an estimated peak evapotranspiration rate; this species has
the most sensitive soil-water-depletion allowance of any fruit crop in
that standard international reference.

Garden-strawberry yield tiers are derived from: a world-average figure
calculated from recent global production and harvest-area statistics; a
"good" tier from a major producing U.S. state's average per-acre yield
statistic (converted to metric units); an "exceptional" tier from the
upper end of a detailed coastal cost study's documented yield range
(converted to metric units via a specific fruit-weight-per-pack-unit
figure); and a "poor" tier from a lower documented yield figure for
traditional matted-row cultivation.

Garden-strawberry labor-hour figures are derived primarily from a
detailed coastal plasticulture cost study's itemized operation hours,
converted to a per-hectare basis; harvest hours are derived by dividing
the cost study's assumed yield by a documented piece-rate picking speed,
since harvest labor in that system is contracted at a flat per-unit price
rather than reported in hours directly. Other tiers and systems are
estimated by scaling from this baseline, with a fairly wide uncertainty
range.

Growing-season-length and climate-zone estimates are derived from
documented planting-to-harvest intervals across the three different
production systems (day-neutral, annual plasticulture, and matted row),
which differ by roughly an order of magnitude in days depending on
system.

Woodland-strawberry yield and yield-profile figures are rough estimates
built from a documented per-plant weekly berry count, an assumed
individual berry weight (since berries are documented as very small but
no specific weight figure was found), and an assumed planting density;
this chain of assumptions makes these among the least certain estimates
in this entry, and would be improved by a dedicated alpine-strawberry
production trial.

Woodland-strawberry composition, water-use, and labor figures are
carried over by analogy from garden strawberry, given the shared genus
and similarly shallow rooting habit, adjusted qualitatively for slower
expected picking speed given the much smaller individual berry size.

Garden-strawberry calorie and protein content are estimated from well-
known general food-composition reference values for raw strawberries,
rather than independently reopened and verified in this research pass.

Day-neutral yield-profile peak daily harvest rate is derived by comparing
a traditional-system seasonal yield figure spread evenly across its
harvest season against a high-input coastal system's documented monthly
peak-harvest-share figure applied to that system's much higher total
seasonal yield; the higher of the two figures is adopted as representing
a high-input peak rate, with a note that a given simulation context
should scale this figure according to production-system intensity.
