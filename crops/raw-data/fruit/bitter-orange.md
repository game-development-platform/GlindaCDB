## Naming and scope

*Citrus* × *aurantium*, a natural hybrid of pomelo (*Citrus maxima*) and
wild mandarin (*Citrus reticulata*) — the same parentage pairing that
produced sweet orange, but a different, independently arisen hybrid [1].
Common names: sour orange, Seville orange, bigarade orange, marmalade
orange [1]. Native to Southeast Asia/South China, spread via trade routes
through India and Iran to the Islamic world by about 700 AD, and
separately into the Mediterranean by Arab traders by the 9th century;
reached Mexico by 1568 [1][2][5].

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Sweet orange** (`orange`, *Citrus* × *sinensis*), the other pomelo ×
  mandarin hybrid — already its own entry in this project, which notes
  this crop only in passing for comparison.
- **Bergamot** (*Citrus bergamia*), a related but distinct hybrid grown
  for its own oil — already its own entry in this project.
- **Chinotto** (possibly a distinct species, *Citrus myrtifolia*, treated
  by some sources as *C.* × *aurantium* var. *myrtifolia*), grown for the
  bittersweet Italian soft drink/aperitif of the same name [1]. Proposed
  as its own candidate row below — it has a materially different product
  (a soft drink, not peel/oil/rootstock) and disputed species status.
- **Laraha**, the landrace of this species that adapted to Curaçao's dry,
  salty, poor soil into a fruit too bitter and fibrous to eat at all,
  grown specifically as the dried-peel source for Curaçao liqueur [9].
  Proposed as its own candidate row below — a geographically isolated
  landrace with a dedicated commercial product and a genuinely different
  growing environment from mainland bitter orange.
- **Daidai** (*C.* × *aurantium* var. *daidai*), the Chinese/Japanese
  variety used in traditional medicine, tea, and as a decorative fruit at
  Japanese New Year [1]. Proposed as its own candidate row below — a
  named variety with its own distinct cultural/medicinal use pattern.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the fruit itself is largely not a food crop; the tree is grown
for its flowers, peel, and rootstock instead.** Unlike its sibling sweet
orange, bitter orange's ripe pulp is "very sour and bitter" [3] and the
Curaçao landrace's fruit is described as too bitter and fibrous to eat at
all [9]. Commercially, the species is valued for three largely separate
products harvested from different organs at different times of year —
spring blossom (for neroli oil and orange flower water), unripe fallen
fruit (for traditional Chinese medicine and unripe-peel essential oil),
and ripe fruit peel (for marmalade, candied peel, and liqueurs) — rather
than for bulk fruit yield the way most fruit crops in this project are
[1][7][8][11].

**Finding 2 — this species was once the dominant commercial citrus
rootstock worldwide, and a single virus largely ended that.** Sour
orange rootstock became dominant after growers in Spain found it
tolerant of Phytophthora foot rot, which was destroying seedling citrus
trees generally [4]. Citrus tristeza virus (CTV) kills the phloem below
the graft union of sweet orange, mandarin, and grapefruit budded onto
sour orange rootstock (lemon scions on the same rootstock are
unaffected) [4][18] (this project's own `orange.md` entry already
documents this from the scion side, Finding 4). Decline epidemics swept
South America in the 1930s and Spain in the 1980s, with several million
Spanish trees lost in the 1960s and a worldwide estimate of nearly 100
million trees lost on sour orange rootstock overall [4]. South Africa saw
the earliest recorded sign of the virus via sour-orange decline in the
1890s and switched to rough lemon rootstock [4]. Florida's decline came
later and more gradually — occasional events until the late 1970s, with
concern renewed after the brown citrus aphid (CTV's main vector) arrived
in 1995 — and sour orange was still the fifth most propagated rootstock
in Florida as of 2011, showing the decline was never total everywhere
[4]. Reviewers describe sour orange's horticultural qualities as a
rootstock as still unmatched by any replacement, making this a real,
unresolved agricultural loss rather than a simple substitution [4].

**Finding 3 — neroli oil requires an extraordinary mass of flowers for a
very small amount of oil, and world production is geographically
concentrated.** Commercial sources converge on roughly 1,000 kg of fresh
blossom yielding about 1 kg of neroli oil (0.07-0.13% by weight) via
steam/hydrodistillation over about four hours, with orange flower water
recovered as a byproduct (roughly 1 litre per kg of flowers charged)
[13][14]. Solvent extraction of the same flowers instead gives an
"absolute" at a similar 0.1-0.14% yield [14]. Tunisia and Morocco
together produce more than 90% of the world's neroli, at roughly 1,500
kg a year [8] — implying a very large flower-harvest tonnage worldwide
each spring (see Estimates, below). One supplier's own farm, in Morocco,
harvests its blossom crop in April-May [16].

**Finding 4 — the fruit has three sharply different processing identities
depending on ripeness and intended use, not just one "bitter orange"
product.** Unripe fruit, collected from naturally fallen fruit in May-June
and sun- or low-temperature-dried, becomes *Zhi Shi* in traditional
Chinese medicine — classed among the strongest Qi-regulating/Qi-breaking
herbs, used raw, bran-fried, or water-fried (raw being considered
strongest), at a reference dose of roughly 3-9 g [12]. The same species'
fully *ripe* fruit, similarly dried, is *Zhi Ke*, described as gentler —
moving and expanding Qi rather than breaking stagnation [12]. Separately
again, essential oil is distilled from dried, unripe *peel*, while a
different, pressed oil comes from *ripe* peel and is used to flavor ice
cream, drinks, and pharmaceuticals [1]. Four genuinely different
products — unripe whole fruit, ripe whole fruit, unripe peel oil, ripe
peel oil — come from the same tree at different points in its annual
cycle.

**Finding 5 — the Curaçao landrace shows a documented case of a stressful
growing environment creating, rather than destroying, commercial value.**
Spanish colonists brought bitter orange to Curaçao in the early 16th
century; the island's thin soil, salt-laden wind, and intense sun were
"entirely wrong" for growing orange trees as food crops, and the local
population (laraha) adapted into fruit too bitter and fibrous to eat —
yet this same adaptation is specifically what gives the dried peel its
prized aroma for Curaçao liqueur [9]. Sources disagree on the exact
history and date (one ties the aroma's discovery to the original 1527
planting, another says it was identified only decades later; commercial
production start-dates and the identity of the first producer are
likewise disputed between Dutch and French claimants) [9], but the
underlying agricultural fact — an inedible, environmentally stressed
fruit becoming the sole source of a named commercial liqueur ingredient
— is consistent across sources.

**Finding 6 — supplement-grade extracts are a genuinely contested safety
question, unlike food-level consumption of the fruit itself.** The U.S.
National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH)
describes oral use "in amounts commonly found in foods" as likely safe,
but calls the cardiovascular evidence for *concentrated extract/
supplement* use inconclusive: some studies link it to raised blood
pressure and heart rate, others do not, and serious reported events
(stroke, cardiac arrhythmia, heart attack) mostly involved
multi-ingredient products, clouding attribution to bitter orange
specifically [6][15]. An FDA laboratory analysis of 59 commercial
supplement products found only 5 of 23 labeled products within 80-120%
of their stated p-synephrine content, and 6 products contained synthetic
amines (methylsynephrine, isopropyloctopamine) that are not legal dietary
ingredients in the US [15] — a labeling/manufacturing-integrity problem
distinct from the biological safety question itself. Germany's BfR has
proposed capping supplement synephrine at 6.7 mg/day [6], and the NCAA
lists synephrine as a banned stimulant [15]. A Stohs (2017) review
counters that roughly 30 human studies found no cardiovascular effect at
commonly used doses [6] — the primary literature is genuinely split, not
settled.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree; needs little
  specialized care once established [1]. One source describes
  individual trees living up to 600 years, but this was not independently
  corroborated [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, but with up to three distinct
  harvest windows per year targeting different organs: spring blossom
  (April-May in one documented Moroccan growing operation) for neroli;
  naturally fallen, still-unripe fruit (May-June) for TCM and unripe-peel
  oil; and ripe winter fruit for peel-based products [12][16]. Not
  researched: whether all three are routinely harvested from the same
  trees in commercial practice, or whether operations specialize in one
  product.
- **Climate zones:** Subtropical/Mediterranean-type climates generally;
  one agronomic database describes tolerance across steppe, semi-arid,
  subtropical, and tropical wet-and-dry climates, though its figures
  could not be independently verified here (the source page returned an
  access error on a repeat check) [10]. USDA-equivalent hardiness is
  reported inconsistently across garden/nursery sources, ranging USDA 8a
  to 11b depending on the source and cultivar; one source describes it as
  hardier than sweet orange, another (PROSEA) says only that it
  "withstands several degrees of frost for short periods," without a
  number [17]. Not sourced: a single reconciled frost-kill threshold.
- **Composition:** Conflicting and weakly sourced. One source (a general
  nutrition-reference site) gives about 34 kcal per 100 g of edible flesh,
  ~87% of energy from carbohydrate, ~87 g water [19]. A separate,
  uncited commercial source gives about 29 kcal, 7.5 g carbohydrate, 0.8 g
  protein, 0.2 g fat, 2.4 g fiber, 44 mg vitamin C, 181 mg potassium, 26
  mg calcium per 100 g [20] — not reconciled with [19], and not treated
  as reliable given the lack of a cited source. Sweet orange's own
  composition (about 45.5 kcal/100g [4], see `orange.md`) is at most a
  rough reference point, not a substitute figure, since this is a
  different hybrid. No authoritative primary database (USDA FoodData
  Central or equivalent) entry specific to this species was located in
  this pass.
- **Edibility:** Not generally eaten raw as food; valued for
  flowers/peel/rootstock rather than fresh fruit (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Not sourced specifically for this species. General
  citrus pattern: self-incompatible cultivars produce more seeds under
  cross-pollination, self-compatible/male-sterile cultivars produce more
  seedless fruit [21]. Notably, sour orange pollen has itself been used
  experimentally as a *cross-pollinizer for sweet orange* in at least one
  study, which found it significantly increased sweet-orange fruit set
  [21] — suggesting bitter orange is not grown seedless and is a
  plausible pollen source for other citrus, though this doesn't confirm
  its own self-compatibility or seed count.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for this species specifically
  with a clean answer. Citrus flowering generally is understood to be
  driven primarily by low temperature and water-deficit stress rather
  than day length, with photoperiod playing at most a secondary,
  permissive role in some genotypes; one crop-requirements database
  lists sour orange's photoperiod response ambiguously as "long day,
  short day and day neutral," which, taken loosely, suggests
  day-neutrality is the closest supported characterization rather than
  a true day-length dependency [23]. Not applicable to tuberization/
  bulbing — a tree crop with no storage organ.
- **Yield:** Not sourced, and the usual "kg of fruit per hectare" framing
  may not even be the right measure for this crop given Finding 1 — no
  source gave a fruit-mass yield figure specific to this species (only
  sweet-orange figures were found, not reused here). A flower-mass
  figure for neroli production is estimated instead (see Estimates,
  below).
- **Water use:** Not sourced for this species specifically. Likely
  broadly similar to sweet orange given the shared genus and similar
  canopy/rooting habit (see `orange.md`'s water-use data), but this is an
  unconfirmed analogy, not a sourced figure, and is not asserted as a
  value here.
- **Propagation:** The species' single largest economic role worldwide is
  arguably as rootstock *seed* source: citrus rootstock seed is typically
  polyembryonic, so nucellar (maternal-tissue) embryos produce seedlings
  genetically identical to the mother tree, letting growers raise
  uniform rootstock populations from seed rather than needing to graft
  each rootstock individually [22]. General Florida nursery practice
  sows one seed per cell at 0.5-1 cm depth, with seed used within a month
  of extraction from the seed coat [22] — not confirmed as
  bitter-orange-specific, but bitter orange is one of the species this
  practice applies to. A nucellus-culture propagation study on bitter
  orange found rooting was best on a medium with 1 mg/l IBA [22].
  Named-cultivar fruit production (laraha, daidai) and ornamental/
  fragrance-flower cultivars are propagated by grafting instead.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** General citrus preference for deep, well- to
  moderately-drained soils, similar to sweet orange (see `orange.md`).
  Notably, the Curaçao landrace is the documented exception: it is
  specifically adapted to thin, dry, salt-exposed soil unsuitable for
  orange cultivation generally (Finding 5) [9]. Separately, sour orange
  rootstock itself (not just the Curaçao landrace) is independently
  documented as salt-tolerant relative to other standard citrus
  rootstocks: most sources rank it second or third, behind Cleopatra
  mandarin and/or Rangpur lime, among common rootstocks, tolerating
  soil salinity well above the general citrus saline-soil threshold of
  roughly 4 dS/m — though one Jordanian trial found Volkamer lemon more
  salt-tolerant than sour orange at salinity up to 15 dS/m, so this
  ranking is not universal across studies [24]. No nutrient-uptake or
  removal figures sourced.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Besides citrus tristeza virus (Finding 2),
  documented viral diseases include crinkly leaf virus and xyloporosis;
  documented fungal diseases include anthracnose, dieback, and heart rot
  [1]. Not independently verified beyond this single summary source.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced. Processing for all three main
  products (distillation, drying, peeling) appears to begin promptly
  after harvest in the sources reviewed, but no specific storage-life or
  temperature regime was found.
- **Establishment:** Rootstock seedlings are nursery-raised from seed
  (Propagation, above); fruiting/flower-production trees for named
  cultivars are grafted.
- **Cultivars:** 'Gou Tou' is named in one nursery listing as a
  particularly cold-hardy selection (zones 8-10, hardy to the mid-upper
  teens °F once established) [17], distinct from the three
  variety-level candidates proposed above (chinotto, laraha, daidai),
  which this entry treats as potential separate rows rather than mere
  cultivars.

## Hazards

- **Supplement-grade synephrine extracts**: genuinely contested
  cardiovascular safety evidence; case reports describe stroke,
  tachyarrhythmia, cardiac arrest, syncope, angina, myocardial
  infarction, ventricular arrhythmia, and death, almost always in
  multi-ingredient products that cloud attribution; an FDA/NCTR animal
  study found synephrine (as extract or pure compound) raised heart rate
  and blood pressure in rats; many human studies at typical doses found
  no effect (Finding 6) [6][15]. Not advised during pregnancy or
  breastfeeding; animal data suggest reduced milk production [6].
- **Drug interactions**: may produce grapefruit-like interactions
  (implying a CYP3A4 mechanism, not independently confirmed here) with
  statins, some calcium-channel blockers (e.g. nifedipine), some
  anti-anxiety medications, and some antihistamines [1].
- **Food-level use**: by contrast, NCCIH describes oral use "in amounts
  commonly found in foods" as likely safe [6] — the hazard above is
  specific to concentrated extracts/supplements, not to eating the fruit
  or its processed culinary products.
- **Product-label accuracy**: a distinct, non-biological hazard —
  commercial supplement products frequently did not match their own
  labeled synephrine content, and some contained synthetic amines not
  legally permitted as dietary ingredients in the US (Finding 6) [15].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (flowers → neroli/orange flower water)**: spring blossom
  harvest → steam/hydrodistillation (~4 hours) → neroli essential oil
  (~0.08-0.13% of flower mass) + orange flower water byproduct (~1
  L/kg flowers); alternatively, solvent extraction of the same flowers
  → orange blossom absolute (~0.1-0.14% yield) [13][14].
- **Chain 2 (unripe fruit → TCM)**: naturally fallen, unripe fruit
  collected May-June → sun- or low-temperature-dried whole → *Zhi Shi*,
  used raw, bran-fried, or water-fried [12].
- **Chain 3 (unripe peel → essential oil)**: dried, unripe peel →
  essential oil, used in food/beverage/pharmaceutical flavoring,
  separate from the flower-derived neroli [1].
- **Chain 4 (ripe fruit/peel → marmalade, candied peel, liqueurs)**: ripe
  fruit's high-pectin pith and pips give Seville orange marmalade its
  set [7]; peel is also candied, dried for liqueurs (Curaçao, Cointreau,
  bitters, Belgian witbier, glögg), or pressed for an oil used in ice
  cream, drinks and pharmaceuticals [1][7].
- **Chain 5 (seed → rootstock)**: polyembryonic/nucellar seed → nursery
  seedling → budded with a desired scion variety of another citrus
  species (Propagation, above); historically the dominant global citrus
  rootstock, now reduced by CTV losses but still in active use (Finding
  2) [4][22].
- **Not otherwise detailed here**: historic use in duck à l'orange;
  Finnish mämmi; Scandinavian baked goods; Greek spoon sweets; Turkish
  jam; Indian chutney; Yucatán cochinita pibil; Surinamese pom; soap
  lather from fruit/leaves; Cuban baseball-bat wood; daidai as a
  decorative New Year fruit (candidate row, Naming and scope) [1].

## Open research questions

- Composition figures conflict (34 vs. ~29 kcal/100g) and neither is from
  an authoritative primary nutrient database; no USDA FoodData
  Central-equivalent entry specific to this species was located.
- No fruit-mass yield figure specific to this species was found anywhere
  — only a flower-mass estimate for neroli (see Estimates). Whether a
  meaningful "yield" figure exists at all for a crop grown mainly for
  flowers/peel/rootstock, rather than fruit, is itself unresolved.
- No water-use (crop-coefficient) figure specific to this species was
  found; an analogy to sweet orange's figures is plausible but
  unconfirmed and not asserted here.
- Hardiness-zone and frost-tolerance figures disagree across sources
  (USDA 8a-11b depending on source/cultivar), with no single reconciled
  number.
- Pollination/self-compatibility and typical seed count per fruit were
  not found for this species specifically (only general-citrus and
  sweet-orange-trial data, used as context, not as this crop's own
  figure).
- `orange.md`'s own open item ("the specific citrus type used in [Nowruz]
  Haft-Sin is not clearly stated") is only partly resolved by this pass:
  one source explicitly names "Narenj (Seville Orange)" for the
  floating-orange tradition, but most other sources consulted just say
  "orange" without specifying sweet vs. bitter — not reconciled.
- The exact history and dating of Curaçao liqueur's commercial origin
  (planting date 1527 vs. a later aroma "discovery"; Dutch vs. French
  originator claims) is disputed across sources and not resolved here.
- Neroli's flowers-per-kilogram figure disagrees by roughly 5× across
  sources (1.5 million vs. 8 million flowers/kg); not reconciled.
- No soil nutrient-uptake/removal figures were sourced.
- No labor-hour figures were sourced for any operation (flower picking,
  fruit collection, peeling, distillation).
- Photoperiod sensitivity for flowering has no clean, species-specific
  answer; the one source found gives an ambiguous "long day, short day
  and day neutral" classification rather than a definite one.
- Sour-orange rootstock's salinity-tolerance ranking relative to other
  rootstocks is not universal across studies — most rank it near the
  top among standard rootstocks, but at least one trial ranks an
  alternative (Volkamer lemon) above it at high salinity. Not
  reconciled.

## Estimates and derivations

**Approximate world blossom harvest for neroli, ~1,500 t/year.** World
neroli oil production is about 1,500 kg/year [8], and roughly 1,000 kg of
blossom yields about 1 kg of oil [13][14]. 1,500 kg oil × 1,000 kg
flowers/kg oil = 1,500,000 kg (1,500 metric tons) of fresh blossom
harvested worldwide per year to support that output. [ESTIMATE/DERIVED].
This is a lower-bound-style estimate: it assumes all reported production
uses the ~1,000:1 ratio and ignores the separate, lower-yielding absolute
(solvent-extraction) pathway, which would require still more flower mass
per unit of finished product if any meaningful share of the 1,500 kg
figure includes absolute rather than steam-distilled oil. A dedicated
production-volume breakdown by extraction method would refine this.
