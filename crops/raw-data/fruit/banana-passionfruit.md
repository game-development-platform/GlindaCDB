## Naming and scope

Banana passionfruit, widely known in its native Andean region as
curuba (with several further regional names across Ecuador, Peru, and
Bolivia), a member of the broader passionfruit genus [1][2][3]. A
closely related, more recently described species — long confused with
this crop, grown the same way under the same market name, and the
dominant invasive "banana poka" of Hawaiian forests — is treated here
only as a cultivar-group note rather than a separate crop, since it is
grown identically and was historically not distinguished from it [10].
This entry's water-use analogies and climate comparisons are drawn from
two other closely related passionfruit-genus crops this project has
also researched.

This crop is genuinely distinct from its closest relatives in several
ways: it is by a wide margin the coldest-growing crop in its genus,
cultivated at substantial Andean elevation where other related species
would fail; its flowers are specifically adapted to hummingbird
pollination via an unusually long pendant floral tube, unlike insect-
pollinated relatives; its product use (a soft-skinned, aromatic, acid
fruit whose pulp is mostly strained into blended drinks) differs from
a hard-shelled dessert-fruit relative; and it carries a genuinely
distinct hazard and legal status — it is one of the worst forest weeds
on several Pacific islands, formally banned from sale and distribution
in at least one country, with its close relative covering hundreds of
square kilometers of invaded forest in Hawaii.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a cool-highland trellis vine with a real but limited
frost margin, the passionfruit crop for high Andean elevation.** Above
a certain elevation, disease pressure rises; below a lower elevation
threshold, pest pressure rises instead; and in the cloudiest, highest
growing zones the vine grows poorly with small, late-ripening fruit
[3]. Frost below freezing can damage flower buds, open flowers, fruit
set, and non-woody young growth, though some lines tolerate a brief
drop to a notably colder temperature without being killed outright —
figures for exactly how cold the plant itself (as opposed to just its
flowers) can tolerate conflict somewhat between sources [1][4]. The
crop needs a substantial annual sunshine total, moderately high
humidity, and light winds — strong wind drops flowers and breaks young
shoots [3][4]. Because the frost sensitivity documented here affects
flowers and fruit set rather than killing the whole plant outright at
the light frosts within its normal growing range, frost functions here
as a yield hazard rather than a stand-ending event.

**Finding 2 — a well-documented phenology with specific stage
durations.** Germination takes several weeks; first true leaves appear
a few weeks later; seedlings spend one to two months in nursery bags
before being transplanted; the main vine framework forms over several
months, followed by about a month more to first flower buds [3]. From
flower bud to open flower takes about two weeks; from fruit set to
fruit growth takes about two months; filling and ripening take about a
month more [3]. Time from transplanting to first flowering runs several
months, with flowering recurring roughly every three months after the
first harvest; a separate source instead describes the first
commercial crop as arriving in the second year after planting — a real
conflict between sources on timing [1][3]. The crop shows two
documented harvest peaks following seasonal rains in at least one major
growing region, with fruit actually available more or less year-round;
in a different introduced growing region, fruiting instead runs across
roughly half the year. A well-managed planting can remain productive
for roughly a decade.

**Finding 3 — documented yields range widely, roughly threefold
between traditional and intensively managed plantings.** Traditional
plantings yield considerably less per hectare per year than intensively
managed plantings at a documented optimal vine density [3]. A national
average yield figure for one major growing country sits toward the
lower end of this range, with good management reported to roughly
double it [11]. A considerably higher yield figure, from dense, well-
managed plantings, comes from fruit-count-per-vine and fruit-count-per-
area figures in an older classic reference — though that same source
also notes that roughly half to three-fifths of fruit can drop
prematurely for reasons not well understood, a real source of yield
variability [1].

**Finding 4 — the vine's close relative is a serious, legally
restricted invasive species in at least two regions.** In one Pacific
country, the vine was documented as naturalized decades ago and is now
formally listed on a national pest-plant list that bans its sale and
distribution entirely, and is separately classified as an environmental
weed of concern [9]. Documented dispersal there happens through
introduced mammals (opossum-like marsupials and rodents) that eat the
ripe fruit and pass viable seed, since the hummingbird pollinators of
its native range are absent; introduced honeybees and bumblebees
instead perform pollination duty there [7][8]. In Hawaii, the closely
related species covers several hundred square kilometers of forest
under the common name "banana poka," spread mainly by feral pigs, and
is managed there through biological control agents (a specific moth
species and a fungal leaf-spot disease) [12].

**Finding 5 — a climacteric soft fruit with a notably short shelf life
at room temperature, extendable considerably under refrigeration.**
The fruit's respiratory climacteric peak — the point of maximum
ripening-related metabolic activity — occurs quite early in ripening,
so commercial fruit is picked well before reaching it; ethylene output
during ripening is very high [3]. Growers typically ripen fruit over a
few days at a specific moderate room temperature; natural (unrefrigerated)
storage extends to roughly a week, moderate refrigeration to roughly two
weeks, and cooler refrigeration to roughly a month depending on the
exact source [3]. More recent controlled studies found that slightly
cooler storage delays the climacteric peak and preserves sugar content
longer, and that cold storage combined with a ripening-inhibitor
treatment preserved firmness and quality for roughly a month [5][6].
The fruit is noted as shipping well overall [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A vigorous, hairy perennial climbing vine,
  trained on a trellis, remaining productive for roughly a decade under
  good management [1][3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, with flowering recurring
  roughly every three months and fruit available nearly year-round in
  at least one major growing region (two seasonal peaks); fruit is
  always picked at the first sign of color change and ripened off the
  vine (Finding 2, Finding 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical highland elevations in its native Andean range;
  subtropical uplands and light-frost temperate climates where
  introduced elsewhere.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (ripe pulp); notably vitamin-C-rich,
  modest in calories and protein.
- **Pollination:** Required; specifically adapted to hummingbird
  pollination in its native range (including at least one documented
  long-billed hummingbird species), with introduced honeybees and
  bumblebees performing this role in regions where it has naturalized
  without its native pollinators; the species is also partly self-
  compatible, though cross-pollination measurably improves fruit set
  over self-pollination with no apparent inbreeding cost (Finding 4).
- **Yield / composition:** See Finding 3 and Estimates below for the
  range between traditional and intensively managed plantings.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced at the species level — no
  study testing this species' own flowering response to day length was
  found. By genus-level analogy only (not a confirmed species-specific
  claim): the commercially studied congener *P. edulis* is a long-day
  plant requiring roughly 11 hours of daylight to flower, which
  suggests mollissima's highland flowering could plausibly be day-
  length influenced too, though some other ornamental *Passiflora*
  species are reportedly short-day instead, and a photoperiod-
  insensitive mutant line exists within *P. edulis* itself, showing the
  trait varies even within the genus. Not applicable to tuberization/
  bulbing, since this is a vine with no such storage organ.
- **Propagation:** Grown from seed via nursery seedlings (the standard
  commercial method); cuttings root poorly, air-layering is used more,
  and grafting and laboratory propagation are experimental or costly
  alternatives.
- **Water use:** A comparatively shallow, weak root system; good
  drainage is essential, with nematodes and a fungal wilt disease
  favored in wet, poorly drained sites; water stress is particularly
  damaging during flowering (both drought and prolonged rain cause
  problems) and during fruit growth and ripening (drought drops
  developing fruit; heavy rain splits ripening fruit and favors fruit
  rot diseases).
- **Labor:** A detailed cost budget (derived from a related crop's
  input-cost guide, flagged as an imperfect analogy) documents
  substantial annual labor for pruning/tying, irrigation, weeding,
  fertilization, and trellis upkeep, plus harvest, grading, and packing
  labor, on an espalier trellis system at a specific documented vine
  density.
- **Soil:** Well-drained loam with substantial organic matter, low
  aluminum, and a specific fertility profile is preferred; a moderately
  acidic soil pH is optimal; boron deficiency is linked to fruit
  cracking. Salinity tolerance, as its own dimension, is sourced
  directly for this species at the seedling stage: a Colombian trial
  grew seedlings at 0, 20, 40, and 80 mM NaCl (roughly 0.70, 2.38, 3.47,
  and 5.49 dS/m EC) and found sharply dropping leaf area and dry matter
  with increasing salinity, especially at the highest level, concluding
  seedlings are "moderately tolerant" during early growth; a follow-up
  study on the same species found leaf area reduced 21-76% across the
  same treatments and photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm) dropped by up
  to 92%, attributing the damage mainly to photosynthetic disruption.
  A separate secondary summary classifies the same underlying data as
  "moderately sensitive" instead — a direct conflict with the original
  study's own framing, not reconciled here. Tolerance in mature,
  established plants (as opposed to seedlings) is not established
  either way.
- **Stand life:** First harvest roughly one to two years after
  transplanting (sources conflict on the exact timing); productive life
  of roughly a decade under good management, with renewal pruning used
  when yield declines.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A root-knot nematode (favored in wet
  soils), several leafhopper and caterpillar pests, mites, a bud-
  boring insect, stem borers, and fruit flies are documented pests;
  powdery mildew, anthracnose, a bacterial leaf-spot disease, a fungal
  wilt disease, and gray mold are documented diseases.
- **Cultivars:** No formally registered cultivars exist; local types
  differ in fruit size and color, with a smaller-fruited type documented
  in one country compared to fruit from two neighboring countries.

## Hazards

A root-knot nematode (favored in wet soils), several leafhopper and
caterpillar pests, mites, a bud-boring insect, stem borers, and fruit
flies are documented pests; powdery mildew, anthracnose, a bacterial
leaf-spot disease, a fungal wilt disease, and gray mold are documented
diseases; frost can damage buds and flowers directly.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the fruit contains a cyanogenic glycoside
(prunasin) at a low documented concentration. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

A closely related species/form of this crop is one of the worst forest
weeds on several Pacific islands, formally banned from sale and
distribution in at least one country as a listed pest plant, and
covers a very large area of invaded forest in Hawaii under biological
control.

Leaves and flowers have a documented folk-medicine use as a mild
sedative; this is not considered a food-safety hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit picked as color turns → graded and crated → ripened
  at room temperature or cool-stored → fresh market; alternatively,
  pulp is strained into juice for blended drinks (including at least
  one documented South American spirit-based aperitif use). Pulp
  combined with sugar is made into jam, jelly, sauce, and pie filling.
  Pulp is also fermented to clean seed for nursery propagation.
- Not covered by this research in detail: ice cream and gelatin-dessert
  use, canned juice processing (noted elsewhere as having quality
  losses with a specific preservative), microencapsulated pulp powder,
  sedative folk-medicine preparation, and vegetative propagation
  technique in detail.

## Open research questions

- Yield figures conflict considerably across sources (a roughly
  threefold range between traditional and intensively managed
  plantings documented in one source, versus a notably higher range
  from an older classic reference) — not reconciled.
- Time to first harvest conflicts between sources (roughly one year
  versus a full second year after planting).
- Cold tolerance of the plant itself (as opposed to just its flowers)
  conflicts between sources (a specific brief-cold threshold in one
  source versus a notably colder tolerance for some lines in another).
- Elevation range for best growing conditions differs somewhat across
  sources, and a separate source suggests the main cultivated variety
  is poorly adapted below a specific elevation threshold where a
  closely related species performs better.
- Documented invaded-forest area in Hawaii for the closely related
  species conflicts meaningfully between two sources.
- The detailed labor-cost budget used here is adapted from a related
  crop's input-cost guide rather than a crop-specific cost study,
  flagged explicitly as an imperfect analogy.
- Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from a related
  passionfruit-genus crop rather than independently measured for this
  crop.
- Seed viability duration, cyanogen content of leaves and unripe fruit,
  and a detailed regional cost study were not accessed or found.
- Photoperiod response of this species itself is not sourced; the
  genus-level analogy to *P. edulis*'s long-day flowering requirement
  is suggestive only, not confirmed for this species.
- Salinity classification of seedlings conflicts directly between the
  original study ("moderately tolerant") and a secondary summary of
  the same data ("moderately sensitive"); mature-plant salinity
  tolerance is not sourced at all. The underlying trial's exact figures
  come from a search-engine abstract/summary, since the source PDF
  itself returned an access error when fetched directly.

## Estimates and derivations

Growth-cycle length to first harvest (roughly 365-730 days from
transplanting) is derived from two conflicting documented figures — a
detailed regional monograph's 12-14 month figure, and a classic
reference's "second year" figure — kept as a range rather than
reconciled.

An exceptional-yield tier (roughly 35,000 kg/ha) is derived from a
classic reference's per-acre figure in pounds, unit-converted, cross-
checked against an independently derived figure from documented fruit
count per hectare multiplied by an assumed typical fruit weight.

Water-use parameters (a seasonal water-use-days index, root-zone depth
assumptions, and drought-buffer figures) are derived entirely by
analogy to a related passionfruit-genus crop this project has also
researched, adjusted slightly for this crop's documented shallower root
system and higher drought sensitivity.

Seed viability duration is estimated from documented seed-handling and
storage practice (fermented, washed, dried, and kept in closed
containers in cool places, consistent with a soil seed bank
documented in at least one introduced region), suggesting relatively
orthodox (not short-lived) seed behavior, though no dedicated viability
study was found.

Labor-hour figures by category are derived from a detailed two-year
cost budget originally developed for a related passionfruit-genus crop
and reproduced in a regional monograph on this crop — flagged
explicitly as an imperfect analogy rather than a crop-specific source,
though its documented yield figures are consistent with other sourced
figures for this crop.

A soil pH optimum is derived as the midpoint of the overlapping range
between two documented pH recommendations from different sources.

A peak-yield-per-day figure and a yield ramp-up period are derived
from a documented year-one versus year-two yield figure in the same
two-year cost budget.
