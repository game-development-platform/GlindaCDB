## Naming and scope

*Rubus occidentalis* L. (Rosaceae), black raspberry or blackcap, also
called black cap raspberry, bear's eye blackberry and scotch cap [1][3]. It
is a diploid species native only to North America [5].
- **Purple raspberries** (*R. neglectus*, black × red) are kept here as a
  cultivar group: vigorous, crown-forming plants with large, soft fruit,
  "fair quality for fresh use" but "excellent for processing" [2][4].
  Cultivars: 'Brandywine', 'Royalty', 'Estate' [1][2].
- **Excluded:**
  - *R. leucodermis*, the native western black raspberry [1][2];
  - *R. glaucus*, a South American tetraploid black raspberry [4];
  - *R. coreanus*, which has its own row (`bokbunja`).
- **Gate check: passes** against red raspberry:
  - canes come "only from the crown, not from the roots" [2];
  - the plant forms clumps rather than suckering, and has arching canes
    with a whitish bloom [1][7];
  - it is less cold hardy, with smaller, seedier, more aromatic fruit
    [5][6][7];
  - it is propagated by tip layering [7].

**Relatives in this project:**
- red raspberry (`raspberry`, `raspberry.md`);
- blackberry (`blackberry`, `blackberry.md`);
- Korean black raspberry (`bokbunja`, `bokbunja.md`).

**Proposed:** none.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a small crop resting on one old cultivar, with official
statistics now discontinued.**
- "In the Northwest, nearly the entire commercial crop is 'Munger'" [2].
  Release date: 1890 per PNW 655 [2]; "introduced in 1897" per Weber &
  Galvani (citing Hedrick 1925) [3], whose own Table 4 prints "1987" [3].
- **Eastern US:** production was centred in New York in the early 1920s,
  but disease, low yield and other crops cut the eastern industry to "a
  few hundred acres across the region" [3]. Black raspberry is the most
  popular bramble in Ohio gardens [6].
- **Oregon (NASS) [8]:**

  | Year | Acres harvested | Yield (lb/acre) | Production (1,000 lb) | Price ($/lb) | Value per acre ($) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 2014 | 1,100 | 3,590 | 3,950 | 2.410 | 8,671 |
  | 2015 | 1,100 | 4,150 | 4,565 | 1.480 | 6,126 |
  | 2016 | 950 | 4,970 | 4,720 | 1.220 | 6,044 |
  | 2017 | 950 | 2,030 | 2,180 | 1.300 | 2,639 |

  "Estimates discontinued in 2018" [8].
- **Cultivar identity is unreliable.** In SSR fingerprinting, 'Bristol',
  'Cumberland', 'Munger', 'New Logan', 'Shuttleworth' and 'Plum Farmer'
  could not be told apart, and nursery 'Black Hawk' matched 'Jewel'
  (Dossett et al. 2012, as reported in [3]).

**Finding 2 — small fruit, a short season, and yields below red
raspberry.**
- **Geneva, NY, 2006-2009 [3]:**
  - all-cultivar mean yield 1,780, 2,210, 4,310 and 4,310 kg/ha over the
    four seasons;
  - four-year cumulative yields from 17,960 kg/ha ('Haut') down to 4,240
    ('Munger', which suffered "extensive winter damage");
  - mean fruit weight 1.9 g in 2006, then 1.4-1.5 g; 'Jewel' was largest
    (2.3 g mean) and 'Black Hawk'NT smallest (1.0 g).
- **Season [3]:** each cultivar fruited for 14-21 days (mean 17.9); the
  season across all cultivars ran 26-33 days (mean 28), from the start of
  July to the end of July.
- **Against red:**
  - the best black yields "compare favorably" with primocane red
    raspberries but are "considerably lower" than floricane reds in
    western New York [3];
  - "Red raspberries are generally more productive than black raspberries"
    [2].
  - In Oregon, however, NASS red raspberry yields for 2014-2017 were
    3,620-4,750 lb/acre [8], about the same as black (conflict, Open
    research questions).
- Black raspberries are "higher in sugar and contain less acid than red
  raspberries" and "command a premium price" [3].

**Finding 3 — managed by tipping, propagated by tip layering.**
- **Tipping:**
  - "Tip primocanes in summer to encourage branching. These canes produce
    fruit the following year" [2].
  - Ohio: without a trellis, tip black canes at 24 inches (purple at 30)
    by removing the top 2-3 inches [6].
  - In the USDA-ARS Corvallis planting, primocane tips were pruned at 1 m
    in early June [9].
- **Pruning (Ohio) [6]:** three times a year: laterals cut back to 8-10
  inches in mid-March, summer tipping, then removal of spent floricanes.
- **Oregon commercial practice:** summer tipping by hand, mechanical
  pruning in winter, machine harvest [1].
- **Tip layering [7]:** bury tips of the summer's new canes 3-5 inches deep
  in the fall; the next spring, sever them, leaving 4-6 inches of the old
  cane, and transplant.

**Finding 4 — viruses and soil diseases limit stand life.**
- **Strawberry necrotic shock virus (SNSV)** is pollen-borne (carried by
  bees). Red and black raspberries are both susceptible, "but black
  raspberries succumb to the disease much faster"; some fields are
  "mostly infected with SNSV within the first two to three years of
  planting" [2].
- **Soil diseases:** "Most are quite susceptible to Phytophthora root rot
  and Verticillium wilt" [2]. Avoid sites that grew tomatoes, potatoes or
  eggplants the previous year [6].
- **Separating plantings [6]:**
  - plant black raspberries away from reds, because reds can carry latent
    infections;
  - plant blacks upwind of reds, because aphid vectors are mostly carried
    by wind;
  - stay 300 ft from wild brambles.
- Other viruses recorded on the species: apple mosaic, black raspberry
  necrosis, raspberry bushy dwarf, raspberry leaf curl [1].
- **Stand life:** no figure in the sources read this pass. Not sourced.

**Finding 5 — a colour crop.**
- The fruit has "a long history of use as a natural colorant and dye"
  because of its high anthocyanin levels [9].
- **Anthocyanins in juice** (cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents) [9]:
  - wild clones 39-996 mg/100 mL;
  - cultivar means 350.6 ('Munger'), 387.3 ('Jewel') and 405.9 ('Mac
    Black') mg/100 mL;
  - higher in 2010 than in 2009 (465.1 vs. 350.1).
- Typically dried or frozen, made into purées and juices, or processed as
  colourants; used in liqueurs, including Chambord (Wikipedia, citation
  needed) [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** perennial crown with biennial canes; deciduous
  shrub to 2-3 m, arching canes to 1.8 m [1]. USDA PLANTS: subshrub,
  "Thicket Forming", rapid growth, lifespan "Short" [10].
- **Harvest pattern:**
  - a single early-summer crop on floricanes, picked every other day in
    the New York trial [3];
  - near Portland, OR, most cultivars ripen "nearly simultaneously from
    late June to early July", 'MacBlack' 10-14 days later [2];
  - 'Niwot' is primocane-fruiting and ripens in September [2][3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** a low establishment-year crop, then
  full yields from the third season in the New York trial (planted 2005;
  1,780 kg/ha in 2006, 4,310 in 2008) [3].
- **Climate zones:**
  - native from New Brunswick to Nebraska and from Quebec to Mississippi
    [1];
  - less cold hardy than red raspberry and "found in more southerly
    locations" [5][6][7];
  - USDA PLANTS: minimum temperature -28 °F, at least 129 frost-free
    days, 24-45 in precipitation [10];
  - NMSU: "prefer moderate winters and may need to be protected in colder
    areas" [7].
  - Chill requirement: Not sourced.
- **Composition:** anthocyanins (Finding 5) [9]; more sugar and less acid
  than red raspberry [3]. Proximate composition (energy, sugars, fibre,
  vitamin C): Not sourced.
- **Edibility:** raw-edible; jams and jellies [1]. PLANTS: toxicity "None"
  [10].
- **Pollination:** bees carry pollen and, with it, SNSV [2]. Self-fertility
  and hive density: Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for this species.
- **Yield:** Finding 1 (Oregon) and Finding 2 (New York).
- **Water use:**
  - New York trial: drip-topped to 25 mm/week before bloom and after
    harvest, and 51 mm/week from fruit development through harvest [3].
  - In 2007, 147 mm of May-July rain (vs. a 286 mm average) and a 35 °C
    peak left yields low "despite supplemental irrigation" [3].
  - PLANTS: medium drought tolerance, medium moisture use, low anaerobic
    tolerance, minimum root depth 12 in [10].
  - FAO-56 "Berries (bushes)" Kc 0.30/1.05/0.50 [11]; it is not
    species-specific.
- **Propagation:** tip layering (Finding 3) [7]. PLANTS also lists bare
  root, container, seed and sprigs [10]. Use nurseries you trust, because
  of cultivar mislabelling [3].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:**
  - Ohio: well-drained, pH 5.8-6.5, sandy loam best; ridges or raised
    beds [6];
  - PNW 655: raised beds 12-18 inches high against root rot (for
    raspberries generally) [2];
  - PLANTS: pH 5.2-7.5, high CaCO3 tolerance, fine and medium soils (not
    coarse), salinity tolerance "None" [10];
  - NMSU (all raspberries, New Mexico): pH 6.5-7.5 [7].
- **Nutrients:** Ohio home guidance gives 10-10-10 at 1 lb per 100 ft of
  row twice after planting, then 2-3 lb per 100 ft twice a year (all
  raspberries) [6]. Removal per tonne: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** HB66 treats red, yellow, purple and black
  raspberries in one chapter: cool to 1 °C within 12 h; hold 2-5 days at
  -0.5 to 0 °C, >90% RH [4]. No black-specific values.
- **Establishment:**
  - Ohio: hills on a low trellis, 2.5 ft in-row × 10 ft between rows
    (purple: 3 × 12 ft, no trellis); trellis wires 40 in high [6];
  - New York trial: 0.6 m × 3 m on a two-wire V-trellis, taken as 5,380
    plants/ha [3];
  - Corvallis: 0.9 m in-row, three-wire trellis [9];
  - PLANTS: 1,200-4,800 plants per acre [10].
  - Early spring planting is preferred [6].
- **Cultivars [2][3]:**
  - 'Munger': medium yield, processing, machine harvestable;
  - 'Jewel': largest fruit, "more disease-resistant", sunscald in hot
    seasons;
  - 'Haut' and 'Huron': highest New York yields;
  - 'Bristol': vigorous and cold hardy, susceptible to anthracnose;
  - 'MacBlack': late;
  - 'Niwot': primocane-fruiting;
  - also 'Allen', 'New Logan', 'Black Hawk', 'Cumberland', 'Dundee',
    'Plum Farmer' [1][3].
- **Weather response:** winter injury ('Munger' in New York); heat plus
  drought cuts yield (2007); sunscald on 'Jewel' [3]. Rain: Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation; drainage matters because of root
  rot [2][6].
- **Soil biology:** Phytophthora and Verticillium (Finding 4) [2][6].
  Mycorrhizae: Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** native to the US and Canada; PLANTS lists
  no invasive, noxious or legal status [10]. Grows in disturbed and
  logged ground [1]. Clean, virus-tested stock is the control [2][3].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced this pass.

## Hazards

- **Viruses:** SNSV (fastest decline), raspberry bushy dwarf, black
  raspberry necrosis, apple mosaic, raspberry leaf curl [1][2].
- **Fungi and bacteria:**
  - Phytophthora root rot, Verticillium wilt [2];
  - anthracnose (*Elsinoë veneta*), spur blight (*Didymella applanata*),
    downy mildew (*Peronospora sparsa*), brown rot (*Monilinia
    fructigena*), crown gall [1][5];
  - orange rust, cane blight (raspberries generally) [6].
- **Insects:** raspberry cane borer, raspberry fruitworm, red-necked cane
  borer, Japanese beetle (raspberries generally) [6]; variegated cutworm
  (*Peridroma saucia*) [1].
- **Postharvest:** grey mould, as in all raspberries [4].

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — no food-safety hazard specific to this species was
found; PLANTS gives toxicity "None" [10].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** hand-picked → cooled → farm-direct and farmers'
  markets [3]; held as for raspberries [4].
- **Chain 2 (processing):** machine-harvested ('Munger', Oregon) → frozen
  or dried → purée, juice, colourant [1][2].
- **Chain 3 (drinks):** fruit wine [3]; liqueur [1].
- **Not detailed here:** colourant extraction, freeze-dried powders for
  medical trials, IQF practice.

## Open research questions

- **Munger release date:** 1890 [2] vs. 1897 [3] vs. "1987" in [3]'s
  own Table 4 (probably a misprint of 1897).
- **Yield relative to red:** "generally" lower [2][3] vs. Oregon NASS
  2014-2017, where red and black yields were similar [8].
- **Soil pH:** 5.8-6.5 [6] vs. 5.2-7.5 [10] vs. 6.5-7.5 (NMSU, all
  raspberries) [7].
- **Plant density:** 0.6 × 3 m is 5,556/ha, but [3] used 5,380, which
  matches 2 × 10 ft exactly.
- **Bokbunja identity:** the old doc's claim that Korean commercial
  bokbunja is mostly *R. occidentalis* had no source that could be
  re-found, so it is dropped. See `bokbunja.md`.
- **Not sourced:** stand life, chill hours, photoperiod, proximate
  composition, self-fertility, labour, nutrient removal, high-tunnel
  yields.
- **Works identified, not yet read:**
  - Bushway, Pritts & Handley (2008), NRAES-35 *Raspberry and Blackberry
    Production Guide* (the MSU copy returned HTML);
  - Dossett et al. (2012a, 2012b) on SSR fingerprinting;

## Estimates and derivations

**Oregon yields (1 lb/acre = 1.121 kg/ha) [8]:**
- 2014: 3,590 × 1.121 = 4.02 t/ha;
- 2015: 4,150 → 4.65 t/ha;
- 2016: 4,970 → 5.57 t/ha;
- 2017: 2,030 → 2.28 t/ha;
- mean 2014-2017: (3,590 + 4,150 + 4,970 + 2,030) / 4 = 3,685 lb/acre
  = 4.13 t/ha.

**Oregon prices (1 lb = 0.4536 kg) [8]:** $2.41 → $5.31/kg; $1.48 →
$3.26; $1.22 → $2.69; $1.30 → $2.87. Value 2014: $8,671/acre × 2.471 =
$21,426/ha.

**New York [3]:**
- mature-plant mean 4,310 kg/ha = 4.3 t/ha (2008-09);
- 'Haut' 17,960 / 4 = 4,490 kg/ha per year;
- 'Munger' 4,240 / 4 = 1,060 kg/ha per year;
- fruit count at 4,310 kg/ha and 1.5 g = 4,310,000 / 1.5 = 2.87 million
  berries/ha.

**Densities [3][6][10]:**
- Ohio 2.5 × 10 ft = 0.762 × 3.048 m = 2.32 m² → 4,305 plants/ha;
- New York 2 × 10 ft = 0.61 × 3.05 m → 5,375/ha;
- PLANTS 1,200-4,800/acre × 2.471 = 2,965-11,861/ha.

**Irrigation [3]:** 25 mm/week = 3.6 mm/day; 51 mm/week = 7.3 mm/day.

**Temperatures [10]:** -28 °F = -33.3 °C.

No other estimates are made in this pass.
