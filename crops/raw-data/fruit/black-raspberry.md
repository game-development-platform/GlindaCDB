## Naming and scope

The eastern North American blackcap and its cultivars [1][2]. Purple
raspberries (hybrids between this species and red raspberry) are
treated here as a cultivar group within this one crop entry, since they
are vigorous, large-fruited, soft, and best suited for processing [2].

Not included here: a related western North American black raspberry
species, and a Korean black raspberry species that has its own
separate crop entry in this project — though notably, most commercial
Korean fields, wine, and juice marketed under that Korean name are
actually documented to be this same species rather than the nominally
distinct Korean species, so Korean fruit-liqueur products made from
cultivated fruit properly belong to this crop's product list.

A closely related red-fruited crop this project has also researched is
the much better-studied sibling crop; many values in this entry are
analogies to that crop, flagged explicitly where used, since this
species has far less dedicated agronomic research of its own.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a small, niche crop limited by viral disease and a
short productive stand life.** US commercial production has shrunk to
a few hundred acres in the eastern US plus one Pacific Northwest
growing region, where nearly the entire commercial crop depends on a
single 19th-century cultivar [2][3]. This crop succumbs to a specific
pollen-borne virus (strawberry necrotic shock virus) considerably
faster than its red-fruited relative, with some fields becoming mostly
infected within just two to three years [2]. A separate virus specific
to this species, an aphid-spread viral mosaic complex, and a soil-borne
fungal wilt disease add further disease pressure [3]. Productivity can
begin to decline after as few as two production seasons due to
cumulative pest and disease pressure [3]. Growers keep red- and black-
fruited plantings physically separated by a substantial distance
specifically to slow virus spread [4].

**Finding 2 — yields run roughly half of the related red-fruited crop,
with notably small fruit.** Typical yields in one major eastern US
growing region run considerably lower than typical red-raspberry
yields in the same region, with individual berries generally under 2.5
grams and smaller still in dry years [3]. Yield statistics from one
major western US growing state over nearly a decade show a wide year-
to-year range [5]. A detailed university trial's best-performing
cultivar reached a documented potential yield in its second harvest
year well above the typical range [3]. Low yields are a documented
reason this fruit commands a price premium, and that premium is
necessary for the crop to remain commercially viable at all [1][3].

**Finding 3 — managed quite differently from the related red-fruited
crop.** Canes grow only from the crown of the plant, never from root
suckers the way the related crop does [2]. First-year canes
(primocanes) are deliberately tipped (the growing tip pinched off) in
summer at a specific height to force branching, which measurably raises
yield several-fold; side branches are then cut back in spring [4][2].
Propagation is almost always by tip layering: drooping cane tips are
buried a few centimeters deep in late summer, allowed to root, then
severed and transplanted the following spring, with one established
plant capable of producing over a dozen new tip-layered plants per year
[4]. At least one commercial growing region performs summer tipping by
hand but winter pruning and harvest by machine [1].

**Finding 4 — prized specifically for its color and suitability for
processing.** The fruit is typically dried, frozen, pureed, juiced, or
processed into natural food colorant, owing to an unusually high
anthocyanin content [1]. It is a key ingredient in at least one well-
known commercial fruit liqueur, as well as general fruit wine
production [1][3]. Ongoing medical research interest in potential
cancer-related health benefits of this fruit has raised its profile,
though this is noted here only as an area of research interest, not
treated as an established food-safety or nutritional claim.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A perennial crown producing biennial canes,
  which arch over and can root at their tips if left untipped
  [1][4].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; a single short early-summer
  crop on second-year canes under standard management, though at least
  one cultivar instead fruits on first-year canes in early autumn.
- **Climate:** Temperate, native to eastern North America; documented
  as less cold-hardy overall than the related red-fruited crop.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Required, bee-pollinated by analogy to the related
  red-fruited crop (not independently researched for this species);
  pollinating insects are also the vector for at least one serious
  pollen-borne virus affecting this crop (Finding 1).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not directly studied for this species.
  By analogy to the related red-fruited crop (not confirmed species-
  specific): standard biennial-cane floral initiation is linked to
  short days plus cool autumn temperatures, while fall-fruiting
  (primocane) types — including this species' own fall-fruiting
  cultivar(s) — are reported as largely day-length-independent, with
  temperature the dominant cue; this species' own fall-fruiting
  cultivars acquired that trait through introgression from red-
  raspberry breeding lines, paralleling the mechanism rather than
  arising independently. Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing — a
  cane crop with no storage organ.
- **Yield / composition:** See Finding 2 and Estimates below for the
  range between typical, good, and exceptional yields.
- **Propagation:** Exclusively vegetative — tip layering (the standard
  method) or laboratory-grown plugs; this species genuinely cannot be
  propagated from root suckers the way its red-fruited relative can,
  since canes arise only from the crown (Finding 3). Using virus-free
  planting stock is important, since conventional propagation from
  infected plants passes viral disease on to new plantings.
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve for
  berry bushes is used by analogy; a detailed university trial
  documented a specific weekly irrigation schedule that roughly doubles
  water delivery during fruit development and harvest compared to the
  rest of the season, and noted that fruit remains notably small in dry
  years even with irrigation. Most cultivars are documented as quite
  susceptible to a water-mold root rot, making raised beds a common
  practice in at least one growing region.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor study was found for this crop
  specifically; figures here are estimated from the related red-
  fruited crop's labor pattern, adjusted for this crop's smaller fruit
  size and the additional labor demand of multi-pass summer tipping.
- **Soil:** Well-drained soil is important, with raised beds and
  synthetic ground cover used in at least one detailed trial; soils
  with a history of certain solanaceous crops (which host the same
  soil-borne wilt fungus) should be avoided. Soil pH preference is
  assumed by analogy to the related red-fruited crop. Salinity
  tolerance, however, is sourced species-specifically (moderate
  confidence, since the primary USDA record itself wasn't opened
  directly): a USDA PLANTS Database profile rates this species'
  salinity tolerance as "None" on that database's categorical scale (no
  numeric EC threshold given) — lower than the red-raspberry analogy
  previously assumed here. Red raspberry itself is inconsistently
  documented on this point: one source rates it "poor," while at least
  one cultivar study found tolerance to high NaCl concentrations and
  even a growth benefit under mild saline-alkali stress, suggesting
  cultivar-level variation may apply to both species.
- **Stand life:** Productivity can begin declining after as few as two
  production seasons due to disease pressure, though some sources
  suggest a longer four-to-eight-year typical productive life under
  better disease management; fields in at least one major growing
  region become mostly infected with a specific pollen-borne virus
  within two to three years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A pollen-borne virus (causing crumbly
  fruit), a separate species-specific necrosis virus, an aphid-spread
  viral mosaic complex, a soil-borne fungal wilt disease, a fungal
  anthracnose disease (with meaningful cultivar-level resistance
  differences), general fruit rots, sunscald in hot years, and winter
  cold-damage susceptibility in at least one major cultivar are all
  documented.
- **Cultivars:** Several named cultivars exist, differing in fruit
  size, ripening time, cold hardiness, anthracnose resistance, and
  Verticillium-wilt susceptibility; purple raspberries (hybrids with
  the red-fruited relative) form a distinct cultivar group within this
  crop, valued for vigor, large soft fruit, and processing suitability.

## Hazards

A pollen-borne virus (causing crumbly fruit), a separate species-
specific necrosis virus, an aphid-spread viral mosaic complex, a soil-
borne fungal wilt disease, a fungal anthracnose disease, general fruit
rots, and sunscald are documented pest/disease concerns. No specific
fruit-toxicity hazards were found in the sources consulted; ongoing
medical research interest in potential health benefits is noted but
not treated as a hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Hand-picked fruit → cooled and packed → cold storage for
  a few days → fresh market. Machine-harvested fruit and culls are
  processed into puree and juice (including use as a natural food
  colorant), dried berries, jam, liqueur, and wine.
- **Propagation chain:** Tip layering or laboratory-grown plugs → new
  plants.
- Not covered by this research in detail: frozen-berry processing
  specifics, and colorant extraction methods beyond basic juice and
  puree production.

## Open research questions

- Release date for the dominant commercial cultivar conflicts between
  sources (two different years given) — not reconciled.
- Overall productive stand life conflicts between sources (decline
  after as few as two seasons in one detailed trial, versus a broader
  four-to-eight-year range from other sources) — this project would
  benefit from modeling stand decline as disease-driven rather than a
  single fixed lifespan figure.
- A substantial number of agronomic values (nutritional composition,
  pollination detail, chill-hour requirement, soil pH range, water-use
  stage timing, and labor-hour figures) are carried over by analogy
  from the related red-fruited crop rather than independently sourced
  for this species, since dedicated research on this crop specifically
  is much more limited. A composition analysis and dedicated labor-cost
  study specific to this crop would be the most valuable replacements.
- Nutrient uptake per tonne of fruit was not researched.
- Overall productive lifespan estimates remain only roughly bounded
  rather than precisely sourced.
- Photoperiod sensitivity for flower bud initiation has not been
  directly studied in this species; the red-raspberry analogy used
  here, and the primocane-fruiting mechanism in this species' own
  fall-fruiting cultivars, are both drawn from indirect sourcing.
- The species-specific "None" salinity rating found this pass came from
  a third-party index attributing the figure to USDA PLANTS, not from
  the USDA PLANTS record itself opened directly — worth confirming
  against the primary database directly in a future pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Nutritional composition, pollination detail, soil pH range, and
chill-hour requirement are carried over by analogy from the related
red-fruited crop, since a dedicated composition analysis and chill
study specific to this species were not available in the sources
used. Salinity rating is the one exception as of the 2026-11-09 pass:
it is now sourced species-specifically from USDA PLANTS (via a third-
party index), not carried over by analogy.

A world-average yield figure is derived from one major Western US
growing state's average yield over roughly a decade, since no broader
international statistics exist for this crop specifically (unlike many
crops, international agricultural databases do not track it
separately).

Good, exceptional, and poor yield tiers are derived respectively from a
documented typical regional yield range's midpoint, a detailed
university trial's best-cultivar second-year potential yield, and the
same Western growing state's weakest documented years.

Growth-cycle length to first harvest is derived from two documented
university trial planting-to-first-harvest timelines, averaged.

Water-use stage timing, a seasonal water-use-days index, and root-zone
depth are carried over by analogy from the related red-fruited crop's
own water-use estimates, which themselves draw on the same standard
international berry-bush crop-coefficient curve; maximum fruit-standing
time and post-harvest quality-decay rate are likewise carried over by
analogy, given similarly high perishability.

Water-stress and waterlogging tolerance ratings, and associated
drought-buffer figures, are estimated judgment calls based on this
crop's documented tendency toward smaller fruit in dry years even under
irrigation, and its documented susceptibility to root rot in wet soil.

Planting density per hectare is derived by unit-converting several
documented trial spacing figures across multiple university studies.

Labor-hour figures across establishment, cultural work, harvest, and
post-harvest handling are estimated from the related red-fruited
crop's labor pattern, adjusted upward for cultural work (given the
multi-pass summer-tipping requirement this crop specifically needs)
and recalculated for harvest based on an assumed hand-picking rate
reflecting this crop's smaller fruit size.

A representative storage-life figure is derived from the documented
range for all raspberry-type fruit in a standard commercial storage
reference that explicitly covers this species.

A peak-yield-per-day figure is derived by dividing the world-average
yield tier by one documented cultivar's typical harvest-window length
in days.
