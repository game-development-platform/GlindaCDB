## Naming and scope

*Garcinia mangostana* L. (Clusiaceae, also called Guttiferae); synonym
*Mangostana garcinia* Gaertn. [1][2][3]. The cultivated tree is now
treated as *G. mangostana* var. *mangostana* [2][4].
- **Names:** mangosteen, purple mangosteen, "queen of fruits" [1][3].
  Manggis (Malay, Indonesian, Dutch); mangkhut or mang khút (Thai); mang
  cut (Vietnamese); mangostan (Spanish, Philippines); mangostanier and
  mangoustanier (French); mangostao (Portuguese) [1][3][5][6].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Mangosteen" for the whole genus:** the name is sometimes used for
    any *Garcinia*, as in button mangosteen [2].
  - **"Gamboge":** growers use it for the yellow latex and the latex
    disorder (Finding 4) [3][5].
- **Range:** the original range is uncertain because the tree has been
  planted so widely [2][4]. Proposed homes include the Sunda Islands and
  Moluccas [1], the Malay Peninsula and Borneo [2], and the Malay
  Peninsula, eastern Indonesia and Borneo [3]. Morton notes apparently
  wild trees in Kemaman, Malaya [1].
  - **Cultivated:** Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, Myanmar, Malaysia,
    Indonesia and the Philippines. Smaller plantings exist in Sri Lanka,
    southern India, northern Australia, Hawaii, Central America, the
    Caribbean, Colombia and Ecuador, and tropical Africa [1][2][3][6].
  - **Latitude:** commercial growing is generally within 10° of the
    equator, but extends to 18°S in Queensland and 22°N in Hawaii [3].
    Morton reports that all attempts north of 20° had failed [1].
- **Origin (disputed):**
  - **Hybrid theory:** the familiar claim is that mangosteen is an
    allotetraploid hybrid of *G. hombroniana* and *G. malaccensis*. This
    comes from Richards (1990) and is repeated in horticultural works
    [3][4].
  - **Yao et al. (2023):** a critical review found the theory unsupported
    [4]:
    - Molecular studies show *G. hombroniana* is only distantly related.
    - Chromosome counts in mangosteen vary too widely to support any
      ploidy-based origin.
    - The wild *G. malaccensis* is now ranked as *G. mangostana* var.
      *malaccensis*, and a third variety, var. *borneensis*, was
      described from Sabah and Kalimantan [2][4]. Both wild varieties
      have male trees [2][4]. Var. *malaccensis* is the leading candidate
      as the sole progenitor [4].
    - The review proposes that mangosteen arose at the edge between
      forest and village orchards ("dusun") [4].
- **Relatives in this project:**
  - **Same genus, other categories:** `kokum.md` (*G. indica*) and
    `malabar-tamarind.md` (*G. gummi-gutta*) are souring spices, filed
    under spice/herb [14]. Kokum is dioecious, with a troublesome share
    of male seedlings; mangosteen has no male trees [2][14].
  - **Orchard companions:** mangosteen is grown in mixed orchards with
    durian (`durian.md`) and rambutan (`rambutan.md`) in Thailand [3].
    Durian and mangosteen are paired culturally as king and queen of
    fruits [2]. Neither crop is related.
  - **Trade statistics:** FAOSTAT reports mangosteen together with
    mango and guava, so no clean world figure exists (see `mango.md`)
    [14].
- **Proposed:**
  - **`button_mangosteen`** (*G. prainiana*): an edible *Garcinia*
    fruit [2]. **Now researched (passed its gate check 2026-11-25; see
    `button-mangosteen.md`):** a small orange thin-skinned Malesian fruit
    that, unlike true mangosteen, is dioecious and needs both sexes (growers
    hand-pollinate), bears young, and is itself used as a rootstock for true
    mangosteen.
  - **`charichuelo`** (*G. madruno*): a tropical American *Garcinia*
    fruit [2]. Several former *Rheedia* fruits of South America are now
    in *Garcinia* [3]. No farming data were read this pass.
  - **`garcinia_dulcis`** (*G. dulcis*): introduced across the Pacific
    as a fruit suited to village production [3]. No farming data were
    read this pass.
  - **Not proposed:** the wild varieties *malaccensis* and *borneensis*.
    They are forest trees with no farming data [4]. Other Pacific species
    (*G. pseudoguttifera*, *G. hollrungii*, *G. jaweri*, *G. floribunda*)
    are named only as edible relatives [3].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a seed-grown crop that behaves like a clone, but not quite.**
- **Apomixis:** the "seeds" are adventitious (nucellar) embryos formed
  without fertilization, so seedlings are copies of the mother tree
  [1][2][5]. Some seeds hold more than one embryo [1].
- **No male trees:** the species is dioecious, but male trees of the
  cultivated variety are unknown [2][4]. Pierre examined about 1,500
  trees in southern Vietnam and found no male [4]. The male-type
  flowers carry anthers with no pollen [1]. Diczbalis describes the
  tree as producing only female flowers [3].
  - **Old claim:** a 1919 report said growers culled male trees. Yao
    et al. judge it dubious, and say no orchard is known to do this [4].
- **Not strictly asexual:** Hambali and Natawijaya (2016, as cited in
  [4]) found triploids (2n = 72) among seedlings. They made triploids by
  pollinating mangosteen with *G. malaccensis* pollen, so the two can
  cross. Yao et al. therefore describe apomixis as facultative [4].
- **Some variation:** a Malaysian survey found 16 distinct types among
  830 accessions [3]. Fingerprinting in Australia found three types, one
  from Borneo with elliptical fruit and sparse, upright foliage [3].
  Corner knew one distinct form, from the Sulu Islands: larger fruit, a
  thicker rind and more acid flesh [1].
- **Seed is recalcitrant:**
  - Viable 3-5 weeks inside the fruit, but about 5 days once removed [1].
  - Up to 3 months in damp peat, sphagnum or coir in airtight
    containers [1].
  - 1-2 months in moist storage at 20 °C; 10 °C is damaging [6].
  - Germination is 20-22 days to sprout and complete in 43 days. Soaking
    for 24 hours speeds it [1]. Germination is related to seed weight,
    so only plump seeds are sown [1].
- **Grafting:** grafts bear earlier but give smaller fruit and a weaker,
  badly shaped tree, so grafting is rarely used commercially [3]. In
  Florida, approach grafting has worked only onto *G. xanthochymus*
  [1]. Cuttings and air layers usually fail [1].

**Finding 2 — one of the longest waits of any fruit tree.**
- **Nursery:** 2-3 years before planting out [3]. Morton gives 2 years
  or more to reach 30 cm [1]. Diczbalis prefers nursery trees about 100
  cm tall [3].
- **First fruit after planting:**
  - North Queensland: 6 years earliest, 8-10 most common [3].
  - Hawaii: 10 years or longer [3].
  - Morton: 7-9 years, but usually 10 or even 20 [1]. Thailand: 12-20
    years [1][6]. Panama and Puerto Rico: 6 years from large seed with
    good care [1].
- **Long rise to peak (fruits per tree):**
  - First crop: 200-300 [1][2].
  - Full-grown tree: about 500 [1][2].
  - By the 30th year of bearing: 1,000-2,000 [1].
  - Madras, trees aged 20-45: 2,000-3,000 [1].
  - Trees still fruit at 100 years [1][2].
- **Economics:**
  - North Queensland gross margins show no positive return within 20
    years [3].
  - A 10 ha Malaysian orchard takes 15-19 years to pay back [3].
  - A 50 kg tree grosses about US$500 a year in Australia, but about
    US$50 in Thailand [3]. Diczbalis attributes the gap mainly to labour
    costs.
- **Breeding:** a shorter juvenile period is the most important research
  aim [3]. Breeding is slow because of the long cycle [2].

**Finding 3 — flowering needs a dry spell.**
- **Trigger:** flowers appear at the shoot tip, generally after 3-4
  rain-free weeks [3]. A Thai study (search snippet) gives about 20 days
  [13].
  - **Thai practice (snippet):** trees are dried until the leaves wilt,
    then sprinkler-irrigated to saturate the soil and induce flowering
    [13].
- **Seasons:**
  - North Queensland: the main flowering is November-December after 2-3
    dry months, with a minor one in August [3].
  - Bimodal rainfall gives two flowerings a year [3]. In India there are
    two fruiting seasons, July-October and April-June [1].
- **Flower to harvest:** 100-120 days, varying with temperature [3].
  Wikipedia gives 5-6 months for fruit maturation, which conflicts [2].
- **Bearing is irregular** from tree to tree and year to year [1][3].
  Thailand's 2024 crop was low because flowering was poor after long dry
  spells. The trees then rested, and the 2025 forecast was 35% higher
  (Finding 6) [9].
- **Not re-read:** the earlier version of this doc described a Thai
  irrigation trial in which overlong stress reduced flowering. That
  study was not identified or re-read this pass, so its claims are
  dropped here and requested (Open research questions).

**Finding 4 — wet weather near harvest spoils the inside of the fruit.**
Two preharvest disorders are the main quality problems [3][8]:
- **Gamboge (yellow latex):** latex seeps into the aril, turning it
  yellow and bitter, and onto the skin [5]. It hardens the rind and
  makes the aril hard to separate [5].
  - **Cause:** HB66 calls it a preharvest disorder of unknown cause [5].
    Morton links external latex to heavy, continuous rain, storm
    bruising and strong sun [1]. Diczbalis links it to insect injury and
    high soil moisture, and to rupture after fruit fall [3]. A Thai
    study (snippet) found 87.7% of fruit affected, possibly from a
    sudden rise in soil moisture [13].
  - **Eating quality:** Morton says external gamboge does not affect
    it [1]. HB66 says latex in the aril makes it bitter [5]. These may
    describe external versus internal latex.
  - **Mitigation:** enough calcium and enough sunlight during fruiting
    [8].
- **Translucent flesh (nue-kaew):** segments become glassy and crisp,
  with lower sugar and acidity [5][8].
  - **Cause:** believed to follow heavy rain during fruit growth, even
    just before harvest [5]. It is reported to rise with excess water
    around 9 weeks after flowering, and with sudden rewatering after the
    soil dries to -100 kPa [7]. Aizat et al. describe lignification
    under low oxygen caused by capillary water [8].
  - **Sorting:** affected fruit sink in water (specific gravity above
    1.0), so it is removed by flotation [5].
- **Conflict (Matra et al. 2019, Bogor):** treatments started 7 weeks
  after flowering [7]. Covering the soil with plastic and not irrigating
  gave the most translucent flesh (60%). The control gave 26.7%,
  irrigation 18.3%, and cover plus daily watering 20.0% [7]. This
  contradicts a simple excess-water cause. The earlier version of this
  doc suspected a garbled summary; the primary paper confirms the
  result. One explanation is sudden wetting after drying, but the
  sources leave it unresolved.
- **Prevention (snippet):** in a 2005 Songkhla trial on 14-year-old
  trees, disorders were avoided by holding a mild soil-water deficit of
  about -70 kPa before harvest [13].

**Finding 5 — a thick, latex-filled rind controls harvest and storage.**
- **Fruit:**
  - Size: 3.4-7.5 cm across [1][6], or 4-7 cm [5].
  - Rind: 6-10 mm thick [1][5].
  - Weight: averages 70-125 g. A Chanthaburi prize fruit weighed 230 g
    [3].
  - Flesh: 31% of the fruit [1], in 4-8 segments [1][5].
  - Seed count: the number of stigma lobes on top equals the number of
    segments. More segments mean fewer seeds, so buyers count the lobes
    [1][2].
- **Drop damage:** a 10 cm drop can harden the rind at the impact point
  within 24 h [5]. Damage in harvest and marketing can affect more than
  20% of fruit [5]. Fruit is picked with a pole and catching bag and
  never allowed to fall [1][3].
- **Ripening:**
  - **Climacteric:** about 29 nL/kg/h ethylene, and treating with
    ethylene brings on its own ethylene production [5]. Diczbalis and
    Aizat et al. agree [3][8]. Wikipedia calls it non-climacteric
    without a citation [2], which conflicts with the postharvest
    sources.
  - **Picking stage:** pink to light red [3]. The minimum harvest stage
    for high quality is distinct pink-red spots over the whole surface
    [5]. Greener fruit never reaches full flavour [5].
- **Storage:** see Agronomic data. The key behaviour is chilling injury.
  Below 10 °C the rind hardens and darkens quickly once the fruit is
  back at room temperature [5]. Fruit can be eaten after long cold
  storage while looking unsaleable.

**Finding 6 — production is large, and yields are modest and volatile.**
- **Thailand:** 301,649 t from 399,020 rai in 2024, or 756 kg/rai. The
  2025 forecast is 407,634 t at 1,037 kg/rai, with the bearing area down
  1.4% [9]. That is about 4.7 and 6.5 t/ha (see Estimates).
  - **Area shrinking:** low prices, especially for low-grade fruit, have
    led growers to remove mangosteen from mixed durian orchards [9].
  - **Exports (snippet):** 284,860 t worth US$491 million in 2024, 91%
    to China [13].
- **Indonesia:** 117,595 t in 2011 rising to 203,100 t in 2015, with
  98,294.6 t exported in 2015 [7].
- **World:** nearly 700,000 t in 2017 [8].
- **Orchard yields:**
  - 10-15-year-old trees: 40-70 kg a year; some above 150 kg [3].
  - The best North Queensland orchard in a two-year survey (2,000 trees)
    gave 10 t/ha, or 50 kg per tree [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** slow-growing evergreen tree, 6-25 m [1][2],
  up to 30 m [3], with a pyramidal crown [1][3]. All parts hold yellow
  latex [1][3]. It flushes 3-4 times a year, or 2-3 commonly in young
  trees [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial; one or two seasons a year (Finding 3)
  [1][3]. Hand-picked 2-3 times a week through the season [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 6-10 years after planting typically;
  up to 20 (Finding 2) [1][3]. About 8-13 years from sowing (see
  Estimates).
- **Climate zones:** humid, ultra-tropical lowland [1][3].
  - **Temperature:**
    - Morton: cannot tolerate below 4.4 °C or above 37.8 °C, and
      nursery seedlings are killed at 7.2 °C [1].
    - Diczbalis: minimum tolerated 8 °C; mean annual 22-28 °C; coldest-
      month mean minimum 15-25 °C; hottest-month mean maximum 23-35 °C
      [3].
    - Wikipedia: growth slows below 20 °C; ideal 25-35 °C with humidity
      over 80%; maximum 38-40 °C; minimum 3-5 °C [2].
    - The minimum differs by several degrees between sources.
  - **Rainfall:**
    - Morton: at least 1,270 mm a year [1][6].
    - Diczbalis: 1,500-5,000 mm [3].
  - **Dry season:**
    - Diczbalis (text): 3-5 weeks [3].
    - Diczbalis (climate table): 1-2 months with under 40 mm [3].
  - **Elevation:**
    - Malaya: below 450 m [1].
    - Madras: 76-1,500 m [1][6].
    - Tropics generally: up to 1,000 m [3].
    - Hawaii: above 300 m it grows but does not fruit reliably [3].
  - **Chill:** none; frost-intolerant.
  - **Light:**
    - Seedlings need at least 50% shade, and young trees moderate shade
      [3].
    - Photosynthesis saturates at low light, like rainforest understorey
      trees [3].
    - In the Northern Territory, trees struggle in full dry-season sun
      even with irrigation [3].
- **Composition:**
  - **USDA (canned in syrup, per 100 g; no fresh-fruit entry)** [10]:
    - Energy and proximates: 73 kcal; water 80.9 g; protein 0.41 g; fat
      0.58 g; carbohydrate 17.91 g; fibre 1.8 g.
    - Minerals: calcium 12 mg; potassium 48 mg.
    - Vitamins: vitamin C 2.9 mg; folate 31 µg.
  - **Morton (fresh, per 100 g edible; Philippines and Washington
    analyses)** [1]:
    - Energy and proximates: 60-63 kcal; moisture 80.2-84.9 g; protein
      0.5-0.6 g; fat 0.1-0.6 g; carbohydrate 14.3-15.6 g; fibre 5.0-5.1
      g.
    - Ascorbic acid 1-2 mg.
    - Sugars 16.4-16.8 g, which exceeds total carbohydrate (see
      Estimates).
  - **Diczbalis:** 76 kcal and 5 g fibre, citing an older USDA table [3].
- **Edibility:** the white aril is raw-edible [1][2][5]. Seeds are eaten
  after boiling or roasting [1][6]. The rind is not traditionally eaten
  because it is hard, astringent and bitter [11].
- **Pollination:** none needed; fruit sets apomictically (Finding 1)
  [1][2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Flowering is triggered by a
  dry spell, not day length (Finding 3) [3]. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - Thailand: about 4.7 t/ha in 2024 and 6.5 t/ha forecast for 2025
    [9].
  - Best North Queensland orchard: 10 t/ha [3].
  - Per tree: 40-70 kg, and above 150 kg in high years [3].
  - Fruit counts in Finding 2 [1].
  - World yield cannot be derived from FAOSTAT [14].
- **Water use:** Not sourced as a crop coefficient.
  - **Irrigation threshold:** Diczbalis's text says to irrigate when
    monthly rain is under 150 mm. The same profile's table says under
    100 mm [3].
  - **Wet sites:** Morton reports the most fruitful trees on stream and
    canal banks with roots almost constantly wet [1]. Panama groves
    thrive in swamps drained by ditches [1]. Yet standing water kills
    nursery seedlings, and Morton advises a water table about 1.8 m down
    [1][6].
- **Propagation:** by apomictic seed (Finding 1) [1][2][3].
  - **Seed sowing:** in tall pots over 30 cm deep, for the long taproot
    [3].
  - **Transplanting:** hard, because of the delicate taproot and few
    side roots. It is not attempted after 60 cm [1].
  - **Clonal risk:** no virus degeneration was sourced. Seedlings carry
    the mother tree's type, so buyers ask about the mother tree's fruit
    before buying seed [3].
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. Labour-intensive steps:
  - **Picking:** by pole, 2-3 times a week [3].
  - **Cleaning:** latex is scraped off the skin, the fruit brushed and
    washed [5], and the calyx cleaned of ants [3].
  - **Grading:** by size and colour [5], and flotation-sorted for
    translucent flesh [5].
- **Soil:**
  - **Preferred:** deep, well-drained, moisture-retentive soils rich in
    organic matter, such as river loams [3], sandy loam or laterite
    [1][6].
  - **Unsuitable:** limestone, sandy alluvium, and sand low in humus
    [1][2].
  - **pH:** North Queensland trees grow at pH 4.8-7.6, with 1.5-7.9%
    organic matter [3].
  - **Salinity:** sensitive. Trees must be sheltered from salt spray and
    saline soil or water [1][6]. No threshold sourced.
  - **Fertilizer:** 3-6 kg per mature tree a year of 12:12:17 NPK [3].
    Indian growers give 45-90 kg manure and 4.5-6.8 kg peanut meal [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** recommendations vary widely [5].
  - **Cold:**
    - 4-6 °C at 85-90% RH for 7 weeks [5]; 3.9-5.6 °C keeps quality for
      49 days in Indian trials [1].
    - Storage at 4 or 8 °C hardens the skin, though the flesh may still
      be acceptable after 44 days [5].
    - 5 °C above 85% RH for 4 weeks [3].
  - **Current practice:** 12-14 °C, about 20 days without chilling
    injury [5]. 13 °C at 85-90% RH gives 14-25 days [5].
  - **Room temperature:**
    - 7-10 days at tropical temperatures [3].
    - 20-25 days in dry, warm, closed storage [1].
  - **Chilling threshold:** 4-8 °C, injury after 39-47 days, with
    hardening and browning of the cortex [5].
  - **Controlled atmosphere:** 5% O2 and 5% CO2 for 1 month [5].
  - **Respiration:** 21 mg CO2/kg/h at 25 °C [5].
  - **Humidity:** low humidity makes the rind harden fast [3]. Fruit on
    display should not be misted [5].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** 10.7-12 m [1][6]; 6 × 9 m or 7 × 7 m, about 50 m² per
    tree [3]. Wikipedia's 20-40 m [2] is out of line with both.
  - **Planting:** pits of 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.3 m, prepared 30 days ahead, at
    the start of the rains [1][6].
  - **Shade:** for 3-5 years [1]. Bananas, papaya, pigeon pea or shade
    trees are used as nurse crops [2][3].
- **Cultivars:** effectively one clone, with a few seed-line types
  (Finding 1) [1][3]. No named cultivars were sourced this pass.
- **Weather response:**
  - **Cold:** see Climate zones. Brief cold spells mostly damage young
    growth, but prolonged frost usually kills a mature tree [2].
  - **Heat and sun:** leaves and fruit scorch at 38-40 °C [2]. Exposed
    fruit may exude latex [1]. Canker follows sunburn after sudden loss
    of shade [3].
  - **Drought:** young trees are drought-sensitive in their first 5
    years [2]. Mulch revived dehydrating trees in Liberia [1].
  - **Wind:** shelter is needed [1].
  - **Rain at harvest:** quality loss (Finding 4).
  - **Frost as a quality event:** not applicable.
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation, with a dry spell for flowering
  and no sudden wetting before harvest (Findings 3-4). No flooded
  culture, though trees tolerate wet banks [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Picking:** at pink to light red, a few days before full ripeness
    [3][5]. Dark red to purple fruit is for local, immediate sale
    [3].
  - **Ripening is successive** across the crop, hence repeated passes
    [3].
  - **Seasons:** Thailand May-September [2]; Sri Lanka May-July in the
    lowlands and later at altitude [1][6]; Puerto Rico July-August in
    sun and November-December in shade [1].
  - **Thai exports:** strong in Europe May-July [3].
  - **Marketing window:** fresh fruit is sold for only 6-10 weeks [2].
- **Soil biology:** brown root disease (*Phellinus noxius*) spreads
  through infected wood and stumps [2]. No mycorrhizal or replant data
  sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** no invasive listing found in this
  pass. Diczbalis says it is not invasive, because its seed and
  seedlings need special care [3]. IUCN lists it as Data Deficient [2].
  - **Quarantine:**
    - It is a fruit-fly host [3][5].
    - The US barred fresh imports until 2007 without fumigation or
      irradiation [2]. The USDA now accepts irradiated Thai fruit [3].
    - Irradiation at 300 Gy is proposed against fruit flies [5], and 250
      Gy against the mealybug *Exallomochlus hispidus* [8].
    - Alternatively, fruit is cut open, inspected and shipped frozen
      [5].
  - **Pests:**
    - Mealybug: a major export threat [8].
    - Thrips and mites: rind scarring [3].
    - Leaf-eaters on young trees [1][3]; a stem-tunnelling ant in
      Puerto Rico [1].
    - Monkeys, bats and rats take ripe fruit [1].
  - **Diseases:**
    - *Pestalotiopsis* leaf blight, canker and dieback [2][3].
    - Thread blight [1][2].
    - Diplodia fruit rot [2][5].
- **Controlled environment:** fruiting trees were grown in English
  greenhouses by 1855 [1][2]. Some were grown in containers under glass
  in Florida [1]. Its low light saturation [3] suggests shade-house
  culture is feasible, but this is an inference, not sourced. Tree size
  and the 10-year juvenile period are the obvious limits.

## Hazards

- **Peel-extract supplements:** the EFSA panel (adopted September 2026)
  could not establish the safety of a peel extract proposed for food
  supplements [11].
  - **Extract and dose:** 44-52% xanthones, at up to 200 mg a day [11].
  - **Rat results:**
    - Liver: raised liver enzymes and bilirubin at the lowest dose.
    - Kidney: markers raised from the mid dose.
    - General: lower body weight in all dosed groups.
  - **Genotoxicity:** could not be ruled out [11].
- **Juice supplement case:** severe lactic acidosis in a patient using
  mangosteen juice as a dietary supplement [12] (abstract only).
- **Pesticide residues:** a Thai study found carbofuran, chlorothalonil,
  dimethoate and metalaxyl above residue limits on fruit [8]. They wash
  off with 10 minutes' soaking and rubbing, and were low in the aril
  [8].
- **Heavy metals:** one Philippine mangosteen herbal product exceeded
  the cadmium limit of 0.42 mg/kg. A peel powder tested free of cadmium
  and lead [8].
- **Ordinary fruit:** no toxicity sourced for the aril. Seeds are eaten
  boiled or roasted [1]. No seed toxicity data were found.
- **Physical:** latex and the purple rind juice stain skin and fabric
  [1][2].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced. Postharvest rots (*Botryodiplodia*,
  *Diplodia*, *Pestalotia*, *Phomopsis*, *Rhizopus*) decay the aril
  [5], but no mycotoxin data were found.
- **Medicinal claims:** rind for dysentery, diarrhoea and skin
  complaints; leaf and bark for fever and thrush; root for menstruation
  [1][6]. There is no high-quality clinical evidence for these [2].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the peel-extract toxicity and the supplement
  case. These need a supplement or extract hazard field, separate from
  eating the fruit. The project's hazard model has no home for them yet.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** pole-picked at pink to light red [3] → latex
  scraped off, washed with a soft brush [5] → calyx cleaned of ants and
  dirt with compressed air or mild detergent [3] → float-sorted, graded
  by size and colour [5] → packed [3][5] → room-cooled [5] → stored at
  12-14 °C [5].
  - **Packing:** single-layer 2.25 kg cartons [5]; trays of 24-42 fruit
    per 3 kg, or 20-24 fruit [3][5]. In Southeast Asia, baskets or
    bundles of 10-25 [5].
  - **First grade:** at least 70 g, blemish-free, clean calyx [3].
  - **Byproducts:** blemished but sound fruit is sold in bulk [3]. Some
    Thai exporters lacquer fruit to hide thrips scarring [5].
- **Chain (export quarantine):** irradiation, or cut-inspect-freeze
  (Biosecurity, above) [3][5].
- **Chain (canned):** segments → 40% syrup → sterilized only 5 minutes.
  Longer pasteurizing loses the delicate flavour; acid fruit is best
  [1][6].
- **Chain (preserves):**
  - Malaya: seedless segments boiled with equal sugar and cloves for
    15-20 minutes for jam [1].
  - Philippines: segments boiled in brown sugar, sometimes with the
    seeds [1].
- **Chain (puree):** flesh → puree → sorbet, fruit wine, conserves [3].
  Thai processing also makes candy and wine [3].
- **Chain (rind):**
  - **Jelly:** rind → 6% salt treatment against astringency → purple
    pectin jelly [1][6].
  - **Remedies:** dried and powdered rind is a traditional remedy [1].
  - **Supplements:** whole fruit or rind → "superfruit" juice and
    xanthone extracts [3][11]. In Australia, returns for juice fruit
    were below fresh-market returns [3].
  - **Tanning and dye:** the rind's 7-14% catechin tannin tans leather
    in China, and gives a black dye [1]. In Indonesia, peel extract dyes
    ikat and batik brown, purple or red [2].
- **Chain (seed):** seed → boiled or roasted → eaten [1].
- **Timber:** dark brown, heavy, moderately durable; used for spear
  handles, rice pounders, building and cabinetwork [1][6]. Non-bearing
  Thai trees are felled for it [1].
- **Other:** twigs are used as chew sticks in Ghana [1][2].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.
  - **Textile motif:** the calyx is a Malay songket pattern, *tampuk
    manggis*, said to symbolize a pleasant character [2].
  - **Food belief:** Chinese food therapy counts it "cooling," to
    balance "heaty" durian [2].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** frozen whole fruit for retail [2];
  fresh-cut segments ("some potential") [5]; xanthone extraction methods
  [8].

## Estimates and derivations

**Thai national yield, about 4.7 t/ha (2024) and 6.5 t/ha (2025
forecast).** OAE figures [9], with 1 rai = 1,600 m² = 0.16 ha:
- **2024:** 756 kg/rai ÷ 0.16 = 4,725 kg/ha. Check: 399,020 rai × 0.16 =
  63,843 ha, and 301,649 t ÷ 63,843 ha = 4.72 t/ha.
- **2025:** 1,037 kg/rai ÷ 0.16 = 6,481 kg/ha.
These are national averages over bearing area. The best managed orchard
sourced gave 10 t/ha [3].

**Yield per tree from fruit counts, about 35-63 kg for an average
full-grown tree.** 500 fruits [1] × 70-125 g [3] = 35-62.5 kg.
- **Peak trees:** 1,000-2,000 fruits [1] give 70-250 kg.
- **Check:** Diczbalis's 40-70 kg for 10-15-year-old trees and "above
  150 kg" [3] fit this range.
- **Caveat:** the inputs come from different places.

**Trees per hectare, about 69-204.**
- **Diczbalis spacing:** 7 × 7 m = 49 m² → 10,000 ÷ 49 = 204 trees/ha.
  6 × 9 m = 54 m² → 185 trees/ha [3].
- **Morton spacing:** 10.7 m square → 87 trees/ha; 12 m → 69 trees/ha
  [1].
- **Check:** the North Queensland orchard's 10 t/ha at 50 kg per tree
  [3] implies 10,000 ÷ 50 = 200 trees/ha, consistent with 7 × 7 m.

**Edible flesh per fruit, about 22-39 g.** 31% edible [1] × 70-125 g
[3] = 21.7-38.8 g.

**Years from sowing to first fruit, about 8-13 typical.** Nursery 2-3
years [3] plus 6-10 years in the field [1][3] gives 8-13 years. Poor
sites add up to 10 more [1].

**Energy checks.**
- **Morton fresh:** low ends: 0.5 × 4 + 0.1 × 9 + 14.3 × 4 = 2.0 + 0.9 +
  57.2 = 60.1 kcal. High ends: 0.6 × 4 + 0.6 × 9 + 15.6 × 4 = 2.4 + 5.4 +
  62.4 = 70.2 kcal. Her 60-63 kcal [1] lies within these bounds.
- **Morton sugars:** her 16.4-16.8 g of sugars cannot fit within
  14.3-15.6 g of carbohydrate. The analyses likely differ in basis, so
  the sugar figure is not used.
- **USDA canned:** 0.41 × 4 + 0.58 × 9 + 17.91 × 4 = 78.5 kcal, against
  the stated 73 kcal [10]. The gap reflects USDA's own conversion
  factors. The values include syrup.
