## Naming and scope

The purple mangosteen (Clusiaceae/Guttiferae family), known by various
local names across Southeast Asia [1][2][3]. It is thought to be an
allotetraploid hybrid of two other *Garcinia* species, with chromosome
counts recorded from 56 to about 130 [4][5]. No wild population is
known; it is a cultigen of the Sunda Islands, Moluccas, and Malay
Peninsula [1][2]. In trade, "mangosteen" unambiguously means this one
species.

Several other *Garcinia* fruits exist (button mangosteen, charichuelo,
and others), none of which has its own crop entry in this project yet;
two further relatives used as spice and acidulant crops are tracked
separately rather than here. No direct *Garcinia* sibling has been
researched by this project yet, so this entry is instead grounded
against a related tropical fruit crop this project has also
researched that shares a drought-triggered flowering mechanism and is
often grown in the same Southeast Asian orchards, sometimes
interplanted with mangosteen. Comparison values from that crop are
used only where explicitly flagged as an analogy below.

This crop passes the distinctiveness test easily against other fruit
crops in this project: it is the only apomictic (clonally
seed-propagated) crop researched so far, has a six-to-twenty-year
juvenile period, needs shade when young, and has a hard purple rind
with its own distinct quality disorders.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the seed is a clone, so the orchard is seed-grown but
behaves like a grafted one.** No sexual fertilization takes place in
this species. The "seeds" are adventitious (nucellar) embryos, and
seedlings are genetically identical to the mother tree [1][2][5].
Cultivated trees are effectively all female; male trees are unknown,
and the rare male-type flowers that do appear carry sterile pollen
[1][2][4]. Fruit sets without any pollination at all. The result is a
crop that is propagated by seed yet behaves, genetically, as a single
clone — one detailed flowering study treats the whole crop as "only one
cultivar," and a large Malaysian survey of hundreds of orchard
accessions found only a small number of genuinely distinct types [4][9].
The seed itself is recalcitrant: viable for three to five weeks while
still inside the fruit, but only about five days once removed, or up
to three months if kept moist in an airtight container [1][13].
Grafting exists (cleft grafting, or approach grafting onto other
*Garcinia* species) but brings real tradeoffs: grafted trees bear
sooner, but produce smaller fruit on a weaker, misshapen tree; a newer
"top grafting" technique is reported to cut the unproductive juvenile
phase substantially [1][4][5][14].

**Finding 2 — one of the longest juvenile periods of any fruit crop
this project has researched.** Seedlings need two to three years in a
shaded nursery before they reach planting size [4][5]. The first crop
comes six to ten years after field planting, and can take as long as
twenty years in poor conditions [1][4][5]. One detailed growers'
heuristic holds that the juvenile phase ends once the trunk carries a
specific number of lateral branch pairs [5]. A detailed economic study
in a Pacific-region growing trial found no positive financial return
within twenty years, and a Southeast Asian ten-hectare orchard took
fifteen to nineteen years to pay back its establishment cost [4]. Yields
keep rising for roughly thirty years: a first crop of a few hundred
fruits on a young tree grows to several thousand fruits a year on
trees thirty or more years old, with documented trees still fruiting at
a century old [1][2].

**Finding 3 — flowering needs a measured drought, with a real
optimum.** A short dry spell of roughly two to four weeks induces
flowering [9]. A detailed physiological study on mature trees found
that the shoot tip must first be at least ten to eleven weeks past its
last vegetative growth flush before drought stress can trigger flower
induction at all. The stress itself is applied until the last
internode visibly wilts, measured in that study by a specific plant
water-stress indicator; irrigating afterward brings flower buds within
about a week. Trees given this carefully managed stress-then-release
treatment bore several times as many fruit as unstressed trees in the
same trial — but overshooting the stress (holding it too long or too
severe) actually reduced flowering and caused leaf drop, suggesting
physical damage to the plant's internal water-transport tissue at the
shoot tip rather than a simple more-stress-is-better relationship [6].
Flower buds then take roughly a month to reach full bloom, and fruit is
ready some three to four months after flowering [4][5][6][10]. In
some regions trees flower twice a year where rainfall is bimodal [4].

**Finding 4 — rain near harvest spoils the inside of the fruit.** Two
pre-harvest disorders are the main quality problem in humid growing
regions [7][8]. The first is a rind-latex disorder in which internal
latex ducts in the rind rupture during rapid fruit growth, staining and
hardening both rind and flesh with bitter yellow resin; this is linked
to excess rain, wind, rough handling, and a specific insect pest,
though at least one source describes the cause as simply unknown — a
conflict not resolved here. Calcium is reported to strengthen the duct
walls and reduce the disorder. The second is a translucent-flesh
disorder, in which segments become water-soaked and later overly firm
and crisp, with reduced sugar and acid content; it is driven by excess
water uptake in a specific pre-harvest window beginning roughly two
months after bloom, with incidence varying widely across different
soil-water regimes in at least one controlled trial. The standard
advice is to maintain a mild soil-water deficit before harvest and to
irrigate only lightly, so that rain does not cause abrupt swings in soil
moisture. This makes mangosteen one of the clearer documented cases
where excess water late in fruit development is itself a named quality
loss, distinct from simple fruit cracking.

**Finding 5 — a hard, latex-filled rind decides handling and
storage.** The rind is several millimeters thick and makes up roughly
two-thirds of the fruit's weight, with edible flesh making up only
about a third [1][3][5]. Even a short drop can harden the rind at the
point of impact within a day, so fruit is picked with a pole and bag
and never allowed to fall; damage during harvest and marketing can
affect a substantial share of the crop [1][3][4]. The fruit is
climacteric, and ripening is triggered by ethylene; it is picked at a
pink-spotted to light-red stage and darkens to full purple within about
five days [3][4][5]. Documented storage regimes vary by temperature:
roughly two to three weeks under moderate cool refrigeration is current
commercial practice, while colder refrigeration can extend flesh
quality for many weeks, though the rind itself hardens and darkens
rapidly once removed from cold storage.

**Finding 6 — modest, irregular yields.** Documented national and
regional yields across major Southeast Asian growing countries cluster
around four to six tonnes per hectare, with a strong, well-managed
orchard reaching roughly ten tonnes per hectare; a detailed research-
station trial using managed drought-stress induction reached the
highest documented figure. National output in at least one major
producing country swings widely from year to year, and that country's
growing area has reportedly been shrinking as growers switch to a more
profitable tropical fruit crop this project has also researched
separately.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A slow-growing, erect evergreen tree, large
  at maturity, with yellow latex throughout all plant parts; one
  harvest a year following a dry-spell flowering trigger, or two where
  rainfall is bimodal, picked over several weeks in repeated passes.
- **Climate:** Humid tropical lowland, commercially grown mostly within
  about ten degrees of the equator, with documented commercial success
  somewhat further from the equator in a couple of specific growing
  regions, but failure well outside the tropics generally.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (the aril/flesh).
- **Pollination:** None needed — the crop is apomictic (clonally
  seed-propagated); fruit and seed set occur without any fertilization
  (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield sits around four to five
  tonnes per hectare; well-managed orchards reach roughly ten tonnes per
  hectare, and an exceptional research-station result using managed
  drought-stress induction reached notably higher; poorly managed or
  young unstressed orchards fall well below the average (Finding 6).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure
  exists for this crop; water-use estimates here are derived from a
  detailed Thai irrigation trial expressed as a fraction of pan
  evaporation, cross-checked by analogy against a related tropical fruit
  crop this project has also researched. The crop needs good drainage
  overall, yet some of the most productive documented trees grow on
  stream and canal banks with roots almost constantly wet — a genuine
  conflict in the source material, not resolved here. A deliberate
  dry-spell drought is required to trigger flowering (Finding 3), while
  a separate, much milder deficit is wanted just before harvest to avoid
  the rain-driven flesh and rind disorders in Finding 4.
- **Establishment:** Seed-propagated (apomictic), with seedlings raised
  in a shaded nursery for two to three years before field planting;
  grafting exists as an alternative but trades earlier bearing for
  smaller fruit and a weaker, more misshapen tree.
- **Labor:** Harvest labor is estimated from a documented hand-picking
  productivity figure; other labor tiers (pruning, post-harvest
  handling including latex scraping and float-sorting for the
  translucent-flesh disorder) are rough estimates scaled by analogy from
  a related tropical fruit crop this project has also researched, since
  no dedicated mangosteen labor-hour study was found.
- **Soil:** Deep, rich organic soil, sandy loam or laterite preferred;
  not adapted to limestone; a wide documented pH range across one
  growing region's orchard soils. Nutrient-removal figures are not
  directly sourced for this crop and are estimated by analogy to a
  related tropical fruit crop grown in the same orchards.
- **Stand life:** A notably long juvenile period (six to twenty years
  to first fruit depending on conditions); yields continue rising for
  roughly thirty years, and documented trees still bear fruit at a
  century old; bearing is irregular from tree to tree and season to
  season throughout the tree's life (Finding 2).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Root rots with no effective documented
  control; a dieback canker disease; sap-sucking insects and mites that
  scar the rind cosmetically; leaf-eating caterpillars on young trees;
  ants in the trunk and under the fruit calyx; and vertebrate pests
  (monkeys, bats, rats) taking ripe fruit.
- **Cultivars:** Essentially a single clone worldwide, given the
  apomictic propagation; a modest number of named local selections and
  released varieties differ mainly in fruit size, shape, and seediness,
  with most such differences only thinly sourced (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked with a pole and catching bag at a pink-
  spotted to light-red stage, never allowed to fall, two to three times
  a week across a multi-week window (Finding 5).
- **Storage:** Roughly two to three weeks under moderate cool
  refrigeration is current commercial practice; colder storage extends
  flesh quality considerably further at the cost of rind hardening and
  darkening once removed from the cold (Finding 5).

## Hazards

Rind-derived compounds (xanthones) used in concentrated juice or
extract supplements have been linked to at least one documented case of
severe lactic acidosis after many months of heavy daily use; ordinary
fruit-eating poses no such risk, since the compounds are concentrated
in rind-based preparations rather than the edible flesh. Condensed
tannins and saponins in the rind limit its use as a livestock feed
supplement unless dosed carefully. The fruit's own bitter yellow latex
(sometimes called "gamboge," a name it shares with an unrelated
purgative resin from a different *Garcinia* species) makes affected
segments bitter but is not otherwise known to be toxic. Seeds are eaten
after boiling or roasting with no documented toxicity.

rind-tannin livestock-feed limit are both deferred in fuller detail

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit is picked by pole and bag, then has its latex
  scraped off, is washed, calyx-cleaned, graded with float-sorting to
  catch the translucent-flesh disorder, packed, and cold-stored. Culled
  or fresh fruit is opened for its flesh segments, which can be made
  into jam, or pureed into sorbet or fruit wine. Seeds are boiled or
  roasted and eaten. Rind is sliced and sun-dried, then powdered as a
  traditional remedy, used for leather tanning and as a black dye, or
  fed to ruminants as a dosed supplement; rind can also be soaked in
  brine to remove astringency and made into a purplish pectin jelly.
  Prunings are used as fuel.
- Not covered by this research: canned-segment and frozen-whole-fruit
  processing for quarantine shipping; rind-based juice and xanthone-
  extract production in detail; irradiation treatment; the crop's
  timber, bark, and root uses in traditional medicine.

## Open research questions

- Storage-temperature and shelf-life figures conflict across sources
  (a moderate-cool regime giving about twenty days versus a colder
  regime reported to give many weeks) — not reconciled.
- Fruit composition figures (vitamin C content and calorie count in
  particular) conflict substantially between sources — not reconciled.
- The juvenile period to first fruiting is reported quite differently
  across sources (as short as five years in one source, as long as
  twenty in another) — not reconciled.
- Cold-tolerance thresholds for mature trees conflict across sources
  by several degrees — not reconciled.
- Waterlogging tolerance is genuinely unclear: one source describes
  some of the most productive trees growing with roots almost
  constantly wet beside water, while the same source elsewhere
  recommends a water table kept well below the root zone — not
  reconciled.
- The cause of the rind-latex-rupture disorder is disputed: one detailed
  source attributes it specifically to excess water, wind, and rough
  handling, while another major postharvest reference simply calls the
  cause unknown.
- One study of the translucent-flesh disorder appears, in a short
  secondary summary, to attribute a high incidence rate to covered soil
  without irrigation rather than to excess water — this appears to
  contradict the otherwise well-documented excess-water mechanism and
  may reflect a garbled summary rather than a genuine conflict; the full
  primary source was not reached to resolve it.
- No dedicated mangosteen labor-hour study (beyond a rough harvest
  productivity figure) was found; other labor tiers are estimated by
  analogy to a related tropical fruit crop.
- No dedicated mangosteen nutrient-removal study was found; current
  figures are estimated by analogy to the same related tropical fruit
  crop, grown in the same orchards.
- This crop's flowering response to drought has both a required minimum
  stress level and a documented overshoot penalty at excessive stress —
  a hump-shaped response rather than a simple threshold — which is
  harder to model than most of this project's other drought-flowering
  crops.
- No wild population of this species is known — it is believed to be a
  hybrid cultigen with no wild ancestor state — so it is not treated
  here as having any wild-gathered counterpart.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from field planting to first harvest are derived from a documented
range of tree ages at first fruiting (as young as six years in a best
case, more commonly eight to ten years in at least one well-studied
growing region) plus a documented three-to-four-month period from
flowering to fruit maturity; a much longer upper bound reflecting poor
growing conditions in some regions is treated as a separate, less
typical case rather than folded into the main range.

Yield tiers are derived from a historical Thai national figure,
cross-checked against a more recent national production-over-area
calculation from the same country; the good tier comes from a
well-documented Pacific-region orchard; the exceptional tier is derived
by converting a research-station trial's per-tree fruit count (from the
managed drought-stress study in Finding 3) into a per-hectare figure
using a documented average fruit weight and planting density; and the
poor tier is derived similarly from that same trial's unstressed
control trees, cross-checked against a historical Philippine national
figure that included a large share of non-bearing young trees.

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days are estimated by converting a documented
Thai irrigation trial's pan-evaporation-fraction schedule (which varies
by growth stage) into an approximate standard reference-evapotranspiration
-based crop coefficient, using an assumed pan-to-reference conversion
factor; this is checked against — and set slightly lower than — a
value used elsewhere in this project for a related tropical fruit crop,
reflecting this crop's slower, shade-adapted growth habit.

Effective rooting depth and soil-moisture-depletion allowance are
estimated judgementally, informed by documented minimum nursery soil
depth and the crop's preference for deep river-loam soils, and set
somewhat deeper than a related tropical fruit crop this project has
also researched whose roots are documented to concentrate very near the
surface. The resulting "days without water before yield penalty" figure
is derived from that assumed rooting depth and depletion allowance
divided by an estimated daily water-use rate, and happens to closely
match a documented regional irrigation practice of watering only once
rainfall has stopped for about a week.

Waterlogging tolerance is rated as a middle value specifically because
the source material conflicts (see Open research questions above):
some of the most productive documented trees tolerate nearly constant
root wetness, while other guidance recommends good drainage and a
lowered water table.

The growth-stage curve is estimated as evergreen and nearly flat, with
a deliberate dip corresponding to the induced flowering drought,
set at somewhat lower overall values than a related tropical fruit crop
this project has also researched, reflecting this crop's slower,
shade-adapted growth.

Nursery seed-rate and seedling-multiplication figures are derived from
a documented planting density, a documented average seed weight, and a
documented number of usable full seeds per fruit, with an allowance for
losses from this crop's recalcitrant (short-lived) seed viability.

Labor-hour figures are derived for harvest from a documented rough
hand-picking productivity rate; other labor tiers (pruning and
maintenance, post-harvest handling including latex scraping and float
sorting) are rough estimates scaled from a related tropical fruit crop's
documented labor split, adjusted for this crop's lighter pruning needs
and extra post-harvest cleaning and sorting steps.

Nutrient-removal rates per tonne of fruit and the optimal soil pH are
estimated by analogy to the same related tropical fruit crop grown in
the same orchards, since no dedicated mangosteen nutrient-removal study
was found; the pH figure is nudged toward the acidic end of a documented
observed range, reflecting the crop's documented intolerance of
limestone-derived soils.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (the multi-year ramp to peak
bearing, peak daily harvest rate, maximum days fruit can stand before
quality decline, and daily decay rate) are derived from the documented
multi-decade yield-ramp pattern in Finding 2, the adopted average yield
divided across a documented multi-week national harvest window, and the
documented few-days window in which picked fruit darkens from pink to
full purple, with the maximum standing-days figure reflecting that the
fruit is especially vulnerable to vertebrate pests and physical damage
if left too long.
