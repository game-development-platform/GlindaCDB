## Naming and scope

This entry covers the true species native to Korea, Japan, and China
[1][2]. The common trade and research name is badly confused: the large
majority of Korea's commercial "bokbunja" fields — well over 2,000
hectares and tens of thousands of farmers as of a recent count — are
actually a different, North American black-fruited crop this project
has also researched, confirmed by both DNA and anthocyanin-chemistry
evidence, with even a tested commercial juice brand turning out to be
that other species [1]. That commercially dominant fruit properly
belongs under this project's separate entry for that related crop; this
entry covers only the true, botanically distinct species.

A traditional unripe-fruit crude drug used in Korean herbal medicine is
specifically the unripe fruit of this true species per the Korean
pharmacopoeial standard; a similarly named crude drug in the Chinese
pharmacopoeial standard instead comes from yet another, different
*Rubus* species entirely, not covered by this entry.

The closely related North American black-fruited crop mentioned above
is the closest researched sibling; many growing values in this entry
are explicit analogies to that crop, flagged where used, since this
true species has comparatively little dedicated agronomic literature of
its own.

This crop is genuinely distinct from its North American sibling in
several ways the simulation models: it ripens several weeks later in
the same growing region; it is a more vigorous, thornier, and markedly
more disease-resistant bramble; its fruit is smaller and softer and
lacks the sibling species' distinctive flavor; and it carries a second,
earlier harvest stage entirely absent from the sibling crop — the
unripe green fruit is itself a recognized pharmacopoeial crude drug,
picked green, blanched, and dried. Hybrids between this species and its
North American sibling are highly sterile, confirming they are
genuinely distinct crops despite the shared common name.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two genuinely different crops share one common name.**
Fields, wine press-cake, and juice sold commercially in Korea under this
crop's common name have tested, in published research, as the North
American sibling species rather than this true species [1]. The true
species is identified by several consistent botanical and chemical
markers: distinctly pink-to-dark-red flower petals (versus white in the
sibling species), a different leaflet arrangement and count, glossier
fruit with less fine hair, and a documented absence of two specific
xylose-containing anthocyanin pigments that are present in the sibling
species, with two different anthocyanin pigments dominating instead
[1][2]. This true species is documented as the only black-fruited wild
raspberry native to Korea [9]. For a player in a Korea-like in-game
setting, buying plants under this crop's common name would in reality
most likely yield the sibling species rather than this true one — a
real-world labeling ambiguity worth noting even though this project
keeps the two as separate crop entries.

**Finding 2 — the unripe fruit is the medicine; the ripe fruit is the
wine.** The Korean pharmacopoeial crude drug made from this crop is
specifically the unripe fruit, harvested in early summer as it turns
from green to greenish-yellow, with stalks removed and the fruit then
steamed or blanched briefly before sun-drying; the dried drug is small,
hard, light, and slightly sour and astringent [6]. A standardized
unripe-fruit extract is marketed in Korea as a functional food
ingredient for cholesterol management, with at least one randomized
controlled trial finding a measurable cholesterol-lowering effect from
daily extract supplementation [7][11]. The ripe fruit, by contrast,
goes into a well-known fermented Korean fruit liquor (brewed from
berries and water, sometimes with added rice, at a documented moderate-
to-high alcohol content, produced in several specific regions), as well
as a simpler home-steeped liquor method combining fresh fruit, sugar,
and a distilled spirit over a multi-week steeping period [8][12].
Leaves and stems are also eaten as a folk side dish and made into tea
and other drinks [10].

**Finding 3 — a vigorous, disease-resistant bramble that ripens
notably late.** The plant is a shrub with flattened, curved prickles
and a waxy bloom on the stems, bearing flower clusters of several to
over thirty individual flowers [2]. Fruit is small, dark red to
purplish-black in color, with selected breeding clones documented
exceeding a specific larger fruit-size threshold [2][4]. Flowering and
fruiting timing varies by growing region, generally flowering in spring
and fruiting from early to late summer depending on latitude and
elevation, with Korean-grown fruit ripening notably later than the
sibling species grown in the same region [1][2][5]. The species grows
naturally across a very wide elevation range in thickets on slopes,
valleys, riverbanks, and roadsides [2]. Its outstanding disease
resistance and high productivity have made it a valued breeding donor
within the broader bramble-fruit breeding community, including for a
widely grown red-fruited relative [1][5].

**Finding 4 — detailed Korean cultivation practice exists, covering
fields of both species together.** A Korean organic cultivation guide
(which does not distinguish between this true species and its
commercially dominant North American look-alike) documents site
requirements (shelter from cold winter wind, avoiding regions with
sharp temperature swings, since the crop is notably cold-weak), soil
preparation (deep, organic, moisture-retentive, well-drained soil with
substantial compost and lime amendment), three propagation methods
(tip layering, root cuttings, and seed, with tip layering and root
cuttings both achieving high success rates while seed propagation is
slow and more variable), detailed planting density and trellis design,
a repeated-pinching cane-training schedule, typical time to first and
peak production, and a documented list of pests and diseases [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A perennial crown with biennial, thorny canes
  reaching one to three meters, whose tips can root naturally where
  they touch soil.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one summer crop per year on
  second-year canes, with two distinct harvest stages: an earlier
  unripe-fruit harvest (for the crude-drug product) and a later ripe-
  fruit harvest (for fresh eating, wine, and juice).
- **Climate:** Temperate to warm-temperate and montane subtropical,
  depending on growing region and elevation; notably weak in extreme
  winter cold.
- **Food status:** Ripe fruit is raw-edible; unripe fruit is not eaten
  raw and is used only as a processed crude drug.
- **Pollination:** Required, bee-pollinated by analogy to related
  bramble crops (not independently researched for this species
  specifically); flower clusters contain many individual flowers per
  cluster.
- **Yield / composition:** See Estimates below; a national yield figure
  exists but mixes in the commercially dominant, botanically distinct
  sibling species, so it should be read as a general regional-
  conditions bramble-fruit figure rather than a true-species-specific
  one.
- **Propagation:** Vegetative — tip layering and root cuttings are the
  standard, high-success methods; true seed is used only in breeding
  programs, with a documented moderate germination rate after cold
  stratification but a slow overall nursery timeline.
- **Water use:** A standard international crop-coefficient curve for
  berry bushes is used by direct analogy to the related North American
  sibling crop, since this true species has no dedicated water-use
  study of its own; moist, deep, well-drained soil is explicitly
  recommended, with the plant kept watered through fruit swell but
  never allowed to stand in water.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-cost study was found for this species;
  figures are estimated largely by analogy to the related sibling crop,
  adjusted for a repeated cane-pinching schedule and for the added
  unripe-fruit harvest pass.
- **Soil:** Deep (cultivated well below typical tillage depth), organic,
  well-drained soil; substantial compost and lime amendment is standard
  Korean practice to counter soil acidity; late-season nitrogen
  fertilization is specifically avoided, since it is linked to winter
  injury.
- **Stand life:** First crop typically arrives in the first or second
  year after planting, with peak production reached around the fourth
  year; overall long-term productive lifespan was not documented,
  though the species' strong disease resistance suggests a longer
  productive life than its more disease-prone North American sibling.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A clearwing moth borer (the main documented
  pest), a swift moth, a longhorn beetle, a fungal leaf spot, fungal
  anthracnose, a bacterial disease, and a witches'-broom disorder are
  documented — though these are documented for mixed fields of both
  this species and its commercially dominant sibling, not confirmed
  specifically for the true species alone.
- **Cultivars:** Cultivar literature is sparse; documented variation
  spans a wild baseline form, a densely hairy-leaved botanical variety
  adapted to higher elevation, a named Korean botanical form, several
  Korean breeding-program selections chosen for fruit size and yield,
  and naturally occurring orange/bicolored fruit-color variants.

## Hazards

No dietary toxicity hazard was found for the fruit itself. A separate
toxicology study of this crop's leaves and stems (eaten as a folk food
and tea ingredient) found no adverse effects in repeated-dose animal
testing even at a high dose well above typical consumption levels,
though the material is not currently registered in Korea's official
food-safety standards codex. Thorny canes present an ordinary physical
handling hazard during cultivation and harvest, as with other bramble
crops.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Unripe fruit → stalks removed, blanched briefly, sun-dried
  → crude drug. Ripe fruit → packed → brief cold storage → fresh
  market. Ripe fruit → fermented → traditional fruit liquor. Ripe fruit
  steeped with sugar and a distilled spirit → home-style infused
  liquor. Ripe fruit → juice. Leaves and stems (harvested in late
  spring) → dried and milled → tea.
- **Propagation chain:** Tip layering or root cuttings → new plants.
- Not covered by this research in detail: factory-scale freeze-dried
  unripe-fruit extract production, industrial frozen-berry processing,
  and use of this species specifically in breeding programs for other
  bramble crops.

## Open research questions

- The commonly cited national production yield figure mixes this true
  species with its commercially dominant North American look-alike, so
  it should not be treated as a true-species-specific yield; a yield
  trial on authenticated true-species plantings or named true-species
  breeding selections would be needed to isolate this.
- A documented per-plant selection threshold from a breeding-program
  clone bank cannot be reliably scaled to a per-hectare yield figure
  without knowing that program's planting density, which was not
  reported.
- Detailed pharmacopoeial drug-quality standards (beyond a single
  documented drying-loss limit) were not consulted in full; the typical
  commercial split between unripe-harvest and ripe-harvest fruit volume
  was not found in any source.
- A quantitative winter cold-hardiness threshold for this species was
  not found; only qualitative statements (weak in extreme cold, plant
  in regions without sharp temperature swings, shelter from winter
  wind) were available, and this project's estimated kill-threshold
  temperature is a judgment call pending a dedicated hardiness trial.
- The species' documented adaptation to high heat and humidity in parts
  of its native range may mean standard heat-stress modeling defaults
  for its general growth-habit archetype are too harsh for this
  specific crop — flagged as an unresolved modeling question.
- No dedicated labor-cost study specific to this species was found; all
  labor-hour figures are estimated or drawn by analogy to the related
  sibling crop.
- Overall long-term productive stand life (beyond "peaks around year
  four") was not documented and is estimated here by analogy and
  disease-resistance reasoning rather than sourced directly.

## Estimates and derivations

Energy and protein composition, pollination detail, soil pH range, and
salinity rating are carried over by analogy from a related bramble
crop this project has also researched, since no composition analysis
of authenticated true-species fruit was found; this true species'
fruit is documented as smaller and softer than its sibling's, so minor
compositional differences likely exist.

A world-average yield figure is derived from a Korean national bramble-
fruit yield statistic, explicitly flagged as mixing in the
commercially dominant sibling species rather than representing the
true species specifically; good, exceptional, and poor tiers are then
estimated as simple multiples of that mixed-species average, following
similar ratios observed between yield tiers in the related sibling
crop's own entry, since no dedicated true-species per-hectare yield
trial was found.

A growth-cycle length to first harvest is derived from documented
typical planting timing and a documented one-to-two-year timeline to
first crop, with the longer (second-year) timeline recorded as the
representative figure, consistent with the related sibling crop's own
typical first-crop timing.

A fresh-to-dry conversion ratio for the unripe-fruit crude drug is
estimated from general bramble-fruit moisture content and a documented
qualitative description of the dried drug as "hard and light," since no
source gave a direct processing-yield figure.

Water-use stage lengths, a seasonal water-use-days index, rooting
depth, and associated legacy water-use figures are all carried over by
direct analogy from the related sibling crop's own water-use estimates,
reasoned to be appropriate given the same general cane-fruit growth
habit and this crop's own documented preference for moist, well-
drained soil; this crop's later ripening season and documented heat/
humidity adaptation may shift its actual water-use timing somewhat
later in the season compared to the sibling crop.

A growth-stage curve is estimated to rise to a peak around the fourth
growing season, held somewhat longer than the related sibling crop's
own growth-stage curve, reasoning from this species' documented disease
resistance; no long-term stand-productivity dataset was found to
confirm this.

A planting-density figure per hectare is derived by unit-converting a
documented Korean commercial spacing figure.

Labor-hour figures across establishment, cultural work, harvesting, and
post-harvest handling are estimated primarily by analogy to the related
sibling crop's own labor estimates, adjusted for this crop's documented
repeated cane-pinching schedule and for an assumed hand-picking rate for
small, soft fruit; harvest labor for the unripe-fruit drug pass is
assumed somewhat faster than ripe-fruit harvest, since firm green fruit
is quicker to pick, though the actual commercial split between the two
harvest passes was not found.

Storage-life, maximum standing time, and post-harvest quality-decay
rate for the ripe fruit are carried over by analogy from the related
sibling crop and a general commercial bramble-fruit storage reference,
adjusted toward the shorter end of the analogous range since this
crop's fruit is documented as softer than its sibling's.

A qualitative winter cold-hardiness threshold is estimated as a
judgment call from this crop's documented native range and the
qualitative cold-weakness statements in the main cultivation-guide
source, acknowledging this is a rough estimate pending a dedicated
hardiness trial.

A peak-yield-per-day figure is derived by dividing the world-average
yield figure by an assumed harvest-window length in days, estimated by
analogy to the related sibling crop's own typical harvest-window
length.
