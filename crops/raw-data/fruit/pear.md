## Naming and scope

*Pyrus communis* L. (Rosaceae), the European pear, described by Linnaeus in
1753 [1][2].
- **Origin:** native to central and eastern Europe and western Asia [2]. It
  is thought to descend from two wild pears, *P. pyraster* and *P.
  caucasica*, which are interfertile with it [2]. Wild pears were gathered
  long before they were cultivated [2].
- **Genome:** a doubled-haploid 'Bartlett' assembly (BartlettDHv2.0) is
  496.9 Mb on 17 chromosomes, with 37,445 predicted protein-coding genes
  (snippet only) [3].
- **Scope:** the high-chill European dessert, canning and perry pears. Summer
  cultivars ('Bartlett'/'Williams'), winter cultivars ('d'Anjou', 'Bosc',
  'Comice') and perry pears are cultivar groups within this row [4][5][6].
- **Gate check against neighbouring rows (re-confirmed 2026-11-24):**
  - **`asian_pear`** (*P. pyrifolia* and relatives) stays separate. It is a
    different species, ripens on the tree, and its return bloom *falls*
    after water stress, while European pear's rises [1] (see
    `asian-pear.md`).
  - **`korla_pear`** (*P. sinkiangensis*) stays separate as a distinct
    hybrid species (see `korla-pear.md`).
  - **`pear_low_chill`** (*P. communis* × *P. pyrifolia* hybrids) stays
    separate. Its hybrids need far less chill and are grown in the
    subtropics (see `pear-low-chill.md`).
  - **Perry pears** stay inside this row as a cultivar group. They are *P.
    communis* cultivars grown for juice, with their own processing chain
    (Finding 7) [6].

**Relatives in this project:**
- Korla pear (`korla_pear`, `korla-pear.md`);
- loquat (`loquat`) and medlar (`medlar`), as fellow Rosaceae pome fruits;
- Asian pear (`asian_pear`) and low-chill pear (`pear_low_chill`);
- quince (`quince`), the most widespread pear rootstock (Finding 6).

**Proposed:**
- **`snow_pear`** (*Pyrus nivalis*): a separate species "chiefly used in
  Europe in the manufacture of perry" [7]. No production data was found
  beyond that line.
- Considered and not proposed: Callery pear (*P. calleryana*), a rootstock
  and invasive ornamental, not a food crop (see `asian-pear.md`), and the
  wild *P. pyraster*, sourced only as an ancestor.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — China dominates world volume, while the US crop sits in
three Pacific states.**
- **World:** 1.58 million ha of pear in 2009, averaging 14.2 t/ha (all pear
  species) [1]. Output in 2024 was 27.6 Mt, of which China grew 21.0 Mt
  (76%), mostly Asian species [7]. Argentina (0.67 Mt), Turkey (0.63),
  South Africa (0.47), the US (0.46) and Italy (0.44) followed [7]. About
  48% of the Southern Hemisphere's pears come from Argentina's Río Negro
  valley [7].
- **US 2023-2025:** bearing area was 40,300-41,500 acres, with yields of
  17.20, 13.50 and 18.40 tons/acre (about 38.6, 30.3 and 41.2 t/ha; see
  Estimates) [8].
  - **States:** in 2025, Washington grew 335,000 tons, Oregon 272,000 and
    California 156,000 [8].
  - **Use:** in 2025, 618,480 tons went fresh and 137,820 processed [8].
    California sends the most to processing (89,230 of 149,910 tons) [8].
  - **Price:** in 2025 the average was $459/ton, with $465 fresh and $437
    processed [8].
- **The 2024 dip:** US yield fell to 13.50 tons/acre in 2024 and recovered
  to 18.40 in 2025, up 38% in output [8]. No cause was sourced.
- **Washington:** 'd'Anjou' and 'Bartlett' were 40% and 37% of the state's
  bearing pear area in 2017. Washington is the top US fresh pear state [9].

**Finding 2 — self-incompatible, spur-bearing, and inclined to alternate
bearing.**
- **Pollination:** the cultivated pear is self-incompatible and needs
  cross-pollination. 'Bartlett', and to some extent 'Conference', are
  exceptions [1].
- **Pollinizer share in budgets:**
  - the Wenatchee 'd'Anjou' budget plants a third of the area to 'Bartlett'
    as pollinizer [9];
  - the South Washington 'Bartlett' budget makes 33% of trees 'd'Anjou'
    pollinizers [10];
  - the Hood River budgets are 75:25 [11].
  - In each case the pollinizer fruit is also sold [9][10][11]. Bee hives
    are a budgeted cost at $114/acre [9][10].
- **Spurs:** fruit is borne on spurs. Flower-bud formation depends on the
  light the spur received the season before, so the canopy is kept open by
  training and pruning [1].
- **Alternate bearing:** water stress can promote bud initiation, and a
  heavy crop suppresses it the next season, so pears often bear in
  alternate years [1]. Fruit thinning is used only to a limited extent in
  pear [1].
- **Vigour:** controlled by water stress, dwarfing rootstocks and growth
  regulators [1].

**Finding 3 — water stress can raise next year's bloom, but over-irrigation
cuts it.**
- **Tissue tolerance:** pear is "not a drought resistant species", and dry-
  season production depends on irrigation [1]. Its tissues tolerate more
  dehydration than peach, plum or apple, though [1]:
  - leaf turgor is lost near −3.1 MPa midday stem water potential (SWP);
  - stomata and photosynthesis are near zero at −2.5 MPa;
  - trunk growth stops below −2.2 MPa, shoots at about −1.7 MPa, and fruit
    growth at about −2.5 MPa;
  - unstressed trees sit at −0.65 to −0.95 MPa, and below −1.1 MPa they
    are stressed.
- **Stages:**
  - Stage I (cell division) lasts the first 7-8 weeks after bloom [1].
  - Stage II, rapid cell enlargement, is the sensitive period. Stress then
    reduces fruit size [1]. For 'Conference', SWP should stay above −1.1
    MPa in Stage II so that at least half the fruit makes fresh-market size
    [1].
- **Carry-over:**
  - Moderate stress in the growing season or post-harvest raises next
    season's bloom [1].
  - In an Israeli 'Spadona' trial, post-harvest deficit irrigation saved
    water without harm while SWP stayed above −2.2 MPa, and raised the next
    year's bloom and yield [1].
  - Severe stress (below −2.8 MPa) cut the next season's crop [1].
  - Autumn re-bloom can follow severe post-harvest stress relieved by water
    a month before leaf fall [1].
  - Over-irrigation markedly reduces flowering in later seasons [1].
- **Production functions:** yield is unaffected by ETc deficits of 15-20%
  and then falls roughly linearly [1].
  - The slope is 1.0 for 'Conference' and 1.2 for 'Blanquilla'/'Spadona'
    [1].
  - A 30% ETc cut lost 12% of yield in 'Blanquilla' but 22% in 'Conference'
    [1].
  - Revenue falls faster than yield (slope 1.3), because fruit under 65 mm
    is penalised [1]. Stage II stress that cut ETc by 30% cost
    'Conference' 60% of its gross revenue [1].
- **Kc:** see Water use. Fruit on the tree raised water use by 36% against
  de-fruited trees in one lysimeter study [1].

**Finding 4 — European pears are picked hard and need cold before they
will ripen.**
- **Why:** most cultivars do not ripen normally without a period of cold
  storage first [4]. Unchilled mature fruit "will simply shrivel and
  decompose without ripening" [5].
  - **Mechanism:** storage at −1 to 0 °C induces ACC synthase and ACC
    oxidase, so the fruit begin making ethylene and ripen once warmed [4].
- **Cold needed, at −1 °C (−1.1 °C) [4]:**
  - 'Bartlett' 2-4 weeks, or about 15 days;
  - 'Bosc' 2-3 weeks, or about 30 days;
  - 'Comice' about 30 days;
  - 'd'Anjou' 7-8 weeks, or about 60 days.
  - The home-orchard source gives "only a day or two" for 'Bartlett' and
    2-6 weeks for the winter pears (see Open research questions) [5].
- **Shortcut:** under-chilled 'Bartlett' and 'd'Anjou' are conditioned
  commercially with 100 µL/L ethylene at 20 °C for 2-3 days before
  shipping or canning [4].
- **Ripening:** 15-21 °C is best. Most cultivars fail to soften at 30 °C
  [4].
  - The handbook gives 4-7 days at 20 °C [4].
  - The home-orchard source gives 5 days for 'Bartlett', 7 for 'Bosc' and
    'Comice', and 9 for 'd'Anjou' at 18-24 °C (65-75 °F) [5].
- **Ethylene sensitivity:** 'Bartlett' is the most sensitive, 'Bosc' and
  'Comice' moderately so, and 'd'Anjou' the least. Stored pears should be
  kept away from ethylene-producing produce [4].
- **Harvest maturity:** set by flesh firmness, with a range by cultivar.
  For example 'Bartlett' is picked at 66.7-84.3 N and 'Comice' at 49.0-57.8
  N with an 8 mm plunger [4].
  - Immature fruit get scald, shrivel and friction marks. Over-mature fruit
    get core breakdown and CO2 injury [4].

**Finding 5 — two storage families, each with its own disorder.**
- **Regime:** −1 °C and 90-94% RH. Storage life is 35-40% longer at −1 °C
  than at 0 °C [4].
  - Rooms can be pulled down at −3.5 to −2.0 °C, then raised to −1 °C [4].
  - Core temperature should reach the holding temperature within 4 days.
    Delayed cooling shortens storage life [4].
- **Danger band:** "Intermediate temperatures of 2.5 to 10 °C are harmful
  to some pear cultivars". 'Bartlett' held there turns dry-textured with
  poor flavour [4].
- **Controlled atmosphere:** 2-2.5% O2 plus 0.8-1% CO2 is the safe
  commercial CA [4].
  - Low O2 (0.8%, under 0.1% CO2) for 3-4 months controls 'd'Anjou'
    scald without chemicals [4].
  - O2 at or below 1% for over 4 months causes black speck [4].
  - High CO2 causes pithy brown core [4].
  - The CA limit for 'd'Anjou' is 8 months [4].
- **Slow-senescing group** ('d'Anjou', 'Winter Nelis', 'Packham's Triumph',
  'Hardy'):
  - keeps its ripening capacity through long storage;
  - suffers superficial scald, from α-farnesene oxidation products, after
    removal;
  - is controlled with ethoxyquin (1,000-2,700 ppm) or low O2 [4].
- **Fast-senescing group** ('Bartlett', 'Bosc', 'Comice', 'Howell'):
  - loses its ability to ripen after long storage;
  - develops senescent scald [4];
  - 'Bartlett', 'Bosc' and 'Comice' are highly susceptible to core
    breakdown, and 'd'Anjou' is somewhat resistant [4].
- **Black end:** a calyx-end disorder of 'd'Anjou'. It is linked to
  waterlogged spring subsoil damaging roots, and to Asian (*P. serotina*)
  rootstocks. Fruit on French (*P. communis*) seedling stocks are generally
  free of it [4].

**Finding 6 — rootstock sets vigour, decline risk and compatibility.**
- **Quince:** clonal quince (*Cydonia oblonga*) is the most widespread
  rootstock, "though its graft compatibility is not good for all
  cultivars" [1]. Spanish trials used quince BA-29 or M-C at 1,100-1,600
  trees/ha [1].
- **'Bartlett' on six rootstocks (UC IPM) [12]:**

  | Rootstock | Pear decline susceptibility | Vigour |
  |---|---|---|
  | 'Bartlett' seedling | Low | Low to moderate |
  | 'Winter Nelis' seedling | Low | Moderate |
  | Old Home × Farmingdale (OH×F), rooted cutting | Low | Moderate, varies by selection |
  | *P. betulifolia* | Low | Very high |
  | *P. calleryana* | Moderate to high | High to very high |
  | Quince | Moderate | Low |

- **Pear decline:** a phytoplasma injected by pear psylla. Its effect
  depends on cultivar and rootstock [13].
- **OH×F in budgets:** the South Washington high-density 'Bartlett' budget
  uses OH×F 97 [10].
- **Vigorous stocks:** Australian high-density 'Bartlett' at 2,500-5,000
  trees/ha was grown on vigorous *P. calleryana* [1].
- **Asian stocks and black end:** see Finding 5 [4].

**Finding 7 — perry pears are a separate product chain with protected
names.**
- **Fruit:** perry pears carry more tannin, acid and phenolics than dessert
  pears, with astringent rather than bitter tannins [6]. 'Barland' was said
  to be too distasteful even for pigs [6].
- **Sorbitol:** pear juice is high in sorbitol, which yeast does not
  ferment, so perry keeps residual sweetness and a softer body [6]. It also
  has a mild laxative effect [6]. The Herefordshire specification allows
  sorbitol "up to 2" (unit not legible in the extract) [14].
- **Process:** pears are left to mature after picking, and the pomace
  stands after crushing to lose tannin before pressing [6].
- **Trees:** perry trees take "up to, if not over, a decade" to bear well
  ("pears for your heirs"). They can stay fully productive for 250 years
  [6].
- **Protected names:**
  - 'Poiré Domfront' is an AOC since 2002 and PDO since 2006, and must use
    at least 40% 'Plant de Blanc' [6].
  - The Gloucestershire, Herefordshire and Worcestershire perries are EU
    PGIs [6].
  - **Herefordshire Perry [14]:**
    - locally grown perry pears only (e.g. 'Blakeney', 'Hellens' Early',
      'Brown Bess');
    - up to 25% cider apple juice allowed;
    - chaptalisation to about 9.5% potential alcohol allowed.
  - Perry pears have been grown in the West Midlands for over 400 years
    [14].
- **Where:** Austria's Mostviertel is the only region that makes more perry
  than cider. Empress Maria Theresa had perry pears planted along its roads
  [6]. In South Gloucestershire, about 90% of orchards were lost in 75
  years, and fire blight killed many in the 1970s and 1980s [6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** deciduous perennial tree [1].
  - **Calendar:** flowers open almost with leaf appearance [1]. On dwarfing
    stocks, shoots grow in one or two spring flushes (April-May in the
    Northern Hemisphere) [1]. Flower buds differentiate at the start of
    Stage II (early to mid-June) [1].
  - **Stand life:** the Hood River budgets give 25 productive years after
    full production [11]. Perry trees can bear for 250 years [6].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing. The fruit is hand-picked from
  ladders at a firmness threshold and ripened off the tree [4][9].
  - US pear marketing runs from July through June [8].
  - **Harvest timing:** heat units over the 9 weeks after full bloom
    (daily (max + min)/2 − 45 °F) predict harvest dates. For example, days
    from full bloom = 147.5 − 0.0353 × heat units for 'Bartlett' [4].
  - **Elevation:** in Oregon's Hood River Valley (152-610 m), bloom and
    first harvest differ by up to three weeks between low and high
    orchards [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing [11]:**
  - **Medium density** (242 trees/acre): commercial yield from year 4, then
    10, 20, 30, 35 and 45 bins/acre to full production in year 8.
  - **High density** (519 trees/acre): from year 3, reaching 50 bins/acre
    in year 8.
  - **South Washington 'Bartlett'** (558 trees/acre): 5, 20, 30, 40 and 57
    net bins/acre in years 2-6 (see Estimates) [10].
  - **Perry trees:** a decade or more [6].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** cool to warm, humid to arid [1]. Warm-region expansion is
    limited by insufficient winter chill and by fire blight [1].
  - **Chill:**
    - a "pear" range of 400-900 hours (snippet only) [15];
    - a UC figure of about 820 hours for 'Bartlett', reported second-hand
      (snippet only) [15];
    - 'Winter Bartlett' and 'Red Bartlett' did not reach full bud break
      even after 1,050 h at 3 °C in one trial (snippet only) [15].
    - No primary chill figure was opened (see Open research questions).
  - **Elevation:** see Harvest pattern (Hood River) [4].
- **Composition (raw pear per 100 g, FoodData Central SR Legacy 169118)
  [16]:**
  - energy 57 kcal; water 83.96 g; protein 0.36 g; fat 0.14 g;
  - carbohydrate 15.23 g; fibre 3.1 g; total sugars 9.75 g (fructose 6.42,
    glucose 2.6, sucrose 0.71);
  - vitamin C 4.3 mg; potassium 116 mg; vitamin K 4.4 µg.
  - Sorbitol is not in the entry. It is notable in pear juice [6][14].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible after off-tree ripening. Winter cultivars need
  cold first (Finding 4) [4][5]. Cooking pears are dry and hard and edible
  only after hours of cooking [7]. Perry pears are too astringent to eat
  [6].
- **Pollination:** cross-pollination is needed except in 'Bartlett' and
  partly 'Conference'. Budgets interplant pollinizer cultivars and hire
  bees (Finding 2) [1][9][10].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** not sourced for flowering. Bud formation
  depends on light on the spur the season before, which is light quantity,
  not day length [1].
- **Yield:**
  - **US 2023-2025:** 30.3-41.2 t/ha [8].
    - California 36.5-42.8, Oregon 30.9-42.4 and Washington 26.9-39.9 t/ha
      (see Estimates) [8].
  - **World 2009:** 14.2 t/ha, all pear species [1].
  - **Budgets:**
    - **Wenatchee 'd'Anjou'** (242 trees/acre): 32 gross bins/acre, about
      39.5 t/ha, with an 87.5% packout [9].
    - **South Washington high-density 'Bartlett':** 57 gross bins/acre at
      full production, about 70.3 t/ha [10].
    - **Hood River:** 45 bins/acre (about 53.0 t/ha) at medium density and
      50 (about 58.8 t/ha) at high density [11].
  - **Post-harvest irrigation:** next-season yield ranged from about 20 to
    about 35 t/ha, highest at medium stress (two-year cumulative, figure
    read from a chart) [1].
- **Water use:**
  - **FAO-66 half-monthly Kc ('Conference' on quince, central leader,
    Lleida) [1]:**
    - April 0.30-0.48;
    - May 0.70-0.80;
    - June 0.85-0.90;
    - July to mid-August 0.90;
    - late August 0.70;
    - September 0.60-0.50;
    - October 0.40-0.35;
    - early November 0.35.
    - Kc rises with vapour-pressure deficit in semi-arid climates [1].
  - **FAO-56, apples, cherries and pears [17]:**
    - no ground cover: 0.45-0.60 initial / 0.95 mid / 0.70-0.75 end;
    - active ground cover: 0.50-0.80 / 1.20 / 0.85-0.95;
    - tree height 4 m.
  - **Seasonal demand:** potential ETc is 600 mm in the FAO example for the
    Ebro valley [1].
    - A combined deficit strategy (Stage I, early Stage II and post-harvest)
      cuts this to 400 mm [1].
    - Stage I deficit alone saves only 6% [1].
    - Stage II deficit saves 33% but costs revenue [1].
- **Propagation:** budded or grafted onto clonal quince, OH×F cuttings, or
  *Pyrus* seedlings ('Bartlett', 'Winter Nelis', *P. betulifolia*, *P.
  calleryana*) (Finding 6) [1][10][12].
  - **Clean stock:** pear decline is spread by psylla, not stock [13].
    Graft-transmissible viruses are covered in `asian-pear.md`, not
    re-sourced here.
  - **Interstems:** no interstem figure was re-sourced this pass.
- **Labor (Washington 2022 budgets, hand labour at $21.76/h) [9][10]:**
  - **'d'Anjou', Wenatchee [9]:**
    - pruning $1,654/acre, about 76 h/acre (about 188 h/ha; see Estimates);
    - picking at $33.18 a bin, plus $12 a bin for checkers and drivers;
    - irrigation labour $207/acre; frost-protection labour $17/acre.
  - **High-density 'Bartlett', full production [10]:**
    - pruning and training $1,000.96/acre (about 46 h);
    - green-fruit thinning $435.20/acre (about 20 h).
  - **Oregon (2021):** harvest at $42.50 a bin [11].
- **Soil:**
  - **pH:** 6.2-6.8 (Virginia Tech) or 6-7 (Penn State) (snippet only) [18].
  - **Drainage:** pears are called the most wet-tolerant deciduous fruit
    tree, but roots submerged for several days are injured (snippet only)
    [18]. Quince stocks are highly sensitive to poor drainage (snippet
    only) [18].
  - **Salinity:** rated sensitive ("S*") in FAO-61, with no threshold or
    slope given [19].
  - **Nutrients:** none re-sourced this pass.
- **Storage (postharvest):** −1 °C, 90-94% RH; CA 2-2.5% O2 plus 0.8-1%
  CO2; up to 8 months in CA for 'd'Anjou'; avoid 2.5-10 °C (Finding 5) [4].
  - **Respiration:** 'd'Anjou' is 2.0-5.0 mg CO2/kg·h at −1 °C and 17 at
    21 °C on day 1 [4].
  - **Ethylene:** under 0.1 µL/kg·h at harvest. At the climacteric peak it
    reaches 80-200 for 'Bartlett' and 10-20 for 'd'Anjou' [4].
  - **Packing:** sized by count per 20 kg box (50-180) [4].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Hood River [11]:**
    - medium density 10 × 18 ft (242 trees/acre, about 598/ha) on a
      Mid-Columbia central leader;
    - high density 6 × 14 ft (519/acre, about 1,282/ha) on a spindle and
      five-wire trellis.
  - **South Washington:** 6 × 13 ft (558/acre, about 1,379/ha) on a V
    trellis [10].
  - **Spain:** 1,100-1,600 trees/ha on quince [1].
  - **Australia:** 2,500-5,000 trees/ha on *P. calleryana* [1].
  - **Establishment costs:** soil preparation $2,451/acre and trees
    $5,046.55/acre in year 1 [10].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **US main six:** 'Bartlett', 'd'Anjou' ('Beurré d'Anjou'), 'Bosc',
    'Hardy', 'Comice' and 'Seckel'. Minor ones include 'Winter Nelis', 'El
    Dorado', 'Kieffer' and 'Packham's Triumph' [4].
  - **'Bartlett' ('Williams'):** a summer pear. It is the most
    ethylene-sensitive, partly self-fertile, needs the shortest cold
    conditioning, and is prone to core breakdown [1][4][5].
  - **'d'Anjou':** a winter pear needing about 60 days of cold. It is
    scald-prone and long-keeping [4].
  - **Spain and Italy:** 'Conference' is more water-sensitive in yield than
    'Blanquilla'/'Spadona' (the same cultivar) [1].
  - **Others:** 'Abate Fetel', 'Rocha' and 'Forelle' are named in the
    encyclopedia list [7].
  - **Perry:**
    - 'Barland', 'Brandy', 'Thorn' and 'Yellow Huffcap' are named as
      making consistently excellent perry [6];
    - 'Plant de Blanc' is the Domfront base [6];
    - 'Blakeney Red' is grown in Gloucestershire [14].
  - **Hardiness:** *P. ussuriensis* has been crossed with *P. communis* to
    breed hardier pears [7].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost (10% / 90% kill, °F (°C), 30 min, 'Bartlett'; 'd'Anjou' similar
    but may bloom earlier) [20]:**

    | Stage | 10% kill | 90% kill |
    |---|---|---|
    | Scales separating | 15 (−9.4) | 0 (−17.8) |
    | Blossom buds exposed | 20 (−6.7) | 6 (−14.4) |
    | Tight cluster | 24 (−4.4) | 15 (−9.4) |
    | First white | 25 (−3.9) | 19 (−7.2) |
    | Full white | 26 (−3.3) | 22 (−5.6) |
    | First bloom | 27 (−2.8) | 23 (−5.0) |
    | Full bloom | 28 (−2.2) | 24 (−4.4) |
    | Post bloom | 28 (−2.2) | 24 (−4.4) |

    Celsius values are converted (see Estimates). Frost is not a quality
    improver in any source found.
  - **Fire-blight weather:** blossom infection risk is tracked with
    degree-hours above 18.3 °C (65 °F) [21].
  - **Heat after harvest:** most cultivars fail to soften at 30 °C [4].
  - **Waterlogging:** spring subsoil waterlogging causes black end (Finding
    5) [4]; see Soil.
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation (under-tree sprinklers in
  Washington) [9]. No flooded requirement. Wind machines are budgeted for
  frost [9].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one fresh stage (pre-climacteric, by
  firmness), plus separate canning and perry uses [4][6][8]. Cultivars are
  classed by storage group (Finding 5) [4].
- **Soil biology:** not sourced this pass (no replant or mycorrhizal data).
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** central and eastern Europe and western Asia [2].
  - **New Zealand:** naturalised but local and infrequent, on roadsides,
    railways and waste places, often from discarded cores. The
    Department of Conservation database does not class it as a weed
    (snippet only) [22].
  - **Australia:** in Victoria it occasionally escapes orchards into nearby
    bushland, and it is also naturalised in Western and South Australia
    (snippet only) [22].
  - **US:** the Callery pear rootstock (*P. calleryana*) is invasive in
    North America [7]. State rules on Callery-rooted pears are in
    `asian-pear.md`.
  - **Psylla:** pear psylla resists insecticides and spreads pear decline
    [13].
- **Controlled environment:** not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Fire blight (*Erwinia amylovora*):** it overwinters in limb cankers,
  spreads by rain splash and insects, and infects mainly through blossoms
  [21].
  - A few active cankers per acre can make a well-timed spray programme
    fail [21].
  - **Control:** copper, streptomycin, terramycin or biologicals at bloom,
    and cutting out cankers 8-12 inches below visible injury [21].
  - **Resistance:** streptomycin resistance occurs throughout California,
    and terramycin sensitivity is falling in the upper Sacramento Valley
    [21].
  - Perry trees are harder to spray because of their size [6].
- **Pear psylla (*Cacopsylla pyricola*):** among the most serious pear
  pests because it develops insecticide resistance and spreads the pear
  decline phytoplasma [13].
  - Honeydew blackens leaves and fruit [13].
  - Each female lays 10-20 eggs at a time. Lacewings and minute pirate bugs
    are key predators [13].
- **Postharvest rots:**
  - blue mould (*Penicillium*), grey mould (*Botrytis*), bull's-eye rot,
    Mucor rot, Alternaria and others, plus field-origin rots (white, black
    and bitter rot, brown rot, Phytophthora) [4];
  - controlled by sanitation, careful handling and rapid cooling [4].
- **Seed cyanogens:** 'Conference' pear seed held 1.29 mg/g amygdalin in a
  UK survey, against 2.96 in apple seed [23]. The flesh is generally
  reported acyanogenic [23].
- **Patulin:** the US and EU juice limits found (50 µg/kg) are written for
  apple products (snippet only) [24]. No pear-specific limit or survey was
  sourced.
- **Postharvest chemicals:** ethoxyquin drenches can cause phytotoxic ring
  marks where fruit touch [4].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** see patulin. No other mycotoxin was reported.

**TO BE ADDRESSED:** patulin in pear juice, methanol in pear brandy,
sorbitol's laxative effect, and seed cyanogens are deferred until this
project has a place for health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):**
  - picked by firmness → cooled to −1 °C within 4 days [4];
  - stored in air or CA, with a scald treatment for 'd'Anjou' [4];
  - packed by count into 20 kg boxes [4];
  - cold-conditioned or ethylene-conditioned → ripened at 15-21 °C [4];
  - packout 87.5% in the Washington budgets [9][10].
- **Chain 2 (canning):** 'Bartlett' is conditioned with ethylene for
  uniform ripening before canning [4]. Processed US output was 137,820 tons
  in 2025, mostly from California [8].
- **Chain 3 (perry):**
  - perry pears are matured after picking → milled → the pomace stands to
    lose tannin → pressed [6];
  - the juice may be sulphited, and is fermented with cultured or wild
    yeast → diluted and sweetened to packaging strength [14];
  - perry is distilled into Calvados Domfrontais (AOC) [6], and 'Williams'
    pears into Poire Williams brandy [7].
- **Chain 4 (other):** canned, juiced and dried pears [7].
- **Wood:** close-grained, stable pearwood is used for woodwind instruments,
  furniture, architects' rulers, woodcut blocks and kitchen spoons, and as
  smoking wood [7].
- **Religious and cultural:**
  - Under Jewish law (*kilei ilan*), most commercial pear trees are grafted
    in a forbidden way, so buyers are advised to use nurseries under
    supervision. A rootstock table lists quince under pear stocks that are
    forbidden (snippet only) [25].
  - "A Partridge in a Pear Tree" is the first gift of "The Twelve Days of
    Christmas" [7].
- **Not detailed here:** pear juice concentrate, purée, and cull and pomace
  uses.

## Open research questions

- **Cold conditioning:** 'Bartlett' needs 2-4 weeks (or about 15 days) at
  −1 °C in the handbook [4], against "only a day or two" refrigerated in
  the home-orchard source [5]. The difference may be harvest maturity or
  temperature.
- **Orchard chill:** 400-900 h for pears generally, about 820 h for
  'Bartlett', and incomplete bud break after 1,050 h in one trial, all at
  snippet level [15]. A primary chill study is needed.
- **Kc:** FAO-66's midsummer Kc of 0.90 [1] is below the FAO-56 mid-season
  0.95-1.20 [17]. The FAO-66 chapter calls it "slightly lower than the
  0.95-1.0 value that has been traditionally recommended" [1].
- **High-density yield:** about 70 t/ha in the South Washington budget [10],
  against 59 t/ha (Hood River) [11] and 30-41 t/ha (US means) [8].
- **2024 crop:** the cause of the low US yield is not sourced [8].
- **Soil:** pH and drainage are snippet-level [18]. No nutrient, salinity
  threshold or replant data was sourced.
- **Quince compatibility:** which cultivars are incompatible, how soon it
  shows, and how interstems bridge it were not re-sourced.
- **Perry specification:** the sorbitol limit's unit in the Herefordshire
  specification is unreadable in the extract [14].

## Estimates and derivations

**US and state yields, t/ha.** short tons/acre × 2.2417 = t/ha [8]:
- US 17.20, 13.50 and 18.40 → 38.6, 30.3 and 41.2;
- California 19.10, 16.30 and 18.80 → 42.8, 36.5 and 42.1;
- Oregon 17.00, 13.80 and 18.90 → 38.1, 30.9 and 42.4;
- Washington 16.50, 12.00 and 17.80 → 37.0, 26.9 and 39.9.

**Budget yields.**
- 32 bins × 1,100 lb = 35,200 lb/acre × 1.1209 = 39,456 kg/ha, about 39.5
  t/ha [9].
- 57 bins (38 'Bartlett' + 19 'd'Anjou' gross) × 1,100 lb = 62,700 lb/acre,
  about 70.3 t/ha [10].
- 45 and 50 bins × 1,050 lb = 47,250 and 52,500 lb/acre, about 53.0 and
  58.8 t/ha [11].
- The South Washington net bins by year (3+2+0, 11+6+3, 18+9+3, 24+11+5,
  33+17+7) are summed from the budget's table, including culls [10].

**Labour hours.** Costs ÷ $21.76/h [9][10]:
- pruning $1,654 → 76 h/acre (÷ 0.4047 = 188 h/ha);
- pruning and training $1,000.96 → 46 h/acre;
- thinning $435.20 → 20 h/acre.
These assume all of each line is hand labour at the stated rate.

**Densities.** 242, 519 and 558 trees/acre ÷ 0.4047 = 598, 1,282 and 1,379
trees/ha [10][11].

**Frost-table conversions.** °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9, e.g. 28 °F = −2.2 °C and
0 °F = −17.8 °C [20].

**Firmness.** 66.7-84.3 N × 0.225 = 15.0-19.0 lbf for 'Bartlett' [4].
