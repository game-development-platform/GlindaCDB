# Dragon Fruit / Pitaya (*Selenicereus undatus* and red-fleshed relatives)

## Naming and scope

The climbing, night-flowering cacti sold as dragon fruit, pitaya, or
pitahaya [1][2]. This entry covers two closely related groups grown and
stored the same way: the white-fleshed, pink-skinned type (the
Vietnamese commercial crop), and red- or purple-fleshed types from
Nicaragua and Israel, treated here as a cultivar group rather than a
separate crop [1]. A spiny-skinned yellow pitaya species, with a
notably cooler climate preference and different storage regime, is a
genuinely distinct species excluded from this entry and treated as its
own crop by this project; a set of yellow-skinned clones of the
white-fleshed species itself (documented in Mexico) are, by contrast,
simply a cultivar note within this entry, since they remain the same
species. This crop shares its unusual CAM (nocturnal) photosynthesis
pathway with another tropical fruit crop this project has also
researched, and a harvest-method/variant duality concept with two
further tropical fruit crops this project has researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a CAM cactus whose flowering growers switch on with
lights.** This crop flowers under long summer daylengths; in its major
commercial growing region in Vietnam, the main natural flowering season
runs March-September [3]. During the short-day months, growers
routinely force off-season flowering with supplemental night lighting —
either simple incandescent bulbs run for several hours in the middle of
the night, or more efficient red/far-red compact fluorescent lamps,
which performed well and saved power across several documented field
trials [3][4]. Lit plants reliably set flower buds within about 6-7
weeks; unlit control plants in the same trials did not flower at all
during this period, and it takes roughly 112 days from induced bud
emergence to harvest [4]. This forced off-season crop, timed to a major
regional holiday, is described in the primary trial source as the major
income source for growers using the technique [3]. Consistent with its
status as a cactus, this species photosynthesizes via the CAM pathway,
with roughly 85% of its daily carbon dioxide uptake occurring at night
under well-watered conditions — a notably high water-use efficiency
mechanism [5]. A dry period before bloom is also documented to help
induce flowering [6].

**Finding 2 — fast-fruiting from cuttings, trained vertically on
posts.** Propagation is almost always by stem cuttings (roughly
12-38 cm long), which grow quickly (about 3 cm a day) and root well
within 4-6 months; cutting-grown plants fruit remarkably quickly,
within 6-9 months of planting, while true seedlings take up to seven
years to first fruit and are used only in breeding [6]. Mature plants
develop stems weighing "several hundred pounds," requiring a genuinely
strong support post (10-15 cm in diameter), since a bare wire trellis is
not adequate [6]. Standard commercial planting density runs roughly
1,100-1,350 plants per hectare; full production is reached only after
up to five years, after which documented mature yields run roughly
20-30 tonnes per hectare, and plantings remain productive for about 20
years [2][6].

**Finding 3 — large nocturnal flowers, single-night bloom, with
pollination strategy varying by clone.** Flowers can reach up to 36 cm
long, open for a single night, and are pollinated in nature by moths and
bats — no bee activity occurs at night [2][6]. Several commercial
cultivars are self-incompatible, so growers plant two to three
compatible clones together and hand-pollinate at night or in the early
morning between them; by contrast, the main Vietnamese white-fleshed
commercial cultivar is self-compatible and does not require this extra
labor [1][6].

**Finding 4 — non-climacteric, with a narrow harvest window and real
chilling sensitivity.** Skin color changes roughly 25-30 days after
flowering, and eating quality peaks somewhat later, around 33-37 days;
the recommended harvest window is roughly 32-35 days after flowering,
with an outer limit around 45 days [1]. Fruit left on the plant beyond
about 35 days is prone to splitting after rain or heavy irrigation [1].
Standard storage is 10°C for about two weeks, extendable to roughly
five weeks in modified-atmosphere packaging; storage at 6°C or below
causes chilling injury within about two weeks, manifesting as
translucent flesh and dark scales [1]. The fruit is non-climacteric, and
ethylene exposure does not improve or accelerate its color development
[1].

**Finding 5 — tolerant of a wide range of soils, but tender to both
frost and extreme heat.** Optimal growth temperature is 18-25°C;
damage occurs below about -2°C (though plants generally recover quickly
from light freezes), and growth stalls above about 38°C, with stem
sunburn a real risk in dry or high-altitude sites — young plantings are
commonly given about 30% shade for their first 3-4 months [6]. The
species is documented as moderately to highly salt-tolerant, including
tolerance of calcareous soils, though it requires good drainage [6]. A
major Vietnamese growing province documented roughly 26,000 hectares and
a provincial average yield around 21.6 t/ha in a recent year, accounting
for nearly half the country's total growing area [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A climbing, hemiepiphytic cactus trained
  vertically on posts; perennial, with several flowering waves per
  season plus an optional light-forced off-season crop.
- **Climate:** Tropical, frost-free subtropical, or semiarid with
  irrigation.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible (halved and scooped).
  Roughly 570 kcal/kg and 3.6 g protein/kg, with notable water content
  and modest fiber.
- **Pollination:** Required for self-incompatible cultivars (hand
  pollination between compatible clones at night or early morning); the
  main Vietnamese commercial cultivar is self-compatible and needs no
  managed pollination. Natural pollinators are moths and bats.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 17,000 kg/ha) is derived
  from averaging two documented regional yield figures; good,
  exceptional, and poor tiers are derived from a documented full-
  production range, an optimum-fertilization trial result, and a rough
  estimate for young or poorly managed plantings respectively (see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure
  exists for this species; water-use parameters here are estimated by
  analogy to another CAM-pathway fruit crop this project has also
  researched, adjusted upward somewhat to reflect this species'
  documented "fairly high" practical water requirement despite its
  CAM water-use efficiency. Drought tolerance is judged high (a
  water-storing cactus), while waterlogging tolerance is judged low,
  since excess soil moisture promotes bacterial and fungal disease.
- **Propagation:** Almost always vegetative, by stem cuttings; true
  seed propagation is used only in breeding, since seedlings take up
  to seven years to first fruit versus 6-9 months for cuttings.
- **Soil:** A wide tolerance range, including sandy and calcareous
  soils; good drainage is essential, and shallow, stony soils can be
  substantially improved through a trench-planting technique with
  amended soil.
- **Labor:** No comprehensive labor-cost budget was found. Training and
  tying stems to posts, and pruning surplus stems after each flowering
  wave, are documented as frequent, recurring tasks; hand pollination is
  needed only for self-incompatible cultivars.
- **Storage:** Standard storage is 10°C for about two weeks (up to five
  weeks in modified-atmosphere packaging); the fruit will not ripen
  further once removed from the plant, and storage below about 6°C
  causes chilling injury.
- **Stand life:** Plantings remain productive for about 20 years; full
  production is reached only after up to five years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A stem and fruit canker fungal disease,
  anthracnose, a fungal rot, and a bacterial soft rot; mites, thrips,
  ants, scale insects, and mealybugs; birds and rodents also damage
  ripening fruit.
- **Cultivars:** Spans the baseline Vietnamese white-fleshed type
  (self-compatible, pink skin, green scales), red-fleshed types from
  Nicaragua and Israel (self-incompatibility varies, red flesh from a
  betalain pigment also responsible for a documented harmless red-urine
  effect), and a yellow-skinned clone group of the same white-fleshed
  species documented in Mexico.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not specifically researched in
  the sources reviewed.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a stem and fruit canker fungal
disease, anthracnose, a fungal rot, and a bacterial soft rot, along
with mites, thrips, ants, scale insects, and mealybugs; the fruit is
also a documented host for fruit flies, subject to quarantine treatment
by irradiation.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — red-fleshed cultivars contain a betalain pigment
that harmlessly colors urine and stool red after consumption, a
documented but benign effect worth noting to avoid alarming consumers;
spines occur on the stems, and the related yellow pitaya species' fruit
is also spiny. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit clipped from the plant at full color → graded by
  weight → stored cold; lower-grade or surplus fruit → scooped pulp →
  juice, natural colorant, preserves, or candy; flowers and unopened
  buds → used as a cooked vegetable or dried into tea; stem prunings →
  new propagation cuttings.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen pulp production in
  detail; the lighting and post infrastructure itself as a modeled farm
  system; dedicated colorant extraction or wine production from the
  fruit.

## Open research questions

- Self-compatibility status is reported somewhat inconsistently: the
  main Vietnamese commercial cultivar is clearly self-compatible, but
  sources differ on how many other commercial cultivars require
  cross-pollination — not fully reconciled here.
- The practical water requirement is described as "fairly high" in one
  source despite this crop's inherently water-efficient CAM
  photosynthesis pathway in another — plausibly reflecting real-world
  irrigation practice through flowering and fruiting rather than true
  physiological water need, but not reconciled here.
- Reported time from flowering to harvest varies across sources (a
  30-35 day range, versus a roughly 40-day estimate, versus a roughly
  112-day figure measured from induced bud emergence rather than
  flower opening) — these likely reflect different starting
  reference points rather than a genuine contradiction, but are not
  fully reconciled here.
- No standard international crop-coefficient figure exists for this
  species, so water-use parameters rely on an analogy to a different
  CAM-pathway fruit crop, which may not transfer perfectly.
- No comprehensive labor-cost study was found; labor-hour figures in
  this entry are rough estimates.
- No wild-harvest tradition was identified for this species in the
  sources reviewed, despite its native range in southern Mexico and
  Pacific Central America.
- Detailed soil pH guidance was not found in the primary sources and
  is estimated here from this species' documented tolerance of
  calcareous, high-pH soils.

## Estimates and derivations

The world-average yield figure (roughly 17,000 kg/ha) is derived by
averaging two independently documented regional yield figures: a
multi-year research-trial average from one semi-arid growing region
using a recommended planting method, and a major Vietnamese province's
documented provincial average for a recent harvest year. No global
production-statistics entry exists for this crop.

The poor-yield tier is a rough estimate at roughly half the lower of
the two source figures above, representing a young, unlit (not
light-forced), poorly pollinated, or shallow-soil planting; a
documented trench-planting technique alone was shown to raise yields
by nearly half over conventional pit planting on shallow soil in one
multi-year trial.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days figure, seasonal water-use
index) are estimated by analogy to another CAM-pathway fruit crop this
project has also researched, since this species has no standard
international crop-coefficient figure of its own; the analogy figure is
adjusted upward from the straight analogy value to reflect this
species' documented higher practical irrigation need, since growers
irrigate through flowering and the exposed soil around support posts
likely raises evaporative water loss compared to a denser-canopy CAM
crop.

Drought-buffer days and yield-penalty-per-dry-day figures are rough
estimates reflecting a high, water-storing succulent's drought
tolerance, balanced against this species' documented preference for
regular watering through its flowering and fruiting period.

Root depth is a rough estimate inferred indirectly from the documented
yield benefit of trench planting on shallow soils, since no direct
root-depth measurement was found.

Growth-stage curve parameters are a rough estimate reflecting a
perennial, evergreen-stemmed crop with relatively flat photosynthetic
activity across the season.

Labor-hour estimates for pruning/training, harvest, hand pollination,
and post-harvest handling are rough estimates, since no comprehensive
regional cost-of-cultivation study was found; hand pollination is
assumed needed only for self-incompatible cultivar plantings.

Soil pH tolerance range is a rough estimate based on this species'
documented tolerance of calcareous, high-pH soils with supplemental
micronutrient treatment, since no specific pH optimum was found in the
primary sources.

Time to first harvest (roughly 240 days, 6-9 months after planting from
cuttings) is taken directly from documented cutting-to-fruit timing; the
multi-year ramp to full production and the roughly 210-day flowering-
and-fruiting plateau are derived from the documented up-to-five-year
timeline to full production and the main growing region's documented
flowering season length. Peak harvest rate is derived by dividing the
world-average yield figure by this plateau length. Standing-crop
duration on the plant (about 10 days) and its decay rate are derived
from the documented 25-45 day total harvest window, after which fruit
left unharvested faces rising splitting risk.
