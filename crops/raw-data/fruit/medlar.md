## Naming and scope

*Mespilus germanica* L. (Rosaceae) [1]. When *Mespilus* is merged into
*Crataegus* (hawthorn), the correct name is *Crataegus germanica* (L.)
Kuntze [1].
- **Names:** medlar, common medlar [1]; historically "open-arse" in
  English and "monkey's bottom" in south-west England [1]. In Turkish it
  is *muşmula* [10][11], and also *döngel* or *beşbıyık*; *ezgil* in
  Azerbaijan; *bushmala* in Georgia [6].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **Loquat** (*Eriobotrya japonica*) is still sometimes called
    "Japanese" or "Chinese medlar" [1]. It is its own row, `loquat`
    (`loquat.md`).
  - **Goji:** "medlar tea" and "red medlar" usually mean goji
    (wolfberry), not this fruit [1].
  - **Azarole:** *Crataegus azarolus* is called the "Mediterranean
    medlar" in at least one study title [7]. See Proposed.
- **Stern's medlar** (*M. canescens*): a critically endangered Arkansas
  endemic, with 25 plants in one 9 ha wood [1]. It is probably a hybrid of
  cultivated medlar with native *Crataegus* (× *Crataemespilus
  canescens*) and is grown mainly for botanical purposes [1]. It is not a
  crop, so no row is proposed.
- **Varieties:** 23 taxa are distinguished within the species [1],
  including:
  - var. *gigantea*, with very large fruit;
  - var. *abortiva*, which is seedless;
  - two variegated ornamental forms.

**Relatives in this project:**
- loquat (`loquat`, `loquat.md`): the same tribe and the shared name
  [15];
- quince (`quince`, `quince.md`): the usual rootstock, and another fruit
  that is bletted [1][2][15];
- pear (`pear`, `pear.md`): another rootstock [1].

**Proposed:**
- **Azarole** (`azarole`, *Crataegus azarolus*): the "Mediterranean
  medlar", a cultivated hawthorn fruit [7]. It shares the name and the
  closest genus.
- **Service tree** (`service_tree`, *Sorbus domestica*): one of the few
  fruits that, like medlar, most varieties can be eaten raw only after
  bletting [1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the harvested fruit is not yet food.**
- **At harvest:** the fruit is "hard and bitter" [2] and astringent [4].
  It rarely ripens on the tree in the UK [2]. Unripe fruit holds about
  2.6% tannin [1], or about 3% [7].
- **Bletting:** fruit is laid out stalk-up, not touching, in a cool, dark,
  frost-free, airy place. After 2-3 weeks it is soft, dark brown and
  slightly wrinkled [2]. The flesh turns from white to brown [1][3].
- **Methods tested** (Slovenia, 'Domača nešplja'; harvested 20 November
  2020) [4]:
  - cellar storage at 8 °C for 25 days;
  - three hoarfrosts on the tree (minimum −1 to −4 °C), picked 3
    December;
  - deep freezing at −20 °C for two months;
  - heating at 60 °C for 3 hours.
- **Other routes:** a Serbian study softened fruit at 10 °C for 21 days
  [9]. The RHS harvests after frost [2].
- **Does bletting raise sugar?**
  - The RHS says "starches turn to sugars" [2], and the encyclopedia says
    sugar increases [1].
  - In the Slovenian trial, cellar bletting lowered total sugars from
    458.5 to 413.8 g/kg dry weight. That is about −10%; respiration
    used up the sugars (see Estimates) [4].
  - In the same trial, cellar storage cut organic acids, and total
    phenolics fell by more than 85% [4].
  - Fruit softened by hoarfrost on the tree kept its sugars and acids
    best (446.0 g/kg). The authors call it the "most ideal" method [4].
  - This is a real conflict between the gardening sources and the
    measured data (Open research questions).
- **Ripening type:** called climacteric [4][6][7].

**Finding 2 — a fruit of hedgerows and field edges, with national
statistics.**
- **Turkey, 2020:** 4,964 t from 2,006 decares (200.6 ha) of recorded
  orchard [10].
  - **Marmara:** 74.2% of the orchard area (1,488 da) [10].
  - **Black Sea region:** 38.5% of production (1,912 t) from just 17 da
    of orchard [10]. There the trees grow as understorey shrubs in
    forest, as field-boundary trees and scattered in home gardens, and
    production comes mostly from those, not from orchards [10]. Most
    regional output is from Samsun and Sinop [10].
  - **Trend:** in the Black Sea region, 1,816 t in 2005 against 1,912 t
    in 2020, while tree numbers fell over 2015-2020 [10].
- **Turkey, 2024-2025:** 5,390 t in 2024 and an estimated 4,370 t in 2025
  (−18.9%) [11]. The 2025 total for all fruit fell 30.4% [11].
- **Elsewhere:** it is grown "quite rarely" in Montenegro, in small
  family orchards with no statistics [6]. There was no information on
  production anywhere in the world [7]. It is also described as "widely
  cultivated in Turkey, Germany, and the Netherlands" [7]. FAOSTAT has
  no medlar series (not sourced).
- **Per tree:** 30-70 kg a year, depending on variety and age [1].

**Finding 3 — late flowering, self-fertile, grafted onto other genera.**
- **Flowering:** May-June, very late compared with other European fruit
  trees [1][3]. Single terminal flowers on short side shoots [1].
  Hermaphrodite, bee-pollinated and self-fertile, so one tree crops
  alone [1][2][3].
- **Frost:** late frosts "hardly cause any damage" [1], but the RHS
  blames poor crops on late frosts or spring storms that damage blossom
  [2].
- **Rootstocks:**
  - **Hawthorn, mountain ash, pear or quince** [1][4];
  - **'Quince A'** is semi-vigorous and **'Quince C'** dwarfing [2];
  - **Quince BA-29** in the Montenegro orchards [6];
  - **Medlar seedlings** "not recommended" because grafts grow slowly
    [1].
- **Bearing:**
  - first crops about 3-4 years after planting [3];
  - full cropping 6-7 years after grafting, kept for 20-25 years [1];
  - trees live 30-60 years [1], or 30-50 years with some 100-year-old
    UK trees [6].

**Finding 4 — a seed that waits two winters.**
- **Dormancy:** the hard, impermeable seed coat means seed "will not
  usually germinate until it has gone through two winters" [3].
- **Breaking it:** commercially by a brief sulphuric-acid soak [3]. At
  home, soak for 24 hours in warm water, then stratify for 2-3 months at
  1-5 °C [3].
- **Viability:** 18-20 months [1].
- **Spread:** birds, squirrels and deer carry the seed [1]. Some
  varieties are sterile and propagated only vegetatively [1].
- **Seedling quality:** seedlings of named varieties tend to make larger
  trees with poorer fruit [2].

**Finding 5 — fruit quality varies widely.**
- **Montenegro (four cultivars, 2010-2012)** [6]:
  - fruit weight 21.4-25.5 g; length 34.5-38.4 mm; width 31.5-36.2 mm;
  - dry matter 26.2-28.8%; TSS 20.45-22.25%; titratable acidity
    1.9-2.28%;
  - 'Royal medlar' was the best on every measure.
- **Size:** wild fruit 2-3 cm, cultivated 3-8 cm [1]; wild up to 25 mm,
  some cultivars 65 mm or more [3]; about 5 cm in the RHS guide [2].
- **Sugars at technological maturity** (Slovenia) [4]:
  - total sugars 127.2 g/kg fresh weight: glucose 54.4, fructose 59.2,
    sorbitol 9.5, sucrose 4.1;
  - that is 1.5-2.2 times the 58.3-85.0 g/kg reported for eleven Turkish
    genotypes.
- **Acids** [4]:
  - quinic 8.8 and malic 8.4 g/kg (94% of total acids), citric 1.1
    g/kg;
  - total 18.3 g/kg, a sugar/acid ratio of 7.0.
- **Czech 'Dutch' (bloom 10 June 2008)** [5]:
  - sampled at 134-174 days after full bloom;
  - edible ("consumption maturity") at 154-164 days;
  - dry matter fell from 37.15% in immature fruit to 29.88% in ripe
    fruit;
  - vitamin C "decreased dramatically".

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous large shrub or small tree, up to 8 m
  [1], usually 3-5 m [6]. Wild forms are thorny shrubs of 1.5-4 m;
  cultivated forms have few or no thorns [1]. Grafted trees reach 4-6 m
  [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; one autumn harvest, October to
  November [2]. Two harvest stages are possible: picked hard for indoor
  bletting, or left to soften on the tree through frosts (Finding 1)
  [1][2][4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 3-4 years after planting [3]; full
  crop 6-7 years after grafting [1].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** temperate to sub-Mediterranean with warm summers and mild
    winters; 18-20 °C favours growth [1].
  - **Cold:** tolerates −20 °C [1]. USDA zones 5-9 and UK zone 6 [3].
    Grown at Bijelo Polje, Montenegro, at 520 m and above, where winter
    lows can reach −27.6 °C [6].
  - **Rainfall and elevation:** wild plants grow in dry areas with 700 mm
    of rain, from sea level to 1,100 m [1].
  - **Exposure:** tolerates strong wind but not maritime exposure [3].
    The RHS advises a warm, sheltered site [2].
  - **Chill requirement:** Not sourced.
- **Composition (per kg fresh fruit at harvest):** sugars 127.2 g and
  acids 18.3 g (Finding 5) [4]. Dry matter 26.2-37.15% [5][6]. Fresh
  fruit is 23-73% carbohydrate [7]. The fruit is rich in potassium,
  calcium, phosphorus, magnesium and iron [1]. Energy is estimated at
  about 56 kcal/100 g from sugars and acids alone (see Estimates). USDA
  FoodData Central has no medlar entry [13].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible only after bletting [1][2]. The seed is not
  eaten (see Hazards).
- **Pollination:** self-fertile and bee-pollinated [1][2][3]. The nectar-
  and pollen-rich flowers attract bees [3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - **Per tree:** 30-70 kg [1].
  - **Per hectare:** not directly measured. The derived range is 5-28 t/ha
    (see Estimates).
  - **Turkish averages:** 24.7 t/ha by recorded orchard area, inflated by
    trees outside orchards (see Estimates) [10].
  - **Italy:** 'Comune' has high yield efficiency and 'Gigante' yields
    little [14].
- **Water use:** Not sourced as a figure. Water young trees in long dry
  spells; established trees in suitable soil need none [2]. Lack of water
  makes young fruit drop [2]. It prefers moist soil [3]. Irrigation can
  stimulate growth in plantations [1].
- **Propagation:** grafting onto other genera (Finding 3) [1][2][4][6];
  seed (Finding 4) [3]. Clonal or viral risk: Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced. It needs "little pruning" [2]; prune in winter
  to an open-centred goblet [2]. Cutting back fruited shoots after harvest
  encourages new fruiting spurs [1].
- **Soil:**
  - **Preferred:** fresh, well-drained loam at pH 6-8 [1]. PFAF says it
    grows on sand, loam or clay from mildly acid to mildly alkaline [3].
  - **To avoid:** very chalky, poorly drained or shallow, poor soils [2].
  - **Salinity:** Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - **Hard fruit:** 4 °C and 90 ± 5% RH for 45 days in a 2026 trial, with
    weight loss and decay rising during storage [8]. Oxalic acid dips
    (1.0-1.5 mM) reduced both [8].
  - **Atmosphere:** 2% O2 and 5% CO2 best retain malic and citric acid
    [4].
  - **Bletted fruit:** check regularly and use as soon as it is ready [2].
    Bletted shelf life: not sourced.
- **Establishment:**
  - **Planting:** while dormant, late autumn to early spring [2].
  - **Spacing:** at least 5 m from other trees or buildings [2]. Research
    orchards are planted at 5 × 5 m in Bolu, Turkey [8] and 7 × 8 m in
    Slovenia [4].
  - **Tree forms:** bush (70-90 cm clear trunk) on dwarfing stock,
    half-standard (1.2-1.5 m), standard (1.8-2.1 m) [2].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **'Nottingham':** small, good-flavoured fruit, prolific and fairly
    compact [2]. It holds the RHS Award of Garden Merit [1].
  - **'Iranian':** an AGM holder with heavy early crops [2].
  - **'Dutch'** ('Giant', 'Monstrous'): large fruit [1].
  - **'Royal':** very high yield [1]; also the best fruit in Montenegro
    [6].
  - **'Early medlar':** early, high quality [1].
  - **'Seedless':** low quality [1].
  - **Others:** 'Hollandia', 'Russian', 'Large Russian' and 'Breda giant'
    [1]; 'Domestic medlar', 'Plovdivska' and 'Rasna' [6]; 'Domača
    nešplja' [4]; 'Comune' and 'Gigante' in Italy [14].
  - **Genetic diversity:** natural populations are diverse [1], and
    Azerbaijan has the most diverse varieties [7].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** harmful when it hits late blossom [2]. It is also a quality
    event: hoarfrost on the tree softens the fruit and keeps it in the best
    state (Finding 1) [4], and the RHS harvests after frost [2].
  - **Wind:** tolerates strong wind [3].
  - **Drought:** young fruit drops [2].
  - **Waterlogging:** avoid poorly drained soil [2].
- **Water regime:** rainfed, with only establishment watering [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Timing:** late October to November, in dry weather, when the stalk
    parts easily [2]. Seed ripens in November [3].
  - **Bloom to edible:** 154-164 days in the Czech study (see Estimates)
    [5].
  - **Maturity at harvest:** peel changing from greenish-brown to light
    brown, seed turning brown, firmness about 45-55 N [8].
  - **Stages:** hard fruit for bletting, or fruit softened on the tree by
    frost [2][4].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** south-east Balkans, Asia Minor, the Caucasus,
    Crimea, northern Iran and possibly Turkmenistan [1].
  - **Introduced:** occasionally naturalised in woods in south-east
    England [1][3].
  - **Weed status:** PFAF says no weed potential [3]. IUCN Red List: Least
    Concern (Petrova & Barstow 2017) [1].
  - **Fire blight:** a host of *Erwinia amylovora* [1]. EU Regulation
    2019/2072 Annex IX bars *Mespilus* plants and pollen, other than
    fruit and seeds, from fire-blight protected zones unless the source
    is recognised as pest-free [12]. UK guidance lists *Mespilus* among
    the host genera barred from export to EU protected zones [12].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. Dwarfing 'Quince C' trees [2]
  could suit container or tunnel growing, but this is an inference only.

## Hazards

- **Seed hydrocyanic acid:** the seeds contain it and "should not be eaten
  in quantity" [3]. The seed is used medicinally against kidney stones,
  with caution because of the toxin [3]. Content: not sourced.
- **Fire blight:** susceptible, like other Rosaceae used in propagation
  [1]. It is regulated in EU protected zones [12].
- **Diseases:**
  - brown rot (*Monilinia fructigena*), worse in wet years [1][2];
  - powdery mildew (*Podosphaera clandestina*) [1][2];
  - leaf spot, or quince leaf blight (*Entomosporium mespili*, syn.
    *Diplocarpon mespili*), which can strip leaves in wet seasons [1][2].
- **Pests:** the leaf miner *Lithocolletis blancardella* in plantations [1].
  Otherwise it is "only rarely attacked" [1].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed cyanide hazard is deferred until this
project has a place for health and safety data and a measured content is
found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (bletted fresh):** hard fruit picked in dry weather →
  optional salt-dip of the stalks against rot → laid out stalk-up for 2-3
  weeks → eaten raw, with cheese and port [2] or with sugar and cream [1].
- **Chain 2 (on-tree frost softening):** left through several hoarfrosts
  → gathered soft → eaten or processed [2][4].
- **Chain 3 (preserves):** bletted pulp → amber jelly [1][2]; "medlar
  cheese", a curd made with pulp, eggs and butter [1]; jam, sauce,
  syrup, leather and juice [7].
- **Chain 4 (spirits and drinks):**
  - schnapps refined with hawthorn (Saarland) [1];
  - cider and pickles from unripe fruit [7];
  - a liqueur made to a monastery recipe at Gračanica [6].
- **Chain 5 (dried or fresh in Turkey):** eaten fresh, as vinegar,
  pickled, boiled, crushed or as dried pulp [7].
- **Unripe fruit:** tannin for tanning leather, and to clarify wine by
  flocculating proteins [1][7].
- **Seed (kernel) oil:** biodiesel; about 41% oleic and 43% linoleic acid
  [1][7].
- **Leaves and fruit cores:** leaf activated carbon removes nickel [1];
  carbonised medlar-core particles remove copper [7].
- **Medicinal:** fruit pulp laxative, leaves astringent [3]; diuretic and
  astringent uses [1]. In Gilan, Iran, the leaves, bark, fruit and wood are
  used [1]. A review tabulates folk uses in Turkey, Iran, Algeria and
  Serbia [7].
- **Wood:** very hard and fine-textured [1]; hard and flexible [3].
- **Ornamental and wildlife:** flowers, autumn colour and fruit for birds
  and mammals [2][3].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. Medlars do have a long literary
  history (Chaucer, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Rabelais) [1].
- **Market:** demand for medlar products far exceeds the very small
  supply in Montenegro [6].

## Open research questions

- **Sugar during bletting:** gardening sources say it rises [1][2], but
  the Slovenian measurement shows cellar bletting cutting total sugars by
  about 10% [4]. A Turkish storage study saw a rise over the first 15-30
  days, then a fall [4]. Not reconciled; it may depend on temperature and
  how long the fruit is stored.
- **Late frost:** "hardly cause any damage" [1] against frost-damaged
  blossom [2].
- **Cold hardiness:** −20 °C [1] against zones 5-9 [3] and a site with
  −27.6 °C lows [6]. The site record does not say whether trees were
  damaged.
- **Production claims:** "widely cultivated" in Turkey, Germany and the
  Netherlands, and "no information on manufacturing", in the same review
  [7]. Turkey's figures are sourced [10][11]; Germany's and the
  Netherlands' are not.
- **Turkish averages:** recorded orchard area cannot explain the
  production (see Estimates) [10]. A tree-count series would settle it.
- **Fruit size:** "about 5 cm" [2] against 21-26 g fruit 32-38 mm wide
  [6].
- **Not sourced:**
  - water use, labour hours and salinity;
  - chill requirement and photoperiod;
  - bletted shelf life and seed cyanide content;
  - soil biology and controlled-environment use.
- **Works identified, not yet read:**
  - **Baird & Thieret (1989), "The medlar ... from antiquity to
    obscurity," *Economic Botany* 43(3):328-372:** history, uses and
    cultivars.
  - **Cristofori et al. (2019), *Eur. J. Hortic. Sci.* 84(6):350-358:**
    per-tree yields of four cultivars on quince [14].
  - **Glew et al. (2003), *Food Chemistry*:** sugars, acids and amino acids
    during development.
  - **Selcuk & Erkan (2015), *Postharvest Biology and Technology*
    100:81-90:** long-term controlled-atmosphere storage.
  - **The older pharmacognosy review and the Turkish genotype survey**
    cited in the earlier version of this doc (not re-identified).
  - **TÜİK's medlar series** (tree numbers, area and production by year).

## Estimates and derivations

**Turkey national check** [10]: the Black Sea region's 1,912 t is 38.5% of
the national total, so 1,912 ÷ 0.385 = 4,966 t, matching the table's
4,964 t.

**Turkish yield by recorded area** [10]:
- Nationally: 4,964 t ÷ 200.6 ha = 24.7 t/ha.
- Black Sea region: 1,912 t ÷ 1.7 ha = 1,125 t/ha, which is physically
  impossible.

So most production is counted from trees outside recorded orchards. That
fits the source's description of scattered field-edge and forest trees
[10]. Neither figure is a usable orchard yield.

**Orchard yield from per-tree figures** [1][2][4][8]:
- 30-70 kg per tree [1].
- At 5 × 5 m, the closest sourced spacing [2][8]: 400 trees/ha × 30-70 kg
  = 12-28 t/ha.
- At 7 × 8 m [4]: 10,000 ÷ 56 = 179 trees/ha × 30-70 kg = 5.4-12.5 t/ha.
- Plausible range: 5-28 t/ha for mature trees. The 5 × 5 m upper end
  assumes full per-tree yields at close spacing, which is unlikely.
  Measured per-hectare data, such as the Cristofori trial, would replace
  this.

**Dry matter, Slovenian fruit** [4]: 127.16 g/kg fresh ÷ 458.53 g/kg dry
= 0.277, so about 27.7% dry matter. This falls within Montenegro's
26.2-28.8% [6] and below the Czech 29.88-37.15% [5].

**Sugar change by method** [4], in g/kg dry weight against 458.53 at
harvest:
- cellar: 413.83 = −9.7%;
- hoarfrost: 446.04 = −2.7%;
- heating: 432.15 = −5.8%;
- frozen: 470.23 = +2.6%.

**Energy, sugars and acids only** [4]: 12.72 g sugars per 100 g × 4 kcal =
50.9 kcal; 1.83 g acids × about 3 kcal = 5.5 kcal. Total about 56 kcal/100
g fresh weight at harvest, before fibre, protein and fat. Range about
50-65 kcal. A proximate analysis would replace this.

**Bloom to edible** [5]: full bloom on 10 June + 154-164 days = 11-21
November. That agrees with the October-November harvest [2].

**Turkey 2024 to 2025** [11]: (4,370 − 5,390) ÷ 5,390 = −18.9%, as
stated.
