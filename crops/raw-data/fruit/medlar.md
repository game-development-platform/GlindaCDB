## Naming and scope

The common medlar (Rosaceae), a large deciduous shrub or small tree,
known by a range of local names across Turkey, the Caucasus, and Iran
[1][6][13]. This crop is frequently confused by name with two unrelated
fruits: a different tree fruit sometimes called "Japanese medlar" in
some languages (which this project treats as its own separate crop
entry, distinct from this one despite the shared vernacular name in a
couple of European languages), and the goji berry, which "medlar tea"
and "red medlar" usually actually refer to [1][4]. A rare North American
relative discovered in Arkansas in 1990 is a distinct wild species, not
a cultivated crop [1].

This entry is grounded against the closest researched sibling crop in
this project: another pome fruit of the same general region, also
grafted onto quince rootstock, almost always processed or stored before
being ripened off the tree. That sibling is not yet confirmed as
completed in this project's published crop set, so it is referred to
here only generally rather than by name.

This crop clearly passes this project's distinctiveness test against
other pome fruits it has researched: real, if modest, commercial
cultivation exists (scattered trees in hazelnut orchards on one
country's coast, small family orchards of named cultivars on a
specific dwarfing quince rootstock in a Balkan country, and
commercially available named cultivars in parts of Western Europe),
and the crop has a genuinely unique post-harvest requirement and
several other distinguishing traits covered below.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a fruit that must soften before it can be eaten.** This
fruit is hard and acidic when ripe on the tree and only becomes edible
after a softening process that lowers tannin and acid content while
raising sugar; the skin wrinkles and darkens and the flesh takes on an
applesauce-like texture. Unripe fruit holds a meaningfully high tannin
content. During softening, an enzyme browns the flesh while starches
convert to sugar. The usual commercial route is to pick the fruit hard
in late autumn, then lay it out in a single layer in a cool, dark,
frost-free, well-ventilated place for two to three weeks; one
controlled study found fruit reached edible maturity after about
twenty-five days at a specific cool storage temperature. Alternatively,
fruit can simply be left on the tree to soften naturally through
repeated light frosts, with one study finding that three distinct frost
cycles changed the fruit's composition least of several tested
softening methods (compared to deep freezing or heat treatment, both of
which also work but alter composition more). The practical consequence
is that the harvested raw commodity is not actually food until after a
timed conditioning step, and once softened the fruit keeps for only a
few days.

**Finding 2 — a self-fertile, frost-dodging tree with modest yields.**
Flowers are hermaphrodite, bee-pollinated, and self-fertile, so a
single tree can crop on its own. Bloom comes very late in the season
(May or June), opening at shoot tips well after the main spring-frost
risk has passed, so blossom is rarely damaged by frost — though late
frosts and spring storms can still occasionally cut the crop. Documented
per-tree yields run from roughly 30 to 70 kg a year, with a mature,
healthy tree more typically in the 40-50 kg range; a full crop comes
some six to seven years after grafting, and productive trees are kept
in production for twenty to twenty-five years, living considerably
longer overall. No per-hectare or national production statistics were
found for this crop in any source reached; yield figures in this entry
are therefore derived from per-tree data and an assumed planting
density rather than any direct per-hectare survey.

**Finding 3 — grafted onto other genera.** This crop is commercially
propagated by grafting onto hawthorn, mountain ash, pear, or quince
rootstock; grafting onto its own seedlings is specifically not
recommended because of their slow growth. Among quince rootstock
options, one is semi-vigorous while another is distinctly dwarfing,
producing smaller trees suited to home gardens; a specific named quince
clone is used in the Balkan commercial orchards documented in Finding
2. Seed is hard-coated and needs soaking followed by several months of
cold stratification; germination is slow and generally poor even then,
and seed retains viability for only around a year and a half.

**Finding 4 — fruit quality varies widely by type and growing
condition.** Fruit size ranges from small (2-3 cm) in wild stands to
considerably larger (3-8 cm) in cultivated selections, with documented
weight, dry-matter, soluble-solids, and acidity figures varying
substantially across both named commercial cultivars and wild/village
genotypes from different growing regions. Once softened, documented
composition includes substantial total sugars (dominated by fructose
and glucose, with smaller amounts of sorbitol and sucrose), meaningful
organic acid content, and a notable phenolic compound content that
drops sharply during the softening process itself.

**Finding 5 — mainly a garden and village fruit, with wild stands.**
This crop is native to the southeastern Balkans, Asia Minor, the
Caucasus, Crimea, and northern Iran, and has been cultivated for
roughly three thousand years, holding real importance in Roman and
medieval times before being largely superseded by other fruits from
around the 17th-18th centuries onward. In at least one country's
northeastern region, both wild and cultivated forms are actively used
side by side, and wild stands are also documented in at least one
Iranian forest region.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous large shrub or small tree;
  perennial bearing with a single autumn harvest window.
- **Harvest pattern:** Fruit is either picked hard for indoor softening,
  or deliberately left on the tree to soften naturally through frost
  exposure and gathered once soft — two genuinely distinct harvest
  stages for the same crop (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Temperate to warm-temperate and sub-Mediterranean
  climates with warm summers; the crop does not thrive in cold,
  unsheltered conditions despite tolerating fairly low winter minimum
  temperatures once dormant.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible when picked hard (astringent and
  acidic); becomes raw-edible only once softened (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Required in principle but self-fertile — a single
  isolated tree can crop on its own, pollinated by bees.
- **Yield / composition:** Yield tiers here are derived entirely from
  per-tree figures and an assumed planting density, since no
  per-hectare or national yield survey was found for this crop in any
  growing region (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-coefficient data exists for this
  specific crop; water-use figures are estimated by analogy to generic
  deciduous pome-fruit orchard crops in similar climates. The tree is
  generally undemanding once established but sheds young fruit under
  drought stress at fruit set, and poorly drained soil should be
  avoided.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by grafting onto hawthorn, mountain ash,
  pear, or quince rootstock; own-root cuttings root poorly, and
  seedling rootstocks of this species itself are specifically
  discouraged due to slow growth.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour study was found for this crop;
  figures throughout this entry are rough estimates, since the crop is
  grown mostly as scattered trees or small family orchards rather than
  large commercial operations with cost records.
- **Soil:** Fresh, well-drained loam is preferred; sources differ
  somewhat on exact pH tolerance, but both describe reasonably wide
  tolerance except for very chalky or waterlogged ground.
- **Stand life:** First fruit typically comes in the third or fourth
  growing season after planting; trees are kept in commercial production
  for twenty to twenty-five years and can live considerably longer
  overall.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Brown rot, powdery mildew, and a specific
  leaf-blight disease affecting a related rootstock genus are the main
  documented disease concerns, along with aphids, caterpillars, honey
  fungus, shoot-boring wasps, and documented susceptibility to fire
  blight in at least one regional survey — though a separate source
  describes fire blight as rare on this crop, particularly in large-
  scale production, a conflict not resolved here.
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named cultivars from England, the Netherlands,
  Hungary, and Balkan countries differ in fruit size, flavor, ripening
  time, and yield; wild and village-type genotypes from one country's
  northeastern region show a very wide range of fruit size and seed
  count, and at least one named seedless cultivar exists but is
  described as lower quality.
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked in dry weather, either hard (for indoor
  softening) or gathered soft directly from the tree after frost
  exposure, in one or several passes depending on the chosen method.
- **Storage:** Hard fruit can be held for weeks under refrigerated
  controlled-atmosphere storage; once softened, fruit keeps for only a
  few days and should be used promptly (Finding 1).

## Hazards

Seeds contain hydrogen cyanide and should not be crushed or eaten;
softened flesh itself is safe. A traditional folk use of seed tea for
kidney stones is documented in at least one source. The crop is also a
known host for fire blight, a serious bacterial disease of pome fruits,
in at least one regional disease survey, though this appears
inconsistent with another source's description of fire blight as rare
on this crop.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Hard fruit is sorted, has its stalks dipped in a strong
  salt solution to deter rot, and is laid out in a single layer to
  soften for two to three weeks before being eaten raw (traditionally
  with sugar and cream, or with cheese and port) or stored briefly
  under refrigerated controlled atmosphere before softening. Softened
  fruit is sieved into pulp (removing skins and seeds) and made into a
  sugar-set fruit cheese, wine, or distilled into schnapps; it is also
  made into jelly or liqueur. Fruit that softens naturally on the tree
  is eaten soft or pickled in vinegar in at least one regional
  tradition. Seeds are stratified to raise seedlings for breeding;
  prunings supply scion wood for grafting onto hawthorn or quince
  rootstock. Leaves are dried for traditional astringent folk remedies.
- Not covered by this research: frozen fruit as an alternative
  softening method; specific heat and chemical ripening-delay
  treatments; tanning and wine-fining uses of unripe-fruit tannin; seed
  oil and research uses of leaf material; the crop's documented wild-
  gathering tradition in several native-range countries.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from full bloom to harvest (roughly 150-170 days) are derived from
the documented late-May-to-early-June bloom period and the documented
late-October-to-November hard-harvest window, cross-checked against a
separately sourced figure describing peak fruit-sugar accumulation at
about 161 days after full bloom.

Yield tiers are derived entirely from documented per-tree yield figures
(a range from two different sources) multiplied by an assumed planting
density based on a documented minimum recommended tree spacing, since
no per-hectare or national yield survey was found for this crop in any
growing region; the resulting figures should be treated as considerably
weaker than yield tiers derived from direct per-hectare data elsewhere
in this project.

Calorie and protein content are derived by combining documented detailed
sugar, acid, and dry-matter composition figures with standard energy
-conversion factors for each component class, cross-checked against (and
found somewhat higher than) a separately sourced nutrition-website
calorie figure of unconfirmed origin.

Water-use figures (seasonal crop-coefficient-days, rooting depth,
soil-moisture-depletion allowance) are estimated by analogy to generic
deciduous pome-fruit orchard crop-coefficient data for a similar
high-latitude climate and stage-length pattern, since no crop-specific
water-use study was found; the resulting "days without water before
yield penalty" figure is derived from an assumed soil-water reserve
divided by an assumed daily water-use rate. Drought tolerance is rated
medium (reflecting the tree's generally undemanding nature balanced
against documented young-fruit drop under dry conditions) and
waterlogging tolerance is rated low (reflecting documented advice to
avoid poorly drained soils).

The growth-stage curve is estimated by analogy to a generic deciduous
pome-fruit tree's seasonal canopy development pattern.

Labor-hour figures across all operations are rough estimates, since no
cost or labor-hour study exists for this crop: harvest labor is derived
from an assumed hand-picking productivity rate reflecting the fruit's
small size and the need for gentle handling from a modest-height tree;
pruning labor is set lower than a comparable pome-fruit crop this
project has researched, reflecting this crop's documented need for only
light pruning; post-harvest labor reflects the documented practice of
laying fruit out in single layers and checking it repeatedly over the
multi-week softening period.

Typical softened-fruit shelf life (a few days) and an associated
processing-deadline figure are both estimated, reflecting the
documented need to use softened fruit promptly before it rots or dries
out.

Years to first harvest and the multi-year ramp to full bearing are
derived from a documented third-or-fourth-season first-fruiting figure
and a documented six-to-seven-year span to reach full cropping after
grafting, with a straight-line yield ramp assumed across that span in
the absence of any more detailed documented bearing curve.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (peak daily harvest rate, maximum
days fruit can stand before quality decline, and daily decay rate) are
derived from the adopted average yield divided across an assumed
multi-week picking period, reflecting that hard fruit can remain on the
tree well past normal ripeness (since frost exposure simply converts it
to the alternative softened harvest stage) while softened fruit decays
comparatively quickly once gathered.
