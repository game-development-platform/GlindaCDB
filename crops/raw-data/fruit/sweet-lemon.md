# Sweet Lemon / Limetta (*Citrus limetta*) and Palestine Sweet Lime (*C. limettioides*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers two closely related, nearly acidless citrus types,
documented together as one species group.

1. **Limetta / sweet lemon** (*C. limetta*; known by regional names
   including "sweet lime" and a specific Iranian name): nearly acidless
   and eaten fresh. This is the primary subject of this entry [1][4].
2. **Palestine (Indian) sweet lime** (*C. limettioides*): another
   acidless citrus, often confused with limetta [3]. Documented here as a
   species group within this same entry.

A third citrus type sometimes grouped under similar common names — a set
of genetically unrelated citron hybrids grown mainly as curiosities or
ornamentals, with minimal commercial importance — fails this project's
distinctiveness test and is instead noted as a cultivar-level detail
under a separate citron research entry this project has also produced,
not covered as its own row here. The naming is further tangled by at
least one source treating that citron-hybrid group as a straightforward
synonym of Palestine sweet lime, while another source treats them as
clearly separate — this entry follows the separation and keeps limetta
and Palestine sweet lime as the two species groups covered.

**Explicitly excluded:** a widely grown South Asian sweet-orange cultivar
that shares a common regional name with this crop group in casual usage.
That cultivar is botanically a sweet orange, grown on tens of thousands
of hectares in one Indian state, and is covered under this project's
separate sweet-orange research rather than here; production and juice-
market statistics tied to that name were excluded from this entry for
that reason.

This entry's distinctiveness rests on a judgment call rather than a
clean statistical case: no dedicated production statistics were found
for this specific group, and its tree, climate requirements, and general
orchard practice are assumed similar to other lemon-type citrus this
project has researched. The case for treating it separately rests on: it
is genuinely acidless, eaten fresh or juiced rather than used as a
souring agent, unlike typical lemon-type citrus; its juice turns bitter
within minutes of exposure to air, a processing-urgency constraint not
documented for other citrus this project has researched; it is
propagated from seed or cuttings rather than budded, unlike typical
lemon-type citrus, and one of its two species groups serves as a
significant citrus rootstock in several countries; it carries distinct
traditional home-remedy uses in at least two countries; and it has its
own specific regional market identity.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an acidless citrus whose juice spoils within minutes.**
Limetta fruit is small and round like a lime, oval and green ripening to
yellow, with pale greenish pulp and a notably thick white pith [1]. Its
flavor is sweet and mild, described as retaining some essential
character of lime, but this flavor changes rapidly on exposure to air and
turns bitter within minutes [1]. Fresh fruit keeps up to roughly two
weeks at room temperature and four to eight weeks refrigerated; frozen
juice keeps roughly six months [1]. A dedicated South American cold-
storage trial on this fruit found a specific moderately cool storage
temperature best for overall freshness over a two-week period, with an
even colder temperature better specifically for preserving polyphenol
content [5].

**Finding 2 — tree form and seed propagation.** The tree grows to a
substantial height, with notably long thorns [1]. It is native to
southern Iran, with South Asian forms believed to have arrived via a
historical trade route during the European colonial era [1]. Its
parentage is understood as either a cross between citron and bitter
orange, or simply a cultivar of lemon [1]. It is propagated by seed,
bearing from five to seven years after planting and reaching peak
production between ten and twenty years [1]. A historical U.S. citrus
variety collection documents several named selections: one notably low-
acid, everbearing selection with a spring main bloom and a distinctive
purple tinge to new growth and flowers; a fully acidless selection
without that purple tinge; and, by contrast, a genuinely acidic (sour)
selection within the same general limetta group, included as a reminder
that "limetta" does not always mean acidless [4].

**Finding 3 — Palestine sweet lime.** This is a Tahiti-lime-like tree,
bearing fruit singly or in small bunches [3]. Its pulp is pale yellow;
one source describes it as non-acid, bland, and faintly bitter, while a
second source instead describes it as having no notable bitterness — a
direct conflict not resolved here [3][2]. It is propagated from cuttings
in at least one major South Asian growing country and is self-compatible
[3]. It serves as a significant citrus rootstock in at least three
countries spanning South Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa [2][3].
It is grown across central and northern India, northern Vietnam, Egypt,
Mediterranean coastal regions, and tropical America, but is not grown for
fruit in at least one major U.S. citrus-growing state due to virus
susceptibility [3]. It is eaten fresh and valued in at least one country
for a traditional "cooling" remedy used in fever and jaundice [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial thorny evergreen citrus tree,
  reaching a substantial height; flowering mainly in spring, with at
  least one named selection flowering and setting fruit across the whole
  year.
- **Harvest pattern:** Year-round perennial bearing; a single harvest
  quality stage (ripe yellow fruit), with no documented green-picked use.
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean, and irrigated arid climates
  (its native range in southern Iran), plus tropical highlands.
- **Composition:** Composition figures are estimated, since no dedicated
  food-composition table was found; this fruit is assumed to sit between
  a typical acidic lemon-type citrus and a sweet orange in calorie
  density, reflecting its lower acidity and comparatively higher
  sweetness.
- **Pollination:** Palestine sweet lime is self-compatible, setting
  maximum fruit from self-pollination; limetta's pollination
  requirements were not specifically researched, and general insect-bee
  pollination support is assumed by analogy with other citrus.
- **Yield:** No dedicated production statistics were found for this
  specific crop group; yield figures here are estimated by scaling down
  from values documented for a lemon-type citrus this project has
  researched, reflecting an assumed lower-input, largely seedling-grown
  production system (see Estimates below for adopted tier values).
- **Water use:** No species-specific standard crop-coefficient curve was
  found; water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths,
  seasonal water totals, pH tolerance, planting density, labor figures,
  and stress multipliers) are all carried over by analogy from a lemon-
  type citrus this project has previously researched, given the shared
  genus and broadly similar tree form; seedling-propagated trees are
  likely somewhat larger and slower to reach bearing age than budded
  trees of that analog crop.
- **Propagation:** Limetta from seed; Palestine sweet lime from cuttings;
  named horticultural selections from budded scionwood (one documented
  historical U.S. collection selection was held on lemon rootstock).
  Palestine sweet lime is itself an important citrus rootstock used
  commercially in several countries.
- **Labor:** No crop-specific labor data were found; labor figures are
  carried over entirely by analogy from a lemon-type citrus this project
  has researched.
- **Soil:** pH tolerance figures are carried over by analogy from that
  same lemon-type citrus analog, since no species-specific soil data were
  found.
- **Stand life:** Peak production between ten and twenty years after
  planting from seed; total productive lifespan was not researched in
  detail.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Palestine sweet lime shows documented virus
  susceptibility severe enough that it is not grown for fruit in at least
  one major U.S. citrus-growing state, though it remains valued there and
  elsewhere purely as a rootstock.
- **Cultivars:** A baseline Iranian seedling type (acidless, sweet-mild,
  used as a traditional cold remedy); a very-low-acid, everbearing
  historical selection with a spring main bloom; a fully acidless
  selection; a genuinely acidic/sour outlier within the limetta group;
  the Palestine/Indian sweet lime species group generally (lime-like
  tree and fruit, bland and faintly bitter, propagated from cuttings,
  used as rootstock, virus-susceptible); and at least two further named
  sweet-lime selections, one of which is itself an acid (non-sweet)
  variant.
- **Harvest:** Fruit is picked ripe and sorted for fresh sale or cool
  storage; juice is specifically pressed to order and consumed
  immediately, given its rapid bitterness onset (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Roughly two weeks at room temperature or four to eight
  weeks refrigerated for fresh fruit; frozen juice keeps roughly six
  months; a dedicated storage trial found a specific moderately cool
  temperature best for overall freshness, with an even colder temperature
  better for polyphenol retention (Finding 1).

## Hazards

- No specific hazards were found for this crop group. Peel-oil
  phototoxicity (a documented concern for furanocoumarins in the peel oil
  of a related lemon-type citrus this project has researched) was not
  specifically researched for this crop group, and this entry does not
  extend that hazard here without a direct source. **TO BE ADDRESSED** —
  worth a dedicated check against a species-specific source before this
  crop's peel oil is treated as hazard-free.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit picked ripe → sorted → sold fresh or placed in cool
  storage at a specific moderately cool temperature. Fruit → juice
  pressed to order and consumed immediately, given its rapid bitterness
  onset. Seed → seedlings, grown on as fruiting trees or as citrus
  rootstock. Prunings (Palestine sweet lime) → cuttings for further
  propagation.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-juice processing;
  peel-oil constituents and extraction; the excluded citron-hybrid group
  and the excluded sweet-orange cultivar noted under Naming and scope.

## Open research questions

- No dedicated production statistics (growing area, national tonnage)
  were found for this crop group specifically; regional production
  figures are entangled with a differently classified sweet-orange
  cultivar sharing a similar regional name, and could not be reliably
  separated out.
- Palestine sweet lime's flavor is described inconsistently between two
  sources — "faintly bitter" versus "no notable bitterness" — not
  resolved.
- A cold-storage trial's optimal temperature recommendation sits well
  below the documented chilling-injury threshold reported for a related
  lime-type citrus this project has researched — a conflict not resolved
  here.
- The exact taxonomic relationship between this crop group and the
  citron-hybrid group excluded under Naming and scope is genuinely
  disputed between sources (treated as a synonym by one source, as fully
  separate by another) — not resolved.
- Harvest-season timing was not researched for this crop group.
- Whether this crop group should ultimately be folded into a broader
  lemon-type citrus entry as an acidless cultivar group, rather than kept
  as its own separate entry, remains an open judgment call noted at the
  point of original research.
- Composition figures, soil nutrient-uptake figures, and detailed labor
  figures were not independently researched and are carried over by
  analogy or estimated from general values.

## Estimates and derivations

Composition figures (calorie and protein content) are estimated as
intermediate between a typical acidic lemon-type citrus and a sweet
orange, reflecting this crop's documented lower acidity and higher
sweetness; a dedicated food-composition table for this specific crop
would replace this estimate.

Water-use parameters, soil pH tolerance, planting density, non-harvest
labor figures, and growth-stress multipliers are all carried over by
analogy from a lemon-type citrus this project has previously researched,
given the shared genus and broadly similar tree form.

Yield tiers are estimated by scaling down from that same lemon-type
citrus analog's documented yield figures, on the assumption that this
largely seedling-grown, lower-input crop group yields somewhat less per
hectare across all tiers; no direct production statistics were available
to calibrate this scaling.

Growing-timeline figures (days from seedling planting to first harvest,
and the yield-profile's ramp period) are derived directly from the
documented five-to-seven-year first-bearing window and the documented
peak-production age range of roughly ten to twenty years after planting.

The yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived by assuming a
roughly three-month main harvest window (consistent with the documented
everbearing-with-spring-main-bloom pattern in at least one named
selection) and spreading the adopted world-average yield tier across
that window; on-tree standing-days and quality-decay-rate figures assume
typical citrus on-tree holding behavior, since fruit of this type is not
documented as needing urgent harvest once ripe (unlike its juice, which
spoils within minutes once pressed).
