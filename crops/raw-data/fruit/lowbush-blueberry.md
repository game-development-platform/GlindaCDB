## Naming and scope

The commercial "wild blueberry" of Maine and eastern Canada. It is a
managed native stand, not a planted crop [1]. A field can hold "as many
as 1,500 genetically distinct wild blueberry plants" [1].
- **Low sweet** (*V. angustifolium* Ait.): the most abundant species in
  commercial fields. It grows 4-15 in tall, with smooth leaves and
  powder-blue berries. The variety *nigrum* has black fruit, with less
  wax, and tends to be sweeter [1]. Chromosome number 2n = 48 [14].
- **Sour top or velvetleaf** (*V. myrtilloides* Michx.): 6-24 in tall,
  with hairy leaves and smaller, less sweet, bright blue berries. It is
  "more cold tolerant and later blooming than low sweet" [1]. It is
  diploid (2n = 24) and hybridizes with *V. angustifolium* in the wild
  [14].
- **Section:** both are in *Vaccinium* sect. *Cyanococcus* (Ericaceae)
  [1][14].
- **Not covered:**
  - **Black huckleberry:** *Gaylussacia baccata* is a weed in these
    fields [1].
  - **Western huckleberries:** none of these occur in Maine [1].
  - **Bilberry:** European bilberry (*V. myrtillus*) does not occur in
    North America apart from a small disjunct population [1]. It is its
    own row, `bilberry` (`bilberry.md`), whose sources place it in
    western North America; see that doc's Naming and scope.
  - **Highbush:** *V. corymbosum* is the planted crop `blueberry`
    (`blueberry.md`) [1].

**Relatives in this project:**
- blueberry (`blueberry`, `blueberry.md`): highbush, the planted
  counterpart;
- lingonberry (`lingonberry`, `lingonberry.md`);
- cranberry (`cranberry`, `cranberry.md`);
- bilberry (`bilberry`, `bilberry.md`).

**Proposed:**
- **Half-high blueberry** (`half_high_blueberry`, *V. angustifolium* × *V.
  corymbosum*): 20-60 in tall; "occurs naturally in ME, but is also used
  commercially" [1]. Gate-checked 2026-11-23 and not promoted: it is
  covered as a cultivar group in `blueberry.md`.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a managed native stand on a two-year cycle.**
- **Origin:** "this crop is not planted but inhabits large fields on
  mountaintops and in glacial outwash plains" [1]. Commercial fields
  "were forests before human intervention" [10].
- **Cycle:** after the August harvest, plants are pruned to the ground by
  mowing or burning. The next year is vegetative (stems, leaves and
  buds), and the year after blooms and fruits. Farmers "typically divide
  their acreage equally between the two cycles in order to harvest a crop
  every year" [1].
- **Pruning rules:**
  - **Height:** mow to within 1 inch of the soil. Pruning higher cut
    yield from 8,000 to 6,000 lb/acre [2].
  - **Timing:** prune only once plants are dormant, after a killing
    frost. Early fall pruning lost 5,000 lb over three crop cycles [2].
  - **Late spring pruning:** buds fall by 20% if pruning is done by May
    1, by 50% by July 1, and by 100% by the end of July [2].
- **Burning against mowing:**
  - **History:** burning was traditional. In the 1950s, hard burning was
    found to injure rhizomes. Most growers switched to mowing, for oil
    cost, after 1970s work showed equal yields [2].
  - **Sanitation:** burning still serves as sanitation; it removes about
    50% of mummy berries [8].
  - **Organic field:** yield was doubled by burning against mowing, and
    tripled by burning plus sulfur [7].
- **Wabanaki origin:** it was "first grown on a large scale by the
  Wabanaki native people who burn-pruned and harvested fields" [1][15].

**Finding 2 — yield is set by pollination, weeds and management.**
- **Maine yields (NASS):** 2,290-5,030 lb/acre harvested in 2012-2024.
  The lows were 2,290 in 2020 and 2,681 in 2018, and the highs 5,030 in
  2012 and 5,000 in 2021 [4] (see Estimates).
- **Ceiling:** "there are some now that have exceeded 10,000 pounds per
  acre" [2]. Irrigation is "the limiting factor in obtaining yields that
  exceed 10,000 pounds per acre" [2].
- **Pollination:**
  - **Pollen:** large and sticky, so insects must cross-pollinate. More
    bee visits give more seeds, larger fruit and earlier, more even
    ripening [2].
  - **Native bees:** at 0.5 bees per square yard of bloom per minute,
    native bees give 20-35% fruit set [5].
  - **Honeybee stocking** [5]:
    | Stocking (with native bees) | Fruit set | Yield, lb/acre (range) |
    |---|---|---|
    | 2 hives/acre | about 65% | about 4,000 (1,800-9,000) |
    | 1 hive/acre | about 45% | about 3,000 (1,000-5,000) |
    | none | — | about 1,800 (1,000-2,800) |
  - **Other rates:** 1-8 hives per acre, placed at 10-20% bloom for
    about 2 weeks [14].
  - **Scale:** Maine imports "over 60 thousand hives each year". Its
    crop rose from 20 to 90 million lb over 30 years, in step with hive
    numbers [2].
- **Weeds:** yield fell from over 5,000 to 1,000 lb/acre as weed cover
  rose from 0 to 100% [2].
- **Canada:** demand for pollination exceeds supply. In 2024, imported
  colonies outnumbered resident ones on PEI for the first time [11].

**Finding 3 — roots only a few centimetres deep, with water use at 70%
of reference ET.**
- **Rooting:** horizontal roots "normally extend only between two and
  four inches below the soil surface but travel radially" [3].
- **Crop coefficient:** Kc 0.70, "fairly constant" from May through
  August, from four years of weighing lysimeters [3].
- **Measured use:** at Deblois, June-August use averaged 0.88 in/week,
  varying from under 0.60 to almost 1.20 in/week [3]. The 1 in/week rule
  of thumb is "fairly accurate" [3].
- **Reference ET (in/week, May-August)** [3]:
  | Site | May | June | July | August |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Deblois (~12 mi inland) | 1.11 | 1.20 | 1.39 | 1.12 |
  | Jonesboro (~5 mi) | 0.91 | 1.07 | 1.27 | 1.05 |
  | Jonesport (~0.5 mi) | 0.85 | 0.97 | 1.09 | 0.95 |
- **Rain shortfall:** fields get enough water in August in "only one year
  out of five". Irrigating both the prune and crop years beats
  irrigating only the crop year [3].
- **Drought response:** relatively drought-resistant, but very dry
  periods give shorter stems with fewer buds [3].
- **Tensiometer triggers:** irrigate at 50% depletion: above 25 cb on
  loamy sand and above 35 cb on sandy loam [3]. On sand, two 0.5 in
  applications on consecutive days are better than one 1 in application
  [3].

**Finding 4 — almost entirely a frozen-food crop.**
- **Maine:** 99% frozen and 1% fresh [1]. Fresh shares ran 0.4-3.1% in
  2012-2024 [4].
- **Canada:** over 98% is processed or individually quick-frozen [11].
- **Prices (Maine, $/lb)** [4]:
  - **Processed:** 0.25 (2017) to 0.75 (2012, 2013 and 2021); 0.51 in
    2024.
  - **Fresh:** 0.79-2.54.
  - **Peak:** the all-market price peaked in 2007 at $1.07/lb [1].
- **Volumes:**
  - **Maine:** 90.8 million lb in 2024 from 19,100 harvested acres [4].
    The state has 38,000 acres of commercial land and about 500 farmers
    [1].
  - **Canada:** 94,111 t in 2024 and 105,020 t in 2023, on 71,843 ha
    including non-bearing land [11].
  - **Canada by province, 2024:** Quebec 46.8% (43,997 t), New Brunswick
    25.6% (24,076 t), Nova Scotia 17.4% (16,340 t) and PEI 10% (9,460 t)
    [11].
- **History:** the berries were first canned in 1886, and there were 21
  canning factories by the 1950s [1].

**Finding 5 — pests and diseases tied to the cycle and to bloom
weather.**
- **Mummy berry** (*Monilinia*):
  - **Spread:** spores are spread by pollinators, so burning symptomatic
    plants in May-June does not stop it [8].
  - **Fungicide timing:** wait until at least 40% of flower buds are open
    to the crown stage (F3) and until infection weather arrives, "over 6
    to 8 hours of rain or fog" [5].
  - **Cultural control:** 2-3 in of mulch stops most mummies germinating
    [8].
- **Botrytis blossom blight:** "highly variable and dependent upon early
  infection and rain around bloom" [5].
- **Leaf diseases:** Septoria leaf spot and rust have increased since
  growers switched from burning to mowing [2]. Valdensia leaf spot gives
  "a 100% yield loss" and is controlled by spot burning [2].
- **Insects:**
  - **Blueberry maggot:** infested berries can be "rejected" [2].
  - **Spotted wing drosophila:** the action threshold is 7-16 males [5].
  - **Spanworm:** the threshold is 10 caterpillars per 10 sweeps, or
    15-20 under reduced inputs [5].
  - **Others:** flea beetle, sawfly, thrips, tip midge, fireworm and
    strawberry rootworm [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous, rhizomatous dwarf shrub, 5-60 cm
  [14] or 4-24 in by species [1]. Flowers are white or pink bells, 4-6 mm
  [14]. Managed on the two-year prune/crop cycle (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** One harvest per field every second year, in
  August [1]. A farm with half its land in each phase harvests every
  year [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Existing stands:** the first crop comes in the second year after
    pruning [1].
  - **New plantings:** seedlings are greenhouse-grown for 18 months to 2
    years, and cuttings are ready after two growing seasons. Interplanted
    bare spots can be "in full production within six years" [10].
  - **Stand life:** effectively indefinite; fields are never replanted
    [1][10]. Rhizomes "can lie dormant up to 100 years" (the source is
    itself tagged as uncited) [14].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Range:** Manitoba to Newfoundland, and south to the Great Smoky
    Mountains and west to the Great Lakes [14].
  - **Habitat:** native to northern New England and Atlantic Canada [1],
    on glacial outwash plains and mountaintops [1].
  - **Elevation:** no figure was sourced.
  - **Chill:** no lowbush figure. Northern highbush needs 600-1,000 h
    below 7 °C [12] (not applied here).
  - **Frost, lowest safe temperature by bud stage** [6]:
    | Stage | °F | °C |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1 Tight bud | -4 | -20.0 |
    | 2 Minor scale separation | 7 | -13.9 |
    | 4 Inflorescence tip visible | 10 | -12.2 |
    | 5 All flowers visible, closed | 19 | -7.2 |
    | 6 Flowers distended, closed | 23 | -5.0 |
    | 7 Fully open flowers | 28 | -2.2 |
  - **Winter injury:** in February 2023, a Nova Scotia polar vortex
    reached -25 °C. That winter's extreme cold and excess rain damaged
    the buds of the 2024 Maritime crop [11].
- **Composition (per 100 g):**
  - **USDA SR Legacy 173949, "Blueberries, wild, frozen":** 57 kcal (238
    kJ); water 85.8 g; protein 0.0 g (as printed); fat 0.16 g;
    carbohydrate 13.85 g; fibre 4.4 g; calcium 17 mg; iron 0.58 mg;
    magnesium 7 mg; phosphorus 13 mg; potassium 68 mg; manganese 2.87 mg;
    vitamin C 1.7 mg; vitamin A 3 µg RAE [13].
  - **Not used:** SR Legacy 169799 is Alaska Native wild blueberries:
    44 kcal, water 88.7 g, protein 0.7 g, carbohydrate 10.4 g [13].
  - **Berry size:** under 1 g, "much smaller... than highbush types"
    [12].
- **Edibility:** Raw-edible; mostly eaten frozen or processed into pie,
  muffin, pancake, jam and sauce products [12].
- **Pollination:** Required; insect cross-pollination between clones
  (Finding 2) [2][5].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Maine:** 2,290-5,030 lb/acre harvested in 2012-2024, about 4,056 on
    average (see Estimates) [4]. "Nearly 5,000 pounds per acre" is an
    extension summary figure (snippet) [16].
  - **Canada:** 1.31 t/ha in 2024 over all area, including non-bearing
    and prune-year land (see Estimates) [11].
  - **Ceiling:** over 10,000 lb/acre [2].
- **Water use:** Kc 0.70 May-August; peak use about 0.97 in/week (Finding
  3) [3]. About 377 mm May-August at Deblois (see Estimates).
- **Propagation:**
  - **Natural spread:** plants spread "most often... through seed
    dispersal" and cover ground by rhizomes. Spread into bare spots is
    "very slow" and takes "decades" [10].
  - **Gap filling:** seedlings (the cheapest option, with variable
    clones), cuttings (uniform, but slower to form rhizomes), tissue
    culture (true to the mother plant, rhizomes form fast) and sod plugs
    [10]. Fire pruning killed young seedlings in trials [10].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** tissue-culture plants "should have no obvious
    fungal or bacterial diseases" [10]. No virus was named in the sources
    read.
- **Labor:**
  - **Machine harvest:** about 80% of acreage, up from 20% a decade
    earlier. A machine picks about 10,000 lb on a good day, roughly ten
    times a hand raker's daily output (undated press snippet) [16].
  - **Hand rakers:** a good raker could earn over $300 a day on box piece
    rates, and one harvester replaced 10-12 rakers (2014 snippet) [16].
  - **Enterprise budgets:** UMaine budgets exist in Excel for 15-500
    acres, conventional and organic [16]. Their labour lines were not
    read.
  - **Hours:** no labour-hour figure was found; see Estimates.
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture:** acidic glacial outwash sands [1][14].
  - **pH:** target pH 4.0 [2][7]. Sulfur at 100 lb/acre lowers pH by
    about 0.1 unit, takes 2-3 years to act and may need reapplying after
    six years. Do not exceed 1,000 lb/acre a year [7]. One organic field
    went from pH 5.0 to 4.0 with 1,000 lb/acre [7].
  - **Sampling:** soil cores at 3-4 in depth [7].
  - **Nutrients:** fertiliser is guided by leaf tests at tip dieback,
    because the rhizome stores nutrients. Excess fertiliser feeds weeds
    [2][7].
  - **Salinity:** Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest)** [12]:
  - **Conditions:** -0.5 to 0 °C at over 90% RH, for up to 2 weeks for
    lowbush.
  - **Precooling:** precooling to 5 °C causes condensation in lowbush
    packed at ambient temperatures, but should be used when picking to
    packing exceeds 21 hours.
  - **CA:** 10-15% CO2 plus 1-10% O2 at or below 5 °C, with shelf life up
    to 6 weeks (all blueberry types).
  - **Chilling and ethylene:** not chilling-sensitive. Ethylene
    stimulates *Botrytis*.
  - **Respiration (general blueberry):** 2-10 mg CO2/kg/h at 0 °C and
    52-87 at 20-21 °C.
  - **Rots:** *Botrytis*, anthracnose and, above 10 °C, *Rhizopus*.
- **Establishment:**
  - **Origin:** not planted; clones colonize clearings after forest
    removal [1][10].
  - **Inputs:** weed control, pruning, pollination, leaf-test fertiliser,
    pest scouting and irrigation, in that order of priority [2].
- **Cultivars:** none; fields are mixtures of wild clones [1].
  Sour top blooms later and is more cold-tolerant than low sweet [1].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost at bloom:** stage thresholds are in Climate zones [6].
    Sprinkler frost protection should start 2 °F above the lowest safe
    temperature, or 4 °F above in a breeze. Big-gun systems are
    unsuitable [6].
  - **Rain at bloom:** drives mummy berry and *Botrytis* (Finding 5)
    [5].
  - **Drought:** fewer buds and smaller berries [3][11]. The Maritime
    crop fell 16% in 2024 [11].
- **Water regime:** Mostly rainfed; supplemental irrigation is spreading
  [2][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Harvest is in August [1]. It is
  once-over, by rake or machine [16]. Prune after the plant goes dormant
  [2].
- **Soil biology:** wild blueberry "partners with mychorrhizae fungi that
  scavenge for nutrients" in its acidic soil [7].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Status:** native; no introduced or invasive range was found
    [1][14].
  - **Conservation:** *V. myrtilloides* is "Secure" (G5) globally, but
    endangered in Indiana and Connecticut, and threatened in Iowa and
    Ohio [14].
  - **Sanitation between fields:** wash boxes, harvesters and sprayers in
    the field of use, and compost or burn winnower refuse [8].
- **Controlled environment:** Only nursery stock: seedlings are grown in
  greenhouses for 18 months to 2 years [10].

## Hazards

- **Hexazinone (Velpar) in groundwater** (snippet level) [16]:
  - **Early surveys:** a 1994 Maine survey found it in 15 of 20
    blueberry-area sites, and a follow-up found it in 35 of 48.
  - **Later trends:** a nine-year study saw five of seven wells
    decreasing. Another study found concentrations "changed very little"
    despite a four-fold rate cut.
  - **Why:** high solubility on sandy soils.
  - **Weed resistance:** after 30 years of use, weeds have become
    resistant, so herbicides need rotating [2].
- **Insecticide residue and bee toxicity:** several registered maggot and
  SWD products are "extremely toxic to bees" and fish [9]. Avoid
  fungicide sprays at bloom [8].
- **Burning:** a field practice risk; hard burning damages rhizomes [2].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (frozen, about 99%):** raked or machine-harvested → winnowed
  and cleaned → frozen or IQF → retail packs and ingredient wholesale
  [1][11].
- **Chain 2 (fresh, about 1%):** hand-harvested into half boxes → cleaned
  → packed in clamshells → -0.5 to 0 °C for up to 2 weeks [5][12].
- **Chain 3 (canned):** historically canned, from 1886 [1].
- **Products:** pie and pastry fillings, pancakes, muffins, jams and
  sauces [12]. Maine's state dessert is wild blueberry pie [14].
- **Indigenous use:**
  - **Food:** fruit eaten fresh or preserved by the Abenaki, Cree,
    Ojibwa, Algonquin, Potawatomi and others (*V. myrtilloides*) [14].
  - **Medicine:** Cree decoctions of the leafy stems; also a quill dye
    [14].
- **Symbols:** state fruit of Maine; Nova Scotia's provincial berry [14].
- **Ritual and religious:** a "long-standing sacred food for the Wabanaki
  people," used "for medicine and ceremony". No specific ceremony is named
  [15]. A Maliseet belief held that the bear's food gave stamina
  (snippet) [16].

## Estimates and derivations

**Unit conversions:** 1 lb/acre = 1.121 kg/ha; 1 in = 25.4 mm.

**Maine mean yield** [4], 2012-2024 excluding 2013 (no acreage given):
- (4,760 + 3,680 + 3,940 + 5,000 + 2,290 + 3,800 + 2,681 + 3,988 + 4,409
  + 4,514 + 4,579 + 5,030) = 48,671 ÷ 12 = 4,056 lb/acre.
- 4,056 × 1.121 = 4,547 kg/ha per harvested hectare.

**Yield tiers** (per harvested crop-year hectare):
- Poor: 2,290 lb/acre (2020) × 1.121 = 2,567 kg/ha [4].
- Typical: 4,547 kg/ha (the NASS mean) [4].
- Good: 5,030 lb/acre (2012) × 1.121 = 5,639 kg/ha [4].
- Exceptional: 10,000 lb/acre × 1.121 = 11,210 kg/ha [2].
- Per hectare of total farm land, halve these figures (half the land is
  in the prune year) [1].

**Maine 2024 check** [4]: 90.8 million lb ÷ 19,100 acres = 4,754 lb/acre
(table 4,760). 90.8 million × 0.4536 = 41,190 t.

**Canada** [11]:
- 2024: 94,111 t ÷ 71,843 ha = 1.31 t/ha over all area.
- If about half the area is harvested: 1.31 × 2 = 2.62 t/ha per
  harvested hectare. This is an assumption; AAFC also includes
  non-bearing land.
- 2022: 115,986 ÷ 68,577 = 1.69 t/ha.
- North American share (2024): 94,111 ÷ (94,111 + 41,190) = 70% Canadian.

**Hive density** [5]: 2 hives/acre × 2.471 = 4.9 hives/ha; 1 hive/acre =
2.5/ha.

**Seasonal crop water use, Deblois** [3]:
- ETo May-August = 1.11 × 4.43 + 1.20 × 4.29 + 1.39 × 4.43 + 1.12 × 4.43
  = 4.92 + 5.15 + 6.16 + 4.96 = 21.19 in.
- × 0.70 = 14.83 in ≈ 377 mm.
- July peak: 1.39 × 0.70 = 0.97 in/week = 24.7 mm/week ≈ 3.5 mm/day.

**Root-zone depth:** 2-4 in = 5-10 cm [3]. Effective root zone taken as
0.10 m.

**Days without water:** at 0.10 m and an assumed 80 mm/m available water
for sandy loam (an assumption, not sourced), about 8 mm is available. At
50% depletion, 4 mm ÷ 3.5 mm/day ≈ 1 day at peak. That matches the
guide's call for twice-weekly half-inch irrigations on sands [3]; the
organic mat probably buffers more than this.

**Harvest labour** (rough): a raker at about 1,000 lb/day (one-tenth of a
machine's 10,000) [16] picks a 4,056 lb/acre crop in about 4 raker-days
per acre. × 2.471 = 10 raker-days/ha ≈ 80 h/ha at 8 h. Machine: 4,056 ÷
10,000 = 0.4 machine-days per acre ≈ 1 day/ha.

**Sulfur rate** [7]: 100 lb/acre per 0.1 pH × 1.121 = 112 kg/ha per 0.1
unit. pH 5.0 → 4.0 needs about 1,000 lb/acre (1,121 kg/ha), matching the
organic-field result.

**Frost conversions** [6]: (°F − 32) × 5/9; e.g. 28 °F = -2.2 °C and -4 °F
= -20.0 °C.

**Price** [4]: 2024 processed $0.51/lb × 4,760 lb/acre = $2,428 per
harvested acre gross; × 2.471 = $6,000/ha.
