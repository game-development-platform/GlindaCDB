# Lowbush / Wild Blueberry (*Vaccinium angustifolium*, *V. myrtilloides*)

## Naming and scope

The commercial "wild blueberry" of Maine and Atlantic Canada, which is a
managed mixture of native clones rather than a planted monoculture
[1][2]:

- mainly the low sweet blueberry (*V. angustifolium*), including a
  black-fruited botanical variety;
- some sour top or velvet-leaf blueberry (*V. myrtilloides*), more
  common on hillier sites.

A taller, cultivated blueberry species this project has also researched
separately sometimes hybridizes with these native clones at field
edges, but is a distinct crop with a very different farming system.
True bilberry and western huckleberries are different plants entirely
and are not covered here [1].

This entry is grounded against that cultivated blueberry crop for
general comparison.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a managed native stand with a two-year rhythm.** After the
late-summer harvest, fields are pruned all the way to the ground, by
either flail mowing or controlled burning [1][4]. In the following
"prune year," plants grow vegetatively and set flower buds; in the next
"crop year," they bloom for a few weeks in spring and ripen in mid- to
late summer [1]. Burning is the traditional method, originally practiced
by Indigenous peoples who periodically burned these fields; repeated
hard burning, however, was found to destroy the soil's organic layer and
expose rhizomes to damage, so most growers switched to mowing, though
occasional burning is still used deliberately to control pests that
overwinter in field litter [1][4]. Because fields are never replanted —
they originate from native understory clones exposed by forest
clearing — a managed farm with half its land in each phase of the cycle
produces a steady crop every single year, even though any given field is
only harvested every other year.

**Finding 2 — yield is set by clone diversity and pollination.**
Average yield in a crop year is documented as "nearly 5,000 pounds per
acre," with dense, intensively managed, productive fields potentially
reaching roughly double that figure [1]. Since the managed clones are
genetically distinct individuals, successful fruit set specifically
requires cross-pollination between different clones [1]. A university
extension recommendation calls for a modest density of rented honeybee
hives per acre during bloom, supplementing wild bumblebees and other
native bees; a single acre of blooming field can carry an extremely
large number of individual blossoms [1][2]. Gaps in a field are filled
using softwood or rhizome cuttings, sod transplants, seedlings bred from
crosses of good clones, or tissue-culture plugs, with mulch helping
newly placed material spread via rhizome growth [1].

**Finding 3 — very shallow roots, with water use well below full
reference evapotranspiration.** The horizontal rhizome-and-root system
of this crop lies only a few inches below the soil surface [3].
Documented crop water use runs at roughly seven-tenths of reference
evapotranspiration, with weekly water-use figures from Maine growing
sites coming in close to — but somewhat different from — an older,
simpler rule-of-thumb irrigation guideline [3]. Growers are advised to
irrigate based on soil-moisture-tension readings, with the specific
threshold depending on soil texture [3]. Irrigating during the "prune"
(non-harvest) year specifically increases flower-bud formation and
therefore raises the following year's crop yield [1] — meaning water
stress in the off year carries over directly into the next year's
harvest.

**Finding 4 — fundamentally a frozen-food crop.** The great majority of
the crop (99% in one major producing region) is cleaned and frozen,
with well under 1% sold fresh [1]. One major producing US state is
described as the largest wild-blueberry producer in the world, and
together with an Atlantic Canadian growing region supplies a very large
share of total North American blueberry production [1]. Storage
guidance for the small fresh-market fraction follows the same general
pattern documented for a cultivated blueberry crop this project has
also researched — refrigerated just above or at freezing, at very high
humidity, for up to about two weeks, with prompt precooling required if
the gap between picking and packing runs long.

**Finding 5 — pests and diseases are tied closely to the management
cycle.** The main disease pressures are a fungal disease causing fruit
mummification and a related blossom blight, both favored by wet weather
during bloom; fungal leaf diseases are documented as worse in mowed
fields than in burned ones [1]. Key insect pests include a fruit-
infesting maggot (managed with an action-threshold monitoring approach),
along with sporadic outbreaks of a looper caterpillar, a flea beetle,
and thrips [1]. The availability of effective pre- and post-emergence
herbicides is specifically credited with making efficient mechanical
harvest practical [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A rhizomatous, very low-growing (dwarf) shrub,
  managed on a two-year biennial production cycle alternating a
  vegetative "prune year" and a fruiting "crop year" (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single once-over harvest per crop year, by hand
  rake or machine, with a large majority of acreage in at least one
  major producing region now harvested mechanically; each individual
  field is cropped only every other year, though a farm with fields
  staggered across both phases harvests every year (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate to boreal-margin climates, native from
  the upper US Midwest through Atlantic Canada and south along higher
  elevations to parts of the southern Appalachians.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated, specifically needing
  cross-pollination between genetically distinct managed clones for good
  fruit set, supplemented with rented honeybee hives alongside wild bees
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Average yield in a harvested (crop-year)
  hectare is documented in the range of several thousand kilograms, with
  dense, well-managed fields potentially reaching roughly double the
  average (Finding 2; see Estimates below). At the whole-farm level,
  since only half the managed land is harvested in any given year,
  per-hectare-of-total-land figures are roughly half these crop-year
  figures.
- **Water use:** Crop water use runs at roughly seven-tenths of
  reference evapotranspiration; the crop is extremely shallow-rooted
  (just a few inches), making it notably drought-sensitive despite its
  moderate overall water demand, since so little soil moisture is ever
  within reach of its roots (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Never planted in the conventional sense — fields
  originate from native understory clones exposed by clearing forest
  cover, with any gaps later filled using cuttings, sod, seedlings, or
  tissue-culture plugs rather than a full planting program (Finding 1,
  2).
- **Labor:** No complete commercial labor-hour budget was found;
  current estimates rely on a documented hand-raking productivity rate
  and rough judgment calls for other operations (see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Strongly acidic, sandy, glacial-origin soils, with a
  documented optimum pH distinctly lower than that of the cultivated
  blueberry crop this project has also researched.
- **Stand life:** Effectively indefinite — managed clonal stands persist
  for decades as rhizomes spread, with rhizomes themselves documented as
  capable of lying dormant for up to roughly a century; repeated hard
  burning (rather than mowing) is the one documented cause of real
  long-term stand decline, since it destroys the soil's protective
  organic layer (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal fruit-mummification disease and
  related blossom blight (worse in wet bloom weather), fungal leaf
  diseases (worse under mowing than burning), a fruit-infesting maggot
  pest, and sporadic looper, flea beetle, and thrips outbreaks (Finding
  5).
- **Cultivars:** Commercial fields are mixtures of wild clones rather
  than named cultivars; the two main species/variety forms differ in
  berry color, size, sweetness, and typical site (lowland versus
  hillier terrain) (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** A single once-over pass per crop year, by hand rake or
  increasingly by machine, timed from mid-summer through roughly early
  autumn (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** The large majority of the crop is frozen almost
  immediately after harvest rather than stored fresh; the small
  fresh-market fraction follows storage guidance similar to a cultivated
  blueberry crop this project has also researched — up to about two
  weeks just above freezing at very high humidity (Finding 4).

## Hazards

No specific food-safety or human-health hazard was found for this crop
in the sources reached; pesticide and herbicide use are treated here as
ordinary crop-management practice rather than a distinct food hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fields are rake- or machine-harvested once over, then
  winnowed and hand-sorted. The large majority of the crop is frozen;
  a small fraction is graded for fresh sale, precooled, and sold under
  refrigeration within about two weeks.
- **Uses:** Frozen fruit is used as a widespread food ingredient; fresh
  sales make up well under 1% of total crop volume. This crop is the
  official state fruit of one US state and the official provincial berry
  of one Canadian province.
- Not covered by this research: freezing and canning processing detail;
  bee-hive rental and placement logistics; juice, jam, or dried-fruit
  production (not researched for this specific crop); controlled-burning
  technique detail.

  between two sources (a narrower versus a wider range) — not
  reconciled.
- Good- and poor-yield tiers used here are rough estimates rather than
  directly sourced figures; a documented multi-year regional yield
  statistics series (from either of the two major producing regions)
  would improve them.
- A dedicated commercial labor-hour budget was not found (a referenced
  university budget document was unreachable); current labor-hour
  figures are rough estimates built from a documented hand-raking
  productivity rate.
- Frost-damage thresholds at bloom were not researched.
- Chill-requirement hours for dormancy break were not specifically
  researched for this crop; it is treated by analogy as similar to the
  cultivated blueberry crop's cold-hardy northern types.
- Detailed nutrient (nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium) uptake rates were
  not researched; fertilization in practice is guided by leaf-tissue
  testing rather than a fixed application-rate table.
- Mechanical-harvester capacity and bloom-to-harvest timing by specific
  clone type were not researched in detail.
- Waterlogging tolerance is an estimate rather than a directly sourced
  rating.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein content (roughly 570 kcal/kg and 7.4 g protein/kg)
are estimated by analogy to the cultivated blueberry crop's own raw
nutrition figures, adjusted slightly to reflect that wild berries tend
to be smaller with proportionally more skin relative to flesh; no
dedicated food-composition-database entry specific to this wild crop
was found.

Yield tiers are derived from a documented "nearly 5,000 lb/acre" average
yield figure (converted to a metric per-hectare basis for a harvested
crop-year field) and a documented "double that" figure for dense,
intensively managed fields as the exceptional tier; good and poor tiers
are estimates falling between and below these documented figures
respectively, pending a proper multi-year regional yield-statistics
series from either major producing region.

Growing-season length and seasonal crop-coefficient-days are derived
from a documented active growing season of roughly five months
(spring bloom through summer harvest) combined with the documented
seven-tenths-of-reference-evapotranspiration crop coefficient.

Effective root-zone depth (a few centimeters) is taken directly from
documented rooting-depth measurements. Days-without-water-before-penalty
is estimated from a rough available-water-holding calculation for a
typical sandy soil at that shallow root depth divided by an assumed
peak-season water-use rate, with an upward adjustment to account for the
buffering effect of the organic surface layer and rhizome mat
documented for these fields. Drought tolerance is rated low (given the
extremely shallow root system) and waterlogging tolerance is a rough
estimate (reflecting the crop's origin in forest-understory and open
barren habitats, with no direct wet-tolerance data found).

A growth-stage curve is estimated judgementally as a deciduous-shrub
pattern — a spring leaf-out and bloom peak, summer fruiting plateau,
and autumn senescence before winter dormancy.

Labor-hour figures across pruning, harvesting, post-harvest handling,
and other field operations are mostly rough estimates; the harvest-tier
figure is derived from a documented hand-raking productivity rate
(converted using an assumed typical harvest-container weight) divided
into the adopted average yield figure, with additional time added for
gleaning, field movement, and container handling beyond pure raking
time; mechanized-harvest tiers are rough estimates given no direct
source was found.

Soil pH optimum and tolerance range (optimum around 4.0, with an upper
bound borrowed by analogy from the cultivated blueberry crop's own
documented pH ceiling) are derived from a directly documented optimum
figure combined with an estimated upper tolerance limit.

Harvest-fraction-by-year (alternating between zero in the prune year
and a full crop in the crop year, for any single field) is derived
directly from the documented two-year management cycle.

Small-seededness is assumed true by analogy to the cultivated blueberry
crop's own documented tiny seed size, since this wild crop's seed mass
specifically was not measured in the sources reached.
