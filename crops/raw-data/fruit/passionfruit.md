# Passionfruit (*Passiflora edulis*)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as *Passiflora edulis* Sims (Passifloraceae), covering both of
its distinct trade forms as cultivar groups within one entry: **purple
passionfruit** (form *edulis*; called gulupa in Colombia, granadilla in
South Africa) and **yellow passionfruit** (form *flavicarpa*; called
maracuyá, or maracujá in Brazil) [1][2][4]. The two forms hybridize
freely, with intermediate traits, but differ sharply enough in
climate, pollination biology, harvest method, storage, cycle length and
labour intensity to warrant separate cultivar-group treatment within
this entry (see Finding 1). Several other *Passiflora* fruit crops
(including sweet granadilla, giant granadilla, and banana passionfruit)
are close relatives this project has also researched, each treated as
its own separate entry; a fourth relative grown mainly for medicinal
use is covered elsewhere.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the two forms are, for practical purposes, two different
crops under one species name.** They differ in nearly every trait
relevant to cultivation. Purple passionfruit suits subtropical and
cool-highland climates (documented growing elevations from roughly
650-1,300 m in one country to as high as 2,000-2,150 m at 16-24°C in
another), is self-compatible, flowers from roughly dawn to noon, is
picked at about 75% colour while still on the vine, stores well chilled
(3-5°C for 3-5 weeks) and is marketed mostly fresh for export, on a
roughly four-year production cycle. Yellow passionfruit instead suits
tropical lowlands (roughly 300-1,200 m at 21-32°C), is self-sterile and
needs cross-pollination by carpenter bees or by hand, flowers roughly
noon to 9-10 pm, is harvested by gathering fruit after it falls from
the vine, chills easily (stored at 7-10°C for about two weeks, with
injury below that), and is marketed mostly as juice, on a shorter
roughly two-to-three-year cycle. Documented Colombian farm-budget
labour figures for purple passionfruit run roughly 3-5 times higher
per hectare per year than for yellow passionfruit [1][2][4][5][6][7][8].

**Finding 2 — detailed labour breakdowns exist from several Colombian
farm-economics studies.** For one documented yellow-passionfruit
planting (a lowland growing region, year two of a two-year cycle):
roughly 625 plants/ha, first fruit at eight months, about 32.5 t/ha
total yield over the cycle, and roughly 966 hours of labour per
hectare in the productive year, with harvest alone making up 59% of
that labour. A second, hybrid yellow-passionfruit planting documented
roughly 30 t/ha in its productive year and roughly 1,728 hours of
labour per hectare, with harvest and handling together making up 65%
of labour. For purple passionfruit, one highland planting documented
roughly 1,428 plants/ha on a four-year cycle, averaging about
20.8 t/ha/yr in its peak years, with roughly 5,580 hours of labour per
hectare per year — pruning (including flower removal and hanging
vines over the trellis wire) made up 31% of that labour. A second
purple-passionfruit planting documented roughly 10.5 t/ha in its
second year and roughly 3,348 hours of labour per hectare, with flower
removal alone making up 29% of labour and hanging/pruning a further
17% [5][6][7][8].

**Finding 3 — a highly climacteric fruit that normally ripens on the
vine and falls.** Ethylene output is very high at the climacteric peak
(roughly 160-400 µL/kg·h). Ripening normally happens while fruit is
still attached to the vine, and fruit picked too early tastes
distinctly unripe; ripe yellow-passionfruit fruit instead falls from
the vine on its own and is gathered from the ground, typically daily.
Fallen fruit measurably contains less sugar, acid and vitamin C than
vine-picked fruit of equivalent ripeness. Shrivel is the main
postharvest quality defect for the crop generally [1][4].

**Finding 4 — short-lived plantings, driven largely by disease rather
than by the plant's natural lifespan.** Collar rot (a water-mold
disease) and a fungal wilt disease limit yellow-passionfruit plantings
to roughly three years in at least two documented growing regions; a
specific viral disease similarly limits purple-passionfruit plantings.
As a partial solution, purple passionfruit is commonly grafted onto
wilt-resistant yellow-passionfruit rootstock [1].

**Finding 5 — unripe fruit and the rind are cyanogenic.** Two
cyanogenic glycosides (prunasin and sambunigrin) are present
throughout the plant, at their highest concentration in very young,
unripe fruit, and at negligible levels in fully ripe, fallen fruit.
Yellow-passionfruit peel specifically has been measured at roughly
285 mg/kg prunasin in at least one study. The rind (roughly half of
total fruit weight) is nonetheless dried and fed to livestock in at
least one documented practice [1][3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A short-lived, woody perennial vine grown on
  a wire trellis at least about 2.1 m high; replanted every two to four
  years depending on form and disease pressure [1][5][6][7].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous perennial bearing. Yellow
  passionfruit is harvested nearly year-round in the tropics (with
  seasonal peaks), gathered daily or two to three times a week as
  fallen fruit; purple passionfruit is picked directly from the vine,
  typically weekly, during its season [1].
- **Climate:** Yellow passionfruit suits true tropical lowlands; purple
  passionfruit suits subtropical and cool-highland climates, including
  frost-free Mediterranean-type sites at higher elevation [1][2].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible when ripe (the pulp); unripe fruit is
  cyanogenic (see Hazards) [1].
- **Pollination:** Required for both forms, but their systems differ
  sharply — purple passionfruit is self-compatible, while yellow
  passionfruit is self-sterile and needs cross-pollination, typically
  by carpenter bees (markedly more effective than honeybees) or by
  hand. Documented hand-pollination rates reach roughly 600 flowers per
  hour, achieving about 70% flower set and 60% of set fruit reaching
  maturity. Rain within about 1.5 hours of pollination prevents fruit
  set, though rain after about two hours has no effect [1][2].
- **Yield:** A world-average figure of roughly 15 t/ha is estimated
  from a major producing country's national production and area
  statistics; well-managed Colombian second-year yields of 25-30 t/ha
  are documented for yellow passionfruit, with commercial yields
  elsewhere reported as high as roughly 35 t/ha; purple passionfruit
  has been documented at roughly 10.5-20.8 t/ha/yr depending on growing
  region and planting age. Poor yields (pollination failure, disease
  pressure, young or struggling plantings) can fall well below these
  figures — one area-wide average from a smaller tropical island
  producing region, including young and failed plantings, worked out
  to under 0.5 t/ha.
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-water-use reference
  covers passionfruit specifically; regional lysimeter studies document
  a crop coefficient rising from roughly 0.4-0.6 in the vegetative stage
  to roughly 1.0-1.25 during flowering and fruiting, which is the most
  water-sensitive period. Good drainage is considered essential, since
  wet soil strongly predisposes the crop to collar rot; documented
  rainfall requirements range from as little as 600 mm/yr in one
  successful commercial growing region up to 2,500 mm/yr in wetter
  highland growing regions for purple passionfruit.
- **Establishment:** Nursery seedlings (or rooted cuttings, or grafts
  for disease-resistant purple-on-yellow combinations) are transplanted
  beside trellis posts and trained up a string to a wire at least about
  2.1 m high; wind protection is needed, and trellis materials
  themselves make up a substantial share of establishment cost
  (documented at roughly a quarter to two-thirds of input costs
  depending on form and region) [1][5][8].
- **Labor:** Highly form-dependent (see Finding 2); harvest (yellow) or
  pruning and flower-removal (purple) are consistently the largest
  single labour categories documented.
- **Soil:** Light to heavy sandy loams of medium texture with good
  water-holding capacity are preferred, with high organic matter
  particularly favoured for purple passionfruit; documented optimal pH
  spans roughly 5.5-8.0 across growing regions and forms [1][5][7].
- **Stand life:** Yellow passionfruit plantings typically last two to
  three years before disease pressure forces replanting (as short as
  roughly 30-35 months in one documented tropical island growing
  region); purple passionfruit plantings typically last about four
  years, with careful pruning reportedly extending productive life to
  five or six years, and plantings at high elevation in at least one
  southern African growing region reported to last as long as eight
  years [1][5][6][7][8].
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific viral disease causing small,
  misshapen, thick-rinded fruit in purple passionfruit; a fungal wilt
  disease and collar rot (a water-mold disease) in yellow passionfruit;
  fungal leaf- and fruit-spot diseases; and fruit flies, scales,
  mealybugs, mites, stink bugs and a root-knot nematode as general pests
  [1][4].
- **Cultivars:** Beyond the two base forms, several named hybrid and
  selected cultivars exist, mostly from Hawaii and Australia, including
  purple-by-yellow hybrids bred for a longer season and higher pulp
  yield than pure purple passionfruit, and several Hawaiian selections
  distinguished mainly by fruit colour, juice percentage and flavour.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include fruit flies that lay eggs in
young fruit, scales, mealybugs, mites, stink bugs, and a root-knot
nematode, alongside the viral and fungal diseases noted above.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — unripe fruit, rind, leaves and stems contain
cyanogenic glycosides (prunasin and sambunigrin), at their highest
concentration in young, unripe fruit and negligible levels in ripe,
fallen fruit; yellow-passionfruit juice separately contains a harman
alkaloid reported to have a mildly sedative effect. Both deferred until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
Separately, cyanogenic rind used as livestock feed carries its own
antinutritional considerations, also deferred for the same reason.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** yellow fruit is gathered (fallen), graded (roughly
  70-75% reaching first quality), and either cold-stored or scooped for
  juice processing (pulp roughly half of fruit weight, rind roughly
  half), pressed into juice with seeds as a byproduct; purple fruit is
  picked from the vine, graded (a notably higher first-quality share,
  roughly 80-93%), and cold-stored. Seeds are dried and pressed for oil,
  with the residual meal usable as a protein-rich feed ingredient; rind
  is chopped and dried, often with molasses, into cattle and pig feed.
- Not covered by this research in detail: frozen or canned juice
  processing; pectin extraction from rind; enzymatic secondary juice
  extraction.

## Open research questions

- Storage temperature guidance for ripe fruit generally (one source
  giving roughly 2-7°C for a week) appears to conflict somewhat with
  the yellow-form-specific chilling-injury threshold (injury below
  about 7°C) given elsewhere — not fully reconciled.
- Plantation lifespan for both forms is reported quite differently
  across sources (ranging from about three years to as long as eight
  years under favourable highland conditions) — likely reflecting real
  differences in disease pressure and management rather than a single
  representative figure.
- Energy content figures for purple-passionfruit pulp differ slightly
  between two sources (roughly 90 versus 97 kcal per 100 g).
- One source's reported nitrogen fertilizer rate (expressed per unit of
  fruit yield) appears implausibly high as stated and was not relied
  upon directly.
- Whether any commercial passionfruit seed is produced as true F1
  hybrid seed, versus propagated entirely by cuttings or grafts, is
  unresolved; named hybrid cultivars exist, but their seed systems were
  not clearly documented in the sources used here.
- Detailed labour-hour figures, the effective root zone and
  soil-moisture-depletion allowance, and the full yield-ramp profile all
  rest substantially on estimates derived from a small number of farm
  budgets rather than broader agronomic study.

## Estimates and derivations

A seasonal water-use index is derived from a regional lysimeter study's
ratio of measured crop evapotranspiration to reference
evapotranspiration, applied across a full year; this is cross-checked
against separately reported stage-specific crop-coefficient values,
which average to a similar overall figure.

Rooting depth and the soil-moisture-depletion allowance used here are
estimated, since no source gave rooting depth directly for this crop;
the estimate reflects documented common use of drip or micro-sprinkler
irrigation on vines planted at moderate-to-high density.

Yield tiers are derived primarily from documented farm-budget figures
for well-managed Colombian plantings of both forms, a national
production statistic from a major producing country for the world
average, and a commercial yield range from a classic horticultural
reference for the exceptional tier; a poor-yield tier is estimated as a
fraction of the world average, informed by a documented area-wide yield
figure from a smaller producing region that included young and failed
plantings.

Labour-hour estimates by operation are derived directly from the farm
budgets in Finding 2, converting each budget's reported labour cost by
its stated local daily wage; a representative "tier" figure for each
form is set near the midpoint of the available budgets for that form,
with harvest-rate (kilograms of fruit per labour-hour) calculated
directly from each budget's reported harvest-labour share and total
yield.

A yield-ramp and peak-daily-yield profile is derived from documented
time-to-first-harvest (about eight months for yellow passionfruit) and
peak annual yield from the farm budgets, divided across an assumed
near-continuous harvest window; standing-fruit tolerance and daily
quality-decay rate are estimated from the documented need to gather
fallen fruit daily or every few days before it spoils.
