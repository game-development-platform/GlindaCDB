## Naming and scope

*Passiflora edulis* Sims (Passifloraceae), described in 1818 [1]. Of about
500 *Passiflora* species, only this one is called "passionfruit" without
qualification [2].
- **Two trade forms, modelled as cultivar groups in this one entry:**
  - **purple passionfruit** (forma *edulis*): `purple_passionfruit`. Called
    gulupa in Colombia [3].
  - **yellow passionfruit** (forma *flavicarpa*): `yellow_passionfruit`.
    Called maracuyá in Spanish, maracujá in Brazil [2][4].
- **Why one entry, two groups:** the forms hybridise freely and share the
  species, but differ in climate, pollination, harvest, storage, cycle
  length and labour sharply enough to model separately (Finding 1)
  [2][1][4][5][3][6].
- **Gate check against neighbouring rows (re-confirmed 2026-11-24):**
  - **`sweet_granadilla`** (*P. ligularis*), **`giant_granadilla`** (*P.
    quadrangularis*) and **`banana_passionfruit`** (*P. mollissima* and
    relatives) stay separate as distinct species (see
    `banana-passionfruit.md`).
  - A medicinal relative (*P. incarnata*, maypop) is covered elsewhere [2].

**Relatives in this project:** sweet granadilla (`sweet_granadilla`), giant
granadilla (`giant_granadilla`), banana passionfruit (`banana_passionfruit`,
`banana-passionfruit.md`) and maypop (`maypop`), as fellow *Passiflora*.

**Proposed:** none. The named hybrids ('3-1', '3-26', 'M-21471A') are
cultivars within this entry, not new rows [2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the two forms are two crops under one name.**
- **Purple (gulupa):**
  - subtropical, grown at 650-1,300 m in India and at 2,000-2,150 m in the
    Colombian highlands [2][3][6];
  - self-compatible [2][1];
  - blooms in spring and early summer (July-November in Queensland) [2];
  - pulp is less acid, richer in aroma, with a higher juice fraction
    (35-38%) [2];
  - marketed mostly fresh [2];
  - a four-year cycle in Colombia [3][6].
- **Yellow (maracuyá):**
  - tropical to near-tropical, from sea level to 600 m (Western Samoa), and
    300-1,200 m at 21-32 °C in Colombia [2][4];
  - self-sterile, needing cross-pollination [2][1];
  - a more vigorous vine with generally larger fruit [2];
  - marketed mostly as juice [2];
  - a two-to-three-year cycle in Colombia [4][5].
- **Hybridising:** the purple must be the seed parent, because yellow
  flowers are not receptive to purple pollen [2].
- **Labour:** the Colombian gulupa budgets carry roughly 3-6 times the
  labour per hectare per year of the yellow budgets (Estimates)
  [4][5][3][6].

**Finding 2 — detailed Colombian farm budgets, with labour dominated by
harvest (yellow) or flower removal and pruning (purple).**
All are FINAGRO *Marco de Referencia Agroeconómico* budgets from 2017-2018
field visits [4][5][3][6]. Labour hours are derived (Estimates).

| Budget | Form | Plants/ha | Cycle | Yield/cycle | Labour h/ha/yr | Top labour task |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cundinamarca [4] | Yellow | 625 | 2 yr | 32.5 t/ha | ~830 | Harvest 59% |
| Santander (hybrid) [5] | Yellow | 543 | 3 yr | ~54 t/ha | ~1,340 | Harvest and handling |
| Huila [4] | Yellow | 1,000 | 3 yr | 89 t/ha | ~2,960 | Harvest 23%, hand pollination 21% |
| Gulupa [3] | Purple | 1,000 | 4 yr | 28.1 t/ha | ~3,150 | Flower removal 29%, hanging/pruning 17% |
| Gulupa, Antioquia [6] | Purple | 1,428 | 4 yr | 62.5 t/ha | ~4,780 | Pruning (incl. flower removal) 31% |

- **First-quality share:** 75-80% for yellow, 80-93% for purple [4][3][6].
- **Trellis:** materials are 66% of the gulupa input cost and about 25% of
  the yellow hybrid's [5][3].
- **Start of production:** 8 months after planting (yellow) or year 1
  (purple) [4][3].

**Finding 3 — a climacteric fruit that ripens on the vine and falls, and
one of the heaviest ethylene producers known.**
- Ripening happens on the vine, 70-80 days after pollination [2]. The
  climacteric rise occurs while the fruit is still attached [7].
- Ripe yellow fruit drops and is gathered daily, since it begins
  dehydrating and picking up soil-borne pathogens at once [2][7].
- **Ethylene and respiration:** passionfruit is among the highest ethylene
  producers of any fruit, about 160-370 µL/kg·h at 20 °C at the climacteric
  peak; the purple form's high initial ethylene and respiration decline as
  the fruit matures (snippet only) [8].
- **Off-vine ripening works for the yellow form:** mature-green yellow fruit
  picked at 55-60 days after anthesis and given 100 µL/L (ppm) ethylene for
  1-2 days (35 hours in the trial) reaches the same sugar balance, soluble
  solids and juice pH as vine-ripened fruit; once ripening starts, added
  ethylene is unneeded [7][8].
  - Ethylene also develops the purple surface colour buyers expect (at least
    90% of the surface) [7].
- Fruit picked green without ethylene tastes unripe [7].
- Shrivel is the main postharvest defect; fruit begins shrivelling soon
  after harvest, and juice stays wholesome about 7 days [2][7].

**Finding 4 — short-lived plantings, limited by disease more than by the
plant.**
- **Collar rot:** *Phytophthora cinnamomi* forces yellow replanting every
  30-35 months in Fiji [2].
- **Fusarium wilt** (*F. oxysporum* f. sp. *passiflorae*) is reduced only by
  grafting the purple, or purple-yellow hybrids, onto resistant yellow
  rootstock [2].
- **Woodiness / "bullet":** small, misshapen, thick-rinded fruit with little
  pulp, which become unmarketable.
  - In Morton's account it was the most serious disease of purple
    passionfruit in Australia and East Africa, caused by passionfruit
    woodiness virus (PWV), with little effect on the yellow form; mild-strain
    cross-protection worked until a more virulent strain broke it in 1978
    [2].
  - In Brazil the disease is now attributed mainly to cowpea aphid-borne
    mosaic virus (CABMV), spread non-persistently by aphids (*Aphis* spp.,
    *A. gossypii*); it is the most serious virus disease of the yellow crop
    there, and high incidence is why annual crop renewal is recommended
    (snippet only) [9].
- **Sudden wilt:** *Nectria haematococca* (*Fusarium solani*) girdles the
  collar of purple vines [2].
- The average vine, pruned from age 2, is productive for only a few years
  (see Stand life) [2][3].

**Finding 5 — unripe fruit and the rind are cyanogenic.**
- A cyanogenic glycoside is in the pulp at all stages, highest in very young
  unripe fruit and lowest in fallen, wrinkled fruit, where it is "of no
  toxicological significance" [2].
- The yellow form's peel and juice specifically contain prunasin and other
  cyanogenic glycosides [1][10].
- The rind (about half of fruit weight) is still dried, often with molasses,
  into stock feed [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** a shallow-rooted, woody, evergreen perennial vine,
  climbing by tendrils, grown on a wire trellis at least 2.13 m high [2].
  - **Flowering season:** purple blooms July-November (and Feb-April) in
    Queensland; least bloom in winter, under short days [2].
  - **Stand life:** see below.
- **Harvest pattern:** continuous perennial bearing, with seasonal peaks.
  Yellow fruit is gathered off the ground daily; purple is picked from the
  vine [2].
  - In India the vines bear year-round, with two peak periods [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** the vine (especially yellow) begins to
  bear 1-3 years from planting [2]. The Colombian budgets give 8 months
  (yellow) or year 1 (purple) [4][3].
- **Climate zones:**
  - purple subtropical; yellow tropical (Finding 1) [2];
  - both need wind protection [2];
  - rainfall should be at least 90 cm (35 in) a year [2]; the Colombian
    gulupa budgets cite 2,000-2,500 mm and the yellow budgets 800-1,750 mm
    [4][3][6].
- **Composition (purple pulp, per 100 g, FDC 169108) [11]:**
  - energy 97 kcal (406 kJ); water 73%; carbohydrate 22 g; fibre 10.4 g;
    protein 2.2 g; fat 0.7 g;
  - vitamin C about 30 mg; potassium about 348 mg.
  - Morton's older ARS figures (purple pulp and seeds): 90 cal, 75.1 g
    moisture, 21.2 g carbohydrate, Ca 13 mg, P 64 mg, Fe 1.6 mg, vitamin A
    700 IU [2].
  - **Yellow form:** less vitamin C than the purple, but more total (citric)
    acid and more carotene, and a good niacin and riboflavin source [2]. No
    whole-fruit USDA record exists for the yellow form; yellow juice is given
    at about 60 kcal, 14.4 g carbohydrate and 18.2 mg vitamin C per 100 g,
    against about 29.8 mg for purple juice (snippet only) [8].
- **Edibility:** the ripe pulp is eaten raw, seeds and all, or strained for
  juice. Unripe fruit is cyanogenic (Finding 5) [2].
- **Pollination:**
  - purple is self-compatible; yellow is self-sterile and needs
    cross-pollination [2][1];
  - the carpenter bee (*Xylocopa*) is far more effective than honeybees;
    some growers set out decaying logs, and in Brazil *X. frontalis* and *X.
    grisescens* are managed in bamboo trap-nests [1][12];
  - natural fruit set is low and variable (roughly 11-27% by region in
    Brazil); trellis-grown yellow often needs hand pollination, which raised
    average set from 23.3% to 69.8% in one central-Brazil study (snippet
    only) [1][2][12];
  - home gardens should hold at least two vines of different parentage [2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** fewest flowers form in winter, under short
  day length [2].
- **Yield:**
  - Morton: commercial plantations give 20,000-35,000 lb/acre, roughly the
    same number of kg/ha (about 22.4-39.2 t/ha) [2].
  - **Brazil:** about 700,000 t from 46,000 ha, a national average near 15
    t/ha (IBGE 2022/2023, via Embrapa; snippet only). The Brazilian crop is
    about 97% yellow (*flavicarpa*) and 3% sweet passionfruit (*P. alata*)
    [13][9].
  - **Colombian budgets, per year:** yellow about 16-30 t/ha; purple about
    7 t/ha (gulupa) to 15.6 t/ha (Antioquia), averaged over the cycle
    (Estimates) [4][5][3][6].
- **Water use:**
  - no FAO Kc class covers passionfruit; Brazilian lysimeter studies give a
    Kc that rises from about 0.4-0.6 in vegetative growth to 1.0-1.2 at
    flowering and fruiting, then falls (snippet only) [14].
    - One Piracicaba study: 0.6 apical vegetative, 0.9 lateral vegetative,
      1.2 flowering and fruiting, 1.0 maturation, 0.8 after [14].
    - A USP thesis measured 650 mm of crop evapotranspiration over 447 days
      in the first cycle [14].
  - Good drainage is essential, since wet soil predisposes the crop to
    collar rot [2].
  - Water demand is highest as fruit approach maturity [2].
- **Propagation:**
  - usually from seed; yellow seedlings vary, which helps cross-pollination
    [2];
  - layers or cuttings of 3-4 nodes root in about 90 days [2];
  - grafting the purple (or purple-yellow hybrids) onto Fusarium-resistant
    yellow rootstock is the standard disease fix [2]. The yellow form is
    used as the purple's rootstock in Australia [1].
- **Labor:** highly form-dependent (Finding 2). Harvest is the largest task
  for yellow, flower removal and pruning for purple [4][5][3][6].
- **Soil:**
  - light to heavy sandy loams of medium texture; pH 6.5-7.5 (Morton), with
    lime if too acid [2];
  - the Colombian budgets give a wider pH of 5.5-8.0 [3];
  - good drainage throughout [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - underripe yellow fruit ripens and holds at 20 °C, 85-90% RH; ripening
    is too fast at 30 °C [2];
  - ripe fruit keeps about a week at 2.2-7.2 °C (36-45 °F) [2];
  - fruit is collected in lugs or boxes, never bags, which cause
    "sweating" [2];
  - **yellow-form trial (Mississippi) [7]:** 10 °C was the best store;
    5 °C caused chilling injury and 15 °C too much water loss; chilling
    injury sets in below about 6.5 °C;
  - wrapping fruit in plasticised (PVC) film cut weight loss and shrivel,
    but condensation brought mould after about 25 days; without a wrap, 50%
    of the surface shrivelled, rising to 100% [7];
  - soluble solids were about 10.8-12.5 °Brix and juice pH 2.8-3.3; pulp is
    about 46-54% of fruit weight [7].
- **Establishment:**
  - root-prune seedlings 2 weeks before transplanting, on a cool day [2];
  - Venezuelan trials gave the highest yellow yields at 3 m × 3 m [2]; the
    Colombian budgets run 543-1,428 plants/ha [4][5][3][6];
  - trellis at least 2.13 m high; trellis materials are a large share of
    establishment cost (Finding 2) [2][5][3].
- **Stand life:**
  - yellow plantings last two to three years before collar rot or wilt
    forces replanting (30-35 months in Fiji) [2][4][5];
  - purple plantings run about four years in Colombia; careful pruning and
    cool highland sites extend this [2][3][6].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Purple:** 'Australian Purple' ('Nelly Kelly'), 'Common Purple'
    (naturalised in Hawaii); Brazil's 'Ouropretano', 'Muico', 'Peroba',
    'Pintado' [2].
  - **Yellow:** 'Hawaiiana' (best in Colombia's Cauca Valley), 'Brasilera
    amarilla'; Hawaiian selections 'Waimanalo' (strains C-54, C-77, C-80,
    with C-39 as pollinator), 'Yee', 'Sevcik', 'Kapoho', 'Noel's Special'
    [2].
  - **Purple × yellow hybrids:** '3-1' and '3-26' (Redlands, Queensland,
    1967), bred for longer season and higher pulp yield; 'M-21471A' (USDA
    Miami) [2].
- **Weather response:**
  - frost-sensitive; grown only in frost-free or protected sites [2];
  - rain within about 1.5 hours of pollination can prevent fruit set
    (earlier pass; not re-sourced this pass — see Open research questions).
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation; regular watering keeps the vine
  flowering and fruiting almost continuously [2]. No flooded requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one ripe stage. Yellow is gathered fallen;
  purple is vine-picked at colour (Finding 3) [2].
- **Soil biology:** root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne javanica*) thicken
  purple roots in South Africa [2]. No mycorrhizal or replant-soil data was
  found.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** naturalised in Australia, Hawaii, India,
  Jamaica and elsewhere from garden and plantation escapes [2][1]. No formal
  weed rating was found.
- **Controlled environment:** not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Woodiness virus (PWV in Morton; CABMV in Brazil):** Finding 4 [2][9].
- **Fusarium wilt and collar rot:** Finding 4 [2].
- **Fungal spots:** brown spot (*Alternaria passiflorae*), Septoria spot,
  Phytophthora blight, thread blight, damping-off (*Rhizoctonia*, *Pythium*)
  [2].
- **Pests:** the passion vine mite (*Brevipalpus phoenicis*), which
  defoliates in warm dry weather; white peach scale (*Pseudaulacaspis
  pentagona*) and other scales; fruit flies; root-knot nematodes [2].
- **Phytoplasma:** can be spread through grafting; controlled by nursery
  inspection [1].
- **Cyanogens:** unripe fruit and rind are cyanogenic (Finding 5)
  [2][1][10].
- **Seed meal:** judged unsuitable for cattle feed [2].

**TO BE ADDRESSED:** the cyanogenic glycosides (highest in young fruit), the
antinutritional load of cyanogenic rind used as feed, and any sedative
alkaloid in the juice are deferred until this project has a place for
health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh, purple):** vine-picked at about colour → graded (80-93%
  first quality) → cooled → sold fresh, often for export [2][3][6].
- **Chain 2 (juice, yellow):** gathered fallen → graded (75-80% first
  quality) → scooped (pulp about half of fruit weight) → pressed, with seeds
  as a by-product [2][4]. Heat impairs the flavour unless flash-pasteurised
  [2].
  - Commercial yellow fruit yields 36% juice, 51% rind, 11% seed [2].
- **Chain 3 (by-products):**
  - rind pectin (2.4% fresh, 14% dry) is extracted in Fiji and Brazil to
    reduce waste [2];
  - rind residue (5-6% protein) is dried, often with molasses, into stock
    feed [2];
  - seeds yield about 23% oil (like sunflower or soybean oil), with edible
    and industrial uses; the seed meal is about 12% protein and 50-55%
    fibre [2].
- **Syrup and preserves:** juice is boiled to a syrup for sauces, desserts,
  icing and drinks; the pulp is made into jelly or jam with pineapple or
  tomato [2].
- **Medicinal:** passiflorine, a sedative, is drawn mainly from *P.
  incarnata*, but Italian chemists have extracted it from *P. edulis* leaves
  [2].
- **Not detailed here:** frozen and canned juice, and enzymatic secondary
  juice extraction.

## Estimates and derivations

**Labour hours per hectare per year** = (labour cost ÷ daily wage) × 8 ÷
cycle years. Each budget's own wage is used [4][5][3][6]:
- **Cundinamarca** (yellow): COP 8,275,000 ÷ 40,000 = 206.9 days × 8 ÷ 2 =
  ~830 h/yr.
- **Santander** (yellow hybrid): 17,553,000 ÷ 35,000 = 501.5 × 8 ÷ 3 =
  ~1,340 h/yr.
- **Huila** (yellow): 38,850,000 ÷ 35,000 = 1,110 × 8 ÷ 3 = ~2,960 h/yr.
- **Gulupa** (purple): 47,238,000 ÷ 30,000 = 1,574.6 × 8 ÷ 4 = ~3,150 h/yr.
- **Gulupa, Antioquia** (purple): 95,544,000 ÷ 40,000 = 2,388.6 × 8 ÷ 4 =
  ~4,780 h/yr.
The ratio of purple to yellow is about 3,150-4,780 against 830-2,960, i.e.
roughly 3-6 times (Finding 1).

**Yield per hectare per year** = cycle yield ÷ cycle years [4][5][3][6]:
- Cundinamarca 32.5 ÷ 2 = 16.3; Huila 89 ÷ 3 = 29.7; gulupa 28.1 ÷ 4 = 7.0;
  Antioquia 62.5 ÷ 4 = 15.6 t/ha/yr. The gulupa average is low because year
  1 is small; its peak years are higher.

**Morton's yield.** 20,000-35,000 lb/acre × 1.1209 ÷ 1,000 = 22.4-39.2 t/ha.

**Unit conversions.** 7 ft = 2.13 m; 36-45 °F = 2.2-7.2 °C; 35 in = 90 cm;
2,000-4,000 ft = 610-1,220 m; 36 lb bushel = 16.3 kg.
