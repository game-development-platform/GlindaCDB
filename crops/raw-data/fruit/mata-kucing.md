## Naming and scope

A tropical lowland-adapted subspecies of the same species as a related
tree-fruit crop this project has also researched separately
(Sapindaceae family). It is known as "cat's eye" (its most common
English translation) across Peninsular Malaysia and Sabah, with
several other distinct local names used in Sarawak, Kalimantan, and
Sumatra [1][3]. Two botanical varieties are recognized: a widespread
one found across Indo-China and the Malay Archipelago, showing its
greatest diversity in Borneo (with dozens of local races potentially
distinguishable there), and a second, spinier-fruited one restricted to
Borneo and the Philippines [2][4]. Taxonomy is unsettled: a 2017
molecular phylogeny found the parent species not to form a single
natural group and proposed raising the widespread variety to full
species rank, a change that would affect naming only, not scope, if
eventually adopted [5]. Within Sarawak specifically, local races fall
into a smooth-fruited type and several warty-fruited types that differ
in fruit shape and ripening color [2][6].

This crop clearly passes this project's distinctiveness test against
its closest sibling — the other, much more widely cultivated relative
in the same species, which this project has also researched
separately as its own crop entry. That sibling needs a short but
genuinely cool winter to flower and does not flower reliably in humid
tropical lowlands, while this crop is fully adapted to the unchanging
heat, humidity, and day length of the equatorial zone and flowers
without any cool-season trigger at all. The production systems also
differ sharply: this crop is still mostly supplied from wild, forest-
garden, and village seedling trees rather than managed commercial
orchards, and it is tracked separately in at least one national
agricultural statistics series. All comparisons to that sibling crop
below are flagged explicitly as analogies, since this crop's own
research base is comparatively thin.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the relative that flowers in the equatorial lowlands.**
This subspecies needs no cool-season trigger at all. In one major
growing state, flowering lasts only one to two weeks sometime between
late June and mid-October, with most fruit harvested in a two-month
window following [8]. A backyard specimen in a tropical Australian
community orchard reportedly fruits several times a year rather than
on a single annual cycle [11]. At least one source suggests that
superior local varieties of this crop could offer an attractive
alternative to its more widely grown sibling specifically for humid
tropical lowland regions where that sibling cannot be reliably grown
[1]. In modeling terms, this is a member of the same plant lineage as a
much more commercially developed relative, but with no chilling or
artificial flower-forcing requirement at all — flowering instead
follows the local wet-dry seasonal rhythm.

**Finding 2 — a tiny formally planted area, with most supply still
wild.** National agricultural statistics for a five-year period in one
major Southeast Asian country show a very small and uneven planted
area (under 100 hectares) and correspondingly small, volatile production
figures from year to year [9][10]. Almost all of the formally recorded
planted area sits in a single state, while the region where local races
show the greatest diversity reports no formal planted area at all,
suggesting that village and wild supply there is simply not captured
by the statistics. In a different growing region, supply is described
as still limited and heavily dependent on uncertain seasonal harvests
from community gardens near forest areas [3]. Documented per-tree
fruiting is on the order of a couple of thousand fruits per season [8].

**Finding 3 — a smaller, tarter fruit than its sibling.** The fruit is
round, roughly 2 cm across (some varieties larger), with rough
brownish skin, borne in small bunches; the flesh is translucent, thin,
and chewy, described in one source as sweet and in another as not
especially sweet and slightly sour and refreshing — a genuine
descriptive conflict not resolved here [3][11]. The edible aril makes
up roughly 60-75% of total fruit weight, with total soluble solids in a
broad documented range; overall composition is described as similar to
its more widely grown sibling but somewhat lower in carbohydrate and
energy content and somewhat higher in minerals [8]. Harvest maturity is
judged by color, though sources conflict here too: one source describes
harvesting only once the fruit is fully brown, while at least two
specific Sarawak-area local races stay green even when fully ripe
[2][11].

**Finding 4 — grown from short-lived seed.** This crop is commonly
raised from seed, though clonal propagation by air-layering is not
difficult, at a wide range of planting spacings [8]. Seeds show
relatively low germination and cannot be stored for long; in one 2025
trial, germination began around the sixth day after sowing, and a
traditional shallot-extract seed-soaking treatment did not meaningfully
improve germination rates [3]. Seedlings have been documented fruiting
within two to three years in a tropical Australian community-orchard
setting [11]. Flowers of both sexes occur together within the same
flower clusters [8]. The largest known germplasm collection of this
subspecies is held at a Malaysian agricultural university [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen tree, ranging from wild forest
  specimens to small (2-3 m) backyard trees in cultivation; perennial
  bearing with a main annual crop timed to local wet-dry seasonality
  rather than any cool-season trigger, with some reports of multiple
  crops a year in especially favorable settings.
- **Climate:** Fully tropical, humid equatorial lowland, generally
  within about ten degrees of the equator and near sea level, with
  substantial annual rainfall.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (the aril).
- **Pollination:** Likely required, by analogy to its more widely
  grown sibling crop, which is insect-pollinated; this crop's flowers
  of both sexes occur together in the same clusters.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented national yields from the one
  growing state with formal statistics are modest and swing sharply
  year to year, reflecting both a very small planted area and
  significant unharvested or young-tree area within it; per-tree fruit
  counts suggest meaningfully higher yields are possible under good
  management (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard water-use study exists for this specific
  crop; figures here are estimated by analogy to its more widely grown
  sibling crop, adjusted for a wetter native climate. The crop is
  documented growing on alluvial river-bank soils and in rich, moist
  garden soil, suggesting moderate waterlogging tolerance alongside low
  drought tolerance given its very wet native rainfall range.
- **Establishment:** Most commonly grown from seed, though air-layering
  and grafting are both documented as practical alternatives.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour study exists for this crop; figures
  here are rough estimates scaled down from its more widely grown
  sibling crop's documented labor pattern, reduced to reflect this
  crop's lower yields and more informal village-level management.
- **Soil:** Alluvial soil, often on river banks, is the most commonly
  documented growing site; no dedicated nutrient-removal study exists
  for this crop.
- **Stand life:** Seedlings have been documented fruiting within two to
  three years in at least one setting; overall tree longevity has not
  been researched.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fruit-piercing moth and fruit flies are
  documented pests.
- **Cultivars:** A smooth-fruited type and several warty-fruited local
  races differing in fruit shape and ripening color, plus the
  spinier-fruited botanical variety found in Borneo and the
  Philippines; no registered or named commercial clones were found,
  reflecting the crop's largely wild and village-level production.
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked by bunch once colored; maturity judgment
  varies by local race (see Finding 3).
- **Storage:** Very limited documented shelf life — sold and consumed
  fresh in season, with no dedicated chilled-storage study found for
  this specific crop (see Estimates below).

## Hazards

This crop's seeds, like those of its more widely grown sibling and
other relatives in the same plant family, are presumed to contain the
same class of toxic compounds (hypoglycin A and a related compound)
documented in that sibling's seed, though this has not been directly
measured for this specific crop.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit bunches are picked once colored, sold fresh, and
  peeled for the edible aril (roughly 60-75% of fruit weight); seed and
  peel are discarded.
- Not covered by this research: any drying, canning, or other
  preservation processing — none was found documented for this crop.
  Its supply also includes a substantial wild-gathered component not
  modeled here in detail.

## Open research questions

- Documented national production and planted area for this crop swing
  very widely from year to year (within the same single growing
  state's statistics) — not resolved, and may simply reflect the
  crop's genuinely small and informally managed production base.
- Fruiting frequency conflicts between sources: one major growing
  region documents a single annual one-to-two-week bloom window, while
  a backyard specimen elsewhere is reported fruiting several times a
  year — not reconciled, and may reflect genuine regional or individual
  -tree variation rather than a contradiction.
- Harvest-maturity color judgment conflicts between sources (fully
  brown in one source versus green-when-ripe for at least two specific
  local races in another) — not reconciled, and likely reflects genuine
  cultivar-level variation rather than a true conflict.
- Flavor description conflicts between sources (sweet versus not
  especially sweet and slightly sour) — not reconciled.
- No dedicated water-use, nutrient-removal, or chilled-storage study
  exists for this specific crop; current figures throughout this entry
  are estimated by analogy to its more widely grown sibling crop, which
  itself grows in a drier, cooler-wintered climate — these analogies
  should be treated as weak.
- Overall tree longevity has not been researched for this crop.
- This crop's supply is still substantially wild-gathered rather than
  formally cultivated in at least two of its documented growing regions,
  which this entry does not attempt to model as a distinct wild-harvest
  system.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from planting to first harvest (roughly two to four years) are
derived from a documented two-to-three-year fruiting age for seedlings
in one tropical community-orchard setting, with a longer upper bound
assumed by judgment for less favorable, forest-type seedling conditions
where no specific figure was found.

Calorie and protein content, and the pollination requirement, are
estimated by analogy to this crop's more widely grown sibling, adjusted
downward slightly for calories and protein to reflect this crop's
documented somewhat lower carbohydrate and energy content relative to
that sibling.

Yield tiers are derived by combining a documented approximate fruit
weight (estimated from the fruit's roughly 2 cm diameter) with a
documented per-tree fruit-count range and a documented planting-density
range, then cross-checked against directly sourced national good- and
poor-tier yield figures from a specific recent growing season and a
specific poor growing season respectively, both drawn from the same
country's agricultural statistics series.

Water-use and soil figures (seasonal crop-coefficient-days, effective
rooting depth, soil-moisture-depletion allowance, and days without
water before yield penalty) are estimated entirely by analogy to this
crop's more widely grown sibling, adjusted toward a wetter effective
climate given this crop's much higher native rainfall range; drought
tolerance is rated low (reflecting the very wet native range) and
waterlogging tolerance is rated medium (reflecting the crop's
documented occurrence on river-bank alluvial soils).

The growth-stage curve is estimated as a nearly flat, aseasonal
evergreen pattern, reflecting the crop's lack of any cool-season
dormancy trigger.

Seed-viability figures (very short, on the order of weeks at most) are
estimated by analogy to this crop's more widely grown sibling and other
relatives in the same plant family, informed by this crop's own
documented short seed life.

Labor-hour figures across all tiers are rough estimates scaled down
from this crop's more widely grown sibling's documented labor pattern,
reduced to perhaps half to two-thirds of that sibling's figures to
reflect this crop's lower yields and more informal, village-level
management style; the harvest window, bloom-plateau length, multi-year
ramp to bearing, maximum fruit standing days, and daily decay rate are
all judgment-based estimates informed by the documented one-to-two-week
bloom duration and the crop's generally short, informal
harvest-and-sale cycle.

Typical unchilled storage duration (a few days) and chilled-storage
figures are both estimated entirely by analogy to this crop's more
widely grown sibling's documented ambient and refrigerated storage
life, since no dedicated storage study exists for this specific crop.
