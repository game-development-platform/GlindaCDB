# Feijoa / Pineapple Guava (*Acca sellowiana*)

## Naming and scope

The evergreen Myrtaceae shrub or small tree long known scientifically
under one genus name and more recently returned, following genetic
study, to a different genus as that genus's only species — this entry
keeps the common name "feijoa" and notes the scientific name as
unsettled between sources [2]. Other common names include pineapple
guava, Brazilian guava, fig guava, and guavasteen [1]. Despite the
common name, it is explicitly not a true guava of the genus *Psidium*
[2]. This is a genuinely distinct crop from true guava, which this
project treats as a separate, closely related crop it has also
researched — feijoa differs in requiring real winter chilling, in
tolerating considerably colder temperatures, in suffering in extreme
heat where guava does not, in its unusual bird-based pollination
system, and in a harvest method based on touch-ripeness and daily
fallen-fruit gathering rather than color-based picking.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — pollinated chiefly by birds that eat the flowers, not by
insects.** This species' fleshy, sweet flower petals are eaten by
various bird species across its different growing regions, which carry
pollen between flowers as they feed; documented bird pollinators include
blackbirds and mynas in New Zealand, and several different thrush and
tanager genera in South America [5][2]. Bees do visit the flowers, but
mostly forage within a single tree, so they cross-pollinate considerably
less effectively than birds [3]. Birds eating the flower petals also
incidentally helps suppress a gray-mold fungal pathogen that would
otherwise build up on fallen petal material [5]. Most cultivars are
fully or partly self-incompatible, and cross-pollination substantially
raises fruit set — from around 50% under self-pollination alone up to
80-90% with compatible cross-pollination in one detailed regional
source, with a somewhat different percentage range given in a classic
reference work [5][1]. Growers accordingly plant two or more compatible
cultivars together; one modern cultivar is specifically described as
the only reliably self-fertile selection in widespread use, though an
older reference describes a different, older cultivar as fully
self-fertile — a genuine conflict between sources not resolved here.
Notably, the flower petals are themselves a minor human food, eaten
fresh and described as having a spicy flavor [1].

**Finding 2 — a cool-subtropical plant with unusually narrow thermal
margins for a subtropical fruit crop.** The species requires a genuine
winter chilling period (documented variously as 50-200 hours, or "at
least 50 hours," below a specific cool threshold temperature) for
proper flower-bud initiation [3][2]. Mature trees survive down to
roughly -10°C, though fruit itself is damaged at a notably less extreme
threshold, and flowers and small fruit can be damaged by even brief
exposure to temperatures just below freezing [1][4][5]. At the other
extreme, temperatures above roughly 32°C harm flowering and fruit set,
and temperatures above roughly 38°C can damage the plant outright,
especially in dry air [5][2][3]. In one South American growing region
the crop is grown at substantial elevation (roughly 1,800-2,700 m,
with an optimum band narrower still), where fruit grown at the higher
end of that elevation range was measurably sweeter, larger, and slower
to develop than fruit grown at the lower end — illustrating a real
elevation-driven quality gradient within a single growing region [5].
Tropical, non-seasonal climates allow year-round harvest flushes, while
seasonal climates produce a single annual crop; flavor is generally
considered better in cooler growing regions [5][1].

**Finding 3 — the fruit stays green at every ripeness stage, so
maturity must be judged by touch rather than color.** Fruit develops
over roughly 120-150 days from full bloom, taking longer at higher,
cooler elevations [5][3]. Because the skin remains green throughout
development, determining ripeness is genuinely difficult by sight alone
[5]. Traditionally, mature fruit was simply allowed to drop naturally
and collected daily from the ground [1]. Modern commercial growers
instead use a touch-picking method: fruit is harvested directly from
the tree once it separates from the stem under light finger pressure,
picked slightly before it would naturally drop, which extends its
usable shelf life for distant markets [4][6]. Documented storage
regimes vary somewhat by source but generally run around four weeks
refrigerated followed by less than a week at room temperature; the
fruit is climacteric [6][3][1][7].

**Finding 4 — a genuinely small global crop with strong, geographically
concentrated local identity.** New Zealand's documented industry
includes roughly 140 growers across some 240 hectares, producing
roughly 1,200 tonnes in a recent year (an industry-wide average yield
of only around 5 tonnes per hectare, reflecting a mix of young and
mature orchards), split between local fresh sale, local processing, and
a modest export volume [6][4]. A South American growing country
documented roughly 300 hectares and nearly 2,800 tonnes produced in a
recent year, concentrated in a few specific districts [5]. The crop has
also been grown commercially in parts of the former Soviet Union since
the late 1920s, and in California and Uruguay [2]. In that same South
American growing country, most of the fruit goes to processed products
such as yogurt and a traditional fermented beverage, with fresh sale
representing the smallest market share [8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen shrub or small tree, typically
  1-7 m (often kept to around 2.5 m by pruning in commercial settings);
  perennial, with a single autumn crop over roughly six weeks in
  temperate growing regions, or year-round harvest flushes in tropical
  highland growing regions.
- **Climate:** Subtropical, mild coastal temperate, or Mediterranean
  climates, and tropical highlands at substantial elevation; requires
  genuine winter chilling (Finding 2).
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible (halved and scooped).
  Composition figures conflict modestly between sources, but broadly
  fall around 600 kcal/kg and 7-9 g protein/kg, with notable fiber and
  vitamin C content.
- **Pollination:** Required for most cultivars (self-incompatible or
  only partly self-fertile); primarily bird-pollinated (petal-eating
  birds), with bees and bumblebees as a secondary, less effective
  pollination source.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 9,000 kg/ha) is derived
  from a documented national area-and-production ratio for one major
  growing country; good and exceptional tiers are derived from
  documented mature-orchard yield ranges; a poor tier is a rough
  estimate reflecting poor cross-pollination or heat-stressed
  conditions (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure
  exists for this species; water-use parameters here are estimated for
  a modest-sized evergreen shrub canopy. The species is documented to
  have notably low water-use efficiency and high stomatal conductance
  compared to some other subtropical fruit crops. A dry period before
  flowering actually helps induce flower buds, but once fruit is
  developing, water shortage causes fruit drop. Drought tolerance is
  judged moderate, and waterlogging tolerance is judged low, since free
  drainage is explicitly required.
- **Propagation:** Clonal cultivars are propagated by hardwood cuttings
  (with rooting hormone and bottom heat), air- or ground-layering, or
  grafting (reported with low success in at least one source); true
  seed is used mainly for producing seedling trees and rootstocks, and
  sources disagree on whether seedlings come fairly true to type or
  not (see Open research questions).
- **Soil:** Deep, organic, alluvial soils are preferred, though sandy to
  clay loam soils are tolerated; performance is poor on light sandy
  soils. A documented calcium-to-magnesium ratio imbalance in the soil
  causes a specific fruit disorder (brown pigmentation, cracking, and
  chlorosis), correctable with dolomitic lime.
- **Labor:** No comprehensive labor-cost study was found. Harvest
  (touch-picking over several passes across a roughly six-week window)
  is documented as the dominant labor component; pruning is modest on
  hedged or branch-bent trees.
- **Storage:** Roughly four weeks refrigerated near 4°C, followed by
  about five to seven days at room temperature; some sources give a
  shorter post-cold-storage shelf life, and documented regimes vary
  somewhat (see Open research questions).
- **Stand life:** Documented productive lifespan runs roughly 30-40
  years, with full production reached around 8-10 years and full
  canopy development by around year 5.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Generally described as remarkably
  pest-resistant, with only minor documented issues: scale insects,
  fruit flies (a documented elevation-related risk threshold in at
  least one growing region), thrips, leaf spots, and an anthracnose
  fungal disease.
- **Cultivars:** Spans a modern, reliably self-fertile commercial
  baseline cultivar and several older selections differing in
  self-fertility percentage, fruit size and shape, flavor, and growth
  habit; at least one cultivar is specifically used in detailed
  thermal-requirement research in a South American growing region.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not specifically researched in
  the sources reviewed.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include scale insects, fruit flies (with
a documented elevation-related risk threshold in at least one growing
region), thrips, leaf spots, and an anthracnose fungal disease; the crop
is otherwise described as remarkably pest-resistant.

No further specific health/safety hazards beyond ordinary produce
handling were found in the sources reviewed for this crop.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** touch-picked fruit → graded by size → cold-stored; culled
  or naturally fallen fruit → pulped (yielding a documented pulp share
  of total fruit weight, with skins as a byproduct) → jam, jelly, or
  chutney; pulp → juice → further fermented into wine; flower petals →
  eaten fresh; leaves → infusions; seed from ripe fruit → seedling
  trees.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen pulp and fruit
  production in detail; dairy-based processed products such as yogurt
  and a traditional fermented beverage; spray-dried powder, vodka, and
  other extract production.

## Open research questions

- Self-fertility reporting conflicts meaningfully between sources: one
  classic reference describes an older cultivar as fully self-fertile,
  while more recent regional sources describe a different, newer
  cultivar as the only reliably self-fertile selection — not reconciled
  here.
- Whether seedlings come reasonably true to type is reported
  inconsistently between a classic reference source and a more recent
  regional extension source — not reconciled here.
- Raw-fruit composition figures (calories, protein) differ modestly
  between a national food-composition database and a general
  encyclopedia reference — not reconciled here.
- Documented post-harvest cold-storage duration and subsequent shelf
  life vary somewhat across sources, likely reflecting different
  storage-regime details or cultivar differences — not fully
  reconciled here.
- No standard international crop-coefficient figure exists for this
  species; water-use parameters here are rough estimates rather than a
  directly sourced figure.
- No comprehensive labor-cost study was found; all labor-hour figures
  in this entry are rough estimates.
- No wild-harvest tradition was identified for this species in the
  sources reviewed, despite its native range in the Brazilian and
  Uruguayan highlands.
- The scientific name for this species remains genuinely unsettled
  between two genus assignments across the sources consulted; this
  entry notes both but does not attempt to resolve the taxonomic
  question.

## Estimates and derivations

The world-average yield figure (roughly 9,000 kg/ha) is derived
directly from a documented national production-and-area ratio for one
major growing country in a recent year; this figure likely sits below
a fully mature orchard's typical yield, since it includes young,
not-yet-mature plantings, and is broadly consistent with district-level
yield figures documented for that same country.

The poor-yield tier is a rough estimate at roughly 60% of a separate
documented industry-wide average yield figure for a different major
growing country (itself an average across orchards of all ages),
reflecting a badly pollinated or drought-stressed orchard, a single
self-incompatible cultivar planted alone, or a low-elevation, heat-
stressed site — all documented to measurably depress fruit set and
yield.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days figure, seasonal water-use
index) are a rough estimate for a modest-sized evergreen shrub canopy
kept to around 2.5 m by pruning, set somewhat below the equivalent
estimate used for a larger-canopied, closely related subtropical fruit
crop this project has also researched, since this species' documented
low water-use efficiency argues against setting the estimate too low.

Soil-moisture-depletion allowance and drought/waterlogging-tolerance
parameters are rough estimates reflecting this species' documented
shallow root system and explicit requirement for free drainage; drought
tolerance is judged moderate, reflecting documented ability to grow
without irrigation in at least one humid subtropical growing region,
balanced against documented fruit drop during extended drought.

Growth-stage curve parameters are a rough estimate reflecting an
evergreen canopy with relatively flat photosynthetic activity across
the season.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest are derived from the documented
touch-picking method applied across several passes over the roughly
six-week harvest window in one major growing country, combined with an
assumed hand-harvest rate for small, delicate fruit; pruning and
post-harvest handling labor are rougher estimates, since no dedicated
regional cost-of-production study was found.

Time to first harvest (roughly 1,000 days, reflecting the documented
2-4 year range for cutting-grown trees) and the multi-year ramp to full
production (reaching full production around 8-10 years) are derived
directly from documented bearing-age figures. Peak harvest rate is
derived by dividing the world-average yield figure by the documented
roughly six-week harvest-window length in one major growing country.
Standing-crop duration on the tree (about 3 days) and its fast decay
rate reflect the documented rapid browning and decay of mature,
dropped fruit within a few days under warm conditions.
