## Naming and scope

*Crataegus azarolus* L. (Rosaceae), a hawthorn grown for its edible fruit [1][2].
It is the species behind the name clash flagged in `medlar.md`: it is called the
**"Mediterranean medlar"** (also Neapolitan medlar), and when the genus *Mespilus*
is merged into *Crataegus*, azarole sits in the **same genus** as common medlar
(*Crataegus germanica*), its closest cultivated relative in this project [1][2][11].

- **Names:** azarole, azerole, azarolo, and Mediterranean / Neapolitan medlar
  [1][2][3]. The name derives via Spanish *acerola* / Arabic *az-zu'rūr*.
- **Varieties and forms:** a monograph recognises about four varieties [2]. Forms
  distinguished in cultivation include the thornier wild-type (grown mainly in
  southern France, Sardinia and Italy), a less-thorny yellow-fruited form, and a
  thornless eastern form extending toward central Asia; fruit colour runs yellow,
  orange and red [2].
- **Name clash — "azzarola hawthorn" / *C. × sinaica*.** A related **hybrid**,
  *Crataegus × sinaica* (azzarola hawthorn), is the taxon behind most of the
  Australian weed records that come up under "azarola/azzarola"; it is not the
  same as *C. azarolus* and its traits should not be read onto this species
  (Invasiveness/biosecurity) [10]. No separate row is proposed for the hybrid.
- **Range:** native from the Mediterranean basin east to Iran — western
  Mediterranean coasts through Cyprus, the Levant (Lebanon, Syria, Palestine),
  Turkey, Iraqi Kurdistan and into eastern Iran, with locally adapted varieties
  across that span; North-African and some European records exist but Kew flags
  parts of the range as uncertain [2][9]. Powo gives the native range as
  "Mediterranean to Iran" [9].

**Gate check (2026-11-25): passes.** Proposed from `medlar.md` as "the
'Mediterranean medlar', a cultivated hawthorn fruit... it shares the name and the
closest genus" [11]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a validly distinct *species* (and, under the narrow genus concept,
  a different *genus*, *Crataegus*, from *Mespilus* medlar) [1][2]. Not a form of
  medlar, loquat, quince or service tree.
- **Range:** a Mediterranean-to-Iran hawthorn, versus medlar's south-east
  Balkans / Asia-Minor / Caucasus centre [9][11].
- **Farming system:** a cultivated fruit tree in its own right (grafted onto
  hawthorn rootstock, named cultivars), historically grown across southern Europe
  for fruit and wood [1][4].
- **Harvest / uses — the sharpest contrast with medlar:** azarole fruit is eaten
  **ripe off the tree** (in warm climates) and needs **no bletting**; medlar is
  inedible until bletted (Finding 1) [1][11].

**Relatives in this project:** `medlar.md` (the proposing doc and closest
cultivated relative under the merged genus), plus the other pome relatives it
lists — `loquat.md`, `quince.md`, `pear.md` — and `service_tree` (also proposed
from medlar) [11]. No new relatives were surfaced for proposing; the *C. ×
sinaica* hybrid is noted above but not proposed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a hawthorn eaten ripe, not bletted.** Unlike medlar, azarole fruit
is edible when ripe: in a warm enough climate it is eaten raw straight from the
tree, and in cooler areas it is turned into preserves [1]. There is no bletting,
on-tree-frost softening or two-stage ripening of the kind that defines medlar —
the sharp use-pattern difference between the two "medlars" [1][11]. The fruit is a
hawthorn haw, larger than most wild hawthorns, yellow to orange to red, with
apple-like flavour and aroma and **several hard pyrenes (stones)** rather than
medlar's open calyx and soft seeds [1][2].

**Finding 2 — a long-cultivated Mediterranean fruit now in decline.** Azarole was
long grown across southern Europe for its fruit and its hard wood, but its
cultivation has been widely abandoned and it is "going out of favour"; some
nurseries still offer red- and yellow-fruited cultivars [1][2]. Named selections
exist — e.g. **'Sultan'**, noted for large fruit, high eating quality and rich
aroma — and Italian accession trials found accessions **GHI4** and **GHI7** had
bigger, tastier fruit than others [7][8]. No current production statistics were
found for any country (contrast medlar, where Turkey publishes figures) [1].

**Finding 3 — propagated by grafting, because seed and cuttings are slow.** The
main propagation route is grafting onto hawthorn (*C. monogyna*) seedling
rootstock; cuttings root poorly, and seed is slow and dormant [3][4]. Seed-grown
trees take about **5-8 years to first bear** and need stratification, germinating
only after up to ~18 months, because of a hard, impermeable seed coat [1][3]. A
2025 in-vitro study reported raising seedlings in about one month versus two to
three years in a nursery [5]. A 2022 clonal-rootstock trial tested apple (MM 106,
MM 111), pear (Fox 11) and quince (Quince A) stocks for hawthorn, noting that
graft success falls as the taxonomic distance between rootstock and scion grows
[4]. Grafted trees flower and bear earlier than seedlings [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous large shrub or small tree (a hawthorn);
  wild-type forms are thorny, cultivated forms less so or thornless [2]. Mature
  height not sourced to a firm figure (hawthorn habit, typically to ~8-10 m).
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; a late-summer-to-autumn fruit harvest.
  Precise months not sourced for this species (Mediterranean hawthorn haws
  typically ripen late summer into autumn).
- **Days/years to first bearing:** ~5-8 years from seed; earlier from grafts [1].
- **Climate zones:** Mediterranean and West-Asian, from the western Mediterranean
  to eastern Iran [2][9]. Full sun is best for fruiting [1]. Chill requirement:
  Not sourced (a deciduous temperate hawthorn, so a winter-chill need is likely
  but no figure was found). Elevation band: Not sourced to a figure; it occurs
  across Mediterranean hill country and into the mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan and
  Iran [9].
- **Composition (fresh fruit):** Tunisia, 14 genotypes — total sugars ≈ 5.8-7.9 g
  per 100 g (58-79 g/kg), vitamin C ≈ 31 mg per 100 g (≈ 314 mg/kg), and total
  phenols ranging very widely at 498.5-1,477 mg per 100 g, plus potassium and
  calcium [6]. Italian accessions showed high sucrose in one pair and otherwise
  near-absent sucrose, with similar polyphenol, flavonoid and procyanidin levels
  across accessions [7]. (Hawthorn fruit is notably phenol-rich.)
- **Edibility:** raw-edible when ripe in warm climates; otherwise used in
  preserves [1]. Seeds/pyrenes are not eaten (see Hazards) [3].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this species. Hawthorns generally have
  insect-pollinated, nectar- and pollen-rich flowers; whether azarole is
  self-fertile was not found.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to any storage organ.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. No per-tree or per-hectare figures were found, and no
  production statistics exist for any country [1].
- **Water use:** Artificial irrigation is usually unnecessary once established
  [1]; no crop-coefficient figure was found.
- **Propagation:** grafting onto *C. monogyna* seedling rootstock is the main
  route; seed is slow and dormant; cuttings root poorly; in-vitro culture
  accelerates seedling production (Finding 3) [1][3][4][5]. Clonal/viral
  degeneration: Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** well-drained soils; a sunny position favours fruiting [1]. pH range,
  texture detail and **salinity tolerance: Not sourced.**
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced. (No bletting or special softening step,
  unlike medlar; fresh shelf life not found.)
- **Establishment:** full-sun site, well-drained soil; planted as grafted trees
  [1]. Spacing: Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** red- and yellow-fruited nursery cultivars [1][2]; **'Sultan'**
  (large fruit, high eating quality, rich aroma) [8]; the Italian trial
  accessions GHI4 and GHI7 (larger, tastier fruit) [7]. A monograph's four
  varieties and the thorny wild-type / less-thorny yellow / thornless eastern
  forms are the main infraspecific groups [2].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced to figures. As a hawthorn it is cold-hardy and
  wind-tolerant; drought, heat and waterlogging responses were not found.
  Frost-as-a-quality-event: **not applicable** — azarole is not frost-softened or
  bletted (Finding 1), the opposite of medlar [1][11].
- **Water regime:** rain-fed; irrigation usually unnecessary once established [1].
  No flooded/paddy requirement (none in the genus).
- **Harvest stages / windows:** single ripe-harvest when fruit colours and
  softens; no second (bletting) stage (Finding 1) [1]. Exact window not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** Mediterranean to Iran [2][9] — so introduced elsewhere.
  - **Weed status:** *C. azarolus itself* was **not** found recorded as a
    naturalised invasive. Most "azarola/azzarola" weed records (South Australia,
    where hawthorn is a declared weed with isolated Mount Lofty infestations;
    Victoria, which assesses it for spread) attach to the **hybrid *C. × sinaica***
    and to *C. monogyna*; invasion by *Crataegus* can create a dense shrub layer
    that shades out ground cover, and azarole is judged *likely to behave
    similarly* if it escaped — a low-but-non-zero risk inferred from relatives,
    not a record for this species [10].
  - **Fire blight:** hawthorns (*Crataegus*) are classic hosts of *Erwinia
    amylovora*, the fire-blight bacterium regulated in medlar's own entry [11];
    azarole-specific regulatory listings were **not sourced this pass** and are a
    research request. Movement/legal restrictions: Not sourced.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced; not a protected crop (an orchard/garden
  tree).

## Hazards

- **Seed/pyrene toxins:** the seeds contain toxins and should be removed before
  eating the fruit [3]; like other Rosaceae pome/haw seeds these are expected to
  be cyanogenic, but a measured content was not sourced.
- **Thorns:** wild-type and many cultivated forms are thorny — a harvest/handling
  hazard [2].
- **Fire blight:** as a *Crataegus*, a host of *Erwinia amylovora* [11]; a
  biosecurity rather than a food-safety hazard.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed cyanogenic hazard is deferred until this project
  has a place for health-and-safety data and a measured content is found (the same
  deferral as `medlar.md`).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** ripe fruit picked → eaten raw (in warm climates) [1].
- **Chain 2 (preserves):** fruit → jams, jellies and fruit preserves, especially
  in cooler areas where it does not sweeten fully on the tree [1][2].
- **Chain 3 (drinks):** fruit → liqueurs; azarole liqueurs are a traditional
  Mediterranean use [2].
- **Wood:** hard and fine — historically a reason for growing the tree, used for
  tool handles and turnery [1][2].
- **Rootstock / ornamental:** grown as an ornamental hawthorn, and the genus is
  widely used as pome rootstock and hedging [3].
- **Medicinal:** hawthorn (*Crataegus*) leaf, flower and fruit have a long
  cardiotonic folk and phytotherapy use; *C. azarolus* twigs/leaves have been
  studied for phenolic bioactivity, but specific preparations were not chased down
  this pass [2].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** candying, wine/cider and any oil use were not
  confirmed for this species.
