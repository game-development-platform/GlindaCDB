## Naming and scope

*Pseudocydonia sinensis* (Thouin) C.K.Schneid. (Rosaceae, subtribe Malinae):
Chinese quince [1]. It is the **sole species of its genus** — a monotypic
genus like true quince (*Cydonia*) — a deciduous-to-semi-evergreen tree of
southern and eastern China, reaching roughly 6-18 m with a dense, twiggy
crown [1][2].

This is the third distinct "quince" the project has researched, and the
gate-check question is which genus each belongs to. All three are separate
genera in the same subtribe:
- **Quince** (*Cydonia oblonga*, `quince`): the true quince, a monotypic
  genus, a self-fertile warm-climate tree whose main non-fruit role is as a
  dwarfing **rootstock** for pear and medlar [6].
- **Japanese / flowering quince** (*Chaenomeles*, `japanese_quince`): a
  separate genus of thorny shrubs with clustered flowers and a huge
  ornamental-cultivar base; its medicinal fruit (*mugua*, chiefly
  *C. speciosa*) is listed under `medicinal-status.md` [6].
- **Chinese quince** (this doc): thornless, with **single (not clustered)
  flowers** and serrated, hairless leaves, which is how it is told apart
  from *Chaenomeles* [1][5].

**Name-clash and synonym (a sourcing hazard, not a trait).** Older and much
medicinal/phytochemical literature places this species in *Chaenomeles* as
*Chaenomeles sinensis* [1][5], so phytochemistry on "*Chaenomeles sinensis*"
is this plant, while "*Chaenomeles speciosa*" is the flowering quince. The
East Asian common name compounds the confusion: the Chinese 木瓜 *mùguā*
covers this tree, the flowering quince **and** papaya; Korean *mogwa* and
Japanese *karin* (花梨) name this species, but in Japanese medicine its fruit
is distinguished as *wa-mokka* (和木瓜) [1]. These are classification/naming
matters, out of scope for the schema-fit check, but they govern which sources
describe this plant.

This species passes the distinctiveness check without question as its own
genus, with a distinct East Asian native range, a distinct use profile
(see Findings), and a strong ornamental/bonsai cultivation path absent in
true quince. No sub-rows are proposed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a hard, astringent pome that, like true quince, is never a
fresh-eating fruit, but whose processed products are different.** The fruit
is a large, very hard, astringent, intensely aromatic pome ripening in autumn
(October) [1][2]. As with *Cydonia*, it is cooked or preserved rather than
eaten raw [1]. But the product set is East Asian rather than the Western
*membrillo*/jelly tradition of true quince: it goes chiefly into **Korean
*mogwa-cheong*** (fruit sliced and preserved in honey or sugar) and the
tea **_mogwa-cha_** made by stirring that preserve into hot water (dried or
powdered fruit is also boiled or steeped) [1][3]; into **Japanese *karin*
products** — fruit liqueur and throat/cough confectionery; and into
traditional Chinese medicine [1]. A weakly-sourced processing note says the
fruit's own pectin is **insufficient to set a jelly**, so added pectin is
needed — a contrast with pectin-rich true quince, if confirmed [2].

**Finding 2 — the fruit's documented bioactivity (high-polymer procyanidins,
anti-influenza activity) is the backbone of its throat-remedy use.** Peer-
reviewed work found Chinese quince fruit has a **higher phenolic content than
both true quince and apple**, dominated by **high-polymer procyanidins**, and
that its phenolics gave the **strongest anti-influenza activity** of the three
in a haemagglutination-inhibition assay [4]. A follow-up found a 50% ethanol
fruit extract's active fraction is high-molecular-weight polyphenols that
neutralise influenza virus by inhibiting haemagglutination and suppressing
viral NS2 protein synthesis, tying the laboratory result to the fruit's long
traditional use for throat complaints [4]. These are **in-vitro / animal**
results, not human-outcome trials [4]. The fruit also carries the oleanolic-
and ursolic-acid triterpenes typical of the group [5]. This bioactivity is a
product/medicinal property (it belongs under Uses and Hazards), not a
cultivation trait.

**Finding 3 — in much of its introduced range it is grown as an ornamental
and bonsai subject, with the fruit secondary.** Its horticultural hallmark is
**exfoliating bark that flakes like a sycamore/plane tree** into a grey-green-
orange-brown patchwork; combined with pale-pink spring flowers, early leaf-
out, autumn colour and large fragrant fruit, this makes it a four-season
ornamental and a **popular bonsai subject** [1][2]. In Western gardens the
living tree — not the fruit — is effectively the product, which is the
schema-exception pattern of "a living plant as the product" (Entry 50) rather
than a harvested organ [2]. It flowers on old wood, so pruning is done after
flowering to preserve bloom and fruit [2].

**Finding 4 — a warm-summer pome at the cool edge of its range, and a
fire-blight host like the rest of the subtribe.** It is hardy to roughly USDA
zone 5-6 through 8 but **needs long, hot summers to ripen fruit well** and to
harden off; in cool-summer climates (the RHS rates it H4) it is grown against
a sheltered south/south-west wall and may fruit poorly [2]. Being in subtribe
Malinae with pear, apple and true quince, it shares their disease exposure:
sources list **fire blight** (*Erwinia amylovora*), quince leaf blight, brown
rot, powdery mildew and honey fungus, with fire blight singled out as able to
"devastate" the plant [2]. No quantified production, yield, water-use or
labour data specific to this species was found in this pass — consistent with
a minor fruit / ornamental crop rather than a tonnage commodity [1][2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous (to semi-evergreen) small tree,
  ~6-18 m; a single autumn fruit harvest [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single end-of-season harvest; fruit ripens in autumn
  (~October) and is aromatic and very hard at harvest [1][2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced. (A long-lived tree; no
  first-bearing age found for this species.)
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate; hardy ~zone 5/6-8 (RHS H4), but
  **requires long hot summers to ripen fruit and improve cold-hardiness**;
  grown against a warm wall at the cool end of its range [2]. No chill-hour
  requirement was sourced for this species (true quince's ~100-450 h is not
  assumed to transfer) [2]. Native to southern/eastern China; no distinctive
  elevation band sourced [1].
- **Composition:** Fruit notably **phenol-rich — higher total phenolics than
  true quince or apple**, dominated by high-polymer procyanidins; carries
  oleanolic/ursolic triterpenes [4][5]. Per-100 g nutrition database figures:
  Not sourced.
- **Edibility:** Not eaten raw — hard and astringent; cooked or preserved only
  (honey/sugar preserve, tea, liqueur, confectionery) [1][3]. A weakly-sourced
  note says the fruit softens and loses astringency after frost (a bletting-
  like effect); treat as unconfirmed (see Open questions) [2].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this species. Flowers are solitary (unlike
  *Chaenomeles*'s clusters); whether it is self-fertile like *Cydonia* was not
  confirmed [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced; a temperate deciduous tree with no
  documented day-length requirement, and not applicable to a tree fruit.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. No production or per-tree yield figure was found for
  this species.
- **Water use:** Not sourced. "Easy to grow in average, moist, well-drained
  soil in full sun" is the only moisture guidance found [1][2].
- **Propagation:** Commonly **by seed** (sown in containers outdoors in
  autumn, i.e. cold-stratified over winter) [2]; grafting/cuttings for named
  or bonsai stock are implied by ornamental use but were not detailed this
  pass. No clonal/virus-certification system specific to this species was
  found (contrast true quince's ACLSV/ASGV/ASPV indexing) [2][6]. Whether it
  serves, or is served by, quince/pear/medlar rootstocks was not sourced — an
  open question given the shared subtribe [6].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Average, moist, well-drained soil, full sun [1][2]. pH preference,
  texture and **salinity tolerance:** Not sourced (true quince's acid-soil
  preference and salt sensitivity are not assumed to transfer).
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for the fresh fruit. Hardness and
  strong aroma suggest handling like other hard pomes, but no regime,
  temperature or shelf-life figure was found; the honey/sugar **preserve**
  (*mogwa-cheong*) is itself the keeping form, refrigerated after opening and
  browning gradually [3].
- **Establishment:** Planted as a seedling or nursery tree; against a sheltered
  warm wall in cool-summer climates [2]. Spacing/density: Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** No named fruiting cultivars were found; a bonsai forum notes
  bark appearance varies between plants ("2-3 varieties"), but no formal
  cultivar list was sourced [2]. This species' selection effort is ornamental
  and informal rather than the fruiting-cultivar base of true quince.
- **Weather response:** Needs hot summers to ripen; frost may soften/sweeten
  the fruit (weakly sourced, see Edibility); fire-blight pressure worsens in
  warm humid conditions as for the rest of the subtribe [2].
- **Water regime:** Ordinary irrigation/rainfall; no flooded/paddy
  requirement [1][2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One autumn harvest window (~October) [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced (no mycorrhizal, replant or allelopathy
  finding).
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to southern/eastern China; **no
  invasive-species listing was found** in this pass. Its main biosecurity
  weight is as a **host of fire blight** (*E. amylovora*), a regulated
  pathogen of the subtribe, so it shares the host-material movement concerns
  of pear/quince where those apply [2]. No cultivation/import legal
  restriction specific to this species found.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced as a greenhouse fruit crop; it is,
  however, a common container/**bonsai** subject (Finding 3) [2].

## Hazards

- The fruit's active polyphenol fraction is pharmacologically active (anti-
  influenza in vitro; traditional throat remedy) [4]; as with other quinces,
  the **seeds are cyanogenic** in this group and are cored out before
  processing — but a seed-cyanide figure specific to *Pseudocydonia* was
  **not sourced** this pass, so this is carried as the same **TO BE
  ADDRESSED** seed-cyanide gap the project defers for stone/pome fruit (see
  `quince.md`) rather than a confirmed figure [6].
- Fire blight is a severe bacterial disease of the plant (a crop-health and
  quarantine consideration, not a human hazard) to which this species is
  highly susceptible [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (food):** large hard astringent fruit → sliced and cored (removing
  seeds) → **preserved in honey or sugar** (*mogwa-cheong*) → stirred into hot
  water as **_mogwa-cha_** tea; or dried/powdered → boiled or steeped [1][3].
  Fruit → **Japanese *karin*** fruit liqueur and throat/cough confectionery
  [1]. Fruit → traditional Chinese / East Asian medicine, exploiting the
  high-polymer procyanidins and triterpenes (Finding 2) [4][5].
- **Chain (ornamental / living plant):** the whole tree is grown and sold as
  an **ornamental and bonsai subject** for its exfoliating patchwork bark,
  spring flowers, autumn colour and fragrant fruit — the living plant is the
  product, not a harvested organ (Entry 50) [2].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** Western-style jam/jelly (made, but pectin
  may need adding, per a weak source); liqueur recipes and ratios; the
  honey-cure ratios and curing time for *mogwa-cheong*; any seed-oil or
  fibre use; formal bonsai horticulture.

## Open research questions

- **Frost/bletting softening** of the fruit (astringency loss after frost)
  rests on one non-authoritative source; whether this species is genuinely
  conditioned off-tree or by frost, like medlar, needs an extension-grade
  source. If confirmed, it would join schema Entry 41 (off-plant
  ripening/conditioning) alongside `quince` and `medlar` [2][6].
- **Reproductive biology:** self-fertile or not, and pollination mechanism —
  not sourced for this species [1].
- **Rootstock relationships:** whether Chinese quince is used as, or grown on,
  *Cydonia*/pear/medlar rootstock (given the shared subtribe) — not sourced
  [6].
- **No quantitative agronomy** (yield, water use, labour, production tonnage,
  first-bearing age, storage regime, cultivar list) was found; this reflects
  minor-crop status and would need Chinese/Korean/Japanese-language
  horticultural sources.
- **Pectin-set claim** (fruit pectin insufficient to gel) is weakly sourced
  and, if true, is an interesting contrast with pectin-rich true quince worth
  confirming [2].
- **Seed-cyanide figure** for *Pseudocydonia* specifically was not found;
  carried under the project's deferred stone/pome seed-toxin write-up [6].
