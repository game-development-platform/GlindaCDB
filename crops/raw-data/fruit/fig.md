## Naming and scope

The edible common fig (Moraceae), the type species of a genus containing
over 800 species [1]. This entry covers one species with four
recognized fig types, which differ meaningfully in pollination biology
but share the same climate, tree structure, propagation methods, and
product range, so they are treated here as cultivar groups rather than
separate crops: common figs (parthenocarpic, setting fruit without
pollination), Smyrna figs (requiring pollination by a specific fig wasp
carried from male trees, a process called caprification), San Pedro
figs (an unpollinated first crop followed by a pollinated main crop),
and caprifig trees themselves (the male, wasp-hosting tree required to
support Smyrna-type production). Caprifig trees are treated as a
necessary farm input for Smyrna orchards and a harvestable product in
their own right, not as a separate crop. The sacred fig (the Bodhi-tree
species) and the ornamental weeping fig are different species,
mentioned here only in passing for religious and hazard context. This
crop shares Mediterranean tree-fruit growing traits (drying as a major
processing path) with another Mediterranean tree-fruit crop this
project has also researched, and shares its pollen-as-consumable-input
concept with another palm fruit crop this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two fundamentally different products, fresh and dried,
with opposite storage needs.** Fresh figs are highly perishable: kept
near freezing and high humidity, they last only one to two weeks in
ordinary air storage, or three to four weeks under controlled-
atmosphere conditions; they are not chilling-sensitive and are
climacteric [3]. Dried figs, by contrast, brought down to roughly
17-20% moisture, keep for months at room temperature [3][9]. This stark
difference in storage behavior means fresh and dried fig production are
essentially two distinct commodity chains from the same tree, requiring
entirely different post-harvest handling and storage assumptions.

**Finding 2 — two crops a year from genuinely separate flushes.** An
early "breba" crop forms in spring on wood that survived the previous
winter, while the main crop forms later on current-season growth and
ripens from late summer into autumn [1]. In one major growing region
the main crop accounts for roughly 90% of total volume; some growers
strip the breba crop from most of their trees to focus tree energy on
the main crop, but deliberately keep it on one particular cultivar,
where it is documented to be just as sweet as the main crop [6]. A
separate cultivar is noted as often producing a particularly successful
breba crop [6]. Because the breba crop is borne specifically on
wood that survived winter, a hard winter freeze or aggressive pruning
removes it entirely for that season.

**Finding 3 — Smyrna-type figs require a genuinely separate
pollination system involving a second tree type and a specific wasp.**
The main drying-fig type used in one major growing region sets fruit
only after pollination by a tiny fig wasp, which is carried to the
female trees from the fruit of male "caprifig" trees [1][5][7]. In
practice, growers pick ripening caprifig fruit during a narrow window
in early-to-mid summer and hang it directly in the female orchard trees
to release the wasps; at least one major growing region has dedicated
local markets specifically for trading caprifig fruit for this purpose
[4][7][9]. Documented commercial practice from a major fresh-fig-
growing region describes specific bagging rates and replacement
schedules for the caprifig fruit, and a historical pollination-handbook
source gives a documented caprifig-to-canopy-area ratio [4]. The process
carries real risk: too many wasps per fruit can cause splitting, and the
wasps themselves are documented to carry a fungal pathogen responsible
for a specific fruit-rot disease — with three or more wasps per fruit,
one study found the fruit's natural opening was always infested [4].

**Finding 4 — drying figs on the tree and on the ground brings real
mycotoxin risk.** For the drying crop, figs are allowed to ripen fully
and partially dry while still on the tree, then fall; in one major
growing region they are gathered by hand every single day throughout
the season, with good and defective fruit sorted into separate baskets
immediately [3][9]. Gathered fruit is then spread in a single layer on
drying racks, checked morning and evening, and piled and covered at
night to protect against dew and insects; the target is a specific
maximum moisture content and a specific maximum fruit-count-per-
kilogram standard in at least one protected designation of origin
[9][3]. The drying season requires reliably dry, mostly cloudless
late-summer weather — rain or high humidity during this period darkens
and splits the fruit, while excessively low humidity instead causes
thick, tough skins [9]. Molds and souring organisms readily enter fruit
that is split or insect-damaged, and export lots from at least one major
producing region are routinely checked for a specific mycotoxin;
documented international regulatory limits exist for two different
mycotoxins in dried figs [3][10].

**Finding 5 — a well-documented, geographically concentrated
production pattern.** World production in a recent year totaled roughly
1.2 million tonnes from around 300,000 hectares (a world-average yield
of roughly 4 tonnes per hectare), with a slightly higher world total and
yield figure given for a more recent year in a separate source; one
country alone accounts for over a quarter of documented recent world
production, with several North African countries following [1][11]. In
that leading producing country, orchards in one major growing region are
documented as almost entirely planted to a single dominant drying
cultivar, grown rainfed on slopes [11][7]. A major US growing region
documented a specific production and area figure for a recent year, with
new plantings reaching commercial bearing at roughly 5-7 years [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous tree or large shrub, typically
  7-10 m; perennial and deciduous, with fresh figs picked every few days
  through the season and drying figs gathered daily as they fall.
- **Climate:** Mediterranean, warm temperate, or subtropical climates;
  reaches into the tropics only at elevation; also grown in some
  continental climates with hot summers, given adequate winter
  protection.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible. Fresh fruit gives roughly
  740 kcal/kg and 8 g protein/kg; dried fruit is far more
  calorie-dense, around 2,490 kcal/kg and 33 g protein/kg, reflecting
  its much lower water content.
- **Pollination:** Varies fundamentally by fig type — common figs are
  parthenocarpic and need no pollination at all; Smyrna-type figs
  absolutely require pollination via caprification (Finding 3); San
  Pedro types need no pollination for their breba crop but do require
  caprification for their main crop.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 4,000 kg/ha) is derived
  from a documented global area-and-production ratio; good and poor
  tiers are derived from documented regional yield figures, with an
  exceptional tier a rough estimate scaled above the good tier (see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure
  exists for this species; water-use parameters here are estimated by
  analogy to a standard reference-work entry for deciduous stone-fruit
  orchard trees with no ground cover, since this crop has a broadly
  similar leaf-out-to-leaf-fall season length and canopy structure,
  adjusted against a documented measured water-use figure from a young
  drip-irrigated planting in one growing region. The species is
  documented to tolerate moderate seasonal drought well, with
  established trees able to reach deep water tables; however, rain or
  high humidity during ripening and drying causes real fruit-quality
  problems (Finding 4). Waterlogging tolerance is judged moderate, since
  the species prefers porous, freely draining soil.
- **Propagation:** Almost entirely by hardwood cuttings taken during
  winter pruning, or by layering (pinning a scratched branch to the
  soil); true seed germinates readily but produces unpredictable fruit
  quality and is used only in breeding. Trees are own-rooted, with no
  grafting or rootstock use.
- **Soil:** Porous, freely draining soil is strongly preferred, and the
  species tolerates genuinely poor soil; one major growing region
  documents deep sandy-clay soils with lime content and a specific pH
  range as optimal. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium each have
  documented, somewhat opposing effects on dried-fig size, quality, and
  skin color in at least one detailed regional source.
- **Labor:** No comprehensive labor-cost study was found. Harvest for
  drying figs is a genuinely daily hand task throughout the drying
  season (gathering fallen fruit, sorting, and rack-turning), while
  fresh-fig harvest involves picking by hand every few days; caprification
  itself is a dedicated multi-week labor task for Smyrna-type orchards.
- **Storage:** Fresh figs are highly perishable (roughly 1-2 weeks
  refrigerated, only 2-3 days at room temperature), while properly dried
  figs keep for months at room temperature — a dramatic difference
  between the two product forms (Finding 1).
- **Stand life:** Commercial bearing typically begins around 5-7 years
  after planting in at least one major growing region, though one
  vendor source claims bearing as early as year 2 for a particular
  cold-hardy cultivar; a specific overall productive lifespan figure was
  not found in the sources reviewed.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal pathogen causing a wasp-vectored
  fruit-rot disease (specific to Smyrna-type caprification), a black
  mold rot, an Alternaria fruit rot, and a souring condition caused by
  yeasts and bacteria carried by vinegar flies into damaged fruit.
- **Cultivars:** Spans a dominant Smyrna-type drying cultivar (needing
  caprification, prone to the wasp-vectored rot risk, but producing
  premium dried fruit under at least one protected designation of
  origin), several common-type cultivars popular for fresh eating
  (differing in skin color, flavor, and breba-crop reliability), a San
  Pedro-type cultivar group, and caprifig (male) trees themselves, which
  include a large number of native varieties documented in at least one
  regional germplasm collection.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable as a hydroponic crop;
  container growing and winter trench-burying are documented as
  practical strategies for growing this crop in otherwise too-cold
  climates.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a fungal pathogen causing a
wasp-vectored fruit-rot disease specific to Smyrna-type caprification, a
black mold rot, an Alternaria fruit rot, and a souring condition caused
by yeasts and bacteria carried in by vinegar flies through damaged fruit.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the plant's latex sap contains a protease enzyme
and phototoxic furanocoumarin compounds in the leaves and shoots,
creating both a general skin-irritant risk and a genuine photosensitizing
hazard on skin contact followed by sunlight exposure, which is why
pickers commonly wear gloves; dried figs carry a documented mycotoxin
risk (two distinct mycotoxins with established international regulatory
limits) when drying or storage conditions are poor; a traditional
fig-based distilled spirit carries the same general methanol-related
processing considerations as other traditional fruit spirits; and
ornamental relatives of this species (not this crop itself) are
documented as mildly toxic to common pets through the same latex
compounds, raising an open question about whether the edible species
itself carries a similar, lesser risk. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fresh figs → packed in a single layer → forced-air cooled;
  breba-crop figs → handled the same way as fresh figs; drying figs →
  rack sun-dried → graded with mycotoxin checks → optionally processed
  into paste; fresh figs → dehydrated or made into jam; culls → fermented
  and distilled into a traditional fig spirit; caprifig fruit →
  harvested and hung in Smyrna-type orchards to enable caprification;
  cuttings → rooted into new nursery trees.
- Not covered in detail by this research: fig latex as a traditional
  rennet substitute; canned and frozen fig production; fig syrup and a
  fig-based coffee substitute; wild and naturalized caprifig populations.

This fruit carries notable religious and cultural significance across
several traditions: it is one of the traditional Seven Species
associated with the Land of Israel in Jewish tradition, and a Jewish
agricultural rule treating a young tree's first three years of fruit as
forbidden also applies to it; it appears as a recurring image in both
Jewish and Christian scripture, including the fig leaf as a symbol of
covering in an early biblical narrative; it is invoked by name alongside
the olive in a specific chapter of the Quran; and in Jain tradition, fig-
class fruits (including this species) are explicitly forbidden food,
grouped with four other related fruits believed to harbor countless tiny
lives, alongside wine, meat, and honey — meaning Jain characters in any
setting modeling this tradition would not eat this fruit. The unrelated
sacred fig species associated with Buddhist tradition is a different
species from this crop. In ancient Greek history, fig exports from one
specific region were famously banned by a lawgiver in the 6th century
BC.

## Open research questions

- Dried-fig target moisture content differs somewhat across sources
  (one giving a lower percentage, another a higher maximum, and a third
  giving a notably different figure in a general nutrient-composition
  table) — not reconciled here.
- World yield figures differ slightly between two statistical sources
  for different recent years.
- Whether a major US growing region's documented yield figure reflects
  fresh or dried weight is unclear from the source.
- Reported fig-wasp emergence timing varies by growing site across
  sources.
- Years to commercial bearing differ sharply between a vendor's claim
  for one specific cold-hardy cultivar and more conservative commercial-
  orchard figures from a major growing region.
- A specific overall productive lifespan for this crop was not found.
- Soil nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium uptake figures used here are
  carried over without a dedicated nutrient-removal study.
- Chilling requirement and salinity tolerance were not researched.
- Whether the edible species itself (as opposed to its ornamental
  relatives) carries the same mild toxicity to common pets was not
  resolved in the sources reviewed.

## Estimates and derivations

Days to first harvest for the main crop (roughly 130-210 days from
leaf-out) is a rough estimate derived from documented leaf-out timing
and the documented main-crop harvest window.

Yield tiers are derived from a combination of sources: a world-average
figure from a documented global area-and-production ratio for a recent
year; a good-tier figure from a documented regional production-and-area
ratio for a major US growing region (though it is unclear whether this
reflects fresh or dried weight); a poor-tier figure derived from a
documented low-end per-tree dried-fig yield in a major rainfed growing
region, converted to a fresh-weight equivalent using the fresh-to-dried
weight ratio described below; and an exceptional tier as a rough
estimate scaled modestly above the good tier, informed by a documented
high yield figure from a young drip-irrigated planting in a different
growing region.

The fresh-to-dried weight ratio (roughly 3:1) is derived from documented
water-content figures for fresh versus dried fruit, converting to a
dry-matter ratio; since tree-dried figs fall only partially dried, the
true field-level ratio likely runs somewhat lower than this calculated
figure.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index,
soil-moisture-depletion allowance) are derived by analogy to a standard
international crop-evapotranspiration reference's entry for deciduous
stone-fruit orchard trees without ground cover, since this crop has no
dedicated entry in that reference; this analogy is cross-checked against
a documented, somewhat lower measured mean crop coefficient from a young
drip-irrigated planting in one growing region. Waterlogging tolerance is
judged moderate, reflecting this species' documented preference for
freely draining soil, rather than the very high tolerance that might be
assumed from its drought resilience alone.

Growth-stage curve parameters reflect a deciduous orchard tree with a
documented leaf-out-to-leaf-fall growing season.

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are rough estimates, since no
hours were found in any source; the underlying tasks themselves are
well documented (daily hand-gathering of fallen fruit during the drying
season, daily rack-turning and covering, and a multi-week caprification
task for Smyrna-type orchards), but no study converting these tasks into
labor-hour budgets was found.

Soil nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake per tonne of yield are
rough estimates, since no dedicated nutrient-removal study was found;
qualitative effects of each nutrient on fruit size, dried-fig quality,
and skin color are documented in at least one detailed regional source
and inform the general direction of these estimates.

Peak harvest rate is derived from the good-yield-tier figure divided by
an estimated main-crop harvest-season length of about 60 days; standing-
crop duration for fresh figs (about 5 days) and their fast quality-decay
rate reflect the documented rapid overripening of fresh fruit at room
temperature within a few days.
