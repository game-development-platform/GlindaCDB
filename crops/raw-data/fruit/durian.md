## Naming and scope

The cultivated durian of Borneo, Sumatra, and the Malay Peninsula,
*Durio zibethinus* (Malvaceae, formerly classified under Bombacaceae)
[1][2][3]. This species is the only one of its genus available on the
international market [2]. At least five other *Durio* species bear
edible fruit and are eaten regionally, mostly wild or semi-wild in
Borneo, but are excluded from this entry as not warranting their own
commercial crop treatment given their minimal formal trade. This crop
shares a dry-spell flowering-trigger strategy with another humid-
tropical tree-fruit crop this project has researched, and a compatible-
pollinizer/hand-pollination concept with several other tree-fruit crops
this project has researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — bat-pollinated at night, and mostly self-incompatible.**
Flowers open in the late afternoon and shed pollen through the evening
into midnight [4]. Pollination in nature comes mainly from small
nectar-feeding bats active at night, chiefly one specific cave-dwelling
nectar-bat species; honeybees visit for nectar in the morning but have
not been shown to actually pollinate the flowers [1][4][5]. The large
majority of commercial durian trees across its main growing region are
self-incompatible, so growers routinely mix cultivars or plant different
cultivars in adjacent rows to ensure cross-pollination [4]. Incomplete
pollination produces visibly misshapen fruit with undeveloped segments
[4]. Hand pollination is a simple, well-established technique: flowers
cut in the afternoon have their anthers rubbed onto the stigmas of a
different cultivar's flowers the next day, and a regional farmer survey
found that most growers in one major production area routinely hand-
pollinate from early evening onward [4][6].

**Finding 2 — flowering requires a dry spell, but not an excessive
one.** A relatively dry spell is documented to stimulate and synchronize
flowering, with some degree of moisture stress described as essential
for prolific flower-bud initiation [4][5]. A regional survey reports
roughly 18 or more consecutive dry days as an optimal induction window
[6]. Once flower buds appear, growers switch to heavy irrigation [4].
Critically, too much stress backfires: a 2024 controlled trial found
that strong drought stress inhibited flower induction regardless of a
tested plant-growth-regulator treatment, and that treatment itself did
not induce flowering [7]. Extreme rainfall at the wrong time is also
documented to lower yields [6].

**Finding 3 — two distinct harvest styles, with markedly different
shelf lives.** Ripe fruit falls naturally from the tree and then ripens
further and splits within 2-4 days [1]. One regional practice instead
cuts fruit (with its stalk attached) from the tree at roughly 85%
maturity, judged by days since flowering and a characteristic hollow
sound when tapped; this cut fruit keeps 7-8 days [1][4]. A different
regional practice instead gathers windfalls from the ground, which are
fully ripe but often cracked on impact and keep only 2-4 days [1][4].
Whole fruit is notably chilling-sensitive: storage below 15°C causes the
husk to blacken between the spines, while storage at 15°C itself extends
shelf life to about 14 days [1][4]. The edible pulp alone, separated
from the husk, keeps up to four weeks at 5°C; the fruit is climacteric,
though most of its characteristic ethylene production actually comes
from the husk rather than the pulp [1].

**Finding 4 — strictly tropical, and notably prone to root rot.** This
crop is grown within roughly 16-18 degrees of the equator, up to about
800 m elevation, requiring 1,500-3,000 mm of well-distributed annual
rainfall [4][5]. Frost kills the tree at any age — leaves drop below
about 8°C, and juvenile trees die outright below about 5°C [4]. A major
soilborne pathogen causing patch canker and root rot can kill trees
quickly on poorly drained soils, which has led growers in at least one
major production region to adopt resistant rootstock species and to
graft additional resistant rootstocks onto already-planted trees for
insurance [4][5][3]. Seed is markedly recalcitrant (cannot be dried or
stored conventionally): viable for only a few days if air-dried, or up
to about a month in damp, fungicide-treated peat, with viability lost
within about two weeks under ordinary conditions [4][5].

**Finding 5 — a genuine boom crop in recent years.** Recent
documented figures (snippet-level) show one major Southeast Asian
producing country with roughly 420,000 hectares planted and close to
1.5 million tonnes produced in a recent year; a second major producing
country with over 110,000 hectares and roughly 850,000 tonnes produced,
a large majority of which was exported; and a third major producing
country with close to 600,000 tonnes produced, almost half of it
planted to a single premium export cultivar [8]. One major importing
country's documented recent-year import volume and value underline just
how large and fast-growing international demand for this fruit has
become [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A large, buttressed evergreen tree, reaching
  up to 45 m in the wild but typically kept to 10-15 m in orchards;
  perennial, bearing once or twice a year with a documented tendency
  toward alternate (biennial) bearing.
- **Climate:** Strictly tropical, humid, within roughly 16-18 degrees of
  the equator, generally below about 800 m elevation.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible (the aril/pulp). Composition
  figures vary somewhat by source but broadly fall around 1,470 kcal/kg
  and roughly 15-27 g protein/kg, with notable fat content for a fruit.
- **Pollination:** Required — most commercial cultivars are self-
  incompatible, and hand pollination between compatible cultivars is
  widely practiced where natural nocturnal bat pollination is
  insufficient or inconsistent.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 4,500 kg/ha) and good tier
  are derived from documented peak-production per-hectare ranges; an
  exceptional tier is derived from a documented optimal-conditions
  yield figure; and a poor tier is a rough estimate reflecting a single
  self-incompatible cultivar, poor pollinator activity, or an unusually
  wet flowering season (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient figure
  exists for this species; water-use parameters here are rough
  estimates for a tall, dense, evergreen humid-tropical canopy.
  Drought tolerance is judged low (the tree needs substantial,
  well-distributed rainfall and supplemental irrigation in dry months),
  while waterlogging tolerance is judged very low, given this crop's
  marked susceptibility to a major soilborne root-rot pathogen.
- **Propagation:** Almost entirely by grafting (side-veneer, approach,
  wedge, or a modified budding technique) onto seedling rootstocks,
  including specifically Phytophthora-resistant rootstock species in at
  least one major production region; true seedlings are variable and
  notably slow to bear, so are used mainly for rootstock production.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained sandy-to-clay loam soils, favoring
  lighter rather than heavier textures; excellent drainage is essential
  given this crop's susceptibility to root rot.
- **Labor:** No comprehensive labor-cost study was found. Harvest
  (cutting fruit with the stalk, or daily gathering of windfalls),
  evening hand pollination, and pruning are documented as the main
  recurring labor tasks.
- **Storage:** Varies sharply by harvest method and storage temperature:
  cut fruit keeps 7-8 days at ambient conditions and up to about two
  weeks at 15°C, while windfalls keep only 2-4 days; storage below
  15°C causes chilling injury (husk blackening) in whole fruit, while
  separated pulp keeps considerably longer (up to four weeks) under
  refrigeration.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees typically begin flowering in years 4-8
  after planting, reaching peak production around years 12-15; the
  species is long-lived in the wild, though a specific commercial
  productive lifespan was not found in the sources reviewed.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A major soilborne pathogen causing patch
  canker and root rot (the dominant disease concern), plus further
  fungal pathogens, leaf spots, beetles, borers, mealybugs, scale
  insects, and nematodes.
- **Cultivars:** A range of named Thai, Malaysian, and Singaporean
  cultivars differing in fruit size, aroma strength, ripening speed,
  eating-stage duration, and self-incompatibility status; one premium
  Malaysian export cultivar now accounts for roughly half that
  country's total plantings.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not specifically researched in
  the sources reviewed.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a major soilborne pathogen
causing patch canker and root rot, further fungal pathogens, leaf spots,
beetles, borers, mealybugs, scale insects, and nematodes.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — sulfur-containing volatile compounds in the fruit
are documented to inhibit a liver enzyme involved in alcohol
metabolism, producing a disulfiram-like adverse reaction if the fruit
is eaten together with alcohol; falling fruit itself (sharp-spined and
weighing several kilograms) is a plausible physical injury risk beneath
fruiting trees, though this was not directly sourced; and a folk belief
holds that raw seeds cause shortness of breath, with the documented
practice being to cook seeds before eating rather than eat them raw.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit cut with the stalk, or gathered as windfalls →
  graded → stored cold or at ambient; fruit opened → separated into
  edible aril/pulp (roughly 14-35% of fruit weight), seed (roughly
  8-18%), and husk; aril → fermented with salt into a traditional
  pickled product, or boiled with sugar into a traditional sweet paste,
  or dried into a snack product or powder; seeds → boiled or roasted
  before eating; husk → fuel and ash; prunings and wood → fuel and
  interior construction material.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen fruit and arils or
  canned aril production in detail; artificial ethylene-based ripening
  practice in detail; durian honey; fruit-propping and tying practice
  in detail.

## Open research questions

- Protein-content figures for the raw fruit conflict meaningfully
  across sources (a US food-composition database figure notably lower
  than two other reference sources' figures) — not reconciled here.
- Soil pH guidance differs somewhat between sources (one giving a
  narrower, more acidic range, another giving a broader range extending
  to more neutral soils) — not reconciled here.
- Seed viability duration under recalcitrant storage conditions is
  reported inconsistently across sources (ranging from a few days to
  about two weeks) — not reconciled here.
- Time from flowering to fruit maturity is reported with some variation
  across sources (a wider range in one detailed source versus a
  simpler "about three months" figure elsewhere) — likely reflecting
  real cultivar and climate variation rather than a true contradiction,
  but not reconciled here.
- No standard international crop-coefficient figure exists for this
  species, so water-use parameters here are rough estimates rather
  than a sourced figure.
- No comprehensive labor-cost study was found; all labor-hour figures
  in this entry are rough estimates.
- A specific commercial productive lifespan beyond peak production
  (years 12-15) was not found in the sources reviewed.
- Salinity tolerance was not researched, and no entry for this crop
  exists in a standard international agricultural-salinity reference
  table consulted for this research.

## Estimates and derivations

The poor-yield tier is a rough estimate at under half the low end of
the documented peak-production yield range, representing a single
self-incompatible cultivar planting, poor nocturnal pollinator
visitation, or an unusually wet flowering season — all documented to
depress yield; a national planted-area average (which includes young,
not-yet-bearing trees) for one major producing country supports a
figure in this low range.

National-level yield figures cited in this entry are derived by
dividing documented national production totals by documented national
planted-area totals for two major producing countries in a recent year;
these use total planted area rather than bearing area, so likely
understate true per-bearing-hectare yield somewhat.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days figure, seasonal water-use
index) are rough estimates for a tall, dense, evergreen humid-tropical
canopy, assumed somewhat higher than a comparable humid-tropical tree-
fruit crop this project has also researched, reflecting this species'
taller stature and lack of any winter rest period; a documented
supplementary daily irrigation volume for mature trees is treated as
representing only supplemental irrigation rather than total water use,
so is not used directly in these parameters.

Soil-moisture-depletion allowance, root depth, and drought/waterlogging-
buffer parameters are rough estimates: soil-moisture-depletion allowance
is set low because precise stress timing is documented as critical for
successful flower induction; waterlogging tolerance is set very low
given this crop's well-documented root-rot susceptibility; and root
depth is a rough estimate based on this crop's documented preference for
deep soils, since no specific root-depth measurement was found.

Growth-stage curve parameters are a rough estimate reflecting an
evergreen canopy with relatively flat photosynthetic activity across
the season.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest, hand pollination, pruning, and
post-harvest handling are rough estimates, since no comprehensive
regional cost-of-production study was found.

Time to first harvest (roughly 1,800 days, about 5 years, reflecting
the earlier end of the documented 4-8 year range for grafted trees to
first flowering) and the multi-year ramp to peak production (reaching
peak around years 12-15) are derived from documented bearing-age
figures. Peak harvest rate is derived by dividing the world-average
yield figure by an estimated harvest-season length, since no sourced
season-length figure was found. Standing-crop duration and its rapid
quality decay rate reflect the documented 2-4 day shelf life of fallen,
fully ripe fruit left ungathered. This crop is treated as frost-
terminated, since frost is documented to kill the tree at any age.
