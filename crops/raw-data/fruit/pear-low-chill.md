## Naming and scope

The "Oriental hybrid" or "sand" pears of the US South: hybrids, mostly
chance seedlings, between the European pear (*Pyrus communis*) and the
Chinese sand pear (*P. pyrifolia*) [1][2][3].
- **Botanical name:** sometimes grouped as *P.* × *lecontei* Rehder, after
  'Le Conte' [2].
- **Cultivars in scope:** 'Kieffer', 'Le Conte', 'Orient', 'Baldwin',
  'Carnes', 'Flordahome', 'Hood', 'Pineapple' and 'Tenn' [1][4][3].
  - Oklahoma State names 'Orient' and 'Kieffer' as the two main hybrid
    cultivars [3].
  - 'Orient' was released by the University of Tennessee in 1945. Its
    parentage is unknown but probably European × Chinese [4].
  - 'Hood' is a parent of 'Flordahome' [1].
- **Gate check against neighbouring rows (re-confirmed 2026-11-24):**
  - **`pear`** (high-chill *P. communis*) stays separate. The hybrids need
    150-400 chill hours against far more for European pears, are grown on
    Callery stock rather than quince, and carry more grit cells [1][3].
  - **`asian_pear`** stays separate. Florida's pear table mixes in Asian
    cultivars ('Shinseiki', 'Ya Li', '20th Century'), which are not in
    scope here [1]. Asian pears are tree-ripened, while the hybrids are
    picked hard [3].
  - **Fire-blight-resistant European cultivars** grown in the South (such
    as 'Moonglow' and 'Magness') belong in `pear` [3].
  - **Callery pear** (*P. calleryana*) is covered as a rootstock and an
    invasive plant only, not as a crop (Finding 5) [5].

**Relatives in this project:** European pear (`pear`, `pear.md`), Asian pear
(`asian_pear`, `asian-pear.md`) and Korla pear (`korla_pear`), as fellow
*Pyrus* rows; low-chill peach (`peach_low_chill`) and low-chill apple, which
share the dormancy-breaker practice (Finding 1).

**Proposed:** none.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the hybrids crop on 150-400 chill hours, and stronger
substitutes are used where winters are warmer still.**
- **Chill by cultivar (hours below 45 °F, Florida) [1]:**

  | Cultivar | Chill hours | Florida area | Flowering | Ripens |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 'Baldwin' | 150 | North and central | Mid-late (March) | Late Aug-Sept |
  | 'Flordahome' | 150 | North and central | Early | July-Aug |
  | 'Hood' | 160-260 | North and central | Early (mid-March) | Mid-July |
  | 'Le Conte' | 250-300 | North and central | Late | July-Aug |
  | 'Kieffer' | 350-400 | North | Late (late March) | Mid-Aug-Sept |
  | 'Tenn' | 350-400 | North | Early (early March) | Early Sept |
  | 'Orient', 'Pineapple', 'Carnes' | not given | North and central (Carnes north) | Early to mid-late | July-Aug |

- **Limits:** none of the hybrids is recommended south of Orlando [1]. The
  Texas guide says many of its recommended hybrid and European pears need
  more than 1,000 h of chill (snippet only), a conflict with the Florida
  figures (see Open research questions) [6].
- **Florida's crop:** at least 85 acres in the 2017 census, with 14 counties
  not reporting; many plantings are a few backyard trees [1].
- **Chill substitutes in Egypt ('Le Conte'), all snippet only [7]:**
  - Fathi and Hussein (2007): zinc sulphate (3.5%) on 15 November to shed
    the leaves, then hydrogen cyanamide on 15 January, the best of the
    combinations tried; zinc sulphate also raised yield.
  - Samra et al. (2009): Dormex at 1% plus 3% mineral oil on 1 February
    hastened bud burst and raised the burst percentage. The authors called
    it cheaper than 4% hydrogen cyanamide alone.
  - A 2013-2014 study: garlic extract and urea phosphite gave the most
    fruit and the highest yield per tree among alternative agents.
  - Khalel et al. (1999) tested 2-4% Dormex at the end of January.

**Finding 2 — fire-blight tolerance is the reason to grow these pears, but
ratings for 'Kieffer' disagree across four sources.**
- **'Kieffer':**
  - "fire blight susceptible" (Florida) [1];
  - "High" fire-blight resistance (Oklahoma) [3];
  - "slightly susceptible", with 17% of trees infected (Mississippi trial)
    [4];
  - "high fire blight resistance", from a Texas extension guide quoted in
    the Mississippi report [4].
- **Other ratings:**
  - 'Orient' and 'Pineapple' are "very fire blight resistant" [1].
  - 'Flordahome' is resistant, and 'Baldwin', 'Carnes' and 'Hood' are
    moderate [1].
  - 'Le Conte' is "somewhat susceptible" [1].
  - 'Orient' is rated "High" in Oklahoma [3].
- **The Mississippi trial (Pontotoc, 1987-1994) [4]:**
  - Trees were on *P. calleryana*, drip-irrigated, with streptomycin at
    bloom every year (12 oz/acre).
  - 'Orient', 'Kieffer' and 'Le Conte' rated 0.3-1.1 on a 1-10 scale, with
    17-33% of trees infected. The three did not differ significantly.
  - The European 'Moonglow' rated 7.5, and every tree was dead of fire
    blight in 1992, five years after planting [4]. Oklahoma rates
    'Moonglow' "High" for resistance [3].
- **Why it matters:** pears bloom earlier and are more blight-prone than
  apples, and choosing resistant cultivars is "the most effective means of
  control" [3].
- **Management:**
  - Florida calls fire blight a cool-weather disease, occurring only in
    spring [1].
  - Infected wood is pruned out 8-10 inches (Florida) or 6-8 inches
    (Oklahoma) below the visible infection [1][3]. Florida also advises
    sterilising tools between cuts [1].
  - Copper during bloom is an option [1].
  - Excessive pruning and fertiliser push the vigorous growth that blight
    attacks most [1][3].

**Finding 3 — yield ramps steeply: almost nothing for six seasons, then
22.7-29.6 t/ha in the seventh.**
- **Mississippi trial, pounds per tree [4]:**

  | Cultivar | 1990 | 1991 | 1992 | 1993 | 4-year total |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | 'Orient' | 9.8 | 4.1 | 9.9 | 194.3 | 218.1 |
  | 'Kieffer' | 7.5 | 4.5 | 5.7 | 184.1 | 201.8 |
  | 'Le Conte' | 2.5 | 9.5 | 5.3 | 149.2 | 166.5 |
  | 'Moonglow' | 0.36 | 0.2 | dead | dead | — |

  - The trees were planted in spring 1987 at 16 × 20 ft [4]. At 336
    trees/ha, the 1993 yields are about 29.6, 28.1 and 22.7 t/ha (see
    Estimates).
  - 'Orient', 'Kieffer' and 'Le Conte' were recommended for Mississippi on
    yield and blight tolerance [4].
- **Frost risk:** full bloom fell between 5 and 29 March. The cultivars
  "bloomed early and were very susceptible to late-spring freezes" [4].
- **Missing data:** no national, state or world yield statistic for this
  cultivar group was found.

**Finding 4 — picked hard and ripened off the tree, but sources disagree on
whether a chilling step is needed.**
- **Harvest sign:** pick when the fruit begins to turn yellowish. Fruit left
  on the tree softens unevenly, softens at the core and gets more stone
  cells, so it turns gritty [1].
- **Grit:** the hybrids often have more stone cells than European pears and
  can feel "gritty". They are used more for processing than for fresh
  eating, though newer cultivars are smoother [3].
- **Ripening:**
  - **Florida:** ripen at room temperature, in a paper bag to hold ethylene
    [1].
  - **Oklahoma:** "European and Oriental hybrid pears require a period of
    chilling to ripen properly". The chilling takes 2 days to 2 weeks by
    cultivar, followed by 65-75 °F (18-24 °C) at high humidity. Unchilled
    fruit shrivel and rot [3].
- **Storage:** 32-40 °F (0-4.4 °C) for one month without significant loss
  (Florida) [1]. Oklahoma advises 30 °F (−1.1 °C) for pears generally, with
  3-8 months by cultivar [3].
- **Harvest dates:** in Mississippi, all three hybrids were harvested on the
  same day each year, between 23 and 31 August [4].

**Finding 5 — Callery pear is the standard stock, and it is invasive, now
banned in several states.**
- **Florida:** pears "should be grafted on *Pyrus calleryana* rootstocks"
  [1].
  - In a five-year study, 'Flordahome' and 'Hood' grew and survived best on
    *P. calleryana*, then *P. betulifolia* [1].
  - Old Home × Farmingdale 51, 97 and 333 and quince were unsatisfactory
    [1].
- **Oklahoma:** hybrids are budded to *P. calleryana* (drought-tolerant and
  disease-resistant) or OH×F selections (highly blight-resistant) [3].
- **Egypt ('Le Conte'), all snippet only [8]:**
  - *P. betulifolia* and *P. communis* were more productive than *P.
    calleryana* and 'Winter Nelis'. Calleryana and betulifolia resisted
    natural fire blight, while *P. communis* and 'Winter Nelis' did not.
  - *P. calleryana* suffers lime-induced chlorosis.
  - On saline-alkaline soils, *P. betulifolia*, then *P. calleryana*, did
    better than *P. communis*.
- **Callery as an invasive:**
  - It is native to China, Japan, Korea and Taiwan, and was introduced to
    the US in 1909 and 1916 [5].
  - It is reported outside cultivation in 152 counties in 25 US states (an
    uncited claim in the entry) [5].
  - Birds spread the seed once frost softens the fruit [5].
  - Wild plants can carry thorns [5].
  - UF/IFAS rates it a "High Invasion Risk" in north, central and south
    Florida [1].
- **Bans** [5][9]:
  - Ohio banned sale and planting in 2023 [5].
  - South Carolina followed in 2024. Its nursery-sales ban, from 1 October
    2024, also covers "any pear grown on Callery rootstock" (snippet only)
    [5][9].
  - Pennsylvania listed it as a Class B noxious weed in 2024 [5].
  - Massachusetts prohibited it in September 2024, with sales ending at the
    end of 2028 [5].
  - Missouri's sales ban starts in 2029 (snippet only) [9].
  - The South Carolina rule means the stock that Florida recommends cannot
    be sold for these hybrids in South Carolina.

**Finding 6 — a nineteenth-century Southern processing pear.**
- **'Le Conte':** introduced to Georgia in 1856 by John Eatton Le Conte, and
  named after him. It is a deciduous tree up to 8 m [2].
- **'Kieffer':** a chance seedling from a Chinese sand pear in Peter
  Kieffer's nursery in Roxborough, near Philadelphia, probably pollinated
  by a nearby 'Bartlett'. It first fruited in 1863 (one source gives 1873)
  (snippet only) [10].
- **Uses:**
  - **Florida:** 'Kieffer', 'Orient' and 'Pineapple' are canning pears;
    'Baldwin', 'Carnes', 'Flordahome' and 'Hood' are fresh and canning
    pears; 'Le Conte' and 'Tenn' are fresh pears [1].
  - **Oklahoma:** 'Orient' is a "heavy bearer, canning/baking" pear, and
    'Kieffer' an "old standard" of coarse texture [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** deciduous perennial tree; a mature pear tree is
  about 16-24 ft (4.9-7.3 m) tall [3].
  - **Florida:** bloom from early to late March [1].
  - **Mississippi:** full bloom 5-29 March, harvest 23-31 August [4]. That
    is 147-177 days from full bloom to harvest (see Estimates).
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing. Fruit is picked hard as it
  yellows and ripened off the tree [1][3].
  - In Mississippi, all three hybrids were picked on one date each year
    [4].
  - In Florida, cultivars ripen from mid-July ('Hood') to September
    ('Kieffer', 'Baldwin', 'Tenn') [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** first significant production in 1990,
  the fourth season after the spring 1987 planting. Large crops came only
  in the seventh season (Finding 3) [4].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Chill:** 150-400 h by cultivar; north and central Florida, none south
    of Orlando (Finding 1) [1].
  - **Hotter places:** dormancy breakers are used in Egypt (snippet only)
    [7].
  - **Frost:** spring freezes "can eliminate crops in frost-prone areas", so
    high sites with air drainage are preferred [3]; see Weather response.
- **Composition:** not sourced for the hybrids. The European pear composition
  is in `pear.md`.
- **Edibility:** eaten raw after off-tree ripening, but often gritty.
  Mostly used for processing (canning, baking) [1][3].
- **Pollination:**
  - "Most Florida varieties do not require cross-pollination but may have
    larger crops when planted with another variety" [1].
    - Florida's table marks 'Baldwin', 'Carnes' and 'Le Conte' as needing
      a pollinizer, and 'Flordahome', 'Pineapple' and 'Tenn' as partially
      self-fertile [1].
  - Oklahoma lists 'Kieffer' and 'Orient' as partially self-fruitful, each
    pollinizing the other, and advises planting a pollinizer within 40-50
    ft (12-15 m) [3].
  - Insects are needed even for self-fertile cultivars [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - **Mississippi, 7th season:** 22.7-29.6 t/ha [4] (see Estimates).
  - **4-year average:** 41.6-54.5 lb/tree (18.9-24.7 kg) [4].
  - No national or world statistic found.
- **Water use:**
  - **Kc:** no hybrid-specific value was found. FAO-56 gives one class for
    "apples, cherries, pears":
    - 0.45-0.80 initial;
    - 0.95-1.20 mid-season, the higher value with active ground cover;
    - 0.70-0.95 end [11].
  - **Florida irrigation:** every 7-10 days in dry seasons, wetting at least
    1 ft deep; mature trees take 50+ gallons (about 190 L) and young trees
    2-3 gallons each time; on sand, frequent light irrigation is better
    [1].
  - **Egypt, snippet only [12]:**
    - the best water-use efficiency was 3.85 kg fruit/m³ at 16.7 m³ per
      tree per year (trees on *P. communis*; cultivar not confirmed in the
      snippet);
    - in a 2023 study, irrigating at 75% of crop water requirement gave the
      highest water-use efficiency.
  - **Drought:** see Open research questions [1][3][13].
- **Propagation:** pears do not come true from seed and are sold grafted or
  T-budded onto Callery (or *P. betulifolia*) seedlings (Finding 5) [1][3].
  - Callery cultivars are self-incompatible [5].
- **Labor:** not sourced for this cultivar group. No budget was found.
  - **Named operations** [1][3]:
    - pruning to a modified leader;
    - cutting out blight 8-10 inches below infection;
    - copper sprays at 10-14 day intervals from bloom for leaf spot;
    - dormant oil in mid-January for scale;
    - streptomycin at bloom in the Mississippi trial [4];
    - fruit thinning to one fruit per cluster, which Oklahoma says pears
      need every season [3].
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture and pH:** best on well-drained, fertile sandy loam, but grown
    on many soils [1]. Oklahoma gives an optimum pH of 6.5. Pears tolerate
    clay better than stone fruit, but production and longevity are greater
    on well-drained ground [3].
  - **Herbicide:** roots are shallow and the bark is thin, so glyphosate
    taken up through the bark damages trees [1].
  - **Fertiliser:** UF/IFAS has not researched pear fertiliser needs. It
    suggests 6-6-6 or 8-8-8 at about 1.5 lb per year of tree age, up to 15
    lb per tree, split between January and June, with Mg and micronutrients
    [1].
  - **Mississippi trial:** 0.25 lb N per inch of trunk diameter [4].
  - **Salinity:** no threshold found. *P. betulifolia* stock tolerates
    saline-alkaline soil better than *P. communis* (snippet only) [8].
- **Storage (postharvest):** 0-4.4 °C for about a month before ripening
  (Florida) [1]; 3-8 months at −1.1 °C for pears generally, by cultivar
  (Oklahoma) [3].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Planting:** one- or two-year-old bare-root trees from late December
    to January; container trees year-round with irrigation [1].
  - **Spacing:** at least 16 ft in the row and 25 ft between rows, about 269
    trees/ha (Oklahoma) [3]; 16 × 20 ft, about 336 trees/ha (Mississippi
    trial) [4].
  - **Training:** modified central leader [1][4].
- **Cultivars:** see Findings 1, 2 and 6 [1][4][3].
  - 'Orient' gave the highest yield in Mississippi [4].
  - The Texas guide lists 'Ayers', 'Warren', 'Orient' and 'Kieffer' among
    its recommended pears (snippet only) [6].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Bloom frost:** temperatures of 26 °F (−3.3 °C) or below usually kill
    open blossoms [1].
  - **Late freezes:** early bloom (5-29 March) made all three hybrids "very
    susceptible to late-spring freezes" in Mississippi [4].
  - **Fire blight:** a spring, cool-weather disease in Florida [1].
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation (drip in the Mississippi trial) [4].
  No flooded requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one stage, hard-mature, ripened off the tree
  [1][3].
- **Soil biology:** not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** the crop itself is not reported as
  invasive. Its standard rootstock, *P. calleryana*, is (Finding 5)
  [1][5][9].
  - Suckers from the stock must be removed, or they grow into the thorny
    wild form [5].
  - Rules against selling pears on Callery stock (South Carolina) restrict
    the crop's nursery supply [9].
- **Controlled environment:** not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Fire blight (*Erwinia amylovora*):** Finding 2 [1][4][3].
- **Pear leaf spot (*Fabraea maculata*):** browns leaves and drops them on
  some cultivars [1].
  - **Control:** copper every 10-14 days from right after bloom. Copper can
    russet the fruit [1].
  - **Ratings:** 'Flordahome' and 'Orient' resist it, while 'Carnes' is
    susceptible [1].
- **Botryosphaeria canker:** black cankers, worst on some Asian pears, with
  "no good chemical control". Manage it with airflow and wide crotch angles
  [1].
- **Scale insects:** controlled by horticultural oil in the dormant season
  [1].
- **Hydrogen cyanamide (dormancy breaker, Finding 1):** an EPA acute-
  toxicity category I (danger) product.
  - It inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, so alcohol taken around exposure
    causes a disulfiram-like reaction.
  - Labels prohibit alcohol before, during and for 24 hours after handling.
    Italian poisoning case series found many illnesses began right after
    drinking.
  - It is not authorised in the EU (snippet only) [14].
- **Seed cyanogens:** pear seed ('Conference', European) held 1.29 mg/g
  amygdalin in a UK survey. No hybrid-specific figure was found [15].
- **Mycotoxins and allergens:** not sourced for this group.

**TO BE ADDRESSED:** hydrogen cyanamide worker safety and seed cyanogens are
deferred until this project has a place for health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** picked hard as it yellows → ripened at room
  temperature in a paper bag [1], or chilled for 2 days to 2 weeks and then
  ripened at 18-24 °C (Oklahoma) [3] → eaten fresh. Hard fruit can be held
  at 0-4.4 °C for about a month first [1].
- **Chain 2 (processing):** canning and baking, the main uses of 'Kieffer',
  'Orient' and 'Pineapple' [1][3].
- **Nursery chain:** Callery or *P. betulifolia* seedlings are budded or
  grafted with the cultivar [1][3].
- **Not detailed here:** preserves, "pear honey" and pickled pears (named in
  the old pass, not re-sourced), and seed-stratification steps for rootstock
  seed.
- **Religious and cultural:** Jewish law forbids grafting between tree kinds
  (*kilei ilan*), and most commercial pears are grafted in a forbidden way
  [16].
  - Whether a hybrid on Callery or *P. betulifolia* (both *Pyrus*) counts as
    a forbidden graft was not found (see Open research questions).
  - Quince stock, which raises the question for European pear, is
    unsatisfactory for these hybrids [1].

## Open research questions

- **'Kieffer' fire blight:** "susceptible" in Florida [1], "High" resistance
  in Oklahoma [3], "slightly susceptible" in the Mississippi trial [4], and
  "high resistance" in a Texas guide [4]. The trial's streptomycin
  programme may explain its low rating.
- **Chill:** 150-400 h in Florida [1] against "more than 1,000 hours" for
  many Texas-recommended pears (snippet only) [6]. It is unclear whether
  the Texas statement covers the hybrids.
- **Postharvest chilling:** none mentioned in Florida [1], but required for
  2 days to 2 weeks in Oklahoma [3].
- **Rootstock:** *P. calleryana* is best in Florida [1], but *P.
  betulifolia* and *P. communis* yielded more in Egypt (snippet only) [8].
  With Callery-rooted pears banned from sale in South Carolina [9], which
  stock replaces it in the US Southeast?
- **Drought:** Oklahoma calls *P. calleryana* drought-tolerant [3], while
  FAO-66 calls European pear "not a drought resistant species" [13].
  Whether hybrids on Callery stock are more drought-tolerant was not
  measured.
- **Yield:** the only yield data are one Mississippi trial [4]. No state or
  national statistic was found.
- **Cultivar origins:** the release years of 'Flordahome', 'Hood' and
  'Pineapple' were not re-sourced.
- **Jewish law:** the *kilei ilan* status of hybrids on *Pyrus* stocks [16].

## Estimates and derivations

**Mississippi trial densities and yields [4].**
- 16 × 20 ft = 320 ft² = 29.73 m², so 10,000 ÷ 29.73 = 336 trees/ha.
- 1993 yields: 194.3, 184.1 and 149.2 lb × 0.4536 = 88.1, 83.5 and 67.7
  kg/tree. × 336 = 29.6, 28.1 and 22.7 t/ha.
- The 4-year averages of 54.5, 50.4 and 41.6 lb/tree have a mean of 48.8
  lb/tree. The report's "average of 54.5 pounds" is the top cultivar's
  figure.
- Planted spring 1987, so 1990 is the 4th season and 1993 the 7th.

**Bloom to harvest [4].**
- 'Orient' 1990: 7 March → 31 August = 177 days.
- 'Kieffer' 1991: 29 March → 23 August = 147 days.
- The other cultivar-years fall between these two.

**Oklahoma spacing [3].** 16 × 25 ft = 400 ft² = 37.16 m², so 269 trees/ha.

**Unit conversions.**
- 26 °F = −3.3 °C; 30 °F = −1.1 °C; 32-40 °F = 0-4.4 °C; 65-75 °F =
  18.3-23.9 °C.
- 50 gal × 3.785 = 189 L.
- 40-50 ft = 12.2-15.2 m.
- 8-10 in = 20-25 cm.
