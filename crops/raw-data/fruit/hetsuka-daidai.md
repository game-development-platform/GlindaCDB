## Naming and scope

Hetsuka daidai (辺塚だいだい) is a local sour citrus (*kōsan kankitsu*,
an acid citrus used for juice and aroma rather than eaten fresh) of the
southern Ōsumi Peninsula, Kagoshima, grown only in the towns of Kimotsuki
and Minami-Ōsumi [1][2]. No species name has been assigned; it is
treated here as *Citrus* sp., family Rutaceae [1][5]. The name comes from
Hetsuka hamlet, on the boundary between Uchinoura (Kimotsuki) and Sata
(now Minami-Ōsumi) [2]. This entry covers cropID `hetsuka_daidai`,
including its large-fruited and small-fruited lines (Cultivars).

**Not a daidai.** The geographical-indication (GI) registration says the
name causes confusion with daidai (bitter orange), but that the fruit and
tree are an entirely different variety: the fruit is smaller, with a thin,
smooth rind, and the acidity is fresh rather than strongly sour and
bitter [2]. A DNA marker (CAPS) study confirms it is genetically distinct
from kabusu, the daidai of southern Kyushu, and from all 13 other
reference varieties tested [1]. It does have pomelo-type cytoplasm. It
clusters closest to kabusu, natsudaidai (Benikanatsu) and kimikan, and
sits about midway between pomelo and mandarin, which fits a pomelo ×
mandarin hybrid like daidai and natsudaidai [1]. Its nuclear genotype
could not rule out kabusu, tachibana, or banpeiyu (a pomelo) as a parent.
It also rules out self-pollinated daidai as its origin. The authors
suggest a cross between daidai and an untested pomelo × mandarin hybrid
as one possibility [1]. Its parents remain undetermined [1].

**Gate check (2026-11-19): promoted.** Proposed in `daidai.md`. It is
genetically distinct from daidai [1]. Its use is different: green acid
fruit picked from late August to October for juice, ponzu and garnish,
not a bitter-orange decorative or marmalade fruit [1][2]. In late October its fruit
is about half the weight of kabusu's, with about a third of the rind
thickness and a quarter to a fifth of the seeds [1]. Its cultivation is confined by
local agreement to two towns (Invasiveness / biosecurity) [2].
Production is small, about 50-58 t a year [1][2][3], but this project
models distinct traits rather than volume.

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Daidai / kabusu** (`daidai`): see that entry.
- **Natsudaidai** (`natsudaidai`): a close relative by DNA [1]; see that
  entry.
- **The other Japanese sour citrus** the comparison study used as
  controls: **yuzu**, **sudachi**, **kabosu** and **shiikuwasha**
  (*C. depressa*) [1]. Each was proposed as its own candidate row, and all
  four — `kabosu`, `shiikuwasha` (`shiikuwasha.md`), `sudachi` (`sudachi.md`)
  and `yuzu` (`yuzu.md`) — have since been researched.
- **Hyuganatsu**, a Miyazaki citrus the study used because its fruit
  looks similar; one of its parents is tachibana [1]. It is eaten fresh,
  not used as a sour citrus. Promoted after a gate check on 2026-11-19
  and researched as `hyuganatsu` (see `hyuganatsu.md`).
- **Kimikan** and **Kawabata mikan**, Kagoshima local citrus used only as
  DNA references [1]. Too little sourced to propose.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a sour citrus that is used green and stays sour for
months.** Fresh fruit is used mainly in September and October, while the
rind is still green [1]. Titratable acidity at the late-October peak use
was 3.80-4.33% across four years [1]. Acidity falls slowly. It usually
dropped below 4% by late November, below 3% by late December in 2019,
and stayed above 3% to late January (2018) or even late March (2021) [1].
The fruit can be used from late August, but then weighs only about
50-60 g [1]. The rind starts colouring in November, but turns only
yellow, never orange or red [1]. Green fruit is preferred, so the
authors suggest storing green fruit for later use, as is done for kabosu
[1]. Locally, the early green fruit stands in for vinegar. Fruit
yellowing in November-December is milder and has been the raw material
for local processed foods [2]. The registration's harvest calendar starts
in August-October [2]. The 2023 shipping calendar was fresh fruit from
September to early October and processing fruit from mid to late
October [3].

**Finding 2 — milder acid than the major sour citrus, and nearly all of
it citric.** In late October, hetsuka daidai's titratable acidity
(3.80-4.33%) was below the major sour citrus, which ran about 4-7% except
for one shiikuwasha [1]. Citric acid was 4.53% and malic acid 0.15%, so
citric is about 97% of the organic acid [1]. Ascorbic acid was 19.2-28.0
mg per 100 mL, against 44.8 for a thornless lemon and 39.8 for kabosu [1].
The juice share is about 50% by weight [2]. In a 1990 survey run by the
prefectural fruit-tree station, market traders and households rated it
highest of the varieties compared, for its juice and its thin,
easily squeezed rind. Opinion on its aroma was split [2].

**Finding 3 — the aroma is a balance, not a compound.** Peel oil is
81.2% limonene, then γ-terpinene 9.8%, β-myrcene 2.6%, α-pinene 1.4%,
p-cymene 1.2% and sabinene 1.1% [1]. No component was unusually high or
low against the reference varieties. A trained sensory panel found no
attribute unique to it either; its strongest note, "fresh leaf," scored
only 3.2 out of 5 [1]. The authors conclude that the distinctive scent
comes from the overall balance of ordinary components [1]. The GI
registration describes a lime-like rind aroma [2]. A separate peel-oil
study also exists [5].

**Finding 4 — two fruit-size lines that are probably sports.** Growers
recognise a large-fruited line and a small-fruited line, selected for
their fruit differences [1]. In 2020-2021 the large line weighed
116.7-130.1 g at mid to late October, against 86.5-87.8 g for the small
line [1]. Both lines, an old tree at Kishira, and a nursery-bought tree
had identical nuclear and chloroplast CAPS genotypes [1]. A cross or a
self would almost certainly give different nuclear genotypes, so the
authors infer the lines arose by mutation [1]. That makes the local
population effectively one clone, propagated vegetatively (Propagation).

**Finding 5 — a crop fenced in by local agreement.** The two towns agreed
to ban sale or removal of nursery trees outside their boundaries, to keep
it a local speciality. A 1995 prefectural survey found no trees outside
the area [2]. Local interest began in 1985, when growers, the
agricultural co-operative and both towns asked the prefectural fruit-tree
station to survey and select good lines [2]. Organised selection and
planting began in 1992, and a growers' section was formed in 1997 [2].
Trees surveyed in 1986 in the old Sata town were estimated at over 50
years old [2]. The registration was dated 15 December 2017 (protected
geographical indication, register No. 57) [2].

**Finding 6 — said to be labour-saving and to resist wildlife damage.** Prefectural
extension staff describe it as a labour-saving citrus with an advantage
against wildlife damage [3]. The source gives no figures or mechanism.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial citrus tree [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one main crop; fresh green fruit
  September to early October, processing fruit mid to late October [3].
  Usable from late August [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate coastal southern Kyushu, southern
  Ōsumi Peninsula [1][2]. Cold hardiness, chill requirement and elevation:
  Not sourced.
- **Composition:** No national food-composition entry was found. Juice:
  citric acid 4.53%, malic 0.15%, ascorbic acid 19.2-28.0 mg per 100 mL,
  Brix 7.6-9.1 across the four years [1]. Peel and juice flavonoids:
  rich in naringin and neohesperidin, with little neoeriocitrin [5].
  Synephrine is lower than in daidai [5].
- **Edibility:** Juice used as a seasoning and vinegar substitute, not
  eaten as fresh fruit [1][2].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced. Fruit carries about 10 seeds (2.2-11.4
  by sample) [1]. Embryony: Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** No per-hectare figure found. Production is about 50 t a year
  [1][2]. Shipments in 2023 were expected at 58 t from 70 households [3],
  or about 0.8 t per household (see Estimates, below). The 2025 plan was
  57.5 t [3].
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Vegetative (nursery trees) [2]. The four accessions
  tested were genetically identical, so the stock is effectively clonal
  (Finding 4) [1]. Reference trees at the university are on trifoliate
  orange rootstock [1]. Grower rootstock and virus status: Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Described as labour-saving [3]. No hours found.
- **Soil:** Not sourced. Salinity: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Storing green fruit is suggested for use
  after November, by analogy with kabosu [1]. No regime found.
- **Establishment:** Planting has been promoted since 1992 [2]. Spacing:
  Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** One landrace, the native hetsuka daidai, is the only
  variety the GI allows [2]. Within it:
  - **Large-fruited line (大玉系):** 100.5-140.5 g from late September to
    late November [1].
  - **Small-fruited line (小玉系):** 83.8-115.8 g over the same period,
    with a lower rind-shape index; fewer seeds in 2020 (2.2) but not in
    2021 (7.4) [1].

  Lines differentiated in the field had already been reported in 2018
  [1]. Fruit weight overall is about 100 g in late October [1]; kabusu
  in the same orchard was 190-230 g, with 8.5-9.1 mm rind against
  2.5-4.1 mm, and about 43-45 seeds against about 9-11 [1].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** No flooded requirement; ordinary orchard crop.
  Irrigation: Not sourced.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Two market stages from one crop: green
  fresh fruit (September-early October) and processing fruit (mid to late
  October) [3]. The registration also describes yellow November-December
  fruit for local processing [2]. Not successive ripening.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to the two towns; not grown
  elsewhere [2]. No invasive listing found in this pass. Nursery trees
  may not be sold or taken outside Kimotsuki and Minami-Ōsumi, by local
  agreement rather than law (Finding 5) [2]. The GI ties the name to the
  native variety grown there [2]. Japan's domestic citrus-greening
  movement controls also cover citrus plants (see `daidai.md`).
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Market defects:** Fruit with external damage from black spot
  (melanose), canker, sooty mould or rust mite is barred from GI sale
  [2]. No outbreak data found.
- **Synephrine:** present, but lower than in daidai [5]. No safety
  assessment was found. TO BE ADDRESSED with the other bitter-orange
  synephrine material.
- **Drug interaction:** furanocoumarin and CYP3A data were not found for
  this variety. A peel coumarin (auraptene) was reported in a snippet
  [5].
- **Fungal contamination of the edible product:** nothing found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (green fruit → fresh seasoning):** late August-early October
  harvest → green fruit squeezed → juice used as a vinegar substitute,
  on fish and in cooking [1][2][3].
- **Chain 2 (October fruit → processing):** mid to late October harvest →
  juice → ponzu and dressing made by local processors [2][3]. Processors
  report that it balances sweetness and savoury flavours better than daidai
  in ponzu [2]. The registration's final product form is fresh fruit
  only [2].
- **Chain 3 (juice → beverage):** a national brewer launched a canned
  chūhai with the juice in October 2020, after about two years of
  development with the co-operative [4].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** local "citrus vinegar" and fish
  garnish use [2]. No yield fraction
  or process data was found for any chain.

## Open research questions

- **Parentage:** pomelo × mandarin hybrid by position, but parents
  undetermined; daidai not excluded as one parent [1].
- **Wildlife resistance and labour saving** are asserted [3] without a
  mechanism or figures.
- **Agronomy:** rootstock, spacing, yield per hectare, soil, cold
  hardiness, pollination, embryony and storage regime were all not
  sourced. A prefectural cultivation recommendation exists [2] but was
  not found.
- **Chemistry:** the 2019 flavonoid, coumarin and synephrine paper [5]
  was seen only as an abstract.
- **Proposed rows:** all now resolved — `kabosu`, `shiikuwasha`, `sudachi`,
  `yuzu` and `hyuganatsu` have been researched.

## Estimates and derivations

**Shipments per household, about 0.8 t.** 58 t expected in 2023 [3] ÷ 70
households [3] = 0.83 t. Range about 0.7-0.9 t, for production of 50 t
[1][2] to 58 t. An area and yield survey would replace it.

**Fresh-fruit share, about 5-13%.** 2023: 3 t fresh of 58 t [3] = 5.2%.
2025 plan: 7.5 t of 57.5 t [3] = 13.0%. Most of the crop goes to
processing.

**Juice per fruit, about 50 g.** About 100 g fruit in late October [1] ×
about 50% juice [2] = about 50 g. Range about 25-70 g for 50-140 g fruit.
