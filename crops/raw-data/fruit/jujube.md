## Naming and scope

*Ziziphus jujuba* Mill. (Rhamnaceae): Chinese jujube, red date, or
Chinese date [1]. It is not the date palm, and not the tropical ber
(*Z. mauritiana*), a different fruit this project has also researched
that is often confused with it [24]. A wild, thorny form with small,
sour fruit (sour jujube) is treated here as a cultivar group within this
entry, since its main economic products (rootstock seed, a sedative
seed-kernel product) are closely tied to the main crop's production
chain. Two other wild relatives — one the subject of specific religious
and cultural associations in parts of the Middle East, and another more
minor wild relative — are explicitly out of scope as separate wild/
foraged-plant entries rather than cultivated crops.

This entry is grounded against a dried-fruit palm crop this project has
also researched (for its staged, stage-dependent product chain) and
against another small deciduous temperate tree fruit this project has
also researched (for its three-harvest-stage structure, rootstock-
seedling propagation, and grafting practice).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an ancient East Asian crop at large scale, still
expanding.** This species has been cultivated in East Asia for over
4,000 years [1][3]. One major East Asian growing country accounts for
the large majority of world production, grown across several million
hectares and several million tonnes annually, concentrated in a handful
of provinces and forming the main income source for an estimated twenty
million farmers in that country [5]. In at least one specific arid
growing region within that country, a substantial local planting area
produced a documented tonnage in a recent year [6]. Smaller but growing
commercial plantings exist elsewhere, including a modest but established
grower base in Australia [1]. In the United States, the crop was first
introduced in the 19th century, with commercial cultivars introduced in
the early 20th century by a notable plant-collecting expedition [3].

**Finding 2 — an unusual fruiting habit: deciduous fruit-bearing
shoots.** Flowers form on current-season deciduous fruit-bearing
shoots (branchlets roughly 10-20 cm long) that fall off with the leaves
each autumn. Each "mother bearing shoot" (a slow-growing fruiting spur)
grows only about a millimeter a year but produces several new fruit-
bearing shoots annually; flower buds form, bloom, and set fruit within a
single season [3]. A traditional pruning rule governs how growers force
new growth from a desired bud by removing the competing secondary shoot
below it. The tree is deciduous, reaching 5-10 m, often thorny, with wood
notably harder than apple wood; mature trees produce commercially for
fifty or more years, and individual trees have been documented living for
over a thousand years in one well-known case [1][3].

**Finding 3 — among the latest-blooming temperate fruit trees, which
helps it dodge spring frost.** Leaf-out occurs in spring, with bloom
lasting two months or more into summer depending on growing region, and
fruit maturing in late summer to autumn [3]. Being among the latest
trees to bloom, it usually escapes damage from spring frost entirely
[3][12][17]. Chilling requirement for dormancy release is reported
inconsistently across sources: one cultivar broke dormancy and flowered
even at a constant warm temperature with no chilling at all (chilling is
evidently not strictly required, though it may speed dormancy release),
while another cultivar in a different study showed a specific measured
chilling requirement, and general reference and nursery sources give
quite different ranges again — not reconciled here. Reported cold
hardiness also varies by source and cultivar, from roughly -15°C up to
cultivars marketed as hardy to nearly -30°C; young trees are consistently
the more vulnerable life stage, with at least one documented case of
young trees killed to the ground (and re-sprouting) in a severe cold
snap, while mature trees in the same region were undamaged. In at least
one warm US growing region, winter injury is not considered a practical
problem at all; there, growing season length rather than winter cold is
the limiting factor, restricting production to early and mid-season
cultivars, with no drying-type cultivar proving commercially suitable
in that shorter-season climate.

**Finding 4 — propagation is almost always by grafting onto sour jujube
rootstock.** Most commercial trees are grafted onto seedlings of the
sour jujube cultivar group described under Naming and scope, chosen for
good germination, availability, and stress/salt tolerance [3][4]. Whip
grafting is used in the dormant or growing season on matched-diameter
stock, with bark grafting used a few weeks after bud break [4]. Root
suckers come true to type only from own-rooted trees; suckers from
grafted trees are simply rootstock growth and must be removed [3][12].
Sour jujube rootstock seed is prepared by soaking stones for about three
days, then stratifying them in moist sand over winter [4]; cultivars do
not come true from seed, so true-to-type propagation always requires
grafting or own-root suckering.

**Finding 5 — three distinct harvest stages serve three different
products.** Chinese production practice recognizes three fruit-maturity
stages: white-mature (full size, greenish-white) used for candying;
crisp-mature (half to fully red, still crisp) used for fresh eating; and
fully mature (dark red, beginning to wrinkle) used for drying [9]. Each
stage has a markedly different cold-storage life: white-mature fruit
keeps longest (several months near freezing), crisp-mature fruit keeps
an intermediate period, and fully red fruit keeps shortest (under a
month) — though at least one study on a specific winter-jujube cultivar
found the half-red stage specifically best suited to long cold storage
[9]. Fresh fruit picked at first ripeness keeps at least two weeks under
ordinary refrigeration [3]. Dried fruit (reduced to under roughly a
quarter moisture by weight, either sun-dried over about three weeks or
oven-dried in under two days at a moderate temperature) keeps for many
months as a traded staple product [1][5][9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous perennial tree, 5-10 m, often
  thorny; perennial bearing.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, hand-picked in multiple passes
  for the fresh market, or harvested once when fully red for drying;
  three distinct harvest-maturity stages are recognized (Finding 5).
- **Climate:** Temperate, warm-temperate, semi-arid, and arid (with
  irrigation), and Mediterranean climates; a wide hardiness range by
  cultivar, with young trees notably more cold-vulnerable than mature
  trees (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible at all three harvest-maturity stages.
- **Pollination:** Not required for several widely grown self-fertile
  cultivars, though cross-pollination with a second cultivar raises fruit
  set and size; at least one notable cultivar needs a pollenizer cultivar
  and insect activity to set fruit at all. The species is also
  documented as a major nectar plant in at least one major growing
  region.
- **Yield / composition:** Raw fruit is calorie- and protein-dense
  compared to many fruits, with a very high vitamin C content relative
  to body weight; dried fruit carries considerably more concentrated
  calories and carbohydrate per unit weight. Yield figures vary
  enormously between a calculated national average (which may reflect
  fresh or dried weight — not stated in the underlying statistics) and
  documented per-tree yields at typical orchard density (see Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** A dedicated multi-year weighing-lysimeter study measured
  seasonal water use and a seasonal crop-coefficient range for this
  species, the first directly measured (rather than estimated) water-use
  dataset encountered in this project's stone/pome-fruit research so
  far. The tree is notably drought-tolerant — in at least one US trial it
  handled both drought and excess soil moisture better than any other
  fruit crop tested at that site — but irrigation is still needed for
  premium fruit set and yield in semi-arid growing regions, and a
  specific optimal irrigation quota is documented for at least one
  hyper-arid Chinese growing region. Rain or heavy irrigation during
  fruit ripening is a well-documented cause of fruit cracking (see
  Hazards below).
- **Establishment:** Grafted nursery trees (cultivar budded onto sour
  jujube seedling rootstock) or own-rooted suckers are planted in full
  sun; softwood cuttings are also used under suitable conditions.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-cost study was found; labor-hour figures
  here are rough estimates, with harvest labor assumed to be
  comparatively heavy since fruit is small and fresh-market fruit is
  picked in several passes.
- **Soil:** Tolerates a very wide range from sandy to clay soils, and an
  unusually wide pH range from moderately acid to fairly alkaline; above
  a certain alkaline threshold, minor-element deficiencies appear.
  Nitrogen is reported as essentially the only fertilizer needed in at
  least one US growing-region guide. Sour jujube rootstock is specifically
  used to overcome saline soil conditions.
- **Stand life:** A few fruit may form in the first one to two years
  after planting or grafting, with reasonable commercial crops from
  roughly year four to five onward; mature trees remain productive for
  fifty or more years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** In the largest growing country, the
  dominant problems are a serious phytoplasma disease spread by
  leafhoppers (capable of killing trees within a few years, with older
  literature reporting losses as high as 80 percent in affected
  plantings), fruit splitting after heavy rain, and a fruit-boring moth
  pest. A different fruit fly pest affects the genus in parts of the
  Middle East and South Asia. In at least one US growing region the crop
  is reported as nearly pest-free, with cotton root rot (a soil-borne
  fungal disease affecting many crops in that region) being the main
  limiting factor where present — replanting jujube on an infected site
  is specifically discouraged.
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named cultivars exists (one major
  growing country has tested several hundred against a catalogue of over
  800), spanning fresh-eating, drying, and dual-purpose types, plus the
  sour jujube cultivar group grown primarily for rootstock seed and a
  seed-kernel product. Cultivars differ considerably in fruit size,
  self-fertility, ripening season, cold-storage suitability, and
  cracking resistance.
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked, in several passes for fresh-market fruit
  (picked from a creamy/firm stage through full red depending on intended
  use) or as a single pass when fully red for drying cultivars, which in
  dry climates may be left on the tree to begin wrinkling before picking.
- **Storage:** Storage life differs sharply by harvest stage, detailed in
  Finding 5 above; all three stages store well near freezing with no
  chilling-injury recorded at that temperature.

## Hazards

Thorny branches are a physical/handling hazard, most pronounced in the
sour jujube cultivar group. Documented pests and diseases include a
serious leafhopper-vectored phytoplasma disease (capable of killing
trees within a few years), a fruit-boring moth, a fruit fly pest
documented in parts of the Middle East and South Asia, a fungal leaf-spot
disease, and (in at least one US growing region) cotton root rot, a
soil-borne fungal disease that precludes replanting on an infected site.
Fruit cracking after rain or heavy irrigation during ripening is a
significant quality-loss disorder, worse in sweeter and riper cultivars;
mitigations documented include rain covers, a protective spray
treatment, and deliberate pre-ripening water stress. TO BE ADDRESSED:
this species' nectar has been documented to cause a specific digestive-
tract ailment in honeybees foraging on its bloom in at least one major
growing region — a genuine animal-health hazard this project has not
yet built a dedicated pollinator-health data structure for, and which
should be flagged for a future pass. The sour jujube seed kernel
(suanzaoren) is used as a traditional sedative/anxiolytic medicine in
East Asian herbal practice; a specific safe dosage was not established in
the sources consulted.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** White-mature fruit is candied in syrup. Crisp-mature fruit
  is eaten fresh or cold-stored near freezing. Fully red fruit is sun- or
  oven-dried into red dates, which may be further steamed and stoned into
  a paste; fully red or dried fruit is also made into wine. Sour jujube
  fruit has its pulp removed, and the stones are cracked for the
  suanzaoren seed-kernel product, or stratified and grown into rootstock
  seedlings for grafting. Own-root suckers are also raised into nursery
  trees.
- Not covered by this research in detail: deliberate trunk/branch
  girdling and gibberellin sprays as fruit-set-enhancing practices;
  jujube honey production; tea, vinegar, juice, and syrup products; wood
  use; the windbreak function of jujube tree rows. No canned or frozen
  jujube product was found.

**Religious and ritual use:**
- In Korean traditional wedding ceremonies, dried jujubes are thrown by
  the groom's parents for the bride to catch in her skirt, together with
  chestnuts, with each caught fruit traditionally said to represent a
  future child.
- In Chinese wedding customs, red dates are presented together with
  peanuts and longan, playing on a word association between the fruit's
  name and the word for "early," wishing the couple early (and numerous)
  children.
- Under Jewish law, the general orlah prohibition on fruit from a tree's
  first three years after planting applies to this species as it does to
  any fruit tree, with years counted from planting.
- A placement convention in Korean ancestral-rite offerings reportedly
  places jujube first among a standard set of fruits offered, tied to a
  traditional mnemonic; this specific claim was not independently
  verified in this research pass and should be treated as unconfirmed.

## Open research questions

- National production-area and output figures for the largest growing
  country conflict meaningfully across sources — not reconciled here;
  relatedly, whether national production statistics are reported on a
  fresh or dried weight basis is not stated in the underlying sources,
  which significantly affects any derived fresh-weight yield figure.
- Chilling-requirement figures conflict substantially across four
  different source types (one facultative/no-chill cultivar, one
  cultivar with a specific measured requirement, a general reference
  range, and nursery-catalog figures) — not reconciled here.
- Cold-hardiness figures conflict between a general reference figure and
  nursery-marketed cultivar hardiness claims — not reconciled here, though
  both agree young trees are more vulnerable than mature trees.
- Waterlogging tolerance is described inconsistently: one US source
  reports exceptional tolerance of excess soil moisture, while another
  source emphasizes the need for fair-to-good drainage — not fully
  reconciled here.
- Which harvest-maturity stage is genuinely best suited to long cold
  storage is reported inconsistently: general sources rank white-mature
  fruit as storing longest, while a study on one specific winter-jujube
  cultivar found the half-red stage preferable for long-term cold
  storage — likely reflecting genuine cultivar-specific variation rather
  than a simple error.
- No dedicated labor-cost study, no direct dried-storage-life trial, and
  no verified per-hectare yield table (independent of the derivations
  used here) were found for this crop.

## Estimates and derivations

The growing-season-length range (bloom to maturity) is derived from the
documented bloom window and fruit-maturity timing, noting that bloom is
spread over roughly two months so individual fruit cohorts take roughly
three to four months from flower to harvest maturity.

Yield tiers are derived from a calculated national average (total
national production divided by total growing area, with the fresh-versus-
dried weight basis of the underlying statistics explicitly noted as
unresolved), a calculated "good" and "exceptional" figure from documented
mature per-tree yields at an assumed typical orchard planting density,
and an estimate for rainfed, disease-affected, or otherwise poor stands
(used as "poor").

Water-use parameters (seasonal crop coefficient, stage lengths, rooting
depth, soil-moisture-depletion allowance, drought-day tolerance) are
derived primarily from a dedicated multi-year weighing-lysimeter study
measuring this crop's actual water use directly, combined with an
estimated growing-season length; the lysimeter's measured seasonal water-
use figure is noted as consistent with a moderate reference
evapotranspiration environment, while a separately documented optimal
irrigation quota for one hyper-arid growing region is consistent with
that region's much higher evaporative demand.

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are rough, largely unsourced
figures, since no dedicated cost-of-cultivation study was found for this
crop; harvest labor in particular is assumed to be comparatively heavy
given small fruit size and the multiple hand-picking passes documented
for fresh-market production.

Soil nutrient-uptake rates are estimated by analogy to another small
temperate tree-fruit crop this project has also researched, since no
direct nutrient-removal study was found for this species; pH optimum is
estimated as the midpoint of the documented tolerance range, kept below
the documented alkaline-deficiency threshold.

The dried-fruit yield fraction (roughly two-fifths to just under half of
fresh weight) is derived from documented fresh-fruit moisture content and
the documented target dried-moisture threshold; since fully red fruit is
already somewhat dried on the tree before harvest in some practices, the
true fraction from that specific stage may be somewhat higher than this
calculation suggests.

Dried-fruit storage duration is a rough estimate reflecting this
product's well-documented role as a long-shelf-life traded staple, since
no direct dried-storage trial was found.

Yield-profile parameters (peak daily yield, standing time before quality
decline) are derived from the documented bearing-age pattern (some fruit
in years one to two, reasonable commercial crops from years four to
five) and the "good" yield tier spread across a documented harvest-
season length; standing time before decline reflects the documented
practice of leaving drying-type fruit on the tree to wrinkle naturally in
dry climates.
