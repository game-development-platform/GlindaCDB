## Naming and scope

*Ziziphus jujuba* Mill. (Rhamnaceae, buckthorn family): jujube, Chinese
jujube, Chinese date, red date; *zao* in Mandarin [1][2][12]. A 2011
decision conserved the name *Z. jujuba* over *Z. zizyphus* [1]. It is not
related to the date palm, or to jojoba [1][2].

**Sour jujube** (*Z. jujuba* var. *spinosa*, also given as *Z. spinosa*):
the wild thorny ancestor with small sour fruit [2]. It is treated here as
a cultivar group, because the crop uses it as the standard rootstock
(Finding 4). Its seed kernel is also an herbal product (Uses) [2][10].

**Separate entries:**
- **Indian jujube or ber** (*Z. mauritiana*): see `indian-jujube.md`. It
  is a tropical, evergreen-tending species that is not cold-hardy [2][22].
- **Wild relatives:** *Z. lotus* and *Z. spina-christi* are not covered.
  They are wild or foraged species with their own religious associations
  (Uses).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — China grows almost all of it, at a modest average yield.**
- **History:** jujube has been cultivated in China for over 4,000 years,
  beginning along the middle and lower Yellow River [2]. Neolithic wild
  kernels are known from the Qi River basin [1]. One review claims over
  7,000 years and cultivation in more than 40 countries [12].
- **China:** about 2 million ha and over 8 million t a year, by one
  review [5]. More than 90% of output comes from six provinces: Xinjiang,
  Hebei, Shandong, Shanxi, Shaanxi and Henan. The main area has shifted
  north-west into Xinjiang [5]. The original pass reported the crop as the
  main income of about twenty million farmers [5]. A claim that China has
  over 98% of world area and output was not relied on [5].
- **Hami, Xinjiang:** 21,800 ha produced 15,000 t of fresh fruit in 2014
  [6], only about 0.7 t/ha. Many plantings were young; that explanation is
  an inference. Mature dwarf, dense orchards in the same study yielded
  7.75-8.78 t/ha (Finding 6) [6].
- **United States:** first planted in North Carolina in 1837 and in
  California in 1876. Commercial cultivars arrived via USDA Chico from
  Frank Meyer in 1908 [2]. The crop reached Texas around 1875 [4]. It is
  still a mostly local novelty crop [2].
- **Australia:** planting began around 2000. By mid-2025 there were about
  60 growers and 50,000 trees [1].

**Finding 2 — an unusual fruiting habit: deciduous fruiting shoots.**
- **Shoot types:** four kinds: primary (extension), secondary (side
  branch), "mother bearing shoot" (a fruiting spur), and fruit-bearing
  shoot (a branchlet) [2].
- **Spurs:** mother bearing shoots grow only about 1 mm a year, but each
  produces 2-5 fruit-bearing shoots a year [2].
- **Fruiting shoots:** 10-20 cm long. They carry flowers and fruit in the
  leaf axils and fall with the leaves [2].
- **Single season:** flower initiation, bloom and fruit growth all happen
  within one season [2].
- **Flowers:** small (5-8 mm), fragrant and pale greenish-yellow, in
  clusters of up to 13 [2].
- **Pruning rule:** "One cut stops, two cuts sprout." Cutting a one-year
  shoot gives no new bud below the cut unless the secondary shoot below it
  is also removed [2].
- **Tree:** 5-10 m [1], or 15-30 ft (4.6-9 m) [2], with very hard wood
  and paired spines on young trees [2]. Commercial trees bear for 50 years
  or more. The "Jujube King" in Shandong is over 1,000 years old and still
  fruiting [2].

**Finding 3 — late bloom avoids spring frost, but chill figures conflict
widely.**
- **Phenology (New Mexico):** leaf-out in March-April; bloom from May to
  July, lasting two months or more; maturity in August-October [2].
- **Frost:** the tree blooms late enough to usually escape spring frost
  [4].
- **Chill, conflicting:**
  - Chinese studies give 775-1,737 hours below 7.2 °C, depending on
    cultivar [8].
  - Western Australian growers report flowering and fruiting in places
    with less chill than that [8].
  - A North American extension source gives 50-200 hours below 45 °F, and
    a Florida source calls the chill requirement low, under 400 hours
    [8].
  - 'Ben-Li' broke dormancy and flowered at a constant 25 °C with no
    chilling, so chilling speeds release but is not strictly required [8].
- **Cold, conflicting:** about -15 °C survival in one reference [1],
  against nursery claims of hardiness near -30 °C [17]. Young trees are
  the vulnerable stage [17]. Winter injury is "not a problem in Texas"
  [4].
- **Season length:** in northern New Mexico (Alcalde) only early and
  mid-season cultivars mature reliably, and no drying cultivar suits
  commercial production [2].

**Finding 4 — grafted onto sour jujube, which also carries the main
disease risk.**
- **Rootstock:** most commercial trees are grafted onto sour jujube
  seedlings, chosen for seed availability and stress tolerance [2][3].
  Tongue-whip, bark and cleft grafting are used [2][3].
- **Rootstock seed:** stones are soaked about three days, then
  stratified in moist sand over winter [3].
- **Suckers:** suckers from own-rooted trees can be grown on. Suckers from
  grafted trees are only rootstock and must be cut off at or below ground
  [2][4]. Root injury encourages suckering [4].
- **Cuttings and seed:** softwood cuttings root in moist conditions [2].
  Cultivars do not come true from seed [4].
- **Disease risk:** jujube witches' broom phytoplasma is spread by
  grafting with infected material, including asymptomatic plants, root
  suckers, seedlings and grafted plants [12][13]. It is not transmitted by
  seed, pollen, root contact or soil [12][13].

**Finding 5 — three harvest stages for three products, and a
cold-storage conflict.**
- **Colour sequence:** dark green → yellow-green ("creamy white") → red
  spots → half red → fully red. Fruit softens and wrinkles a few days
  after it turns fully red [2].
- **Chinese practice:** white-mature fruit is candied, crisp-mature
  (half to fully red) fruit is eaten fresh, and fully mature fruit is
  dried [9].
- **Fresh fruit:** picked from the creamy stage to fully red but still
  firm [2].
- **Drying cultivars:** best picked fully red, or left on the tree to
  start wrinkling in dry climates. In humid areas, fruit must be dried
  quickly to avoid yeast and mould [2].
- **Storage:** fresh fruit keeps two weeks or more at 4 °C [2]. 'Dongzao'
  storage studies use 0 ± 1 °C and 90-95% RH for 75-90 days [9].
- **Conflict:** general sources rank white-mature fruit as storing
  longest [9]. A 'Dongzao' study found half-red fruit best for long cold
  storage: it had the lowest weight loss and respiration [9]. High CO₂
  with low O₂ injures 'Dongzao' [9].

**Finding 6 — drought-hardy, but measured water use is moderate to
high.**
- **Aksu, Xinjiang (2016-2019):** a weighing lysimeter under drip
  irrigation measured 532-592 mm over the growing season, with a crop
  coefficient of 0.85-0.93. Fitted basal coefficients were 0.4, 1.0 and
  0.5 for the initial, middle and late stages [7].
- **Yazd, Iran:** lysimeters under full irrigation measured 828 mm a
  year, with an annual Kc of 0.45 and a peak of 0.60 at full canopy. 30%
  and 60% deficits cut use to 514 and 386 mm [7].
- **Hami, Xinjiang (2013-2015):** a 12-13-year-old dwarf drip orchard,
  2 × 5 m (1,000 trees/ha), in a climate with under 50 mm of rain and
  3,000 mm evaporation. The optimum was 900 mm of irrigation with
  1,500-1,800 kg/ha of fertiliser, yielding 7.75-8.78 t/ha [6]. Another
  Xinjiang trial at 6,253 plants/ha found 600 mm optimal, against 1,100
  mm of flood irrigation [6].
- **Texas:** jujube survived both drought and excess soil moisture better
  than any other fruit plant in trials at College Station [4]. The same
  guide still asks for fair to good drainage [4].
- **New Mexico:** drought tolerant, but irrigation is needed in its
  semi-arid climate for good fruit set and yield [2].

**Finding 7 — witches' broom is the defining disease.**
- **Pathogen:** jujube witches' broom ('Zaofeng'), caused by '*Candidatus*
  Phytoplasma ziziphi' [12].
- **Effect:** shoot proliferation and phyllody; yield and quality loss;
  trees die within a few years [12].
- **Incidence:** 30.3% in Changli, Hebei, in 1956. An outbreak in Henan
  in the 1980s reached 20-70% in the main areas [12]. It has spread to
  all main Chinese areas, including Xinjiang and Guangdong, and to every
  province of Korea [12].
- **Range:** also recorded in Japan, India and Italy [12][13].
- **Vectors:** leafhoppers, *Hishimonus sellatus* and probably *H.
  hamatus*. *Hishimonus* is not known in California [13].
- **Control:** resistant cultivars such as 'Xingguang' exist. Tetracycline
  treatment works, but orchard and sanitary measures are needed [12].
- **Risk outside Asia:** California proposed an "A" pest rating, its
  highest risk category. The concern is jujube, *Prunus* and *Malus*
  hosts, and infected propagation material [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous tree, 5-10 m, usually thorny [1][2].
  Bears on deciduous fruiting shoots (Finding 2) [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial with a long harvest. Fresh-market fruit
  is hand-picked in several passes as it colours. Drying fruit is picked
  once, fully red, or later [2]. Chinese growers shake trees over tarps or
  knock fruit down with poles; trunk shakers may suit large drying
  orchards [2]. The season runs August-October in New Mexico and
  July-August in Texas [2][4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - Trees flower in the year of planting or grafting, and some bear a
    little fruit [2].
  - Most cultivars give a few fruit in year 2, with reasonable yields
    from years 4-5 [2].
  - Some cultivars are slow to bear early: 'Dongzao' and 'GA866' [2].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** temperate to warm-temperate, semi-arid and arid with
    irrigation [1][2][6].
  - **Cold:** survives about -15 °C in one reference [1]; nursery claims
    are colder [17].
  - **Chill:** 50-1,737 hours across sources; it may be facultative
    (Finding 3) [8].
  - **Elevation:** productive trees grow up to 5,595 ft (1,705 m) at
    Española, New Mexico [2].
  - **Season length:** sets the limit in short-season areas [2]. Hami has
    160 frost-free days and 4,260 °C·days above 10 °C [6].
- **Composition:**
  - **Raw, per 100 g:** water 77.9 g; energy 79 kcal (331 kJ);
    carbohydrate 20.2 g; protein 1.2 g; fat 0.2 g; vitamin C 69 mg;
    potassium 250 mg [1].
  - **Dried, per 100 g:** water 19.7 g; energy 287 kcal; carbohydrate
    73.6 g; protein 3.7 g; fat 1.1 g; vitamin C 13 mg; potassium 531 mg
    [1].
  - **Vitamin C conflict:** an extension guide gives 200-600 mg/100 g in
    fresh fruit [2], against 69 mg in the nutrient table [1].
  - **Sugars and carbohydrate:** fresh mature fruit has 20-40% soluble
    solids; dried fruit is 70-85% carbohydrate [2].
- **Edibility:** Raw at all three stages [2][9]. Leaves contain ziziphin,
  which suppresses sweet taste [1].
- **Pollination:**
  - **Self-fertile cultivars:** some set fruit alone, for example 'Li',
    'Alcalde #1' and 'Shanxi Li'. Cross-pollination still improves set
    and size [2].
  - **Pollenizer-dependent cultivars:** a few set no fruit without a
    pollenizer and insects, for example 'Sugarcane' [2].
  - **Advice and pollinators:** plant two or more cultivars. Pollinators
    include bees, flies, ants and ladybugs [2].
  - **Nectar:** it is a good nectar plant [2], but see Hazards.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Per tree:** 40-100 lb or more (18-45 kg) for a mature tree [2].
  - **Hami dwarf orchard:** 7.75-8.78 t/ha at 1,000 trees/ha; the
    fresh-or-dried basis is not stated [6].
  - **China average:** about 4 t/ha (see Estimates, below) [5], basis not
    stated.
- **Water use:** 532-592 mm a season with Kc 0.85-0.93 in Aksu; 828 mm a
  year with annual Kc 0.45 in Yazd. The optimal irrigation quota is
  600-900 mm in hyper-arid Xinjiang (Finding 6) [6][7]. The original pass
  also reported an eddy-covariance study [7].
- **Propagation:** Grafted onto sour jujube seedlings, or own-root
  suckers. Softwood cuttings and tissue culture are possible (Finding 4)
  [2][3]. *Clonal/viral risk:* witches' broom phytoplasma spreads in
  grafting and propagation material [12][13].
- **Labor:** Not sourced. Fresh fruit is hand-picked in several passes,
  while drying fruit can be shaken or knocked down [2]. See Estimates for
  a rough per-tree picking load.
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture and pH:** sand, loam or clay, pH 5.0-8.5, and it survives
    poor soils [2]. Above pH 7.8, minor-element deficiencies may appear
    [4].
  - **Nitrogen:** nitrogen is the only fertiliser needed in Texas. Use 0.2
    lb N per inch of trunk diameter, up to 1.0 lb per tree. Young trees
    get it in three monthly splits [4]. Do not fertilise new trees until
    leaf-out [2].
  - **Salinity:** one review gives a yield-decline threshold of about 4.9
    dS/m [19]. Sour jujube seedlings grew at 100-300 mM NaCl, wilted at
    400 mM and died at 500 mM in a non-peer-reviewed preprint [19]. Sour
    jujube rootstock is used for saline soils [19].
- **Storage (postharvest):** 4 °C for two weeks or more [2]; 0 ± 1 °C at
  90-95% RH for 75-90 days in 'Dongzao' studies [9]. Half-red fruit stores
  best in one study (Finding 5) [9]. Dried fruit is a long-keeping staple.
  No dried-storage trial was found [1][9].
- **Establishment:** Full sun, with annual pruning to improve set and
  quality [2]. High-density Xinjiang plantings use 2 × 5 m (1,000/ha) or
  2 × 0.8 m (6,253/ha) [6]. Remove suckers [4].
- **Cultivars:** China has over 800 cultivars, and drying types once made
  up about 90% of Chinese production [2]. Among those found:
  - **'Li':** large, round, up to 3 oz (85 g); self-fertile; mid-season
    [2]. The best-flavoured in Texas; ripens after 'Lang' [4].
  - **'Lang':** large and pear-shaped; for drying; splits if it rains at
    maturity [2]. The most widely grown in Texas [4].
  - **'Dongzao':** China's top fresh cultivar; slow to bear early.
    Gibberellin sprays or girdling are used to improve set [2][11]. It is
    the subject of most storage research [9].
  - **'Sugarcane':** needs pollenizers [2].
  - **'Honey Jar':** precocious; bears in the planting year [2].
  - **'Alcalde #1':** early; suits marginal areas [2].
  - **'Sherwood':** late; for long seasons [2].
  - **'GA866':** very high sugar [2].
  - **Other New Mexico cultivars:** 'Sihong' (for drying),
    'Kongfucui' (very productive), 'So', 'Maya' and 'Russian 2' [2].
  - **Chinese and Korean:** 'Huping Zao', the main Shanxi cultivar;
    'Junzao', 'Huizao', 'Xingguang' (witches'-broom resistant) and
    'Pozao' (susceptible) [6][12][14].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Spring frost:** escaped by late bloom [4].
  - **Winter cold:** harms young trees most [17].
  - **Rain at maturity:** causes cracking (Hazards) [2][14].
  - **Humidity at harvest:** raises the risk of yeast and mould on drying
    fruit [2].
  - **Drought and excess soil moisture:** both tolerated [4].
- **Water regime:** Rainfed in humid areas; drip- or flood-irrigated in
  arid areas; no flooded requirement [2][4][6].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** White-mature for candying, crisp-mature
  for fresh fruit, fully red for drying (Finding 5) [2][9]. The fully red
  stage is best for drying [2].
- **Soil biology:** Mycorrhizal inoculation improved seedling growth under
  salt stress, with the largest effect at 100 mM NaCl [19]. In Texas,
  cotton root rot is a major limiting factor, and losses should not be
  replanted [4].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Madagascar:** reported invasive in western Madagascar, threatening
    protected areas [1].
  - **Suckering:** the tree suckers freely [4].
  - **Biosecurity:** witches' broom phytoplasma is a quarantine concern
    in propagation material (Finding 7) [13]. A fruit fly attacking the
    genus occurs in parts of the Middle East and South Asia [16].
    European crops have lost 80-90% to moth larvae, including false
    codling moth [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced, apart from trees grown at a
  constant 25 °C in a dormancy study [8].

## Hazards

- **Witches' broom phytoplasma** (Finding 7) [12][13].
- **Fruit cracking:**
  - **Cause:** rain during maturation cracks some cultivars at every New
    Mexico site, but the cracks usually stay dry and uninfected [2]. In
    China, splitting after heavy rain near harvest can ruin a crop [2].
  - **Calcium sprays:** CaCl₂ sprays cut cracking in 'Huping Zao'. In
    'Junzao', EDTA-Ca worked better than CaCl₂, best sprayed at the
    white-ripe stage [14].
  - **Other controls:** rain shelters, coatings and crack-resistant
    breeding [14]. The original pass reported a pre-ripening water-stress
    trial [14].
- **Pests:**
  - peach fruit moth (*Carposina niponensis*), formerly China's top pest
    and occasional in New Mexico [2];
  - false codling moth and other larvae in Europe [1];
  - a fruit fly in the Middle East and South Asia [16];
  - Alternaria leaf spot [2];
  - cotton root rot in Texas [4].
- **Honeybee "jujube flower disease":** foragers collecting jujube
  nectar can die in large numbers in northern China. It is a major
  obstacle to colony growth there, and some beekeepers feed colonies
  rather than let bees work jujube [20]. The cause is unsettled:
  - an early study ruled out infection and pollen poisoning, blaming dry
    weather, thick nectar and overwork;
  - others proposed high potassium or alkaloids;
  - a 2024 study found midgut damage with a detached peritrophic
    membrane [20].

  TO BE ADDRESSED: this project has no pollinator-health hazard data
  structure yet.
- **Thorns:** paired spines on young trees and sour jujube are a handling
  hazard [2].
- **Sour jujube kernel:** suanzaoren is a traditional sedative. No safe
  dosage was established in the sources [10].
- **Fungal contamination of the product:** yeast and mould on drying
  fruit in humid areas [2]. No mycotoxin finding.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** fruit is hand-picked at the creamy-to-red stage,
  then kept at 4 °C or at 0 ± 1 °C for long storage [2][9].
- **Chain 2 (dried "red dates"):** fully red fruit is shaken down or
  hand-picked, then sun- or oven-dried to under about a quarter moisture
  [2][15]. Dried fruit may be steamed, pitted and made into paste, or used
  in baking in place of raisins or dates [2]. NMSU imported a pitter and
  slicer in 2018 [2].
- **Chain 3 (candied):** white-mature fruit is pricked and cooked in syrup
  [9][15]. A vacuum-candying variant exists [15].
- **Chain 4 (other products):**
  - smoked "black jujube" [1][2];
  - juice, jam, wine, tea, powder, porridge and mooncake filling [2];
  - Italian *brodo di giuggiole* syrup and Australian jujube beer [1];
  - jujube honey in Morocco's Atlas Mountains [1].
- **Chain 5 (sour jujube):** pulp is removed and stones cracked for the
  suanzaoren kernel [10]. Alternatively, stones are stratified and grown
  into rootstocks [3].
- **Wood:** hard and oily. It was used for woodblock printing from the 8th
  century, with up to 2,000 copies per block [1].
- **Not covered in detail:** girdling and gibberellin as fruit-set
  practices [2][11]; medicinal uses in Chinese, Korean and Kampo medicine
  [1].

**Religious and ritual use:**
- **Islam:** in Arabic-speaking regions, jujube and *Z. lotus* are linked
  to the lote-tree (*sidrah*) of the Quran [1].
- **Hinduism:** the name Badrinath comes from *badarī* (jujube tree) and
  *nātha* (lord), an epithet of Vishnu [1].
- **Korean weddings:** in the *pyebaek* ceremony, the groom's parents
  throw dried jujubes and chestnuts for the bride to catch in her skirt.
  Each fruit caught is said to stand for a future child [18].
- **Chinese weddings:** red dates are given with peanuts and longan. The
  name *zao* puns on "early," wishing for early children [18].
- **Judaism:** the *orlah* ban on fruit in a tree's first three years
  applies as for any fruit tree [21].
- **Unconfirmed:** a claim that Korean ancestral-rite offerings place
  jujube first among fruits was not verified.

## Open research questions

- **China:** about 2 million ha and over 8 million t [5], with no
  official year or weight basis. The Hami regional figure gives 0.7 t/ha
  against 7.8-8.8 t/ha in mature trial orchards [6].
- **Chill:** 50-200, under 400, and 775-1,737 hours, or facultative [8].
- **Cold hardiness:** -15 °C [1] against nursery claims near -30 °C [17].
- **Vitamin C:** 69 mg [1] against 200-600 mg/100 g [2].
- **Water use:** 532-592 mm seasonal [7], 828 mm annual [7], and 600-900
  mm optimal quotas [6]. These reflect different climates and bases.
- **Best storage stage:** white-mature [9] against half-red [9].
- **Drainage:** tolerates excess moisture [4], yet needs fair to good
  drainage [4].
- **Witches' broom:** no current national loss estimate [12][13].
- **Not sourced:** labour hours, per-hectare yields for fresh-market
  orchards, photoperiod, controlled environment, salinity in dS/m for
  grafted trees, and dried-fruit storage life.

## Estimates and derivations

**China's average yield.** 8 million t ÷ 2 million ha [5] = about 4 t/ha.
The fresh-or-dried basis is unknown.

**Hami regional yield.** 15,000 t ÷ 21,800 ha [6] = 0.69 t/ha. The trial
orchard gave 7.75-8.78 t/ha [6], about 11-13 times higher.

**Tree density.** 10,000 m² per hectare [6]:
- 2 × 5 m = 10 m² → 1,000 trees/ha;
- 2 × 0.8 m = 1.6 m² → 6,250 trees/ha (the source gives 6,253).

**Yield per tree in Hami.** 7,754-8,783 kg ÷ 1,000 trees [6] = about 7.8-
8.8 kg per tree for 1.65 m dwarf trees. That is far below the 18-45 kg of
a full-size mature tree [2].

**Per-hectare yield from per-tree figures** (an assumption, not a measured
figure). If full-size trees were planted at 400 per hectare (5 × 5 m), 18-
45 kg per tree [2] would give 7.2-18 t/ha. The spacing is assumed.

**Dried-fruit yield.** This corrects the original pass's "two-fifths to
just under half."
- Dry matter: raw fruit has 100 - 77.86 = 22.14 g per 100 g, and dried
  fruit 100 - 19.7 = 80.3 g [1].
- So 100 g of fresh fruit gives 22.14 ÷ 0.803 = about 27.6 g of dried
  fruit, or about 28% of fresh weight.

Fruit left to wrinkle on the tree [2] already weighs less at harvest, so
the ratio from harvested weight would be higher.

**Water.**
- 900 mm = 9,000 m³/ha [6], or about 9 m³ per tree at 1,000 trees/ha.
- The Aksu seasonal use of 532-592 mm [7] is 5,320-5,920 m³/ha.

**Nitrogen per tree (Texas).** 0.2 lb N per inch of trunk diameter [4],
or about 36 g N per 2.5 cm, capped at 1.0 lb (454 g) per tree, reached at
a 5-inch (12.7 cm) trunk.

**Unit conversions.**
- 40-100 lb = 18.1-45.4 kg [2];
- 3 oz = 85 g [2];
- 15-30 ft = 4.6-9.1 m [2];
- 5,595 ft = 1,705 m [2].
