## Naming and scope

*Nephelium ramboutan-ake* (Sapindaceae), under several synonyms used in
older literature. The common name derives from a regional word meaning
"to twist," describing how the fruit is opened with both hands [2]. This
crop's closest relative is a different, considerably more commercially
significant *Nephelium* fruit this project has also researched, from
which many agronomic values here are taken by close analogy given the
shared genus and growing habit.

This entry passes this project's distinctiveness check for a separate
crop row, though narrowly, and more on market identity and eating
experience than on fundamental farming differences. In favor of a
separate entry: at least one national agricultural statistics agency
reports this crop's growing area, production, and yield entirely
separately from its close relative, with national yields documented at
roughly half that relative's figures; the eating experience is
genuinely distinct — a thicker, blunt-tuberculed or short-spined rind
rather than the relative's characteristic hair-like spines, flesh
described in source material as notably sweeter and much more easily
separated from the seed than its relative, and a seed that is itself
eaten (raw, or boiled/roasted into a cocoa-like drink), unlike the
relative's seed; it is also documented as less productive than its
relative and confined to a narrower, more specifically humid lowland
site envelope, with irregular flowering that in some documented
populations occurs only about once every four years. Against a separate
entry: cultivation practice is described in source material as broadly
similar to its close relative (though requiring less growing space per
tree), the same clonal propagation and the same male/hermaphrodite
flowering-biology complication apply to both, and this crop occupies a
much smaller growing area nationally than its relative (well under 5%
of that relative's growing area in at least one country's statistics).
On balance this project treats it as its own minor crop entry, heavily
informed by analogy to its close relative; folding it into that
relative's entry as a cultivar group would be a reasonable alternative if
a leaner catalogue were preferred, at the cost of losing its distinct
seed-based products and separate statistics.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a close relative of a better-known tropical fruit, eaten
for sweeter, easily-separated ("freestone") flesh and an edible seed.**
The fruit is ovoid, several centimeters long, with color documented
somewhat inconsistently across sources as dark or light red, yellow, or
purple-red; the edible aril (flesh) is white to yellowish-white and
roughly a centimeter thick [1][9]. The seed, several centimeters long, is
eaten raw with an almond-like taste, or boiled or roasted into a cocoa-
like drink [1][2]. Seed kernels are documented as holding a very high
proportion of solid white fat, historically used as a lamp fuel in at
least one source region [1][9]. The flesh itself is eaten raw or made
into jam, and is also documented as a canned product in at least one
source [1][9]. Composition of the flesh shows fairly high water content
with modest protein, carbohydrate, and fat content per unit weight [1].

**Finding 2 — national statistics show genuinely low yields, and a
striking year-over-year change in growing area.** One national
agricultural statistics agency's detailed tables show, across two
consecutive years, a harvested-area yield in the low single digits of
tonnes per hectare, with only roughly 60-70% of planted area actually
harvested in either year, and a documented "potential" yield figure
considerably above the actual harvested yield in both years [4][5]. The
same tables show the crop concentrated in a small number of regions
within that country, with a meaningful total production value in both
years. Notably, the total planted area reported fell by more than half
between the two consecutive years in these statistics — this could
reflect either a genuine, fairly dramatic production decline or some
inconsistency in reporting, and is not resolved here. A regional plant-
resources reference work reported no yield-level information at all for
this species, consistent with it being a genuinely minor, under-studied
crop [7].

**Finding 3 — a functionally dioecious tree: hermaphrodite flowers that
behave, in practice, as purely female.** Individual trees carry either
hermaphrodite or purely male (staminate) flowers; in one documented field
study, hermaphrodite trees considerably outnumbered male trees in a
single surveyed population [8]. Critically, the anthers of hermaphrodite
flowers are reported to naturally never open, even though their pollen
remains biologically viable — a built-in mechanical barrier against
self-pollination, making the species obligately cross-pollinating in
practice despite technically having "perfect" flowers [8]. Hermaphrodite
and male flowers differ somewhat in stamen count and size, and male trees
bear more flowers per inflorescence than hermaphrodite trees [8]. Because
seedlings may turn out to be either male or hermaphrodite, growing from
seed is specifically discouraged commercially; grafting is strongly
preferred specifically to guarantee a tree will actually bear fruit [1][2]
— the same male/hermaphrodite complication documented in this crop's
close relative. The species is insect-pollinated [9].

**Finding 4 — a narrow, very humid lowland site envelope, with flowering
triggered by a preceding dry season.** Source material places this
species in a fairly narrow elevation band, in "very humid regions," with
soil that reportedly "must be constantly moist," yet the tree is also
specifically reported to bear best after a long, preceding dry season —
a combination of requirements rather than a simple constant-moisture
preference [1]. Its native range spans a broad swath of tropical South
and Southeast Asia, typically found in rainforest along riverbanks and
slopes at generally low elevation, occasionally found much higher [9].
Wild populations are documented as relatively infrequent in at least one
source region's lowland forests but notably abundant in another region
[1]. The species has been introduced to the Caribbean and Central America
in the early-to-mid 20th century, with at least one Central American
country growing it on a small scale with some fruit sold commercially
[2]. Hydrocyanic acid has been detected in the bark and leaves [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial evergreen tropical tree, generally
  kept under 10 m in cultivation though capable of growing considerably
  taller in the wild, with substantial buttress roots in old wild trees.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single-stage harvest, borne irregularly following
  a defined dry season; flowering frequency can be quite infrequent in at
  least one documented regional population (Finding 2, Finding 4).
- **Climate:** Tropical, very humid lowland; a defined but not excessive
  dry season specifically improves flowering and bearing (Finding 4).
- **Composition:** Moderate carbohydrate content with low protein and fat
  content in the edible flesh; the seed itself is separately edible and
  rich in solid fat (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Insect-pollinated; functionally dioecious despite
  nominally hermaphrodite flowers, due to a built-in barrier against
  self-pollination (Finding 3).
- **Yield:** Documented national yields are low, roughly half of this
  crop's close relative's yields in the same national statistics, with a
  "potential" yield figure well above what is actually realized in
  practice (Finding 2).
- **Water use:** No dedicated crop-water-use study was found; water-use
  parameters here are estimated entirely by analogy to this crop's close
  relative, given the shared genus, shared humid-tropical habitat, and
  shared dry-spell flowering trigger. If anything, this crop may be less
  drought-tolerant than its relative, given the specific "constantly
  moist soil" requirement during fruit growth documented in source
  material.
- **Propagation:** Primarily budded/grafted trees, since seedlings may
  turn out male or hermaphrodite and growing from seed is discouraged
  commercially; bud grafting, approach grafting, marcotting (air-
  layering), and direct seeding are all documented as viable propagation
  methods, with budding traditionally timed to the rainy season. This
  species is also itself documented as a rootstock option for its close
  commercial relative.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour study was found; labor figures here
  are estimated entirely by analogy to this crop's close relative, scaled
  down somewhat to reflect this crop's lower documented yield and
  generally smaller tree size in cultivation.
- **Soil:** Well-drained soil that must nonetheless stay constantly
  moist; no dedicated soil-chemistry or nutrient-uptake study was found,
  so these figures are estimated by analogy and by simple derivation from
  the fruit's own composition.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees begin bearing within several years of
  planting; productive lifespan was not researched.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not specifically researched; this crop's
  thicker, non-spined rind likely changes its susceptibility to at least
  one disorder well documented in its close relative, though this is an
  inference rather than a sourced finding.
- **Cultivars:** No named commercial cultivars were found; documented
  variation is limited to fruit color (red versus yellow forms) and a
  broader regional gene pool of wild types in one source region
  described as particularly abundant and diverse.
- **Harvest:** Single-stage harvest of ripe fruit.
- **Storage:** A notably short fresh shelf life — only a few days at room
  temperature, about a week under refrigeration, per lower-authority
  source material; no dedicated postharvest-science study was found for
  this species specifically.

## Hazards

- As with other Sapindaceae fruit, this crop is presumed to carry a risk
  related to a specific hypoglycemia-inducing toxin class associated with
  the family, though this was not independently measured for this species
  specifically, and the seed is documented as eaten raw in at least one
  source. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this Sapindaceae-
  family toxin hazard is deferred until this project has a place for this
  kind of plant-toxin health/safety data, shared with this crop's close
  relative and other Sapindaceae fruit this project has researched.
- Hydrocyanic acid has been detected in the bark and leaves (not the
  edible portions); no documented livestock-poisoning record was found
  for this specific hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** whole fruit → twisted open by hand → separated into
  freestone flesh and seed. Flesh → eaten fresh, made into jam, or
  canned. Seed → eaten raw, or boiled/roasted into a cocoa-like drink; or
  → dried and pressed into a high-fat kernel product historically used as
  lamp fuel. Seedlings → grown on as rootstocks for this crop and its
  close commercial relative; budded trees are propagated from confirmed
  hermaphrodite-flowering parent trees.
- Not covered in detail by this research: canned-product processing
  detail; rind-dye use; leaf and root medicinal use.

## Open research questions

- The near-halving of national planted area between two consecutive years
  in the available statistics is not resolved — it may reflect a genuine
  production decline or a reporting inconsistency.
- Fruit size and color are given somewhat inconsistently across sources
  (a several-centimeter range with some disagreement on typical color).
- Whether minor relatives of this genus propagate mainly by seed (per one
  regional reference work) or whether seed propagation is specifically
  discouraged (per another source) is not fully reconciled — plausibly
  both are locally true depending on whether rootstock or fruiting-tree
  production is meant.
- Elevation range is given somewhat inconsistently across sources (a
  narrower cultivated range versus a wider range including occasional
  much-higher-elevation wild occurrences).
- Water-use, rooting depth, soil pH tolerance, and most labor figures are
  carried over entirely by analogy from this crop's close relative, since
  no dedicated study exists for this species; this is the weakest part of
  this research.
- Yield figures for the "exceptional" and "poor" tiers are rough estimates
  rather than sourced figures.
- Storage behavior is sourced only from lower-authority, non-scientific
  material; no dedicated postharvest-science study was found.
- A detailed treatment of this crop in the regional plant-resources
  reference work used elsewhere in this research was only available at
  snippet level, not read in full.
- Production in at least two other countries where this species is known
  to be grown was not researched beyond passing mentions.
- The specific claim that flowering may occur only about once every four
  years in at least one regional population was not investigated in
  further detail.
- Levels of the Sapindaceae-family hypoglycemia-associated toxin
  specifically in this species' seed were not measured in any source
  found.
- Whether the bark/leaf cyanogenic-compound hazard poses any practical
  livestock-feeding risk was not researched; no record of livestock
  poisoning was found.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric content (roughly 570 kcal/kg) and nitrogen-uptake-per-tonne
(roughly 1.3 kg/t) are derived directly from one source's detailed
composition figures for flesh (carbohydrate, protein, and fat content
converted to energy using standard conversion factors, and protein
content converted to nitrogen using a standard protein-to-nitrogen
ratio).

Yield tiers are derived mainly from the two-year average of one national
statistics agency's harvested-area yield figures for the "average" tier,
that same agency's documented "potential" yield figure for the "good"
tier, and judgment-based estimates for the "exceptional" tier (set near
this crop's close relative's national yield figure in the same
statistics) and the "poor" tier (reflecting a low-yielding or non-
flowering year, given this crop's documented irregular bearing).

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, rooting depth, soil-
moisture-depletion allowance, drought-buffer days, yield-penalty rate,
atmospheric response curve) are carried over in full by analogy from this
crop's close commercial relative, given the shared genus, humid-tropical
habitat, and dry-spell flowering trigger, adjusted only qualitatively
toward lower drought tolerance given this crop's specific "constantly
moist soil" requirement.

Seed-viability figures (very short viable life under moist storage,
shorter still at ambient conditions, essentially none under dry storage)
are likewise carried over by analogy from this crop's close relative and
from the broader soapberry family's generally short-lived, recalcitrant
seed behavior.

Labor-hour figures, harvest-window length, orchard ramp-up time, and
on-tree standing/quality-decay figures are all estimated by scaling down
this crop's close relative's documented labor-hour figures to reflect
this crop's lower documented yield and smaller cultivated tree size,
treated as rough, wide-range estimates rather than sourced figures.
