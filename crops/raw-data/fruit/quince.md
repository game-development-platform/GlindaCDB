## Naming and scope

*Cydonia oblonga* (Rosaceae, subtribe Malinae) is the sole species of its
genus [1][2]. This is explicitly distinct from the unrelated "flowering"
or Japanese quinces (genus *Chaenomeles*, separately researched as its own
distinct crop for this project after being found genuinely distinct) and
from the Chinese quince (a separate genus, not covered by this research)
[1]. A small number of intergeneric hybrids exist — a quince-pear hybrid
used mainly as a seedless virus-indicator curiosity, and a quince-apple
hybrid tested experimentally as a rootstock — these are noted but not
treated as separate crops [2].

This species passes this project's distinctiveness check without
question, as its own genus. It is closely related to a pome fruit this
project has also researched, sharing the same taxonomic subtribe, and is
in fact that fruit's classic dwarfing rootstock (Finding 1).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a fruit almost always cooked rather than eaten raw, and a
genuinely dual-purpose crop: fruit production and a rootstock industry
for a different fruit tree.** Raw fruit is hard and essentially
unpalatable, requiring cooking for virtually all culinary uses [1][2].
Properly ripened fruit goes chiefly into jellies, marmalades, and
preserves [3]; long cooking with sugar turns the flesh a distinctive red,
and a firm cooked paste is a culinary staple in parts of Southern Europe
and Latin America [1][2]. In several Western countries historically, this
species has actually been grown mainly as a dwarfing rootstock for a
related pome fruit tree rather than for its own fruit — a practice dating
back centuries in at least one European region, with clonal propagation
in dedicated stool beds established by the early 17th century [2]; a
long-standing commercial storage reference work calls rootstock use this
crop's main commercial purpose [3]. This creates a genuinely distinctive
dual cultivation path: fruit production for processing, versus a
stool-bed operation producing a propagation product consumed by an
entirely different crop (the pome fruit it serves as rootstock for) —
one of relatively few crops this project has researched with that
"rootstock product for another crop" pattern.

**Finding 2 — climacteric fruit that is deliberately picked under-ripe,
stored, then ripened off the plant.** This fruit is climacteric and
specifically needs a dedicated warm ripening process after storage to
reach proper processing quality [3]. Harvest timing is judged by a
specific ground-color change (from deep to light green); fruit picked too
green scalds in storage, while fruit affected by a specific scab disease
does not store well regardless of timing [3]. Storage conditions are
similar to early-season apples — a few months just above freezing at high
humidity — with no controlled-atmosphere benefit documented and no
commercial precooling practice found [3]. Despite its notable hardness,
the fruit bruises easily and needs gentle handling [3]. Ripening fruit
gives off a strong scent and ethylene, so it is specifically stored away
from other pome fruit to avoid off-flavors and accelerated ripening in
those other fruit [11].

**Finding 3 — a warm-climate pome fruit whose commercial range is
substantially limited by a severe bacterial disease.** This species is
native to a region south of the Caspian Sea and the Trans-Caucasus,
adapted to hot, dry climates and acid soils, with most commercial
production concentrated in very warm growing regions [1][2]. Its winter
chill requirement is low. Critically, "the genus is one of the most
susceptible to fire blight in its broader plant family" — a severe
bacterial disease that sharply limits commercial viability in regions
with warm, humid summers; one Eastern European country's quince industry
was effectively devastated by this disease for two decades before
resistant hybrid selections bred onto a specific rootstock dramatically
reduced infection rates [2][9]. Rain falling near ripening cracks the
fruit, severely in at least one named cultivar [2]; and in cold northern
climates, many cultivars simply fail to ripen before winter sets in [2].

**Finding 4 — self-fertile, solitary, later-blooming flowers than a
closely related pome fruit, giving somewhat lower frost risk.** All
known varieties are self-pollinating, though cross-pollination has been
shown to improve yields [1][2]. Flowers are solitary, borne singly on
current-season growth, and bloom later in spring than a closely related
pome fruit this project has researched — reducing (though not
eliminating) spring frost risk relative to that crop [2]. Blossom damage
has still been documented near a specific cold threshold [11].

**Finding 5 — production is concentrated in a small number of countries,
with a sizeable spread between older and more recent global figures.** A
recent year's global production total is dominated by one Middle Eastern/
Mediterranean country, followed by several Central and South/East Asian
and North African producers [1]. An earlier global estimate (roughly 15
years prior) gives a considerably smaller total growing area and
production figure, with essentially negligible production in at least
one major Western country, where the crop persists mainly as a small
specialty planting [2].

**Finding 6 — cyanogenic seeds, alongside a historically useful seed
mucilage.** Seeds contain nitrile compounds that release hydrogen cyanide,
toxic only if consumed in substantial quantity [1]; separately, whole
soaked seeds release a mucilage historically used as a traditional cough
remedy and wound dressing in at least one regional folk-medicine
tradition [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous small tree or multi-stemmed
  shrub.
- **Climate:** Warm-temperate, Mediterranean, semi-arid, and temperate
  climates, with a relatively low winter chill requirement; substantially
  constrained in practice by susceptibility to a severe bacterial disease
  in warm, humid-summer regions (Finding 3).
- **Composition:** Moderate calorie content with low protein per unit
  weight; essentially never eaten raw in most cultivars, requiring
  cooking (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Self-fertile (all known varieties), though insect
  cross-pollination improves yield (Finding 4).
- **Yield:** A very wide range from an older global-average figure up to
  considerably higher figures documented in modern intensive national and
  trial plantings (Finding 5; see Estimates below for adopted tier
  values).
- **Water use:** No dedicated crop-coefficient table exists for this
  species specifically; water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to
  a standard international reference's combined crop-coefficient category
  for several related deciduous pome and stone fruit, given the shared
  subtribe and growth habit. Rooting depth and drought-buffer period are
  likewise derived from that same combined-category reference. Excess
  water near ripening causes fruit cracking, and overly lush,
  heavily-watered or over-fertilized growth is specifically noted to
  favor the fire-blight disease described in Finding 3.
- **Propagation:** Vegetative only — hardwood or softwood cuttings,
  layering (including dedicated stool beds for rootstock-clone
  production), or grafting onto quince rootstock; seedling-grown trees
  are historically documented for at least one old American cultivar, but
  this is not standard commercial practice. Grafting onto a related pome
  fruit's own roots is specifically documented as unreliable, a one-way
  compatibility relationship. Several named clonal rootstock selections
  exist specifically for use as a dwarfing rootstock in the related pome
  fruit's orchards, differing in vigor reduction, drought/chalk
  tolerance, and frost sensitivity; some scion varieties of that related
  fruit are directly incompatible with these rootstocks and require an
  interstem graft.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour cost study was found for this crop;
  all labor figures here are rough estimates. Harvest requires careful,
  gentle hand-picking given the fruit's tendency to bruise despite its
  hardness.
- **Soil:** Adapted to acid soils; high-pH soils cause stunting and iron
  chlorosis. No dedicated nutrient-uptake study was found.
- **Stand life:** First fruit typically within a few years of planting;
  productive lifespan was not well researched, though it is understood to
  be a long-lived orchard tree. Young trees sucker profusely and must be
  trained to a single stem. Decline is driven mainly by the fire-blight
  disease described in Finding 3, plus a defoliating leaf-and-fruit-spot
  disease; the tree bears on the tips of current-season shoots rather
  than on persistent fruiting spurs.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fire blight is the single most limiting
  disease (Finding 3); a separate leaf-and-fruit-spot disease causes
  defoliation; a specific rust disease requires a nearby juniper host
  plant to complete its life cycle, creating a genuine "avoid planting
  near junipers" site consideration; additional documented pests include
  powdery mildew, a blossom-blight brown rot, aphids, scale insects,
  mealybugs, leafrollers, and codling moth.
- **Cultivars:** A meaningful spread of named cultivars from California,
  Turkey, the Balkans, Armenia, and historic American breeding, varying
  in chill requirement, vigor, yield, and fruit characteristics; most are
  specifically bred or selected for processing and preserving use rather
  than fresh eating.
- **Harvest:** Single autumn harvest, timed deliberately before the first
  frost and based on a specific ground-color ripeness cue (Finding 2).
- **Storage:** A moderate (roughly two-to-three-month) storage window
  under cool, humid conditions, followed by a deliberate off-plant
  ripening step at room temperature before the fruit is cooked or
  processed (Finding 2).

## Hazards

- Seeds contain nitrile compounds that release hydrogen cyanide; toxic
  only in substantial quantity, and the edible flesh itself is not a
  hazard. Seeds are typically removed (cored out) before the fruit is
  processed into paste or jelly. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up
  of this seed-cyanide hazard is deferred until this project has a place
  for this kind of plant-toxin health/safety data, shared with other
  stone/pome fruit this project has researched with a similar seed-
  cyanide profile.
- Fire blight is a severe bacterial plant disease to which this species is
  highly susceptible; this is a crop-health/regulatory consideration
  rather than a human-safety hazard, but it is severe enough to be
  effectively limiting in some growing regions.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit → sorted → cold-stored for a few months → ripened at
  room temperature. Ripened fruit → cored (removing the cyanogenic seeds)
  → cooked with a substantial proportion of sugar into a firm, deep-red
  paste. Fruit → simmered or pressed → juice → jelly. Fruit → fermented
  mash → distilled into a traditional fruit brandy in at least one
  regional tradition. Cores/seeds → dried for the traditional mucilage
  remedy described in Finding 6. Separately, in dedicated stool beds:
  rooted shoots → harvested as rootstock propagation material for a
  related pome fruit; prunings → further propagated as cuttings.
- Not covered in detail by this research: canned quince product; quince
  cider and wine; the grafting process for the related pome fruit itself
  (covered under that crop's own research); pectin extraction; broader
  folk-medicine uses beyond the seed mucilage noted above.

## Open research questions

- Winter chill-hour requirement figures conflict between a specific,
  well-documented figure for two named cultivars and a much wider range
  given in lower-authority source material — not reconciled.
- World growing area and average yield are only well-documented for an
  older reference period; a considerably higher recent global production
  figure has no matching area figure, so a current world-average yield
  cannot be directly derived, and the adopted world-average figure likely
  understates modern intensive-orchard yields considerably.
- Labor-hour figures for every operation are rough estimates, since no
  dedicated cost study was found for this crop.
- Most water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to a combined
  reference category for several related deciduous fruit trees, since no
  species-specific crop-coefficient study was found.
- Soil nutrient-uptake figures were not found in any source.
- Whether formal cross-border plant-health regulations specifically list
  this species as a regulated fire-blight host was not checked in this
  pass.
- Productive lifespan was not well researched beyond a general
  understanding that it is a long-lived orchard tree.
- A separate East Asian pome fruit sometimes also called "quince" in
  translation, used for juice production in at least one world region,
  remains a candidate for possibly being treated as its own, separate
  crop entry; it has not yet been researched in detail by this project.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-timeline estimates (bloom-to-first-harvest span, days to first
meaningful harvest, and ramp-up to full bearing) are derived from
documented first-fruiting ages of a few years after planting or grafting,
with full bearing assumed to follow several years after that based on
typical deciduous-orchard-tree patterns rather than a dedicated source.

World-average and poor-tier yield figures are derived from an older
global production-and-area estimate (roughly 15 years prior to this
research), divided to give a world-average per-hectare figure, with the
poor tier estimated from a low-end documented per-tree yield figure
converted to a per-hectare basis using an assumed traditional planting
density; this is acknowledged to likely understate yields in more modern,
intensively managed orchards, where considerably higher national and
trial-plot yields are documented.

Seasonal water-use totals, crop-coefficient-days, and the associated
drought-buffer period are derived from a standard international crop-
water-use reference's combined category for several related deciduous
pome and stone fruit (the closest available analogy, since no dedicated
quince crop-coefficient row exists), using a specific regional stage-
length example and standard soil-moisture-holding assumptions.

Trees-per-hectare planting density is derived two ways: from a modern
cultivar-trial's documented yield-per-tree and yield-per-hectare figures
(implying a fairly dense modern planting), and separately assumed for
traditional wider-spaced orchards, with both figures retained since
planting density varies considerably by production system.

Labor-hour figures across all operations are rough, wide-range estimates
with no underlying cost study, including harvest labor, which is derived
from an assumed hand-picking rate appropriate to a large, carefully
handled fruit.

Soil pH-tolerance figures are estimated within the sourced "adapted to
acid soils, chlorotic on high pH" qualitative range, since no formal
tolerance-band study was found.

The yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived from the adopted
"good" yield tier divided by an assumed harvest-window length; on-tree
standing-quality decay rate is a placeholder estimate, since the fruit's
notable hardness suggests it holds well on the tree until frost but no
dedicated figure was found.
