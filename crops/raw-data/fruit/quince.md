## Naming and scope

*Cydonia oblonga* (Rosaceae, subtribe Malinae) is the sole species of its
genus [1][2]. It is distinct from the "flowering" or Japanese quinces
(genus *Chaenomeles*, researched separately for this project as
`japanese_quince` after being found genuinely distinct) and from the
Chinese quince (*Pseudocydonia sinensis*, a separate monotypic genus) [1].
A small number of intergeneric hybrids exist — a quince × pear hybrid
(×*Pyronia veitchii*) used mainly as a seedless virus-indicator curiosity,
and a quince × apple hybrid tested experimentally as a rootstock — noted
but not treated as separate crops [2].

This species passes the distinctiveness check without question, as its own
genus. It is closely related to **pear** (*Pyrus communis*), which this
project has researched (`pear`), sharing the same taxonomic subtribe, and is
in fact pear's classic dwarfing rootstock (Finding 1); it is also used as a
rootstock for **medlar** (*Mespilus germanica*, cross-linked `medlar`)
[15].

**Candidate for its own row — Chinese quince (*Pseudocydonia sinensis*).**
A separate East Asian pome fruit also called "quince" in translation, grown
for juice, "quince tea" (Korean *mogwa-cha*) and traditional medicine. It is
a different genus from *Cydonia* and clearly passes the distinctiveness
check, so it is added to the status table as `PROPOSED` (`chinese_quince`)
with a cross-link back to this row; it has not yet been researched in detail
[1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a fruit almost always cooked rather than eaten raw, and a
genuinely dual-purpose crop: fruit production and a rootstock industry for
pear and medlar.** Raw fruit is hard and essentially unpalatable, requiring
cooking for virtually all culinary uses [1][2]. Properly ripened fruit goes
chiefly into jellies, marmalades and preserves [3]; long cooking with sugar
turns the flesh a distinctive red, and a firm cooked paste — Spanish
*membrillo* / *dulce de membrillo*, "quince cheese" — is a culinary staple
in Spain, Portugal and Latin America (notably Argentina) [1][2][13]. In
several Western countries this species has historically been grown mainly as
a dwarfing rootstock for pear rather than for its own fruit — a practice
dating back centuries in parts of Western Europe, with clonal propagation in
dedicated stool beds established by the early 17th century [2]; a
long-standing commercial storage reference calls rootstock use this crop's
main commercial purpose [3]. This creates a genuinely distinctive dual
cultivation path: fruit production for processing, versus a stool-bed
operation producing a propagation product consumed by entirely different
crops (the pear and medlar trees it serves as rootstock for) — one of
relatively few crops this project has researched with that "rootstock
product for another crop" pattern [15].

**Finding 2 — climacteric fruit that is deliberately picked under-ripe,
stored, then ripened off the plant.** The fruit is climacteric and
specifically needs a dedicated warm ripening process after storage to reach
proper processing quality [3]. Harvest timing is judged by a ground-colour
change (from deep to light green); fruit picked too green scalds in storage,
while fruit affected by scab does not store well regardless of timing [3].
Storage conditions are similar to early-season apples — a few months just
above freezing at high humidity — with no controlled-atmosphere benefit
documented and no commercial precooling practice found [3]. Despite its
notable hardness, the fruit bruises easily and needs gentle handling [3].
Ripening fruit gives off a strong scent and ethylene, so it is stored away
from other pome fruit (pears, apples) to avoid tainting them and
accelerating their ripening [11].

**Finding 3 — a warm-climate pome fruit whose commercial range is
substantially limited by fire blight.** This species is native to the region
south of the Caspian Sea and the Trans-Caucasus, adapted to hot, dry
climates and acid soils, with most commercial production concentrated in
very warm growing regions [1][2]. Its winter chill requirement is low
(roughly 100-450 hours below 7 °C by cultivar; see Agronomic data) [16].
Critically, "the genus is one of the most susceptible to fire blight in its
broader plant family" (*Erwinia amylovora*) — a severe bacterial disease
that sharply limits commercial viability in regions with warm, humid summers
and is the single most important disease of the crop [2][18]. It is a
regulated quarantine pathogen (an EPPO A2-listed pest) with quince among its
primary hosts; in Serbia, for example, *E. amylovora* was confirmed on pear
and quince in 1989, later spreading to apple, hawthorn, medlar and *Sorbus*
[18]. Resistant breeding is the main line of defence: inheritance of fire-
blight resistance is recessive, and a resistant male parent transfers it
more effectively; one hybridisation study produced 223 hybrids with a
majority-resistant F1 in the best crosses [18]. One Eastern European
country's quince industry was reported devastated by the disease for two
decades before resistant hybrid selections on a specific rootstock sharply
cut infection rates [2][9]. Rain near ripening cracks the fruit, severely in
at least one named cultivar [2]; in cold northern climates many cultivars
simply fail to ripen before winter [2].

**Finding 4 — self-fertile, solitary, later-blooming flowers than pear,
giving somewhat lower frost risk.** All known varieties are self-pollinating,
though cross-pollination has been shown to improve yields [1][2]. Flowers are
solitary, borne singly on current-season growth, and bloom later in spring
than pear — reducing (though not eliminating) spring frost risk relative to
pear [2]. Blossom damage has still been documented near a specific cold
threshold [11].

**Finding 5 — production is concentrated in a few countries, led by
Turkey.** 2023 world production was about 687,000 t, led by Turkey (~192,000
t), then China (~112,000 t), Uzbekistan (~96,000 t) and Iran (~90,000 t),
with Morocco fifth (~44,000 t); Turkey and China together make up roughly
44% [14]. An older global estimate (roughly 15 years before the 2026
research) gives a considerably smaller total area and production, with
essentially negligible production in the United States and the United
Kingdom, where the crop persists mainly as a small specialty planting [2].

**Finding 6 — cyanogenic seeds, alongside a historically useful seed
mucilage.** Seeds contain nitrile compounds that release hydrogen cyanide,
toxic only if consumed in substantial quantity [1]; separately, whole soaked
seeds release a mucilage historically used as a traditional cough remedy and
wound dressing in at least one regional folk-medicine tradition, and of
current interest as a food-coating and scaffold material [10].

**Finding 7 — clonal-only propagation makes virus carry-over the key
sanitation problem, managed by certification and heat/cryotherapy.** Because
quince does not come true and is propagated only vegetatively (cuttings,
layering/stool beds, or tissue culture), latent viruses pass down the clone
and accumulate unless the stock starts clean [2][15][18]. The viruses tested
in fruit-tree certification for *Cydonia* are apple chlorotic leaf spot virus
(ACLSV), apple stem grooving virus (ASGV) and apple stem pitting virus
(ASPV); ACLSV in particular spreads only through grafting and propagation —
no insect vector, seed or pollen transmission is known — so the control is
simply to propagate only from virus-tested material [18]. Certification
programmes index *Cydonia* alongside *Malus*, *Pyrus* and *Chaenomeles*, and
latent viruses can be eradicated from quince rootstock (e.g. 'Quince A') by
thermotherapy followed by shoot-tip cryotherapy [18]. This is the
clonal/viral-degeneration risk the current checklist calls out for
vegetatively propagated crops.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous small tree or multi-stemmed
  shrub; a single autumn fruit harvest [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single end-of-season harvest; fruit bears on the tips
  of current-season shoots rather than on persistent spurs, and its hardness
  lets it hold on the tree until frost (Finding 2; Harvest stages) [2][11].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** First fruit typically within a few years
  of planting or grafting; full bearing assumed several years later (see
  Estimates) [1][2].
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate, Mediterranean, semi-arid and temperate
  climates, roughly hardiness zones 5-9, with a **low winter chill
  requirement** of about 100-450 hours below 7 °C by cultivar (named
  cultivars 'Orange', 'Pineapple' and 'Smyrna' around 300 hours) [16].
  Native to the hills south of the Caspian Sea and the Trans-Caucasus; no
  distinctive high- or low-elevation band was sourced. Commercial range is
  constrained less by climate than by fire blight in warm, humid-summer
  regions (Finding 3) [2][18].
- **Composition:** Moderate calorie content with low protein per unit
  weight; essentially never eaten raw in most cultivars, requiring cooking
  (Finding 1) [1].
- **Edibility:** Cooked only in virtually all cultivars — raw flesh is hard,
  astringent and unpalatable; cooking develops aroma and (with long cooking)
  a red colour [1][2].
- **Pollination:** Self-fertile (all known varieties), though insect
  cross-pollination improves yield (Finding 4) [1][2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced as a factor; a temperate
  deciduous tree flowering on current-season growth, with no documented
  day-length requirement for flowering, and not applicable to
  tuberisation/bulbing.
- **Yield:** A wide range from an older global-average figure up to
  considerably higher figures in modern intensive national and trial
  plantings (Finding 5; see Estimates for adopted tiers) [2][6][11][12].
- **Water use:** No dedicated crop-coefficient table exists for this species;
  water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to a standard international
  reference's combined crop-coefficient category for several related
  deciduous pome and stone fruit, given the shared subtribe and growth habit;
  rooting depth and drought-buffer period are derived from the same combined
  category [4][5]. Excess water near ripening causes fruit cracking, and
  overly lush, heavily-watered or over-fertilised growth favours fire blight
  (Finding 3) [2][11].
- **Propagation:** Vegetative only — hardwood or softwood cuttings, layering
  (including dedicated stool beds for rootstock-clone production), or grafting
  onto quince rootstock; seedling-grown trees are historically documented for
  an old American cultivar but are not standard [2]. Quince does not come
  true from seed and carries latent viruses down the clone, so certified
  virus-tested stock (ACLSV/ASGV/ASPV) and, where needed, heat/cryotherapy
  cleanup are used (Finding 7) [18]. **Role as rootstock for other crops:**
  quince is the most widespread dwarfing rootstock for pear and is also used
  for medlar; named clones include Quince A and the most-dwarfing Quince C
  (East Malling selections), the more vigorous and more cold-tolerant BA29
  (Provence), and Sydo, Eline and Adams, differing in vigour reduction,
  drought/chalk tolerance and frost sensitivity [7][15]. **Graft
  compatibility:** many pear scions (notably 'Bartlett'/'Williams' and 'Bosc')
  are incompatible with quince and require an interstem bridge — usually
  'Beurré Hardy' or 'Doyenné du Comice'; conversely, grafting pear onto its
  own roots and using pear rootstocks for quince both work poorly [2][7][15].
- **Labor:** No dedicated labour-hour cost study was found; all labour figures
  are rough estimates. Harvest needs careful, gentle hand-picking given the
  fruit's tendency to bruise despite its hardness [3].
- **Soil:** Adapted to acid soils; high-pH/chalky/alkaline soils cause
  stunting and iron chlorosis, and quince rootstocks are specifically
  unsuited to chalk [2][15]. **Salinity:** moderately salt-sensitive (pear is
  ranked among salt-sensitive crops, and USU rates quince salt tolerance
  "medium"); the practical concern on quince rootstock is that chloride can
  be carried to the scion and cause late-season leaf necrosis, with
  Na/Cl-exclusion ability differing by quince genotype — growth of BA29 and
  EMA quince declined as salinity rose from 0.75 to 4 dS/m [17]. No dedicated
  nutrient-uptake study was found.
- **Storage (postharvest):** A moderate (roughly two-to-three-month) window
  under cool, humid conditions just above freezing, followed by a deliberate
  off-plant ripening step at room temperature before cooking; no commercial
  precooling or controlled-atmosphere benefit documented (Finding 2) [3].
  Extremely scenty and ethylene-producing, so stored apart from other pome
  fruit (Finding 2) [11].
- **Establishment:** Planted as grafted trees (for fruit) or rooted
  stool-bed shoots (rootstock); young trees sucker profusely and must be
  trained to a single stem [2].
- **Stand life:** A long-lived orchard tree; first fruit within a few years,
  productive lifespan not well quantified. Decline is driven mainly by fire
  blight (Finding 3) plus a defoliating leaf-and-fruit-spot disease; bears on
  current-season shoot tips rather than persistent spurs [2][11].
- **Cultivars:** A meaningful spread of named cultivars from California,
  Turkey, the Balkans, Armenia and historic American breeding, varying in
  chill requirement, vigour, yield and fruit characteristics; most are
  selected for processing and preserving rather than fresh eating [2][8][11].
- **Weather response:** Frost-tender blossom (damage near a specific cold
  threshold) but later-blooming than pear, lowering frost risk; heat- and
  drought-adapted (native to hot, dry regions); waterlogging- and excess-
  nitrogen-prone to worse fire blight; rain near ripening cracks the fruit,
  severely in a named cultivar; cold-climate ripening failure in many
  cultivars (Findings 3, 4) [2][11].
- **Water regime:** Ordinary irrigation or rain-fed; no flooded/paddy
  requirement [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One autumn harvest, timed before the first
  frost on a ground-colour ripeness cue; the hard fruit can hold on the tree
  until frost (Finding 2) [3][11].
- **Soil biology:** A specific rust (*Gymnosporangium*, cedar/juniper-quince
  rust) requires a nearby juniper host to complete its life cycle, creating a
  genuine "avoid planting near junipers" site rule; fire blight and a
  defoliating leaf-and-fruit-spot disease are the main limits; no mycorrhizal
  or allelopathy finding sourced [2].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to the south-Caspian/Trans-Caucasus
  region; no invasive-species listing was found, and it persists mainly as a
  dooryard or specialty tree outside its range [1][2]. Its main biosecurity
  weight is as a **regulated host of fire blight** (*E. amylovora*, EPPO A2),
  so movement of quince planting material is restricted or certified in some
  jurisdictions (Finding 3) [18]. *Legal restrictions:* beyond fire-blight
  quarantine of host material, none specific found.
- **Controlled environment:** Tissue culture (micropropagation) is used to
  bulk clean clonal stock and, with thermotherapy/cryotherapy, to eradicate
  latent viruses; no greenhouse fruit-production system was sourced (Finding
  7) [18].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fire blight is the single most limiting disease
  (Finding 3); a leaf-and-fruit-spot disease causes defoliation; the
  juniper-dependent rust above; plus powdery mildew, blossom-blight brown
  rot, aphids, scale insects, mealybugs, leafrollers and codling moth [2].

## Hazards

- Seeds contain nitrile compounds that release hydrogen cyanide; toxic only
  in substantial quantity, and the edible flesh itself is not a hazard. Seeds
  are cored out before the fruit is processed into paste or jelly. **TO BE
  ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this seed-cyanide hazard is deferred
  until this project has a place for plant-toxin health/safety data shared
  with other stone/pome fruit with a similar seed-cyanide profile [1].
- Fire blight is a severe bacterial plant disease to which this species is
  highly susceptible and a regulated quarantine pathogen (EPPO A2); this is a
  crop-health/regulatory consideration rather than a human-safety hazard, but
  is severe enough to be effectively limiting in some growing regions, and
  restricts movement of host planting material (Finding 3) [2][18].

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit → sorted → cold-stored for a few months → ripened at room
  temperature. Ripened fruit → cored (removing the cyanogenic seeds) → cooked
  with a substantial proportion of sugar into a firm, deep-red paste
  (*membrillo* / *dulce de membrillo*, "quince cheese") [13]. Fruit →
  simmered or pressed → juice → jelly/marmalade. Fruit → fermented mash →
  distilled into quince brandy/*rakia* in Balkan and Caucasus traditions.
  Seeds → soaked for the traditional mucilage remedy (Finding 6) [10].
  Separately, in dedicated stool beds: rooted shoots → harvested as rootstock
  propagation material for pear and medlar; prunings → further propagated as
  cuttings [2][15].
- **Not covered in detail:** canned quince; quince cider and wine; the
  grafting process for pear itself (covered under `pear`); pectin extraction;
  broader folk-medicine uses beyond the seed mucilage.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-timeline estimates (bloom-to-first-harvest span, days to first
meaningful harvest, and ramp-up to full bearing) are derived from documented
first-fruiting ages of a few years after planting or grafting, with full
bearing assumed to follow several years later based on typical deciduous-
orchard-tree patterns rather than a dedicated source.

World-average and poor-tier yield figures are derived from an older global
production-and-area estimate (roughly 15 years before the 2026 research),
divided to give a world-average per-hectare figure, with the poor tier
estimated from a low-end documented per-tree yield converted to a per-hectare
basis using an assumed traditional planting density; this likely understates
yields in modern intensive orchards, where considerably higher national and
trial-plot yields are documented [2][6][11][12].

Seasonal water-use totals, crop-coefficient-days and the associated drought-
buffer period are derived from a standard international crop-water-use
reference's combined category for several related deciduous pome and stone
fruit (the closest available analogy, since no dedicated quince row exists),
using a regional stage-length example and standard soil-moisture-holding
assumptions [4][5].

Trees-per-hectare planting density is derived two ways: from a modern
cultivar trial's documented yield-per-tree and yield-per-hectare figures
(implying a fairly dense modern planting), and separately assumed for
traditional wider-spaced orchards, with both retained since density varies by
production system [6][12].

Labour-hour figures across all operations are rough, wide-range estimates
with no underlying cost study, including harvest, derived from an assumed
hand-picking rate for a large, carefully handled fruit.

Soil pH-tolerance figures are estimated within the sourced "adapted to acid
soils, chlorotic on high pH" range; the salinity rating is taken as
"moderately sensitive / medium" from the genotype-level and tree-database
sources, since no field tolerance-band study was found [17].

The yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived from the adopted
"good" yield tier divided by an assumed harvest-window length; on-tree
standing-quality decay rate is a placeholder estimate, since the fruit's
hardness suggests it holds well on the tree until frost but no dedicated
figure was found.
