## Naming and scope

*Passiflora quadrangularis* (Passifloraceae), known by many regional
names across Latin America and Southeast Asia [1][2][4]. The common name
"granadilla" alone is ambiguous — it refers to a different *Passiflora*
species in the Andes and to the purple passionfruit in South Africa — so
this entry is scoped specifically to *P. quadrangularis*, the giant
granadilla.

One entry covers the whole species. The large-fruited *macrocarpa* form
"hybridizes readily with smaller strains and there are intermediate
types" [1], so it is treated here as a cultivar group rather than a
separate entry.

This crop is grounded against its two closest siblings in the same
genus and trellis-growing system, both closely related crops this
project has also researched.

Giant granadilla is a clear case for its own entry, distinct from those
siblings, on several fronts that matter for cultivation modeling. It is
a lowland humid-tropics vine, with regional guidance recommending a
fairly narrow elevation band and noting poor fruiting at sea level and
slow growth at higher elevation, while its closest sibling crops thrive
at distinctly higher elevations and cooler temperatures; fruit set
specifically needs reasonably warm conditions and the vine is damaged by
cool temperatures [1][2][5][6][7]. It produces the largest fruit in the
genus — commonly 10-30 cm long and up to 2.5-3 kg — and is the only
granadilla-type passionfruit whose thick mesocarp (2.5-4 cm) is eaten
alongside the arils, with the immature fruit additionally cooked as a
vegetable and old-vine roots sometimes baked like yams, giving it two
harvest stages plus a secondary root product that its sibling crops
lack [1][2][6][8][9]. Natural fruit set is notably low and the large
form cannot self-pollinate at all, making hand pollination routine
practice in at least one major growing region, and a documented
fruit-set ceiling of roughly four to ten fruit per vine holds back
further setting until those fruits ripen [1][2][8][10]. The raw root is
emetic, narcotic, and poisonous, and the immature seeds and skin are
cyanogenic — notably, those cyanogenic tissues are exactly what is eaten
in the vegetable use [1][9]. It is also tracked as a distinct named
product and commodity category in at least one national agricultural
statistics system [1][11].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a lowland tropical trellis vine whose single fruit gives
several products, including a vegetable use while immature.** A ripe
fruit has a thin, delicate skin, a thick layer of firm, mealy white
flesh described as very mild in flavor, and a central cavity of
sweet-acid arils with seeds [1]. Peel and flesh together make up roughly
60% of fruit weight, with juice yield around 15% of whole fruit weight
[8][12]. Ripe-fruit products include fresh flesh and arils eaten in
fruit salad or with shaved ice, aril juice, a jelly made by boiling the
unpeeled flesh for a couple of hours, flesh cooked with sugar or
candied, and a fortified wine in at least one growing region; canned
flesh in syrup, frozen sherbet, and canned nectar are also recorded
[1][2]. The immature fruit is steamed or boiled and served as a
vegetable, or breaded and fried, with at least one Southeast Asian
tradition and one South Asian tradition both cooking the unripe fruit
this way [1][2][9]. This gives the crop two distinct harvest stages
(immature and ripe) from the same vines, plus a secondary root harvest
when old vines are eventually removed.

**Finding 2 — flowering, fruit set, and a fruit-set ceiling.** First
flowers appear roughly nine months after planting, with fruit ripening
roughly sixty to eighty-five days later depending on the source and
growing region [1][2][8]. Individual flower development from bud to
opening takes a few weeks on average. Each flower opens only once, very
early in the morning, and dies if not pollinated within that same day;
the stigma becomes receptive to pollen only a few hours after opening,
narrowing the effective pollination window considerably [8]. Natural
fruit set rates documented across different trial sites and seasons
range quite widely, from under ten percent up to over forty percent in
a single flowering cycle, with heavy rain during flowering shown to
suppress set entirely in at least one trial [8]. A notable ceiling
effect has also been documented: after four to ten flowers have set
fruit on a vine, further setting of the remaining flowers stops
entirely, even under hand pollination, only resuming once those earlier
fruits ripen — this produces one to three distinct harvest peaks per
year rather than continuous fruiting [2][10]. Hand pollination, when
practiced, must be done within a few hours of flower opening, and is
recommended as standard practice when natural set is observed to be
low [1][13]. The vine may produce few or no fruit in dry atmospheric
conditions, and in the hottest part of the growing season it may flower
without setting fruit at all [1][8].

**Finding 3 — yields are moderate per hectare, from relatively few, very
large fruits.** Reported fruit counts per vine vary enormously by
cultivar type and vine age: large-fruited types commonly produce in the
range of 25-35 fruits per year, while medium and small-fruited types can
produce 70-120 fruits per year in favorable conditions; young vines
(two to three years old) typically produce far fewer [1][2][6]. Per-
hectare yield figures from different regional sources cluster broadly in
the range of roughly 15-25 tonnes per hectare per year under two
harvests annually, though national-level statistics and individual cost
studies for the same growing region do not always agree closely with
each other [1][6][11][14].

**Finding 4 — seed must be sown fresh.** Seed germinability depends
strongly on how long the fruit was left to mature before harvest: seed
from fully mature fruit (around sixty days old) germinates at a very
high rate, while seed from fruit harvested even a few days earlier shows
sharply reduced germination, and seed from fruit under about forty days
old fails to germinate at all [8]. A short fermentation of the seed
within its aril (around two days), followed by washing and a few days
of air-drying, produced very high germination in one trial, while a much
longer fermentation period sharply reduced it [8]. Fermented seed does
not store well: germination dropped drastically within about a month of
storage regardless of whether it was kept cool and dry or at ordinary
room temperature, leading the original researcher to conclude that
viability does not last beyond about a month under those conditions [8].
Vegetative propagation by cuttings of mature wood, rooted in wet sand,
is also practiced and can fruit in the first year, while seedlings
typically take one to two years to first fruit [1][7].

**Finding 5 — toxic root and cyanogenic tissues.** The leaves, skin, and
immature seeds of this species contain a cyanogenic glycoside, and total
cyanide-releasing-compound content across vegetative and floral tissue
and roots has been measured at levels reported up to roughly 140 parts
per million in at least one study [1][9]. The pulp separately contains a
compound (passiflorine) that, if consumed to excess, causes lethargy and
drowsiness [1]. The raw root is specifically described as emetic,
narcotic, and poisonous [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A vigorous herbaceous vine, woody at the base,
  typically 10-15 m but reported up to 45 m in at least one tropical
  growing region, growing from a fleshy root that enlarges with age;
  perennial, with no chilling requirement [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, fruiting more or less
  continuously in some tropical growing regions and in one to three
  distinct seasonal peaks elsewhere, constrained by the fruit-set
  ceiling described in Finding 2; two harvests per year are documented
  in at least one regional guide [1][2][6].
- **Climate:** A true lowland-to-mid-elevation tropical crop requiring
  warm temperatures day and night with little fluctuation and high
  humidity; frost-sensitive and intolerant of cold, with reported
  vine damage below roughly 10°C and death at colder temperatures in at
  least one secondary source [1][7].
- **Food status:** Ripe arils and flesh are raw-edible; the immature
  fruit is not raw-edible and is eaten only cooked, since its immature
  seeds are cyanogenic (Finding 1, 5).
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated, principally by large
  carpenter bees, with hand pollination routinely used as a standard
  backup practice in at least one major growing region given generally
  low and inconsistent natural fruit set (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** The edible portion (arils plus flesh) is
  relatively low in both calories and protein compared to many fruit
  crops; peel and flesh together make up roughly 60% of fruit weight,
  arils with seeds roughly 30-40%, and juice roughly 15% of whole fruit
  weight (Finding 1, 3).
- **Water use:** Specific crop-water-use data for this species was not
  found; water requirements are estimated by analogy to a closely
  related, better-documented passionfruit crop this project has also
  researched, given the shared genus and broadly similar vine-on-trellis
  growth habit (see Estimates below). The crop is notably sensitive to
  both prolonged dry conditions during flowering (which suppresses fruit
  set) and to poor drainage, which the sources describe as highly
  damaging.
- **Establishment:** Seedlings or rooted cuttings are set out and
  trained onto a strong horizontal trellis built at roughly two metres
  height; plants are commonly topped at a modest height early on to
  force multiple leaders.
- **Labor:** Entirely hand-managed, including routine hand pollination
  during flowering peaks and careful hand harvesting and packing of the
  delicate, bruise-prone fruit; no mechanised options were identified in
  any source (Finding 2; see Estimates below for labor-hour derivations).
- **Soil:** Deep, fertile, moist but well-drained soil is strongly
  preferred across a range of soil types (volcanic, alluvial, sandy, and
  weathered granitic soils have all been documented as workable);
  moderately acidic pH is preferred, and the species does not tolerate
  high soil salinity; at least one case of vines dying from chlorosis in
  highly alkaline soil is documented.
- **Stand life:** Seedlings can first fruit within one to two years and
  cuttings sometimes within the first year; the species is relatively
  short-lived in commercial production, with at least one secondary
  source describing vines as typically replaced after around six years
  of fruiting.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A stink bug that punctures young fruit
  (causing withering, drop, or hard lumps in the flesh), fruit flies
  (managed by bagging fruit), a leaf beetle that defoliates nursery
  plants, a viral disease described as the only serious disease in at
  least one major growing region, several fungal leaf and wilt diseases,
  a stem-end rot, and nematodes are all documented (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** No formally registered cultivars were found; named
  local strains and landraces differ mainly in fruit size, sweetness,
  and the proportion of edible flesh, with the large-fruited *macrocarpa*
  group (sometimes called 'Gigante') unable to self-pollinate at all and
  producing fewer but much larger fruit than medium and small types.
- **Harvest:** Two distinct harvest stages from the same vines — an
  immature stage taken and eaten as a cooked vegetable, and a mature
  ripe stage taken for fresh and processed fruit use — plus an
  occasional secondary root harvest when old vines are eventually
  removed (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Ripe fruit is notably delicate and bruises easily,
  requiring very careful handling and packing; it keeps roughly two
  weeks under cool storage, detailed further below.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a stink bug that punctures young
fruit, fruit flies (managed by bagging fruit on the vine), a leaf beetle
that defoliates nursery plants along with related beetle species and a
sucking bug in at least one growing region, a viral disease described as
the only serious disease of this crop in at least one major growing
region, several fungal leaf-spot, anthracnose, and wilt diseases, a
stem-end rot, and nematodes. Vines can also develop chlorosis and die in
highly alkaline soil, and fruit set fails entirely under dry atmospheric
conditions, heavy rain during flowering, or peak-heat stress.

regions outside its native range, including having "run wild" in at
least one subtropical growing region, and at least one regional
invasive-species risk assessment rates it as a high invasion risk.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Ripe fruit is clipped by hand from the vine and carefully
  graded and packed given its delicate, bruise-prone skin, then either
  sold fresh or cool-stored. Ripe fruit is opened to separate the arils
  (eaten raw, juiced, or seed saved) from the flesh (used fresh in fruit
  salad, candied, or made into jelly by boiling the unpeeled flesh) and
  the peel. Whole ripe fruit with added sugar is also fermented into a
  wine in at least one growing region, sometimes fortified with
  distilled spirit and aged for several months. Immature fruit is
  cooked as a vegetable. Old-vine roots are occasionally baked and eaten
  like a true root/tuber crop when vines are removed, despite the root's
  documented toxicity. Prunings are rooted as cuttings for new plants,
  and fermented, washed, and air-dried seed from arils is used to raise
  seedlings.
- Not covered by this research in detail: pectin extraction from peel
  and mesocarp (laboratory-scale trials have reported substantial crude
  pectin yields on a dry basis); canned flesh in syrup, frozen sherbet,
  and canned nectar beyond what is noted above; carbonated drinks and
  ice cream manufacturing; leaf tea and other folk-medicine preparations.

No religious or ritual food use specific to this crop was identified in
the sources consulted; a historical use of the broader passion-flower
form as religious symbolism is documented for the genus generally, but
that is unrelated to production practice or to any ritual use of this
particular crop's fruit.

## Open research questions

- National production statistics for the same growing region and year
  conflict meaningfully between different government sources (roughly a
  25% discrepancy in both hectares and tonnage) and are not reconciled
  here.
- Reported per-hectare yield figures vary across regional guides, cost
  studies, and national statistics in ways not fully reconciled, likely
  reflecting genuinely different growing conditions, cultivar mixes, and
  measurement methods rather than a single representative figure.
- Recommended elevation range varies dramatically across sources, from
  a fairly narrow lowland band in one regional guide up to considerably
  higher elevations in another growing region — likely reflecting real
  regional and cultivar differences in adaptation rather than a single
  universal optimum.
- Drought tolerance is described inconsistently: one source describes
  the crop as resisting drought well with modest irrigation needs, while
  another describes a need for copious watering and few fruit set under
  dry atmospheric conditions — not reconciled here.
- Labor-intensity figures from different cost-accounting sources for the
  same growing region do not align well with hours-based labor
  estimates derived here, possibly reflecting a difference between
  "jobs" and "person-hours" as units of measurement; not resolved.
- Seed viability is described inconsistently: one detailed propagation
  study found germination collapsing within about a month of storage
  under any tested condition, while another source describes stored seed
  still germinating after up to twelve months when soaked before sowing.
- A reported calcium content figure in one source's food-composition
  table appears implausibly high and may be a units error (milligrams
  misreported as grams) rather than a genuine measurement.
- A cyanogen assay specifically of the cooked immature fruit (as opposed
  to raw vegetative tissue) was not found, leaving the actual dietary
  cyanide exposure from the vegetable-use preparation unquantified.
- Chilling-injury temperature specific to this species was not found;
  a related passionfruit crop's documented chilling threshold is noted
  only as a loose analogy, not a direct measurement.
- Whether harvesting fruit at the immature (vegetable) stage frees the
  vine to set additional flowers sooner, given the fruit-set ceiling
  described in Finding 2, was not researched.
- A dedicated per-operation labor budget for this crop, a direct
  cyanide assay of root tissue, detailed root chemistry, and several
  region-specific agricultural datasets referenced only secondhand in
  available sources were not independently obtained.

## Estimates and derivations

Time from planting to first ripe fruit (roughly 330-730 days) is derived
from a combination of a documented roughly nine-month interval to first
flowering plus a further two-month interval to first ripe fruit at the
fast end, and a documented up-to-two-year interval to first fruiting for
seedlings at the slow end; propagation by cuttings can shorten this
considerably.

A "poor" yield tier of roughly 6,000 kg/ha/yr is derived by combining a
representative planting density with a low per-vine fruit count typical
of young or poorly pollinated vines and a modest average fruit weight;
this represents a young or underperforming planting rather than mature,
well-managed production.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, effective rooting depth,
soil-moisture-depletion allowance, and drought-day tolerance) are
estimated entirely by analogy to a closely related, better-studied
passionfruit crop this project has also researched, on the reasoning
that both are vines in the same genus grown on a trellis in the lowland
tropics. This analogy is treated as imperfect: this species has a
notably denser canopy that can intercept nearly all incoming sunlight at
a planting, and a fleshy, enlarging taproot not present in the analogous
crop, both of which could plausibly shift real water use in either
direction from the borrowed estimate. Drought tolerance is left at a
middle "Medium" rating given directly conflicting source statements
(one describing good drought resistance, another describing a need for
copious watering).

Seed viability under storage is estimated at roughly a month or less
under either cool-dry or ambient conditions, based directly on one
detailed propagation study's finding that fermented seed's germination
rate collapsed from a high initial value to a very low one within about
a month regardless of storage condition; this conflicts with a separate
source describing successful germination from seed stored considerably
longer, which is not reconciled here.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest, grading, and packing are derived from
one regional cost-accounting source's reported per-hectare labor
expenditure, converted to hours using an assumed (unsourced) regional
day wage, and cross-checked against an independent estimate built from
total expected fruit count and an assumed careful hand-picking rate —
the two approaches arrive at broadly similar figures. Maintenance-labor
and hand-pollination-labor estimates are derived respectively by analogy
to a closely related granadilla-type crop's documented trellis-
maintenance labor (adjusted downward given this crop's lower fertilizer
input frequency) and from an estimate of total flowers needing hand
pollination at an assumed pollination rate; both carry wider uncertainty
than the harvest-labor estimate.

Soil pH optimum (roughly 6.0) is derived as the midpoint of the overlap
between several sources' differing pH range recommendations.

Yield-profile parameters (time to first harvest, ramp length, peak daily
yield, maximum days fruit can remain on the vine, and post-maturity
quality decay rate) are derived from the flowering-to-ripening timeline
and fruit-count figures described above, combined with one source's
specific documented figure for how long ripe fruit can remain on the
vine before being picked.

The immature (vegetable-stage) harvest window and its assumed short
shelf life are estimated by analogy to other cooked-vegetable immature
fruits (such as chayote and immature squash), since no direct market or
storage data for this specific use was found.

The fraction of whole fruit weight represented by arils (roughly
30-40%) is derived from two different sources' fruit-composition
breakdowns, which do not fully agree with each other depending on
exactly how "flesh" versus "pulp" is defined in each source.
