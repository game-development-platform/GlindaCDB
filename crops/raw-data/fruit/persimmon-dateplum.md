# Date-plum (*Diospyros lotus*)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as *Diospyros lotus*, the date-plum, also called the Caucasian
or lilac persimmon. Regional names include amlok (in South Asian
languages), a Persian name meaning "date-plum," a Turkish name tied to
a Black Sea port city, a Russian name, and Japanese and Chinese names
tied to its use as a tannin source. It is not the lotus of classical
Greek literary tradition, which this research did not cover, and it is
not the jujube, a different genus sometimes also called "red date" in
East Asian markets. No named cultivars were found for this crop; it is
grown from wild and seedling trees only.

This entry is the thinnest-documented of the persimmon-genus entries
this project has researched, but still passes as a genuinely distinct
crop: its main commercial product in many regions is planting material
(rootstock seedlings) for the Asian persimmon this project has also
researched, it bears tiny fruit typically eaten dried rather than
fresh, its unripe fruit serves as a tannin source for a traditional
Japanese pressed-and-aged product shared with that same Asian
persimmon entry, and its native range is Caucasus and Central Asian
upland terrain distinct from either the Asian or American persimmon's
own native ranges. It shares rootstock seed, seedling, scionwood and
nursery-tree items, along with the pressed-and-aged tannin product and
its aging process, with the Asian persimmon and American persimmon
entries this project has also researched; its fresh fruit, green fruit,
and dried-fruit products are treated as its own, separate from those
entries.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a wide Eurasian native range, with real elevation and
cultivation spread.** Native across Eurasia from East Asia to the
western Mediterranean, and also cultivated in parts of the Americas
and North Africa. In the Caucasus it mostly occurs below about 600
meters elevation, but in Central Asia it is found up to roughly 2,000
meters. It is common in a specific Black Sea coastal region of Turkey.
In parts of Pakistan it is cultivated at elevations around 1,500
meters in several named mountainous districts. It is also native to
China and was brought to Japan in ancient times [1][3][6][12].

**Finding 2 — a slow-growing, dioecious tree whose fruit is inedible
until fully ripe and is traditionally best after frost.** The tree
typically reaches around 15 meters (occasionally up to 30), with a
spreading crown and flaking bark; it is notably slow-growing, with one
nursery source suggesting 20-50 years to reach full size. It is
dioecious and self-incompatible, meaning only female trees bear fruit
and a planting needs several trees to ensure pollination; small
greenish to yellowish-white flowers appear in early summer. The fruit
itself is small (roughly 1-2 centimeters across, though at least one
nursery source gives a somewhat larger figure), ripening from yellow
to a blackish-purple color in autumn, with juicy flesh around seeds that
have an unusually hard endosperm. As with other astringent persimmon-
genus fruit, it is edible only once fully ripe, and tradition holds it
is best after a frost — consistent with the frost-driven astringency
loss documented for other persimmon-genus crops this project has also
researched [1][6][9].

**Finding 3 — its dominant modern commercial role in many regions is
as rootstock seed for the Asian persimmon, with measurable graft-
compatibility tradeoffs.** This species is documented as the common
rootstock choice for the Asian persimmon in many growing regions. A
Turkish nursery trial comparing this species' seedlings against Asian-
persimmon seedlings found this species' seedlings grew notably thinner
and shorter, had meaningfully lower graft-take success with two named
Asian-persimmon cultivars (roughly 44-45%, versus roughly 70% success
when grafting onto Asian-persimmon seedling rootstock itself), while
seed germination rates were broadly similar between the two species.
Separately, this species' rootstock is documented as partly
incompatible with three specific widely grown non-astringent Asian-
persimmon cultivars, and as raising fruit drop in at least one widely
grown astringent Asian-persimmon cultivar when used as its rootstock
[4][5].

**Finding 4 — long, genuinely variable seed dormancy, with no single
agreed stratification protocol.** Seed dormancy in this species is
long, and the optimal cold-stratification duration differs
meaningfully across independent studies: one Iranian study found 70
days best among durations tested from 0 to 70 days; a Turkish study
used 60 days; a Brazilian study found a much shorter 30-day
stratification raised seedling emergence by roughly 14 percentage
points to about 44% at a specific incubation temperature, with no
plant-growth-regulator treatment needed; and a commercial seed
supplier recommends a 24-hour soak followed by a full 90 days of cold
stratification. These figures are not reconciled here, since they may
reflect real differences in seed source population or testing
methodology [4].

**Finding 5 — fruit composition data is thin and comes mostly from
regional food-science studies rather than a standard nutrition
database.** General sources describe the fruit as plentiful in sugar,
malic acid and vitamins, and as having a high tannin percentage before
full ripening. A Turkish food-science study measured specific extract
values for vitamin C and total phenolic content per unit weight, found
fructose to be the dominant sugar with no detectable sucrose; a
separate Korean study of fruit powder found sugar content higher in
mature fruit and phenolic content higher in immature fruit. No standard
energy (caloric) value specific to this species was found in any source
used here [1][3][8].

**Finding 6 — a specific, geographically concentrated Japanese tannin
industry draws on this species' green fruit.** A named cultivated form
of this species is grown widely across a specific swath of Japan
(roughly from the Shin'etsu region to the Tohoku region) specifically
as a raw material for a traditional Japanese tannin product, the same
pressed-and-aged product this project has documented as a shared item
with the Asian persimmon entry. A specialist wood-science journal
source notes that this species' tannins have a notably high proportion
of a particular chemical ring structure compared to other tannin
sources [6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous perennial tree, typically around
  15 meters (occasionally to 30), slow-growing [1][9].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; fruit is picked or gathered
  after autumn frost [1].
- **Climate:** Temperate, warm-temperate and Mediterranean climates,
  extending into Central Asian upland terrain up to roughly 2,000
  meters; cold-hardiness limit is not well established, with nursery
  sources ranging from a specific moderate-cold figure up to a notably
  milder hardiness-zone claim — an unresolved conflict [1][9].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible only once fully ripe (traditionally after
  frost); green fruit is not edible raw [1][6].
- **Pollination:** Required — the species is dioecious and
  self-incompatible, so a planting needs multiple trees to ensure fruit
  set; pollinators are assumed to be insects by analogy with other
  persimmon-genus crops, though this was not directly researched for
  this species specifically [1].
- **Yield:** No orchard-yield study or national statistic exists for
  this crop, which is grown mostly for seed, dried fruit, or tannin
  rather than as a conventional fresh-fruit orchard crop; yield tiers
  used here are estimated from tree size and fruit size relative to the
  related American persimmon's own documented orchard-yield figures,
  scaled down to reflect this species' much smaller individual fruit.
- **Water use:** No dedicated reference crop coefficient exists for
  this species in the standard international crop-evapotranspiration
  reference; a closely analogous stone-fruit crop-coefficient curve is
  used here as the basis, as for the other persimmon-genus crops this
  project has researched.
- **Establishment:** Seedling trees, or seed sown after cold
  stratification; no standard orchard planting density was found, since
  this crop is grown mostly as scattered seedling trees or nursery
  rootstock rather than in a conventional dense orchard planting [1][4].
- **Labor:** No labor-hour source was found specific to this crop;
  estimates here are scaled from the related American persimmon's own
  documented labor figures, adjusted down somewhat for harvest (given
  this crop's lower per-hectare yield) but up for post-harvest handling
  to reflect the extra hand labor involved in drying very small fruit.
- **Soil:** Well-drained loam preferred; the species tolerates a
  variety of soils but does not tolerate stagnant moisture, with root
  rot documented following poor drainage. Estimated optimal pH around
  6.5 (range roughly 5.5-8.0), reflecting its Mediterranean and
  Caucasian native range, much of it on limestone-derived soils that
  suggest some alkaline tolerance, though no pH study specific to this
  species was found [9].
- **Stand life:** No bearing-age or productive-lifespan study was
  found specific to this species; a bearing age of roughly five to
  eight years is estimated here by analogy with the related American
  persimmon's own documented seedling bearing-age range.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not researched in depth for this species;
  no specific pest or disease source was found.
- **Cultivars:** No named cultivars were found; this crop is grown from
  wild populations (documented in Caucasus and Black Sea growing
  regions) and from regionally distinct cultivated seedling populations,
  including South Asian populations grown specifically for dried fruit
  and Japanese populations grown specifically for tannin production, as
  well as nursery-raised seedling lines grown specifically as rootstock
  source material for the Asian persimmon.

## Hazards

No specific pest or disease information was found for this crop.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — tannin in unripe fruit is a documented bezoar-
forming risk, consistent with the same hazard documented for the
related Asian and American persimmon entries. No animal-specific
toxicity source was found for this species specifically, though the
livestock hazard documented for the American persimmon may apply by
analogy given the shared genus and fruit chemistry. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** ripe fruit has its seed cleaned out and cold-stratified
  into rootstock seedlings, which are grafted with Asian-persimmon
  scion wood; ripe fruit is also dried whole into a traditional South
  Asian dried-fruit product; green (unripe) fruit is pressed into a
  tannin-rich juice that is aged for one to three years into the same
  traditional Japanese tannin product documented for the Asian
  persimmon entry; fully ripe fruit is also eaten fresh, traditionally
  after a frost.
- Not covered by this research in detail: a boiled-fruit preparation
  noted in at least one regional source; any molasses or alcohol
  product from this species; wood use; folk-medicine use; and the wild
  form of this species as a distinct foraged resource.

As with the other persimmon-genus crops this project has researched,
Jewish religious law treats this species' fruit under the same
first-three-years fruit prohibition applied to fruit trees generally,
counted from the date of planting. No other ritual or religious use
specific to this crop was found; a historical Greek name for the genus
meaning roughly "god's fruit" reflects naming history rather than an
ongoing ritual practice.

## Open research questions

- Cold-hardiness limit for this species is reported very
  inconsistently across nursery sources, ranging from a specific
  moderate-cold figure to a notably milder hardiness-zone claim.
- Optimal cold-stratification duration for seed is reported
  inconsistently across at least four independent sources (ranging from
  30 to 90 days), possibly reflecting real differences in seed source
  population.
- Fruit size is reported somewhat differently across sources (roughly
  1-2 centimeters versus a slightly larger figure from at least one
  nursery source), as is bloom timing (early summer in most sources
  versus a slightly earlier window in at least one US botanical-garden
  source).
- No energy (caloric) value specific to this species was found in any
  source used here.
- Yield figures, labor-hour figures, bearing age, and overall
  cold-hardiness all rest on estimates or analogy to the related
  American persimmon rather than direct, species-specific measurement,
  since no dedicated orchard-yield or labor study exists for this
  largely wild and seedling-propagated crop.
- Detailed pest and disease information, salinity tolerance, wood use,
  and a reported traditional molasses product were not found or not
  researched in depth.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 110-160 days from bloom to ripe fruit)
is derived from documented bloom timing (early summer) through to
documented ripening timing (autumn, typically October-November).

Energy content (estimated around 800 kcal/kg) is estimated as
intermediate between the Asian persimmon's own documented flesh energy
content and the American persimmon's own documented, notably higher
figure, reasoning that this species' small fruit with proportionally
larger seeds likely has somewhat less edible flesh per unit weight than
either relative; no direct source was found for this species'
composition in standard nutrition-database terms.

Yield tiers are estimated from the related American persimmon's own
documented orchard-yield figures, scaled down to roughly half the
American persimmon's "good" tier to reflect this species' much smaller
individual fruit size at a comparable tree size, since no direct
orchard-yield study exists for this crop; "average" and "poor" tiers
additionally allow for seedling stands where roughly half the trees are
non-fruiting males.

A seasonal water-use index is derived from a closely analogous
stone-fruit crop-coefficient curve (the closest match in the standard
international crop-evapotranspiration reference, since this species has
no dedicated entry there), integrated across an estimated growing
season; the associated drought-buffer estimate reflects a moderate
rooting-depth assumption, and the derived daily yield-penalty rate sits
between the values derived for the Asian and American persimmon
entries, reflecting a tree described as drought-tolerant once
established but sensitive to poor drainage.

Labor-hour estimates by operation are derived by scaling from the
related American persimmon's own documented and estimated labor
figures, adjusted down for harvest to reflect this crop's lower
per-hectare yield, but adjusted up for post-harvest handling to reflect
the extra hand labor involved in drying very small fruit.

Soil nutrient uptake figures are carried over by direct analogy from
the Asian persimmon entry, since no species-specific nutrient-removal
study was found; pH tolerance range reflects this species' Mediterranean
and Caucasian native range on often-limestone soils.

A yield-ramp profile (time to first harvest, ramp to full bearing, peak
daily yield) is estimated by analogy with the related American
persimmon's own documented seedling bearing-age range, since no direct
bearing-age study exists for this species; peak daily yield is
calculated by dividing the "good" yield tier by an estimated harvest
window, and a long on-tree standing period reflects this species'
documented tendency for frost to improve rather than end the fruit's
usability.
