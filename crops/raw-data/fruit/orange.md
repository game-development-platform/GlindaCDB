## Naming and scope

Modeled as *Citrus* × *sinensis*, a natural hybrid of pomelo and
mandarin — roughly 42% pomelo and 58% mandarin by ancestry, with
pomelo as the maternal line [1]. Four cultivar groups are recognized,
following both a standard USDA postharvest reference and a general
encyclopedia source [1][3]: round (common/juice) oranges, navel
oranges, blood (pigmented) oranges, and acidless oranges. Each group
is treated as a cultivar group with its own fruit commodity and
harvest stage within this one entry, since the groups differ sharply
in end use — round/juice oranges are mostly juiced; navel juice turns
bitter from limonin when exposed to air or pasteurized, so navels are
eaten fresh instead; blood oranges develop colour only after cold
nights; and acidless oranges are fresh-market-only and spoil quickly.

A closely related sour/bitter hybrid of pomelo and wild mandarin
exists commercially (valued for marmalade, flower-derived neroli oil
and orange flower water, dried peel for liqueurs, and as a historic
citrus rootstock) but is outside the scope of this entry. A separate,
smaller citrus crop (bergamot) is covered in its own entry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the world's main juice crop, and cultivar choice decides
the product.** About two-thirds of all oranges are round (common)
oranges, mostly juiced [1]. In the United States, one major
citrus-growing state sends over 90% of its oranges to processing,
while another is the largest domestic fresh-market producer; in one
recent season, the processing-oriented state juiced the great majority
of its crop while the fresh-market state sold the great majority of
its crop fresh [3][15]. Navel juice turns bitter from limonin when
exposed to air or pasteurization, so navel oranges are eaten fresh
rather than juiced [4][16]. Acidless oranges are considered unsuitable
for juice processing and spoil quickly in storage [1]. Because cultivar
group so strongly determines end use, this entry tracks a separate
fruit commodity and harvest stage per group.

**Finding 2 — oranges store better on the tree than after picking.**
Oranges are non-climacteric, meaning harvested fruit will not continue
to ripen or improve; fruit genuinely stores better left on the tree
than in cold storage [3]. One major juice cultivar hangs on the tree
late into summer, giving what growers describe as two overlapping
crops in a single year, and can even "regreen" (lose ripe colour) if
left hanging in warm weather [3][4]. Once picked, oranges keep up to
about 12 weeks in storage: around 0-1°C in regions where fruit rarely
shows chilling injury, or a somewhat warmer 3-8°C in regions where
fruit is more chill-sensitive and can be injured below about 3-5°C
[3]. A separate reference gives roughly three months at 11°C, or five
months at 2-4°C [4].

**Finding 3 — blood oranges need cold nights to colour; navels need a
cool climate to grow well.** Blood-orange anthocyanin pigment forms
only when nights are cold; in one well-documented growing region near
a volcano, winter days run around 15°C while nights fall to 4°C down to
−2°C, a swing of up to 14°C [5][6]. In warmer growing regions, this
colour rarely develops except during unusually cold weather, and the
pigment continues building even after harvest in cold storage [4][5].
Separately, the major navel cultivar needs a relatively cool climate
and should not be grown below about 1,000 m elevation in the tropics,
while a different major cultivar is considered the most satisfactory
orange for tropical growing [4]. In warm climates generally, ripe peel
can stay green rather than colouring; early-season fruit is commonly
degreened with ethylene gas for one to three days before sale [3].

**Finding 4 — a historically important sour citrus rootstock has been
devastated by a specific virus.** A sour, closely related citrus type
was the classic rootstock for sweet orange and other citrus: it
resists foot rot and was once used on about half of one major
citrus-growing state's orange and grapefruit trees. However, a specific
virus kills the phloem below the graft union of sweet orange,
mandarin and grapefruit budded onto that rootstock (lemon scions on
the same rootstock are unaffected), and the virus's main insect vector
caused roughly 16 million rootstock trees to die after it arrived in
two South American citrus-growing countries. In response, growers in
at least one major production region shifted to tolerant rootstocks
over several decades and established a certified, disease-screened
budwood program [4][18].

**Finding 5 — water-stress timing matters more than total water
supplied.** A standard crop-water-response reference (largely built on
orange data) identifies flowering and fruit set as the most
water-sensitive stage, since water stress there increases young-fruit
drop; autumn stress shrinks fruit and increases a specific peel
disorder in navel oranges. In controlled studies, navel oranges given
22% and 46% less than full crop water use lost about 7% and 22% of
yield respectively, while one juice cultivar given 35% less water lost
about 20% of yield. By contrast, one documented Arizona orchard on a
sensor-based deficit-irrigation regime used 66% less irrigation water
than a conventional schedule with no yield loss at all. Measured
seasonal crop water use across documented growing regions ranges
roughly 820-1,500 mm per year depending on climate [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree, typically about
  7.5 m tall (up to 15 m when very old); no chill requirement for
  flowering (subtropical evergreen) [1][4][13].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, with one main harvest season
  per cultivar group — a major round/juice cultivar typically October-
  December, navel oranges in winter, and a major late-season cultivar
  from late spring into summer; fruit holds on the tree for months
  without major quality loss [3][4].
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean and humid subtropical
  climates generally; a late-season cultivar is considered best suited
  to true tropical lowlands, while navel cultivars need a cooler
  climate and generally fail below about 1,000 m elevation in the
  tropics [4].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; roughly 490 kcal/kg and 10 g protein/kg
  for fresh edible flesh, with juice somewhat lower in energy per unit
  weight [4].
- **Pollination:** Not required for fruit set — most citrus, including
  sweet orange, is self-compatible; blossoms yield very little pollen
  and no artificial pollination is practiced commercially. Navel
  oranges are seedless through a spontaneous mutation and are
  propagated only by grafting; most other cultivars are nearly
  seedless to lightly seedy, varying by growing region. Honeybees are
  a major pollinator and citrus blossom is among the most abundant
  nectar sources in at least one well-documented growing region [4][13][16].
- **Yield:** A world-average figure of roughly 19 t/ha is a reasonable
  benchmark, consistent with a combined international citrus average
  of around 16.3 t/ha; "good" commercial yields run around 30 t/ha in
  well-managed fresh-market regions, with exceptional high-density
  trial yields reaching 60 t/ha (and one especially favourable trial
  reaching far higher still); poor yields in a stressed production
  season can fall to around 5.75 t/ha. A documented smallholder
  tropical-highland production budget reaches full bearing of about
  15 t/ha between years 8-13 of a 20-year production cycle, totalling
  roughly 203 t/ha harvested over the whole cycle, split roughly
  50/40/10% across first, second and third grade fruit.
- **Water use:** Crop coefficient runs roughly 0.5-0.75 through the
  season depending on canopy cover and ground-cover management, peaking
  in autumn; rooting depth is typically 1.2-1.5 m under 70% canopy
  cover. The crop is notably sensitive to waterlogging — root vigour
  and yield drop sharply once the water table rises to within about 78
  cm of the surface, and wet soil predisposes the crop to a root-rot
  disease. Drought tolerance is moderate; the single most sensitive
  period for water stress is flowering through fruit set, while summer
  stress (cell expansion) is comparatively well tolerated and can even
  raise fruit sugar content if not severe [4][10][12][13].
- **Establishment:** Budded nursery trees (bud wood of the desired
  cultivar grafted onto a rootstock seedling), typically planted out
  after about one growing season in the nursery; weed control is
  important for the first two to three years after planting [4].
- **Labor:** A documented smallholder tropical-highland production
  budget finds labor to be roughly a third of total production cost,
  with harvest and handling the largest single labor category (nearly
  half of all labor hours) followed by weeding; picking rates in a
  mechanized fresh-market operation have been measured at under 10
  standard boxes per picker-hour [4][10].
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained sandy or loam soils are preferred;
  moderate salt sensitivity; optimal pH is roughly neutral to mildly
  acidic, with alkaline soils causing nutrient-deficiency-driven
  decline in at least one well-documented growing region. Documented
  micronutrient deficiencies include copper, zinc and molybdenum, each
  with a distinctive visible symptom [4][10][12].
- **Establishment pathway and stand life:** First commercial harvest as
  early as year three from planting in a documented smallholder budget,
  reaching full bearing by roughly years 8-13 and then gradually
  declining over a 20-year economic cycle; individual trees, however,
  can live far longer than this economic cycle — some very old
  Mediterranean trees are reported still bearing thousands of fruit
  annually. Documented causes of commercial stand decline include a
  fungal blight destroying large numbers of trees annually in at least
  one major production region, the rootstock-killing virus noted above,
  and a burrowing nematode associated with slow orchard decline [4][10][18].
- **Disease/pest profile:** The rootstock-killing virus noted above; a
  fungal blight; a burrowing nematode; foot rot (a water-mold disease
  worsened by wet soil); and bacterial canker (one historic nursery
  outbreak in a major production region destroyed over 15 million young
  trees) are all documented [4].
- **Cultivars:** Round/juice cultivars include early-, mid- and
  late-season types varying in cold tolerance, juice colour, and
  suitability for tropical growing; navel cultivars are grafted-only
  (seedless) and prized fresh, including at least one notable pigmented
  navel type; blood cultivars require cold nights to colour and vary in
  bitterness and season; acidless cultivars are sweet, very low in
  acid, fresh-market-only, and prone to quick spoilage or granulation
  if harvest is delayed.

## Hazards

Peel oil (predominantly limonene) is a documented skin allergen and
irritant, triggered by handling fruit, sucking on oranges, or shaking
trees to harvest; at least one case report describes a hives reaction.
Blossoms and wood sawdust are documented respiratory allergens,
including asthma triggered by sawdust exposure. A seed extract has been
used historically as a folk malaria remedy and has reported toxic
effects (respiratory depression, spleen contraction) at high doses.
Quarantine pests of concern include Mediterranean and Caribbean fruit
flies (managed by cold treatment or fumigation) and bacterial canker.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the plant compound synephrine occurs naturally in
orange fruit and juice at food-typical levels; this is a distinct,
much lower-dose context than concentrated synephrine supplements
(more relevant to the closely related sour/bitter citrus type), but
the food-level occurrence itself is deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data. Processing byproducts
(citrus pulp, peel) used as livestock feed carry their own handling
and composition considerations, also deferred for the same reason.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit is washed, waxed, degreened as needed, and graded,
  then either cold-stored for fresh sale or pressed for juice; pressed
  juice may be concentrated for storage and shipping, with peel and
  pulp residue dried into pelleted livestock feed; peel oil is recovered
  separately; culls go to juice; in at least one major production
  region, juice is also fermented into wine and distilled into brandy;
  peel is candied as a confectionery product; nursery propagation
  proceeds from rootstock seed through seedling to budded tree.
- Not covered by this research in detail: frozen concentrate, canned
  juice and segments, and juice powder processing; industrial recovery
  of pectin, flavonoids, molasses, seed oil, or alcohol from peel and
  processing waste; aseptic not-from-concentrate juice storage and
  flavor-pack technology; citrus honey.

In Nowruz (the Iranian New Year, rooted in Zoroastrian tradition and
observed by all of Iran's religious communities), an orange floating
in a bowl of water is a traditional item on the ceremonial haft-sin
table, symbolizing the Earth suspended in space; the specific citrus
type used is not clearly stated in the sources consulted, and may in
some cases be the closely related sour/bitter type rather than sweet
orange.

## Open research questions

- World production and yield figures conflict meaningfully across
  sources (a roughly 67 million tonne figure from one source against a
  roughly 77 million tonne figure with a notably higher implied
  per-hectare yield from another, and a distinctly lower figure from a
  government statistical source) — not reconciled here.
- Whether cross-pollination affects fruit set (versus only seed
  content) in nominally seedless or near-seedless cultivars is
  unresolved; at least one regional report of unusually seedy fruit
  from an otherwise nearly-seedless cultivar suggests cross-pollination
  may sometimes affect seediness even where it does not affect set.
- Detailed labor-hour figures for operations beyond harvest and one
  documented smallholder budget's own categories rest on estimates
  rather than direct measurement.
- Soil nutrient uptake and removal figures are estimated rather than
  drawn from a dedicated citrus nutrient-removal study; one documented
  fertilizer application rate implies meaningfully higher nitrogen
  input than the uptake estimate used here, with the gap presumably
  reflecting losses not captured by a simple uptake figure.
- A clear, sourced temperature threshold model for cold-night-triggered
  blood-orange colour development was not found — only regional
  examples of typical day/night temperature swings in colour-producing
  growing regions.
- Citrus greening disease (a major modern citrus health threat
  elsewhere) was not covered in the sources used for this entry.
- Detailed juice-yield-per-unit-fruit figures beyond a legal minimum
  standard in one jurisdiction are estimated rather than directly
  sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 210-450 days from bloom to harvest) is
derived from documented spring bloom timing combined with the full
span of harvest dates across cultivar groups, from early-season
round/juice cultivars through to a late-season cultivar that can hang
on the tree past the following spring's bloom.

Yield tiers are derived primarily from recent regional per-acre yield
statistics (converted to metric units) for "good" and "poor" benchmarks,
a combined international citrus yield statistic for the world average,
and a high-density orchard trial result for the exceptional benchmark.

A seasonal water-use index and associated drought-buffer estimate are
derived from a standard crop-evapotranspiration reference's
stage-length and coefficient tables for citrus under moderate canopy
cover, cross-checked against a separate reference's monthly coefficient
figures for several named growing regions, which bracket the derived
value acceptably.

Labor-hour estimates by operation are derived chiefly from one
documented smallholder tropical-highland production budget's reported
labor cost and its own internal breakdown by task share, converted
using that budget's local daily-wage-equivalent figure; harvest labor
specifically is cross-checked against a separately documented picking
rate from a mechanized fresh-market operation.

Soil nutrient uptake figures are estimated from a documented fertilizer
application-rate range divided by yield, which should be read as an
application-rate proxy rather than a true removal measurement, since it
does not account for losses.

A harvest-fraction-by-year curve (used to model yield ramping up to and
declining from full bearing) is derived directly from the documented
smallholder budget's own year-by-year yield figures as a fraction of
that budget's full-bearing yield.
