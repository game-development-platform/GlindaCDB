## Naming and scope

*Citrus* × *sinensis* (L.) Osbeck (Rutaceae), the sweet orange [1][2].
- **Origin:** unknown in the wild; "assumed to have originated in
  southern China, northeastern India, and perhaps southeastern Asia" [1].
  It reached the Mediterranean possibly after 1450 or around 1500 [1].
- **Cultivar groups [2]:**
  - **round** ('Valencia', 'Hamlin', 'Pineapple', 'Shamouti');
  - **navel** ('Washington Navel');
  - **blood or pigmented** ('Moro', 'Tarocco');
  - **acidless** ('Succari').
- **Scope:** one cropID for all four groups; the groups are cultivar
  groups with different end uses (Finding 1).
- **Out of scope:** sour orange (`bitter_orange`), here only as a
  rootstock; mandarins, tangors and clementines (`mandarin`,
  `clementine`).

**Cam xoàn** ('Xoàn' orange), a Mekong Delta sweet orange, is covered
here as a cultivar. It was proposed as its own row (`cam_xoan`) from
`kunenbo.md`, because one 2025 paper labels it *C. nobilis* [12].
**Gate check (2026-11-22): not promoted.**
- *Taxonomy:* two other Vietnamese studies name it *Citrus sinensis*
  cv. Xoan [9][10], and the 2025 paper itself puts it beside cam sành
  rather than with it [12]. The trial trees were grafted on cam mật,
  the Vietnamese sweet orange [10][11]. It is a sweet-orange cultivar,
  not the King mandarin of `kunenbo.md`. A coin-shaped mark at the
  flower end is said to tell it from cam mật [9].
- *Range:* the Mekong Delta: Trà Ôn and Vũng Liêm (Vĩnh Long) and
  Phụng Hiệp (Hậu Giang), where it covers about 300 ha and is the main
  fruit tree of Phương Phú commune [9][10][12].
- *Farming system:* grafted orchard citrus like other Delta citrus,
  planted at 1,300 trees/ha in the one trial found. Salt intrusion is a
  concern; seeds failed to germinate in vitro at 10 g/L NaCl [9][10].
- *Harvest:* about 9 months from flower to harvest [12].
- *Uses:* eaten fresh [13]; a low-acid fruit (titratable acid 0.42%,
  pH 4.4, 8.5 °Brix, against 1.26% acid for cam sành) [12]. It has the
  highest flesh share of five Delta citrus (70%) [12]. Fruit weighed
  154-256 g by size class [12], or 163-245 g in a potassium trial with
  yields of 5.8-8.1 kg per three-year-old tree [10].
These are cultivar-level differences. The low acid places it near this
entry's acidless group, though no source calls it acidless.

**Relatives in this project:**
- bitter orange (`bitter_orange`, `bitter-orange.md`): the sour orange
  rootstock;
- mandarin (`mandarin`, `mandarin.md`) and clementine (`clementine`);
- cam xoàn (`cam_xoan`): covered here as a cultivar.

**Proposed:** none.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the product depends on the state, not just the cultivar.**
- **Florida:** "over 90% going for processing" [2]. NASS 2023-24 to
  2025-26: 90%, 91% and 89% of Florida oranges processed [4].
- **California:** "the largest producer of oranges for the fresh market"
  [2]; 64%, 76% and 65% of its oranges went fresh in the same three
  seasons [4].
- **World:** 17.1 of 45.9 million tons of fresh oranges are forecast to
  go to processors in 2025/26, about 37%; Brazil and Egypt are two of the
  top three processing countries [5]. World juice output is about 1.4
  million tons (65 °Brix equivalent), 1.0 million of it Brazilian [5].
- **Cultivar and season:** in California, navels are marketed October to
  mid-June and Valencias mid-March to October; in Florida, early and
  midseason oranges October-March and Valencias January-July [4].

**Finding 2 — citrus greening is shrinking the Florida industry.**
- **Disease:** huanglongbing (HLB), a bacterial infection (*Candidatus*
  Liberibacter asiaticus) spread by the Asian citrus psyllid
  (*Diaphorina citri*) [6]. "Once infected, most trees die within a few
  years. There is no cure" [6]. Fruit becomes small, lopsided, partly
  green and bitter, and "can only be sold for juicing" [6].
- **Range:** throughout Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, Puerto Rico and
  the US Virgin Islands, and parts of six more states including
  California and Texas [6]. Quarantine under 7 CFR 301.76 controls
  nursery stock [6].
- **Florida in numbers [4]:** bearing orange area fell from 231,300 acres
  (2023-24) to 126,800 (2025-26), a 45% drop in two seasons (see
  Estimates). Yields were 78, 73 and 102 boxes/acre, against 335, 317
  and 371 in California [4]. That is about 7-10 t/ha in Florida against
  28-33 t/ha in California (see Estimates).
- **Brazil:** production forecast at 13.5 million tons in 2025/26, below
  the 5-year average, with "improved practices to manage citrus
  greening" named [5].

**Finding 3 — rootstock choice is a disease decision.**
- **Sour orange:** resists foot rot and was preferred on
  high-water-table soils, until tristeza (found in Florida in 1952) made
  growers switch; it later came back and was on about half of Florida's
  orange and grapefruit trees (1987) [1]. The brown citrus aphid is "the
  main vector of the tristeza virus" [1].
- **Alternatives [1]:** Cleopatra mandarin (dwarfs trees, lowers yield,
  raises acidity); rough lemon (abandoned in Florida for blight); Carrizo
  citrange (tristeza-resistant, exocortis- and blight-susceptible);
  trifoliate orange (cold-hardy, for low wet soils); Swingle citrumelo
  (resists tristeza, exocortis, xyloporosis and citrus nematode).
- **Clean stock:** Florida has run a Citrus Budwood Registration
  programme since 1953 [1].

**Finding 4 — water-stress timing matters more than the total.**
- **Most sensitive:** flowering and fruit set; stress raises young-fruit
  drop [3].
- **Summer:** fruit shrinks under stress but recovers when irrigation
  returns; moderate summer-autumn stress did not cut Valencia yield in
  Arizona [3].
- **Autumn:** smaller fruit at harvest and more creasing in navels [3].
- **Season-long:** navels at 22% and 46% less than full ETc lost 7% and
  22% of yield; 'Shamouti' with 35% less water lost 20% [3]. Valencia in
  Arizona on a tensiometer schedule used 66% less water with no yield
  loss [3].
- **Revenue, not tonnage:** early stress cut navel peel creasing and so
  raised the fancy-grade share; for 'Lane Late', deficits that shrank
  oversized fruit raised revenue by about 80% [3].
- **Seasonal ETc:** 820-1,200 mm (Florida), 1,080-1,500 mm (Arizona),
  1,300 mm (South Africa), 800-850 mm (coastal eastern Spain and Israel)
  [3].
- **Drought survival:** trees at about −6.5 MPa predawn recovered water
  status within a week of rewatering, after severe defoliation [3].

**Finding 5 — fruit stores best on the tree.**
- "Under normal weather conditions, fruit store better on the tree than
  in cold storage" [2]. Oranges are non-climacteric [2].
- **Storage:** up to 12 weeks; 0-1 °C and 85-90% RH (Florida, Texas)
  or 3-8 °C and 90-95% RH (California, Arizona) [2]. Fruit begins to
  freeze at about −1 °C [2].
- **Chilling injury:** California and Arizona fruit below about 3-5 °C;
  Florida and Texas fruit rarely [2]. Morton gives 3 months at 11 °C, or
  up to 5 months at 2-4 °C [1].
- **Degreening:** ethylene for 1-3 days when warm nights delay colour
  [2]. Peel may stay green when mature in warm climates, and 'Valencia'
  can regreen [2][3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen tree to 7.5 m, up to 15 m with great
  age [1]; leaves live about two years [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** one main crop per cultivar group a year, held on
  the tree for months (Finding 5) [2][4]. Valencia and mandarins bear
  alternately [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced this pass.
- **Climate zones:**
  - "The orange is subtropical, not tropical" [1]. Growing-period range
    12.8-37.8 °C; ideal winter dormancy at 1.7-10 °C [1].
  - **Chill:** none needed; cool nights trigger colour change [3].
  - **Frost:** fruit is damaged at −1.1 to −3.3 °C; mature dormant trees
    survived 10 hours below −3.9 °C; young trees can be killed by brief
    frosts; prolonged cold and drought make it worse [1]. Freezes "may
    reduce the crop by 20 or even 40%" in Florida [1]. Protection: wind
    machines, heaters and, best, overhead sprinklers [1].
  - **Frost as a quality event:** frozen fruit is a loss, not a quality
    gain [1].
  - **Heat:** severe, prolonged heat in early summer causes heavy "June
    drop" [3].
  - **Elevation:** 'Washington Navel' "should not be grown below an
    elevation of 3,300 ft" (1,000 m) in tropical countries; 'Valencia'
    quality is good from sea level to 1,600 m in Colombia and it is "the
    most satisfactory orange for the tropics" [1].
  - **Rainfall:** often grown on 1,000-1,500 mm; a dry climate gives
    deeper peel colour [1].
- **Composition (per 100 g, fresh fruit; Morton's table) [1]:** 47-51
  kcal; moisture 86.0 g; protein 0.7-1.3 g; fat 0.1-0.3 g; carbohydrate
  12.0-12.7 g; fibre 0.5 g; ash 0.5-0.7 g; calcium 40-43 mg; phosphorus
  17-22 mg; iron 0.2-0.8 mg; potassium 190-200 mg; vitamin A 200 IU;
  ascorbic acid 45-61 mg. Fresh juice: 40-48 kcal and 37-61 mg ascorbic
  acid [1].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible [1].
- **Pollination:** most citrus is self-compatible [3]; orange blossoms
  give "very little pollen" and are not hand-pollinated [1]. 'Washington
  Navel' is seedless or nearly so; 'Valencia' nearly seedless except in
  Chile [1]. Citrus honey is 25% of California's honey [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Flowering is mainly in spring
  [3].
- **Yield:**
  - **California 2023-24 to 2025-26:** 335, 317 and 371 boxes/acre at 80
    lb, about 28-33 t/ha [4].
  - **Florida:** 78, 73 and 102 boxes/acre at 90 lb, about 7-10 t/ha [4].
  - **Navel trial (San Joaquin Valley):** 43-48 t/ha gross under deficit
    regimes [3].
  - **Density trials:** 11-year totals in Florida rose from 2,380 to
    4,484 boxes/acre as spacing narrowed from 7.5 × 6 m to 4.5 × 3 m [1];
    Australian high-density Valencia gave 60 t/ha at 6 years, and 135-260
    t/ha in a dense trial before declining after the 4th crop (as
    printed) [1].
- **Water use:**
  - **FAO-56 [7]:** citrus with no ground cover, Kc ini/mid/end
    0.70/0.65/0.70 at 70% canopy, 0.65/0.60/0.65 at 50% and
    0.50/0.45/0.55 at 20%; with active ground cover 0.75/0.70/0.75,
    0.80/0.80/0.80 and 0.85/0.85/0.85. Add 0.1-0.2 in humid climates.
    Stage lengths 60/90/120/95 days (365, from January, Mediterranean)
    [7].
  - **FAO-66 [3]:** published monthly Kc means 0.61-0.76; spring mean
    0.63, summer 0.66, autumn 0.73. Midday stem water potential above
    −1.0 MPa means no stress.
  - **Seasonal ETc:** Finding 4 [3].
- **Propagation:** shield-budded on seedling rootstocks, the bud 5-7.5 cm
  above ground; trees ready after one nursery season; seed used within 3-4
  weeks unless stored [1]. Nucellar seed often comes true, but budding
  keeps cultivars true [1]. Clean stock: budwood registration (Finding 3)
  [1]; certified nursery stock and quarantine for HLB [6].
- **Labor:** hand picking at 6.5 boxes (90 lb) per hour, rising to 9.1
  with fibreglass ladders and abscission agents [1], about 370 kg per
  picker-hour (see Estimates). Other operations: Not sourced this pass.
- **Soil:**
  - **Preferred:** deep, well-drained soil (Lakeland fine sand in
    Florida, deep loams in California) [1].
  - **Water table:** at 78 cm or less, root growth, vigour and yield are
    "greatly reduced" (Egypt) [1].
  - **Alkaline soil:** chlorosis and decline in neglected trees (south
    Florida) [1].
  - **Micronutrients:** copper deficiency causes exanthema, zinc
    mottle-leaf, molybdenum yellow spot [1].
  - **Salinity:** sensitive (S); ECe threshold 1.3 dS/m, slope 13.1% per
    dS/m, for fruit yield [8]. Yield halves at about 5.1 dS/m (see
    Estimates).
- **Storage (postharvest):** Finding 5 [1][2]. Respiration 2-6 mg
  CO2/kg·h at 0 °C, 20-31 at 20 °C [2]. CA of 5-10% O2 + 0-5% CO2 helps
  but is little used because it does not control decay [2]. Fresh-cut
  segments keep about 12 days [2].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** the old standard was 7.5 × 7.5 m; many groves are now
    close-planted and hedged [1].
  - **Density trial:** 215, 358 and 716 trees/ha [1].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Round:** 'Valencia' (late, tropical lowlands), 'Hamlin'
    (cold-tolerant), 'Pineapple' (medium-seedy), 'Shamouti' [1][2].
  - **Navel:** 'Washington Navel'; 'Frost Nucellar' and 'Lane Late' in
    the water trials [1][3].
  - **Blood:** 'Moro', 'Tarocco' [2]. The cold-night colour mechanism
    was not re-sourced this pass.
  - **Acidless:** 'Succari' [2].
  - **Cam xoàn:** see Naming and scope [9]-[13].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Freeze:** see Climate zones [1].
  - **Heat:** June drop [3].
  - **Wet harvest:** turgid fruit picked with dew, fog or after rain gets
    oil spotting (oleocellosis) [2].
- **Water regime:** irrigated or rainfed upland; no flooded regime.
  Waterlogging and a high water table harm roots; wet soil favours
  Phytophthora foot rot [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Legal maturity:** California requires a soluble-solids-to-acid
    ratio of 8 or more (10 if green-yellow) [2][3]. Florida sets minimum
    soluble solids (8.5-9.0%) and ratios (10.0-10.25) by date, 0.4% acid
    and 4.5 gallons of juice per box [2].
  - **On-tree storage:** fruit held for months, with gibberellic acid
    used to prolong it; over-long hanging brings drop and loss of acid
    and flavour [3].
- **Soil biology:** the burrowing nematode causes spreading decline;
  Florida spent over US$21 million in 22 years on tree removal, soil
  treatment and buffer zones [1]. Citrus blight killed up to half a
  million Florida orange trees a year, and root-graft tests showed it is
  infectious [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Weediness:** Not sourced.
  - **HLB:** quarantine and nursery protocols (Finding 2) [6].
  - **Citrus canker** (*Xanthomonas citri* subsp. *citri*): first found in
    the US near the Florida-Georgia border in 1910; found again in
    Miami-Dade in 1995; now throughout Florida and in parts of Alabama,
    Louisiana and Texas; regulated under 7 CFR 301.75 [6]. A 1980s
    nursery outbreak in Florida destroyed over 15 million young trees
    [1].
  - **Sweet orange scab** (*Elsinoë australis*): first found in Texas in
    2010; now in seven states [6].
  - **Fruit flies:** fresh oranges from fruit-fly areas need cold, vapour
    heat or fumigation treatment, or fly-free certification [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Peel oil:** about 90% d-limonene; causes dermatitis, mouth irritation
  and rashes when peeling large amounts; shaking a tree caused hives in
  one case [1].
- **Respiratory:** reactions near blossoms or broken peel; orange-wood
  sawdust has caused asthma [1].
- **Seed extract:** used for malaria in Ecuador; causes respiratory
  depression and contraction of the spleen [1].
- **Postharvest decay:** green and blue mould, stem-end rots, brown rot,
  sour rot and anthracnose [2].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** picked → packinghouse (washed, degreened if
  needed, fungicide, waxed, graded) → precooled by room or forced air →
  stored or shipped [2].
- **Chain 2 (juice):** canned and carton juice, frozen concentrate (now
  the larger product), and juice powder since 1963 [1].
- **Byproducts [1]:** pulp (about three-quarters of extraction
  by-product) pelleted as stockfeed at 6.6-7.0% protein; peel oil for
  flavour, perfume and solvents; pectin; candied peel; seed oil; orange
  flower and petitgrain oils; wine and brandy.
- **Other:** hard yellowish wood for turnery and manicure sticks [1].
- **Not detailed here:** not-from-concentrate juice storage, flavour
  packs, molasses and alcohol from waste.

## Estimates and derivations

**Box weights to t/ha** [4]: California boxes are 80 lb, Florida 90 lb.
California 2025-26: 371 × 80 = 29,680 lb × 0.4536 = 13,463 kg/acre ÷
0.404686 = 33.3 t/ha. 2023-24: 335 × 80 lb → 30.0 t/ha; 2024-25: 317 →
28.4 t/ha. Florida 2025-26: 102 × 90 = 9,180 lb → 10.3 t/ha; 2023-24: 78
→ 7.9 t/ha; 2024-25: 73 → 7.4 t/ha.

**Processed and fresh shares** [4]: Florida processed 16,269 ÷ 18,060 =
90% (2023-24), 11,201 ÷ 12,280 = 91%, 11,587 ÷ 12,950 = 89%. California
fresh 29,145 ÷ 45,400 = 64%, 32,355 ÷ 42,800 = 76%, 32,200 ÷ 49,500 = 65%.

**Florida area loss** [4]: (231,300 − 126,800) ÷ 231,300 = 45%.

**World processing share** [5]: 17.1 ÷ 45.9 = 37%.

**Picking rate** [1]: 9.1 boxes × 90 lb × 0.4536 = 371 kg per hour; 6.5
boxes → 265 kg per hour.

**Density trial per year** [1]: 4,484 boxes/acre × 90 lb × 0.4536 ÷
0.404686 = 452 t/ha over 11 years, about 41 t/ha a year; 2,380 boxes →
240 t/ha, about 22 t/ha a year. Assumes Morton's 90 lb field box.

**Salinity points** [8]: relative yield = 100 − 13.1 × (ECe − 1.3); 50%
at 1.3 + 50 ÷ 13.1 = 5.1 dS/m; zero at 1.3 + 100 ÷ 13.1 = 8.9 dS/m.

**Temperatures** [1]: Morton's °F figures converted as printed (30-26 °F
= −1.1 to −3.3 °C; 25 °F = −3.9 °C; 52 °F = 11.1 °C; 36-39 °F =
2.2-3.9 °C).
