## Naming and scope

*Citrus hassaku* hort. ex Tanaka, a Japanese citrus of the family Rutaceae
[1][4]. The name hassaku (八朔) is the first day of the eighth month of
the old lunar calendar; the story is that the founding priest said the
fruit could be eaten from about then, although at that date the fruit is
in fact still small and inedible [2]. It was first noted in 1860 under
the name "jagada" and named hassaku in 1886 [1][2]. This entry covers
cropID `hassaku`, including its bud sports and selections (Cultivars).

**Parentage.** It has long been described as a pomelo-type natural hybrid
[1][2][6]. An allele-sharing analysis of Japanese citrus (Shimizu and
colleagues, 2016) gives its parents as pomelo (*C. maxima*) and the
Kunenbo mandarin (*C. nobilis*) [1][7]. A self-incompatibility genotyping
study independently places it as a Kunenbo hybrid, with S-genotype S4S5
[7]. The American germplasm collection still records the parents as
unknown, presumed pomelo × mandarin with pomelo predominant [6].

**Gate check (2026-11-19): promoted.** Proposed in `natsudaidai.md`. It is
its own species with its own origin (Innoshima, Hiroshima, about 1860)
[1][2], about 36,000 t a year in 2010-2011 [2], and a different
pollination, storage, and virus story from natsudaidai. It is
self-incompatible and needs a pollinizer [3]. It must *not* be cured
before storage, because curing provokes the rind disorder *kohanshō*
(Finding 2) [3][4]. Its industry was rebuilt around a mild-strain
tristeza cross-protection selection (Finding 3) [3][8]. Its seed is
monoembryonic, where natsudaidai's is polyembryonic [1][6].

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Natsudaidai / amanatsu** (`natsudaidai`): a different Japanese hybrid
  that shares the same prefectural guide chapter and serves as hassaku's
  pollinizer [3]. See that entry.
- **Kunenbo mandarin** (*C. nobilis*), the inferred pollen or seed parent
  [7], also inferred as a parent of satsuma and at least a dozen other
  varieties [7]. It is not covered by this project's `mandarin` row
  (*C. reticulata*). Proposed as its own candidate row (`kunenbo`).
- **Pomelo** (`pomelo`), the other parent: see that entry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — picked unripe, ripened in store, eaten three months
later.** The fruit's natural maturity is February to March, but it is
harvested in mid to late December to avoid cold injury and alternate
bearing [4]. The Yamaguchi guide's harvest window is late December to
early January, with fruit eaten March to April [3]. The encyclopedia
gives harvest December to February, with 1-2 months in cool, dark store
to soften the acid before shipping [2]. Fruit ships mainly March to May,
when other citrus is scarce [4]. In Riverside, California, the same
variety ripens October to November [6]. That is a climate difference,
not a source conflict, but it means the Japanese calendar should not be
read as a species constant.

**Finding 2 — a storage disorder that rules out the usual curing.**
Japanese late citrus such as amanatsu is normally cured to a few percent
weight loss before storage. Hassaku is explicitly *not* cured, "to
prevent *kohanshō*" [3]. Kohanshō is a pitting of the rind: 5 to 9 cell
layers below the epidermis collapse, and the sunken patches then brown
[4]. Its mechanism is unknown [4]. It appears from March in ambient
stores and not at all while fruit is held at 5 °C, but it develops when
cold-stored fruit is moved to ambient temperature [4]. The guide
therefore warms fruit gradually after March [3]. Individual
polyethylene bagging straight after harvest is the main preventive
[3][4]. In an Ehime University trial, conditioning fruit at 35 °C for 24
hours cut affected fruit from 29 of 50 to 3 of 50 after 4 weeks, and
from 35 to 11 of 50 after 8 weeks [4]. Treating fruit immediately after
harvest was much more effective than treating it after storage [4].
Bagging also cut weight loss, from about 15% after 8 weeks unbagged to
3.6% in the best bagged treatment [4]. Vegetable oils also suppress the
disorder [4]. On fruit from trees with tristeza stem pitting, the spots
are wedge-shaped (Finding 3) [4]. A second rind defect, *yani-ka* (oil
spotting), comes from leaf and branch rub damaging the rind during fruit
enlargement after the rainy season, and is managed with windbreaks [3].

**Finding 3 — an industry rescued by a mild virus.** "Hassaku dwarf," a
severe stem-pitting strain of citrus tristeza virus (CTV), spread through
Hiroshima around 1950 [2]. Japanese reviews call it the most severe virus
disease of citrus in Japan [8]. Outside Japan, CTV stunted trees and
destroyed the crop after 15-20 years [1]. A Hiroshima hassaku tree was
found to carry a mild strain, HM-55, isolated by Sasaki and described in
1967 [8]. That tree became the mother tree for nursery stock [8]. The
selection Hassaku No. 55, chosen in 1960 from a mild-strain line on
Innoshima, is described as giving interference (cross-protection)
against severe CTV strains [3]. The Japanese encyclopedia instead
describes No. 55 as "virus-immune" [2]. Those are different claims, and
the cross-protection wording matches the virology literature [8]. HM-55
protected sour orange in trials but not Marsh grapefruit seedlings [8].
At the time no virus-free hassaku tree existed, so cross-protection,
not clean stock, was the only route [8]. The current Yamaguchi guide
still prescribes replanting with mild-strain nursery trees [3]. The
American collection's accession was instead freed of tristeza by
thermotherapy [6].

**Finding 4 — self-incompatible, with seedy, single-embryo seed.**
Hassaku is self-incompatible, so natsudaidai or similar varieties are
interplanted with it [3]. Its S-genotype is S4S5 [7]. The fruit is seedy
and its seeds are monoembryonic [1][6]. Many Japanese citrus, including
its pollinizer natsudaidai, have polyembryonic seed (see
`natsudaidai.md`). Single-embryo seed is sexual, so seedlings will not
come true to type. That is an inference from the embryology, not stated
in the sources. It fits the grafted, selection-based industry in
Finding 3.

**Finding 5 — bitter when cooked, so almost all of it is eaten fresh.**
The bitter compounds are naringin, nomilin, and limonin. Heating
increases the bitterness, so processing has needed ion-exchange
debittering, and nearly all the crop is eaten fresh [2]. The membranes
are thick and are usually peeled off with the rind [2]. The flesh is
low in juice but well flavoured [1][6]. Juice of hassaku inhibited the
drug-metabolising enzyme CYP3A in human liver microsomes, less strongly
than grapefruit (Hazards) [10].

**Finding 6 — a Hiroshima fruit that Wakayama now grows.** The original
seedling arose at Jōdo-ji temple on Innoshima, now part of Onomichi,
Hiroshima [1][2]. A shipping cooperative was founded at Taguma in 1925,
and commercial planting dates from about then [1][2]. Japanese plantings
passed 2,500 acres by 1964, mostly in Hiroshima [1][6]. Wakayama took
up the crop around 1965, starting in Kokawa (now Kinokawa) [9].
Wakayama now produces about 60-70% of the national crop: 60% [1], 68%
[2], and about 70% in one undated source [9]. Production was 15,300 t
from 807 ha in 1979, 35,919 t in 2010, and 36,073 t in 2011 [2]. Wakayama
valued its 2023 hassaku output at 2.3 billion yen, 3% of the
prefecture's fruit output, and 2024 at 3.4 billion yen (4%) [9]. About
2,000 households on Innoshima still grow it [2]. Two parent trees were
moved in 2021 from Mihara to the prefectural agricultural technology
centre in Higashi-Hiroshima [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree, vigorous and upright,
  virtually thornless; leaves large and pomelo-like [1][6].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one crop a year, picked in
  winter and ripened in store (Finding 1) [3][4]. Alternate bearing is a
  stated reason for early picking [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced. The shared fertiliser
  table treats trees as young through year 6 and moves to the mature
  rate from year 8 according to crop load [3].
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate coastal Japan: Wakayama, Hiroshima,
  Ehime, and Tokushima [2]. Harvest before mid-winter avoids cold injury
  [3][4]. Planted increasingly outside Japan from the 1960s [1][6].
  Chill requirement and elevation: Not sourced.
- **Composition:** National food composition tables (8th edition, 2023
  supplement), item 07105, hassaku juice sacs, raw. Refuse is 35%. Per
  100 g edible: 47 kcal, water 87.2 g, protein 0.8 g, fat 0.1 g,
  carbohydrate 11.5 g, fibre 1.5 g, potassium 180 mg, calcium 13 mg,
  vitamin C 40 mg, folate 16 µg, β-cryptoxanthin 170 µg [5].
- **Edibility:** Eaten raw after storage, with the thick membranes
  removed [2]. Bitterness rises on heating (Finding 5) [2].
- **Pollination:** Required. Self-incompatible (S4S5); natsudaidai or
  similar is interplanted as pollinizer [3][7]. Fruit is seedy [1][6].
  Pollinator species and planting ratios: Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Flower buds differentiate
  January to March (shared calendar) [3]. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Target of 4 t per 10 a (40 t/ha) or more every year for
  Hassaku No. 55 in Yamaguchi [3]. The 1979 national figures imply about
  19 t/ha (see Estimates, below) [2]. No recent national area figure
  was found.
- **Water use:** No crop coefficient found. The shared guide irrigates
  after about 15 rainless days in summer [3].
- **Propagation:** Grafted; top-working in early April and side/bud
  grafting until mid-late September (shared guide) [3]. Clonal/viral
  risk is central: nursery trees carry the mild CTV strain for
  cross-protection, and poor strains and old trees are replanted with
  them (Finding 3) [3][8]. Monoembryonic seed does not give true-to-type
  seedlings (Finding 4) [1][6]. The Riverside accession is on Carrizo
  citrange rootstock [6]. Rootstock in Japan: Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced for hassaku. The shared guide's 130.4 h per 10 a
  budget is for amanatsu (see `natsudaidai.md`) [3].
- **Soil:** Not characterised by pH, texture, or salinity in the sources.
  The shared guide applies 100-200 kg of dolomitic lime per 10 a where
  magnesium is short [3]. Mature-orchard fertiliser for hassaku is N 32,
  P2O5 22, K2O 26 kg per 10 a, split 30/25/25/20% across late March, mid
  June, early September, and early November, for a 4 t crop [3]. Young
  trees get 10-20 kg N per 10 a rising from year 3 to year 6 [3].
  Salinity tolerance: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** No curing. Store at 3-5 °C, at 90-95% RH for
  bare fruit or 70-80% for film-wrapped fruit [3]. A simple method lines
  each crate with perforated film (1.8 mm holes at 1.5 × 6 cm spacing,
  0.05 mm thick) and holds fruit at ambient temperature to early April
  [3]. Kohanshō is the main disorder (Finding 2) [3][4]. The American
  collection describes the fruit as storing "moderately well" [6].
- **Establishment:** Shared guide only: replanting at 125 trees per 10 a
  with 1.2-1.5 m working lanes, and lowering trees over 3 m to about 2 m
  [3].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Hassaku No. 55:** Innoshima, selected 1960 from a mild-strain line;
    about 400 g, oblate, yellow-orange, slightly rough rind, firm flesh,
    rather low juice; cross-protects against severe CTV [3].
  - **Nōma beni-hassaku:** a bud sport from Mukaishima (now Onomichi),
    registered December 1976; about 290 g, orange rind, more sugar and
    slightly less acid; upright as a young tree [3]. The encyclopedia
    notes red hassaku being grafted onto No. 55 [2].
  - **Satsuki hassaku:** a Yura (Wakayama) speciality with less
    bitterness [2].

  Both Yamaguchi selections are harvested late December to early
  January and eaten March to April [3]. Fruit size is about 400 g in
  general [2][3], 9-10 cm across [1][6].
- **Weather response:** Cold injury is avoided by December picking
  rather than tolerated [3][4]. Wind rub causes yani-ka, so windbreaks
  are used [3]. Young trees are wrapped for winter and typhoon
  precautions are scheduled (shared guide) [3]. Heat, drought, and
  waterlogging: Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** Ordinary rain-fed orchard with supplementary summer
  irrigation; no flooded requirement [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One stage, picked once, late December to
  January (or December to February), eaten March to May after storage
  [2][3][4]. Not successive ripening.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Arose in Japan; grown elsewhere since
  the 1960s [1][6]. No invasive listing, assessment, or account of escape
  was found in this pass. It is no longer commercially available in
  California, where the accession has no approved budsource; budwood
  goes through the state's clean-stock programme [6]. Domestic movement
  of citrus nursery stock out of Japan's citrus-greening regulated
  islands is subject to inspection (see `daidai.md`).
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Citrus tristeza virus (hassaku dwarf):** the defining disease;
  managed by mild-strain cross-protection (Finding 3) [2][3][8]. No
  yield-loss figure was found beyond the "crop destroyed after 15-20
  years" account [1].
- **Kohanshō and yani-ka:** rind disorders that cut market value
  (Finding 2) [3][4].
- **Drug interaction:** hassaku juice inhibited CYP3A in human liver
  microsomes, less than grapefruit [10]. A hospital drug-information
  bulletin lists hassaku among citrus whose peel contains furanocoumarins
  while the flesh contains little, and notes that content varies even
  within one variety [10]. No clinical interaction study for hassaku was
  found. TO BE ADDRESSED.
- **Citrus canker:** the shared guide's canker programme is aimed mainly
  at natsudaidai [3]; no hassaku-specific canker data was found.
- **Fungal contamination of the edible product:** nothing found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fruit → fresh):** December-January harvest → no curing →
  immediate film-wrapping or perforated-film crates → 3-5 °C or ambient
  store → gradual warming → shipped March-May → peeled, membranes
  removed, juice sacs eaten [2][3][4]. Refuse 35% [5].
- **Chain 2 (fruit → processed food):** limited, because heating raises
  bitterness; ion-exchange debittering has been needed [2]. No product
  list or yield fraction found.
- **Chain 3 (young fruit → extract):** an extract of young fruit has been
  used in skin-care treatment for atopic dermatitis [1]. No dose or
  process data found.
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** a historical and pharmacological study
  of the species (2005) was seen only as a title [1].

## Open research questions

- **Recent production:** no national figure after 2011 [2] was found; the
  government fruit statistics tables were not opened. The Wakayama share
  is given as 60%, 68%, or about 70% by different sources [1][2][9].
- **No. 55's protection:** "virus-immune" [2] vs. cross-protection [3][8].
  Cross-protection is the better-supported reading, but the encyclopedia
  claim is kept visible.
- **Season:** Japanese harvest December-February [2][3][4] against
  October-November ripening in Riverside [6]. Climate explains the
  direction but not the size of the difference.
- **Kohanshō mechanism** is unknown [4]; why immediate bagging or 35 °C
  conditioning works was not explained.
- **Drug interaction:** microsome data only [10].
- **Labour, rootstock, soil pH, salinity, water use, photoperiod,
  elevation, years to bearing, soil biology** were not sourced for
  hassaku.
- **Kunenbo** (`kunenbo`) passed its gate check on 2026-11-19 and is
  researched in `kunenbo.md`.

## Estimates and derivations

**1979 national average yield, about 19 t/ha.** 15,300 t [2] ÷ 807 ha
[2] = 19.0 t/ha. The area may be total rather than bearing area, which
would understate yield per bearing hectare. Against the 40 t/ha
Yamaguchi target [3], that is just under half. Range roughly 19-40 t/ha.
A current statistics table would replace it.

**1964 planted area, about 1,000 ha.** 2,500 acres [1][6] × 0.4047 ha
per acre = 1,012 ha. It was "more than" 2,500 acres, so slightly more.

**Edible portion of a typical fruit, about 260 g.** 400 g fruit [2][3] ×
(1 − 0.35 refuse [5]) = 260 g, or about 120 kcal at 47 kcal per 100 g
[5]. Range about 190-260 g for 290-400 g fruit (Nōma beni-hassaku to
No. 55 [3]).
