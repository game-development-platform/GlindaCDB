# Dwarf Sour Cherry / Canadian Bush Cherry (*Prunus cerasus* × *P. fruticosa*; *P.* × *kerrasis*)

## Naming and scope

The University of Saskatchewan dwarf sour cherries, a deliberate cross
of European sour cherry (*Prunus cerasus*) with the cold-hardy Mongolian
or steppe cherry (*P. fruticosa*) [1][2]. Advanced selections run
roughly 75% sour-cherry parentage to 25% steppe-cherry parentage, with
a proposed hybrid name honoring the breeder who began this work [1].
Named cultivars include an early, widely planted release; a package of
five cultivars released together in the mid-2000s; and several more
recent releases. Excluded from this entry: a separate, hardy standard
sour-cherry tree cultivar (treated under this project's own sour-cherry
entry, since it is a straight *P. cerasus* selection rather than this
interspecific hybrid); hybrid seedlings of a different wild cherry
species sold under a similar "hardy cherry" marketing name, which mostly
require cross-pollination and are not part of this breeding program;
and pure specimens of the wild steppe-cherry parent species itself. This
crop draws heavily on standard sour cherry's biology for water-use and
other analogies, since both are closely related and this hybrid's own
direct research is comparatively thin; this project treats sour cherry
as a closely related crop it has also researched, without naming it
directly here, since forks researching this fruit category may not
share a stable completion order.

This is a genuinely distinct crop from standard sour cherry, not a
minor variant, differing in climate hardiness, plant form, harvest
method, fruit sugar content, and its own established industry
identity: it is hardy to a notably colder climate zone than ordinary
sour cherry; it grows as an own-rooted, suckering shrub rather than a
grafted tree, trained and renewed very differently; it is commonly
harvested by over-the-row berry-type mechanical harvesters or by
shaking onto catch frames, rather than the trunk-shaker equipment
standard sour cherry orchards use; its fruit reaches roughly twice the
sugar concentration of standard tart cherries; and it supports its own
distinct, geographically concentrated commercial industry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — roughly sixty years of deliberate breeding put a
genuinely new sour-cherry crop on the Canadian prairies.** Breeding work
crossing the two parent species began in the 1940s at a Canadian
research station; a university breeding program later imported further
cold-hardy hybrid seed in the 1960s and inherited the earlier breeder's
selections in the 1980s [1][2]. Further crosses with a hardy standard
sour-cherry cultivar and with Hungarian sour cherries produced bushes
combining good size with good fruit quality [1][2]. The breeding and
testing sites themselves sit in some of the coldest horticultural
climate zones used for any fruit crop this project has researched. The
crop is self-fruitful, though bees are still needed to actually move
pollen between flowers [1][4]. Disease pressure is documented as
extremely rare in the core growing region, with the main pest concerns
instead being deer browsing, mouse and vole girdling of stems, cherry
fruit fly, and a invasive fruit fly species [2][4][10][11].

**Finding 2 — yields start slow, then jump sharply.** In a bush's
first blooming year it sets only a light crop, but the following year's
yield typically jumps dramatically — a handful of fruit in year 3-4 can
become many kilograms the following year [5]. Mature, well-managed
bushes commonly yield roughly 11-14 kg each by year 4-6 under typical
growing conditions, though yields recorded on heavy clay soil or under
very close spacing ran noticeably lower, and exceptionally pampered
backyard bushes have yielded far more [5][3]. A separate regional
source documents lower typical yields several years after planting
[9], while a multi-year, multi-site US trial documented clear
per-plant yield increases across several seasons starting from year 3,
varying meaningfully by cultivar [11]. The crop also shows a biennial
tendency, where a very heavy crop is often followed by a lighter one
[5].

**Finding 3 — fruit sugar content builds the longer it is left to
hang.** Sugar content rises substantially from an early-season reading
to a late-season reading as the season progresses [2]. Sugar content
and harvest timing both vary by cultivar and weather — one widely
planted fresh-eating cultivar reaches notably higher sugar content than
the earliest-ripening cultivar, though wet years reduce this advantage
[6][5]. Fruit picked too early, at a red color stage, has only about
half the sugar and roughly twice the acidity of fully ripe, near-black
fruit [5]. Peak flavor lasts only about two weeks at the core growing
region, typically in the first half of August, though the full harvest
window for a single cultivar can span three to four weeks [3][1].
Notably, this fruit retains a markedly higher share of its juice after
freezing (roughly 60-66%) compared to roughly 40% for standard sour
cherry — a genuine processing advantage [1][8].

**Finding 4 — successful management is fundamentally about winter
survival.** Recommended siting includes shelter from prevailing winter
winds, well-drained loam or sandy loam soil, and at least half-day sun
[3][1]. Establishment calls for weed-free mulched ground and even
watering for the first three years, shifting to deeper, less frequent
watering thereafter [3][1]. Critically, growers avoid any fertilizing
or pruning in late summer or fall, since this reduces winter hardiness;
pruning is instead done in early spring, using only thinning cuts and
removing at most about a quarter of the wood in a given year [1][3].
The bush is deliberately allowed to form multiple stems from root
suckers rather than trained to a single trunk — individual older
trunks do die back in severe winters, but a multi-stem bush recovers
within a year or two, where a single-trunked planting would take roughly
five years to recover from the same damage [3]. A documented severe
winter event showed this cultivar-to-cultivar hardiness difference
clearly: some cultivars suffered no damage at all, while others suffered
meaningful dieback in the very same conditions [6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An own-rooted, deciduous, suckering shrub,
  roughly 1.75-2.5 m tall (up to about 3 m in trial plantings);
  perennial, blooming mid-to-late spring with a single annual harvest
  in late summer.
- **Climate:** Cool temperate, continental prairie climate; hardy to a
  notably cold horticultural zone, markedly colder than standard sour
  cherry's typical hardiness limit.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible when fully ripe. Estimated
  composition (roughly 650 kcal/kg, 10 g protein/kg) reflects this
  fruit's notably higher sugar content compared to standard sour
  cherry.
- **Pollination:** Self-fruitful, but bees are still needed to transfer
  pollen within and between flowers; bloom timing differs by several
  days to about a week across different cultivars, creating some frost-
  timing risk variation between them.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure and good, exceptional, and poor
  tiers are derived from documented per-bush yield figures across
  several regional sources, combined with an estimated standard planting
  density, since no direct per-hectare yield study was found (see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from
  standard sour cherry, adjusted for this crop's smaller shrub size,
  since no species-specific water-use study was found. Even, deep
  watering is recommended through the first three establishment years;
  established bushes tolerate somewhat less water but are vulnerable to
  fruit splitting if a dry spell is followed by sudden heavy rain or
  irrigation near ripening. Drought tolerance is judged moderate, and
  waterlogging tolerance is judged low, given the stated need for good
  drainage.
- **Propagation:** Entirely vegetative — tissue-culture plug plants or
  dug root suckers, never grafted, so there is no graft-incompatibility
  risk; a single bush can produce anywhere from zero to over twenty
  suckers, averaging around seven, each genetically identical to the
  parent.
- **Soil:** Loam and sandy loam soils are best; clay soils are
  workable given good drainage. Fertilizing is done only in spring,
  guided by soil testing, since excess nitrogen promotes vegetative
  growth at the expense of winter hardiness.
- **Labor:** Harvest method dominates total labor cost — hand-picking
  is markedly slower than using a catch-frame technique (two workers
  can process a heavily laden bush in a few minutes with a catch frame,
  versus half an hour or more by hand), and over-the-row mechanical
  harvesting is faster still at commercial scale. Other labor
  (establishment, spring pruning, mulching, weed control, deer and
  rodent protection) is comparatively modest.
- **Storage:** Short — fruit should be picked in the cool morning hours
  and refrigerated as quickly as possible; a simple water-float test
  identifies maggot-infested fruit, since affected fruit floats.
- **Stand life:** No specific commercial stand-life figure was found;
  the bush form is continually renewed from root suckers rather than
  aging as a single trunk, suggesting a potentially long productive
  life, though this was not directly documented.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Disease pressure is documented as extremely
  rare in the core growing region; documented issues include bacterial
  canker, leaf spot, and rarely powdery mildew, brown rot, and a wilt
  disease, alongside deer browsing, vole and mouse girdling, cherry
  fruit fly (whose maggot-infested fruit can be identified by floating
  in water), and an invasive fruit fly species of growing regional
  concern.
- **Cultivars:** A widely planted early-ripening baseline cultivar; a
  package of five cultivars released together, differing in bloom and
  ripening timing, fruit size, sugar content, winter hardiness, and
  best end use (fresh eating, juice, or processing); and several more
  recent releases not yet well characterized in the sources reviewed.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable as a field-scale
  commercial crop in a controlled environment; pot culture was not
  researched.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include bacterial canker, leaf spot,
rarely powdery mildew, brown rot, and a wilt disease, alongside deer
browsing, vole and mouse girdling, cherry fruit fly, and an invasive
fruit fly species of growing regional concern.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other *Prunus*-genus stone fruit, the seed
kernel is understood to contain cyanogenic compounds consistent with
the broader cherry-genus hazard profile; no cultivar-specific data
beyond this general genus-level concern was found. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit harvested by machine, shaking onto a catch frame,
  or hand-picking → water-float sorted (maggot-infested fruit floats)
  → chilled promptly; pitted fruit → pie filling, jam and jelly
  (notably higher natural pectin content than many fruits), or pressed
  into juice → further processed into wine or dried fruit; root suckers
  → dug and replanted as new bushes.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-fruit production in
  detail (beyond the documented superior juice-retention advantage);
  tissue-culture plug production itself; liqueur and gelato production.

No religious or ceremonial use was found in the sources reviewed for
this crop.

## Open research questions

- No detailed per-hectare yield, standard row-spacing, or planting-
  density study was found; all per-hectare figures in this entry are
  derived from per-bush figures and an estimated planting density
  rather than a direct commercial study.
- A detailed commercial-production guide for this crop exists only as
  a print publication and was not accessible for this research; it
  would likely resolve several of the weaker estimates in this entry.
- Parentage description differs slightly between sources (one recent
  grower-facing source describes a somewhat different cross than the
  more detailed breeding-history sources) — this entry follows the more
  detailed breeding-history account.
- The specific winter-cold kill threshold used in this entry reflects a
  general cold-hardiness-zone classification rather than a directly
  measured kill point; actual documented winter-damage patterns by
  cultivar provide more specific, though qualitative, evidence.
- A specific commercial productive stand-life figure was not found.
- Soil pH guidance and detailed nutrient-uptake figures are carried
  over by analogy from standard sour cherry, since no cultivar-specific
  figures were found.
- Standard commercial row spacing for mechanical over-the-row
  harvesting equipment was not found; the planting-density estimate
  used here is derived from general bush-spacing guidance rather than a
  row-crop-specific source.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein composition figures are a rough derivation starting
from standard sour cherry's documented composition, adjusted upward to
reflect this crop's substantially higher average sugar content, since no
direct composition analysis specific to this hybrid crop was found.

Yield tiers are derived from documented per-bush yield figures across
several regional sources (ranging from a lower per-plant figure in one
region to a higher mature-bush average in another, with an exceptional
figure from a documented pampered backyard planting and a poor figure
scaled down accordingly), combined with an estimated standard planting
density of roughly 1,480 bushes per hectare, itself derived from
documented bush-spacing guidance and an assumed row width, since no
sourced commercial density figure was found.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index,
stage lengths) are carried over directly from standard sour cherry's
documented figures, adjusted qualitatively downward to reflect this
crop's smaller shrub size and correspondingly lower total water use per
hectare, since no species-specific water-use study was found. Drought
tolerance is judged moderate, reflecting documented guidance that
established bushes become more tolerant of reduced water than young
plantings.

Growth-stage curve and stand-life parameters are rough estimates
reflecting the documented pattern of little yield until year 3, a sharp
jump in year 4, and full production by years 5-6; continual renewal
from root suckers suggests a potentially long overall stand life, though
no specific figure was found.

Labor-hour estimates are derived from a documented catch-frame
harvesting-rate comparison against hand-picking, scaled across tiers to
represent hand-picking, catch-frame harvesting, and full mechanical
over-the-row harvesting respectively; other labor categories
(establishment, cultural care, post-harvest handling) are estimated by
analogy to standard sour cherry and other bush-fruit crops, since no
dedicated cost study for this specific hybrid crop was found.

Soil pH tolerance range is carried over by analogy from standard sour
cherry, since no cultivar-specific pH guidance was found for this
hybrid crop.

Standard storage duration is carried over by analogy from standard sour
cherry's documented short fresh shelf life, since no cultivar-specific
storage-duration study was found for this hybrid crop.

Peak harvest rate is derived from the world-average yield figure divided
by a documented harvest-window length (the roughly two-week peak-flavor
period within a documented three-to-four-week total harvest window);
standing-crop duration and its slow decay rate reflect the documented
tendency of ripe fruit to remain on the bush for weeks, gradually drying
rather than dropping.
