## Naming and scope

*Mangifera casturi* Kosterm. (Anacardiaceae), described by Kostermans in
1993 from a Kalimantan type specimen [1][11]. The epithet comes from the
local name *kasturi* [1].
- **Names:** kasturi; Kalimantan mango (English) [1][2]; kastooree, the
  spelling used at Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden [5]; 'Casturi' in
  the US germplasm literature [4]; mangga kasturi (Indonesian) [11].
- **Local types:** people in South Kalimantan know several forms by
  their own names: Kasturi, Cuban (also Kasturi Mawar), Pelipisan and
  Pinari [2][7]. Rawa-rawa is a closely related species grown alongside
  them [2][7].
- **Name clash:** "kasturi" covers more than one fruit. Local people use
  it for all five of the mangoes above [7], so a market "kasturi" may be
  any of them.
- **Range:** endemic to a small area around Banjarmasin in South
  Kalimantan, Borneo [1][4]. It is now known only in cultivation, mainly
  in Banjar and Hulu Sungai Selatan regencies [11]. Outside Indonesia it
  is held in botanic-garden and germplasm collections, including Miami
  and Hawaii [1][3][4][6].
- **Conservation status:** Extinct in the Wild on the IUCN Red List (2016
  assessment [1]). The World Conservation Monitoring Centre placed it in
  that category in 1998 [8][11].

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from
`bachang.md` [14]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a recognized species [1], though genomic work proposes it
  arose by repeated natural crossing of mango (*M. indica*) and *M.
  quadrifida* (Finding 1) [2]. It is not a mango cultivar, and it is not
  close to bachang.
- **Range:** a single province of Borneo, extinct in the wild [1][11].
  No other researched crop in this project has that range.
- **Farming system:** old trees in mixed gardens with rice and other
  crops, on dry land and tidal swamp (Finding 2) [8][11]. Suited to the
  ever-wet tropics, where mango struggles (Finding 3) [3][9].
- **Harvest:** one crop a year at the start of the rains, with most fruit
  in January [8][11]. Mango in this project is driven by a dry season
  [14].
- **Uses:** a small, purple-black, strongly scented fruit eaten fresh and
  processed locally [7][8]. It is also used as a graft interstock for
  other wild mangoes [5].

**Relatives in this project:**
- mango (`mango`, `mango.md`): the same genus, and a proposed parent
  [2][14].
- bachang (`bachang`, `bachang.md`): the proposing doc. Bachang (*M.
  foetida*, locally hambawang) was one of the related species compared
  with kasturi in the 2021 genetic study [2][14].
- binjai (`binjai`, `binjai.md`): another Kalimantan wild mango [14].

**Proposed:** none new. The other local types (Cuban, Pelipisan, Pinari)
belong in this doc. Rawa-rawa is a separate species, but no use beyond
local eating was found for it [2][7].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a hybrid swarm, not one uniform fruit.**
- **Origin:** an SNP study proposed that kasturi is a natural hybrid of
  *M. indica* and *M. quadrifida*. A 2021 study using microsatellites and
  DNA barcoding supports it [2].
- **Types:** four types (Kasturi, Cuban, Pelipisan, Pinari) were
  identified. They likely arose from repeated hybridization with several
  *Mangifera* species [2].
- **Morphology:** a 2024 study of 26 trees in Banjar grouped Kasturi,
  Pelipisan, Cuban and Rawa-rawa together, and Pinari apart [7]. Fruit
  shape, size, skin colour, flesh texture and flavour separate the types
  [7].
- **No registered varieties:** none of the types has been confirmed or
  registered by the authorities [2].

**Finding 2 — old garden trees with few replacements.**
- **Where it grows:** mixed gardens also planted with rice, cassava,
  bamboo, banana, citrus and rubber [8]. In Mataraman (Banjar) the
  gardens are rice fields with kasturi trees more than 50 years old,
  which residents did not deliberately plant [11]. It grows on dry land
  and on tidal swamp land [8][11].
- **Decline:**
  - **Ageing trees:** many trees are old, and yields are falling [11].
  - **Land use:** land is being converted to plantations, settlements
    and industry, and forests are being illegally logged [8].
  - **Farmers:** they are reluctant to plant it because it takes so long
    to bear. Some owners have considered felling it to plant rubber,
    citrus or rice [13] (snippet).
- **Size:** the tree grows tall and large, which makes it hard to fit in
  a house yard like the common mangoes [11].
- **Conservation planting:**
  - **Sultan Adam Forest Park:** a BGCI-supported project documented 36
    trees there. It propagated 41 plants conventionally and 50 by
    tissue culture [10].
  - **Barito Kuala:** a local-fruit park in Muara Kanoco village grows
    it with other South Kalimantan fruits [11].
  - **Agricultural services:** local services are surveying trees and
    propagating them for the community [6].

**Finding 3 — a mango for the wet tropics, but an irregular bearer
abroad.**
- **Climate:** a plant of the moist tropics with heavy rainfall and no
  dry season. It "seems to be immune to anthracnose" and is rarely
  attacked by beetles [9] (citing Kostermans and Bompard).
- **Hawaii:** a local nursery introduced it as a tree that might give
  mango-like fruit in wet climates without anthracnose [3]. Florida fruit
  were relatively free of anthracnose 7 days after harvest [4].
- **Bearing:**
  - **Bogor:** a seed-grown tree in the Bogor Botanic Gardens first bore
    after 10 years and then bore regularly twice a year [3] (citing
    Kostermans and Bompard).
  - **South Florida:** it does not flower consistently [3].
  - **Hawaii:** one tree planted in 2002 fruited in only one year, 2015
    [3].
  - **Outlook:** the study's authors see irregular bearing in Florida and
    Hawaii as a problem for commercial production [3].
- **South Kalimantan:** fruits once a year at the start of the rainy
  season, around January [8][11].

**Finding 4 — a small, sweet, strongly scented fruit.**
- **Fruit:** ovoid, 5-7 cm long, thin-skinned, bright green with dark
  spots, ripening purple-black [1][8][11]. The flesh is dark orange,
  fibrous, sweet and fragrant [1][8][9].
- **Weight:** fruit size varies by place (see Estimates):
  - **South Kalimantan:** about 65 g [11], and a mean of 75 g across eight
    trees [7].
  - **Abroad:** 92-122 g in Miami, mean about 105 g [4]; about 100 g in
    Hawaii [3].
  - **Older figure:** 50-84 g [1].
- **Quality (Miami):** firm flesh, Brix 17.8-20.6, pH 4.5, titratable
  acidity 1.09-1.15% [4].
- **Types compared:** the Kasturi type has strong aroma and a strong
  turpentine flavour. Cuban is very juicy with a milder aroma. Pinari is
  the largest (145 g), with softer, less juicy flesh [7].
- **Nutrition:** in a five-species comparison, *M. casturi* pulp had the
  best overall nutritional quality, ahead of two *M. indica* cultivars
  [12] (abstract only; no values seen).

**Finding 5 — a graft interstock for other wild mangoes.**
- **Success rates:** at Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, kasturi grafted
  on mango 'Turpentine' rootstock succeeded in 90% or more of grafts. *M.
  griffithii* grafted on top of it then succeeded in 90% [5].
- **Other species:** it also worked as an interstock for *M. lalijiwa*
  and *M. merillii* [5]. Bachang and bambangan still failed on mango,
  and other interstocks were being tried for them [5][14].

**Finding 6 — fruit fly: damaged fruit only, in one season.**
- **Survey:** in Hawaii in July 2015, oriental fruit fly (*Bactrocera
  dorsalis*) was trapped in the kasturi tree every week [3].
- **Infestation:**
  - **On the tree:** none of the 40 undamaged fruits was infested; one
    cracked fruit was [3].
  - **On the ground:** 3 of 40 fallen fruits were infested [3].
- **Conclusion:** kasturi counts as a "suitable host". Undamaged fruit on
  the tree might be a conditional non-host, but that needs more sampling
  [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen tree [1][9].
  - **Height:** 10-30 m [1][4], up to 25 m [9], or 25-50 m [8]. Measured
    Banjar trees of the Kasturi type averaged 24 m [7].
  - **Bole:** clear for up to 15 m, 40-115 cm across [8][9].
  - **Crown:** half-round, with spreading branches [7].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial; one crop a year in South Kalimantan
  [8], twice a year in Bogor [3]. Irregular in Florida and Hawaii [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 10 years for a seed-grown tree in
  Bogor [3]. Grafted trees: Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** ever-wet lowland tropics (Finding 3) [9]. Chill
  requirement: Not sourced (tropical).
- **Composition (per 100 g edible portion):** Not sourced as values. A
  comparative study ranks it highest of five *Mangifera* species [12].
  Brix 17.8-20.6 [4].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible when ripe [1][8].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this species. *Mangifera* flowers are
  hermaphrodite or male, so self-crossing can occur [2]. The panicle is
  about 28 cm long [8].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced per tree or per hectare.
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Needs rain with no dry season [9]; grows on
  tidal swamp as well as dry land [8][11].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** one or several seedlings from one seed (polyembryonic)
    [1][9]. Conflict: the 2024 Banjar survey recorded monoembryonic seed
    for all five types [7].
  - **Grafting:** grafts readily on mango (Finding 5) [5].
  - **Tissue culture:** propagated this way [1][10].
  - **Clonal risk:** Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced as type. The 2024 project recorded soil pH,
  moisture and temperature as growth factors, with no values given [10].
  Salinity: Not sourced. It grows on tidal swamp land [8].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced as temperature or shelf life.
  Little anthracnose 7 days after harvest [4].
- **Establishment:** Not sourced (spacing).
- **Cultivars:** Kasturi, Cuban, Pelipisan, Pinari (Finding 1) [2][7].
  None is registered [2].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced for kasturi. A *M. lalijiwa* tree on
  the same Hawaii farm bore better in years that were drier at flowering
  [3].
- **Water regime:** rain-fed [8][11].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **South Kalimantan:** at the start of the rains, most fruit in
    January [8][11].
  - **Miami:** harvested June-July 2012 [4].
  - **Hawaii:** fruit collected in July 2015 [3].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** no invasive listing found in this pass.
  - **Fruit fly:** a field host of oriental fruit fly (Finding 6) [3],
    which matters for moving fruit.
  - **Seed import:** US restrictions on importing *Mangifera* seed are
    covered in `bachang.md` [14].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Sap and skin contact:** none sourced for kasturi. The Fern database
  lists "None known" [9]. Mango's sap and peel cause urushiol-type
  dermatitis (`mango.md`) [14]. Whether kasturi, a proposed mango hybrid
  [2], does the same is unknown.
- **Food safety:** none sourced.
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for kasturi.
- **Medicinal claims:** bark and leaf extracts are described as
  antibacterial, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, and are said to be
  used in folk medicine [7][8]. These are reported uses, not tested
  treatments.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — whether the sap or peel is a skin irritant, as
  with mango, bachang and binjai [14]. The project's hazard model has no
  field for crop handling hazards yet (the same gap as bachang, bambangan
  and binjai).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh):** garden tree → fruit picked when purple-black →
  local market [6][8] → eaten fresh [1][8].
- **Chain (processed):** ripe fruit → jam, syrup, juice, *manisan*
  (sweet preserves) and other products [8].
- **Chain (grafting):** seedling mango rootstock → kasturi interstock →
  scion of a wild *Mangifera* species that fails directly on mango [5].
- **Timber:** used for its wood [1]; the sapwood is pale yellow [9].
- **Price:** about Rp 7,500/kg, "not far different" from mango, in an
  undated Ministry of Agriculture document [13] (snippet).
- **Medicinal:** see Hazards [7][8].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. It is South Kalimantan's
  official flora emblem, under Minister of Home Affairs decree 48 of
  1989 [11].

## Open research questions

- **Origin:** a hybrid of mango × *M. quadrifida*, or a species with
  later introgression? The 2021 ITS results partly contradict the hybrid
  hypothesis [2].
- **Seed embryony:** polyembryonic [1][9] or monoembryonic [7]? It
  matters for true-to-type seedlings.
- **Bole size:** the 2024 survey gives stem "diameter" of 305 cm for the
  Kasturi type [7], against 40-115 cm elsewhere [9]. The 305 cm may be a
  circumference.
- **Fruit size:** why are fruit grown in Miami and Hawaii [3][4]
  heavier than fruit from South Kalimantan [7][11]? Possible causes are
  type, tree age or orchard care.
- **Composition values:** the 2017 comparison [12] was not read in full.
- **Years to bearing and yield** of grafted trees: Not sourced.
- **Hazard:** sap and peel irritancy (see Hazards).
- **Works identified, not yet read:**
  - Kostermans, A.J.G.H. and Bompard, J.M., *The Mangoes: Their Botany,
    Nomenclature, Horticulture and Utilization* (Academic Press, 1993),
    the *M. casturi* entry.
  - Rhodes, L. and Maxted, N., "*Mangifera casturi*," *The IUCN Red List
    of Threatened Species* (2016), doi:10.2305/IUCN.UK.2016-3.RLTS.T32059A61526819.en.
  - Ledesma, N. and Arisandi, N., *Acta Horticulturae* 1415 (2025), full
    text [6].
  - Gunawan and colleagues, ethnobotany and conservation of kasturi by
    the Banjar people, *Biodiversitas* (2024) [13].

## Estimates and derivations

**Fruit weight by place.** Hawaii [3], from the collection table:
- **Undamaged fruit on the tree:** 4.08 kg / 40 fruits = 102 g.
- **Undamaged fruit on the ground:** 4.30 kg / 40 = 108 g.
- **Comparison:** this is close to the Miami mean of about 105 g [4], and
  about 1.4-1.6 times the South Kalimantan figures of 65 g [11] and 75 g
  [7]: 102 / 75 = 1.36; 102 / 65 = 1.57.

**Stone share, about 14-20% of fruit weight.**
- **South Kalimantan:** seed 10.4 g / fruit 75.45 g [7] = 13.8%.
- **Miami:** stone 20.78 g / fruit 105 g [4] = 19.8%.

So flesh plus skin is about 80-86%. Skin weight is not sourced, so the
edible share is lower than this.

**Edible flesh per fruit, under about 60-90 g.** 75 g × 0.86 = 65 g
(South Kalimantan); 105 g × 0.80 = 84 g (Miami). These are upper bounds,
since skin is not subtracted.
