# Sweet Granadilla (*Passiflora ligularis*)

## Naming and scope

*Passiflora ligularis*, a passionflower-family vine known as granadilla
in several Andean countries, or "sweet granadilla" in English to avoid a
specific regional naming clash: in at least one Southern Hemisphere
country, "granadilla" instead refers to a purple-fruited passionfruit
species this project has also researched, a crop with a notably different
product profile. Native to the Andes, and documented as invasive in
several island nations and archipelagos outside its native range. No
commercial cultivars exist — this is an open-pollinated species, with
types distinguished informally by fruit size, shape, and shell
appearance. A giant passionflower-family fruit and a banana-shaped
passionflower-family fruit remain open research candidates, not yet
covered by this project.

This entry passes this project's distinctiveness check clearly against
its passionflower-family siblings (a yellow- and a purple-fruited
passionfruit this project has also researched): it is its own dessert
fruit with a hard, brittle shell, cracked open and eaten fresh for its
sweet, low-acid pulp, whereas the yellow-fruited sibling is mainly a
juice crop and the purple-fruited sibling a tart fresh fruit; it carries
its own national production statistics and is a meaningful agricultural
export in at least one producing region; its flowers lack sufficient
capacity to self-pollinate and depend on large bees or hand pollination,
unlike the self-compatible purple-fruited sibling; it is climacteric and
stores for roughly three to four weeks at a specific cool temperature,
a notably different storage profile from either passionfruit sibling;
and it is grown at a cooler, higher elevation than its passionflower-
family siblings.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an Andean highland vine grown on an overhead trellis for a
fresh dessert fruit.** Documented optimal growing conditions place this
crop at a specific mid-elevation band in the Andes, with pests increasing
and fruit size dropping below that band, and fungal problems reducing
the number of harvests above it [3]. A moderate temperature range, several
hours of daily sun, moderately high humidity, light wind, and a mildly
acidic to neutral loam soil are all documented as optimal [3][2]. The vine
is trained onto a flat overhead wire trellis structure supported on posts;
documented planting densities vary considerably between sources, from a
recommended wider spacing down to a notably denser spacing used in at
least one field trial [2][4][8]. First harvest comes about a year after
transplanting, with the planting remaining productive for roughly five to
eight years, sometimes longer [2][4]. This is a semi-perennial vine with
flowering and fruiting cycles that overlap continuously throughout the
year, rather than a single discrete annual season [3].

**Finding 2 — a well-documented phenology with specific day counts.** In
the nursery, seedlings emerge roughly three weeks after sowing and are
transplanted once the first tendril appears, at roughly two months [3].
In the field, the shift from vegetative to reproductive growth comes
roughly nine to ten months after transplanting, with full flowering
reached considerably later still [3]. After flowering, fruit reaches full
size within about the first month, continues filling with pulp for
roughly three months, and reaches peak commercial maturity roughly three
months after flowering, with slightly different exact day counts given
across sources [1][2][3]. In commercial plantings, three distinct growth
cycles run simultaneously at any given time: fruit actively ripening,
flowers setting into young fruit, and new reproductive branches forming
[3].

**Finding 3 — documented yields span a very wide range.** A national
yield average from this crop's leading Andean producing country sits at
the lower end of documented figures, with a separate historical average
figure running meaningfully higher [3][2]. A specific one-hectare field
trial produced yields in its first three harvests that its own author
characterized as below expectations once averaged across a full bearing
year [4]. Separate agricultural trade-press claims report yields several
times higher than any directly measured trial figure, which this entry
treats as an unverified upper-bound claim rather than a reliable figure
[4]. A separate source gives yet another, intermediate yield range [8].
National growing area and production in this crop's leading producing
country grew measurably over a documented multi-year period [2].

**Finding 4 — detailed labor figures sourced from a field-trial cost
study.** A specific one-hectare trial's cost accounting documents labor
inputs broken out by establishment activities (land clearing, planting,
fertilizing, spraying, irrigation, drainage, trellis construction, and
pruning) and by recurring maintenance cycles thereafter (fertilizing,
pest control, weeding, ring-weeding, thinning, and monitoring, performed
by a permanent part-time worker), plus a documented piece-rate harvest
and packing wage structure [4].

**Finding 5 — dry-season physiological disorders, and a dependence on
large bees.** This crop has shallow fibrous roots, making it drought-
intolerant; serious physiological damage begins below a specific soil
water-potential threshold, and drought reduces flower-bud formation more
than it causes existing flower drop [3]. Documented dry-season disorders
include sunscald (brown patches, blistering, and sunken gray or yellow
skin areas), flower drop, fruit cracking, and several specific nutrient
deficiencies; documented prevention measures include leaving foliage
over the fruit for shade, avoiding heavy pruning near harvest time,
collecting fallen fruit multiple times daily, irrigating, and supplying
extra calcium [3]. Low humidity combined with hot wind dries out floral
stigmas and wilts flowers, while higher humidity favors pollen viability,
stigma receptivity, and bee activity [3]. Hand pollination is documented
as equaling good natural bee pollination in effectiveness, and one
source specifically warns that a decline in large-bee populations would
threaten the crop's viability [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Vigorous perennial climbing vine, a semi-
  perennial production cycle lasting under six years, with stems
  reaching several meters in length.
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous/overlapping harvest, since flowering
  and fruiting occur in overlapping cycles essentially year-round, though
  at least one source describes two identifiable main harvest peaks per
  year; harvested at a single commercial-maturity stage, a climacteric
  fruit that can be picked somewhat before full ripeness and finishes
  ripening off the vine.
- **Climate:** Tropical highland (Andean, a specific mid-elevation band)
  as the primary growing zone; subtropical uplands in this crop's
  documented invasive range outside its native area.
- **Composition:** Composition figures here are estimated by analogy to
  this crop's passionflower-family sibling, since no dedicated food-
  composition table was opened for this species specifically; this
  fruit's pulp is noted elsewhere as sweeter and less acidic than that
  sibling's.
- **Pollination:** Required — flowers lack sufficient capacity for self-
  pollination and depend on large bees (carpenter bees, bumblebees) or,
  where such bees are scarce, hand pollination, which is documented as
  equally effective.
- **Yield:** An unusually wide range from a national average up through
  a historical higher average, a directly measured field-trial figure,
  and an unverified trade-press claim at the high end (Finding 3; see
  Estimates below for adopted tier values).
- **Water use:** No species-specific standard crop-coefficient curve was
  found; water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to this crop's
  passionflower-family sibling, given the shared genus, vine-on-trellis
  growth habit, and shallow rooting depth, adjusted for this species'
  documented shallower minimum effective soil depth and lower drought-
  damage threshold. Documented seasonal rainfall requirements vary
  considerably across sources, from a lower range up to a notably higher
  range in at least one source's narrative text — a conflict not
  resolved here.
- **Propagation:** By direct seeding into nursery beds, transplanted at
  the first-tendril stage; an open-pollinated species with no commercial
  hybrid varieties, cross-pollinated by large bees, selected informally
  for fruit size, shape, and shell appearance.
- **Labor:** Reasonably well documented via a single detailed field-trial
  cost study (Finding 4), though this is one trial's experience rather
  than an industry-wide survey, and its yields were below that trial's
  own expectations, which likely inflates the resulting labor-per-tonne
  figures somewhat.
- **Soil:** A narrow, mildly acidic to neutral pH preference; well-
  drained loam, sandy loam, or clay loam soils at least a specific
  minimum depth; a documented fertilization program for one field trial
  included organic matter at planting and twice yearly thereafter, plus
  specific mineral and foliar nutrient applications on a recurring
  schedule.
- **Stand life:** First harvest at roughly twelve months after
  transplanting; productive for roughly five to eight years, sometimes
  described as longer; an overall semi-perennial production cycle of
  under six years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Sunscald, flower drop, fruit cracking, and
  several nutrient-deficiency disorders are the main documented dry-
  season problems (Finding 5); fruit-fly-type pests favored by dense,
  unpruned canopy, and fungal disease pressure that rises above the
  optimal elevation band.
- **Cultivars:** No commercial varieties exist; informal local selections
  vary in shell color and fruit size, and a collection of elite genotypes
  has been assembled in this crop's leading producing country, though
  that collection effort was not researched in detail.
- **Harvest:** Fruit is cut from the vine with scissors, keeping the
  stem attached, during cool, bright, humid hours; fallen fruit is
  collected from the ground multiple times daily; graded under a
  specific national quality-grading standard with defined defect-area
  thresholds for different quality categories.
- **Storage:** Roughly three to four weeks at a specific cool
  temperature and high humidity, or one to two weeks at room temperature
  thanks to the fruit's hard protective shell.

## Hazards

- A cyanogenic-glycoside toxin class is documented in unripe fruit and
  rind of this crop's passionflower-family sibling, but this was not
  independently researched for this species specifically, and this entry
  does not extend that hazard to this species without a direct source.
  **TO BE ADDRESSED** — worth a dedicated check against a species-
  specific source before this crop is treated as food-safe in all growth
  stages.
- This species is documented as invasive outside its native range in
  several island nations and archipelagos; this is an ecological/
  regulatory concern rather than a food-safety hazard.
- No consumer feed-safety hazards were found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit → cut from the vine with the stem attached → graded
  under a national quality standard → sold fresh or placed in cool
  storage. Culls and surplus fruit → blanched briefly → shell removed →
  pulp → either pasteurized into nectar or cooked down into a compote/
  preserve, with a documented pulp-recovery yield from whole fruit.
  Pulp from open-pollinated selections → seed for nursery propagation.
- Not covered in detail by this research: covered/greenhouse production
  systems; juice, ice cream, and yogurt product lines beyond the general
  nectar/compote chain noted above.

## Open research questions

- Documented seasonal rainfall requirements vary considerably across
  four separate sources, with one source's own narrative text giving a
  notably higher figure than its own data table — not resolved.
- Yield figures diverge sharply between directly measured sources (a
  national average and a field-trial figure) and an unverified trade-
  press claim several times higher — not resolved, and the higher figure
  is treated here as unverified rather than adopted at face value.
- Recommended planting density varies by roughly 60% between two
  sourced recommendations — not resolved.
- Fruit-maturity timing (days from pollination or from flowering to
  ripeness) is reported somewhat differently across three sources — not
  resolved.
- Postharvest chilling-injury guidance directly conflicts: one source
  warns of chilling injury across a wide temperature band that overlaps
  with another source's recommended storage temperature — not resolved.
- A documented irrigation-rate figure ("a volume of water per plant per
  day") is ambiguous in its original units and could not be confidently
  converted — not resolved.
- Composition figures are estimated entirely by analogy to a
  passionflower-family sibling crop, since no species-specific food-
  composition source was found.
- Whether this species carries the same cyanogenic-glycoside toxin
  documented in unripe fruit of a sibling species was not researched.
- A regional cultivation manual from a neighboring Andean country, a
  detailed regions chapter from this crop's leading producing country's
  agricultural research agency, and a greenhouse-production cost study
  were all identified but not opened in this research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (transplant to first harvest, roughly 365-470
days) is derived from the documented roughly-twelve-month first-harvest
figure at the low end, and from a detailed phenology study's flowering
timing plus an additional ripening period at the high end.

Composition figures (calorie and protein content) are carried over by
analogy from this crop's passionflower-family sibling, adjusted
qualitatively for this fruit's documented sweeter, less acidic pulp,
since no dedicated food-composition table was opened for this species.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy seasonal water
slots, drought tolerance, days without water before yield penalty) are
carried over by analogy from that same sibling crop, given the shared
genus and vine-on-trellis growth habit, with the soil-moisture-depletion
allowance set somewhat lower than that sibling's, reflecting this
species' documented shallower minimum effective rooting depth and lower
drought-damage threshold.

Yield tiers are derived from: this crop's leading producing country's
national average yield for the "world average" tier; a historical
average figure from a cited agronomic reference for the "good" tier; an
unverified trade-press claim (explicitly flagged as unverified) for the
"exceptional" tier; and a single harvest from a specific field trial for
the "poor" tier.

Soil pH optimum is derived as the midpoint of two sources' overlapping
recommended ranges.

Labor-hour figures are derived directly from a single detailed field-
trial cost study, converting documented labor-days at a stated daily
wage into hours, separately for establishment, recurring maintenance
cycles, and piece-rate harvest/packing labor; because that trial's
yields ran below its own author's expectations, the resulting labor-
per-tonne figures likely run somewhat high, and other production-
intensity tiers are estimated by scaling from this one baseline with a
fairly wide uncertainty range.

The yield-profile's peak daily harvest rate is derived by spreading the
adopted national-average yield tier evenly across a full year, given
this crop's year-round overlapping flowering and fruiting cycles; on-
tree standing-days and quality-decay-rate figures are estimates
reflecting the documented practice of collecting fallen fruit from the
ground multiple times daily and the fruit's hard protective shell.

Seed-viability duration figures are rough estimates based on this
species' hard, dark, orthodox-appearing seed and on a documented seed-
viability range for its passionflower-family sibling, since no dedicated
seed-bank record was found for this species specifically.
