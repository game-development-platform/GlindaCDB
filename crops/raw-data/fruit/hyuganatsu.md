## Naming and scope

*Citrus tamurana* hort. ex Tanaka, family Rutaceae [1][3]. Hyuganatsu
(日向夏) joins Hyūga, the old name of Miyazaki Prefecture, with *natsu*,
"summer" [1]. Fruit grown outside Kyushu is sold as konatsu (小夏) or Tosa
konatsu in Kochi, and as New Summer Orange (ニューサマーオレンジ) on the Izu
Peninsula, Shizuoka [1][2][4]. This entry covers cropID `hyuganatsu`,
including the konatsu bud sports and the Shizuoka selections (Cultivars).

**Origin.** A chance seedling found in the garden of Makata Yasutarō in
Akae, Miyazaki, around 1820 [4][5]. The Japanese encyclopedia gives only
the Bunsei era (1818-1829) [2]. The UC Riverside collection page instead
gives "late 19th century" [3]. That conflicts with the two Japanese
sources, and the 1887 naming [2][4] fits the earlier date. The fruit was
at first too sour to eat [2]. The educator Tamura Toshichika named it in
1887 and sent scions to his father in Kochi, where cultivation is said to
have begun [2]. Tanaka's species name honours Tamura [2].

**Parentage.** It was long thought to be a yuzu derivative, or a yuzu ×
pomelo chance hybrid [1][2]. The 2016 DNA marker study of Japanese
citrus gives tachibana (*C. tachibana*), Japan's native wild mandarin, as
the pollen parent, with the seed parent unknown [2][6]. The American
collection still lists the parents as unknown. Its curators variously
judged it pomelo-like or a sweet-orange hybrid [3].

**Gate check (2026-11-19): promoted.** Proposed in `hetsuka-daidai.md`.
It is its own species with its own origin [1][2][4]. It is eaten in an
unusual way: the white pith (albedo) is eaten with the flesh (Finding 1)
[2][4]. It needs artificial cross-pollination, and seed content is
managed with tetraploid pollen or gibberellin (Finding 2) [7][8][9]. It
has a protected house crop as well as an open-field one (Finding 3)
[10][11]. National production was about 5,700 t in 2010 [2].

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Haruka**, once thought a hyuganatsu sport, now inferred to be a
  hyuganatsu × natsudaidai hybrid [1]. Proposed as its own candidate row
  (`haruka`). **Gate check (2026-11-19): not promoted.** *Taxonomy:* a
  single registered cultivar (1996), a natural seedling of hyuganatsu
  with natsudaidai inferred as pollen parent [1][17]; not a species or
  landrace. *Range:* southern Japan, mainly Ehime, Nagasaki and
  Hiroshima [1], the same warm late-citrus belt. *Farming system:* a
  grafted late citrus like hyuganatsu; the only cultivation study found
  is a Hiroshima note that poor rind colour comes from shade [18]. No
  pollination, rootstock, storage or yield data of its own was found.
  *Harvest:* late winter to spring [1], within hyuganatsu's window.
  *Uses:* eaten fresh with the sweet, spongy albedo intact, as
  hyuganatsu is [1]. What differs is fruit chemistry and size: lower
  acid, citric and malic acid in about equal shares, and about 200 g
  fruit (Cultivars) [1][17]. That is a cultivar-level difference, so
  it is recorded under Cultivars here.
- **Tachibana** (*C. tachibana*), the inferred pollen parent [2][6], a
  wild Japanese mandarin. Used as a DNA reference in `hetsuka-daidai.md`.
  **Now researched (passed its gate check 2026-11-27 as a cultural/
  ornamental heritage citrus; see `tachibana.md`).**
- **Natsudaidai** (`natsudaidai`): the usual pollen source, including
  Kawano natsudaidai interplanted on the Izu Peninsula (Finding 2) [8][11].
  See that entry.
- **Yuzu** (`yuzu`, still PROPOSED), the older presumed parent [1][2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a citrus eaten pith and all.** Hyuganatsu is peeled thinly,
taking only the yellow outer rind and keeping the white albedo, which
has no bitterness or astringency [2][4]. The albedo is eaten with the
flesh, usually sliced and sprinkled with sugar [1][2]. Japan's food
composition tables list it two ways [12]:
- **Segments with albedo:** 30% refuse, 2.1 g fibre per 100 g.
- **Juice sacs alone:** 55% refuse, 0.7 g fibre per 100 g.

Eating it the usual way therefore raises the edible share of a fruit by
about half and triples its fibre per 100 g (see Estimates, below). The pulp is
acidic, mildly sweet, and strongly aromatic, closer to grapefruit or
lemon than to a mandarin [2]. Its oil is reported higher in
trans-β-farnesene, l-carvone, and ketones than other citrus [1]. The
fruit does not ripen after harvest, so retail fruit is ready to eat [5].

**Finding 2 — self-incompatible, and seeds are managed rather than
avoided.** Hyuganatsu needs artificial pollination, with the labour and
pollen supply that implies [7]. Pollinated with ordinary diploid
pollen, the fruit is very seedy [7]. A Miyazaki breeding note mentions a
"self-compatible hyuganatsu" line, which implies the normal type is
self-incompatible [7]. Among the Kochi sports, Nishiuchi konatsu's
self-incompatibility is weaker than hyuganatsu's, which reduces fruit drop
[2]. Growers have three ways to manage seeds:
- **Tetraploid pollen:** in Fukuoka trials, natsudaidai tetraploid pollen
  applied to buds just before opening gave 40.0% fruit set, against 33.9%
  for open pollination. The fruit grew faster early and had few complete
  seeds, so it was easier to eat [8].
- **Gibberellin:** with pollen excluded by netting, a 500 ppm gibberellin
  spray at full bloom raised set from 26.7% to 81.6%. The fruit was
  seedless but smaller than the tetraploid-pollinated fruit [8].
  Gibberellin was not registered for hyuganatsu at the time of that 1989
  report [8]; current registration was not checked.
- **Gibberellin "pollen":** Kochi University dusted flowers with powder
  carrying gibberellin. At 3.6% gibberellin, January fruit set was 45-47%,
  against 2-7% for untreated bagged flowers. All the fruit was seedless
  and markedly smaller than open-pollinated fruit, with a thicker rind [9].

Any cross-pollination produces seedy fruit, so seedless production needs
isolation or pollen exclusion at bloom [8]. A Miyazaki growers' group has
grown seedless house hyuganatsu with Miyazaki University since 1992 [2].
Miyazaki is now breeding triploids from early and self-compatible
hyuganatsu lines; the best cross gave one triploid in 754 seedlings [7].

**Finding 3 — the same fruit sold from November to May.** House
(greenhouse) hyuganatsu goes on sale from about late November, with
shipments from the end of the year and a peak in January-February [10][11].
Open-field fruit starts being picked in late February and peaks in
March-April [11]. On the Izu Peninsula, stored fruit is harvested in March
and tree-ripened fruit in April-May [13]. The Japanese encyclopedia says
the fruit matures around June [2], and Riverside gives a January-May
season [3]. The sources describe it as late-ripening throughout [3].

**Finding 4 — an ageing orchard industry kept going by cutting trees
back.** Hyuganatsu reached the Izu Peninsula in 1920 [13]. Plantings
there grew to 35 ha by 1963 and passed 100 ha by 1985 [13]. By 2021 they
had shrunk to 45 ha as growers aged [13]. Trees over 50 years old are still
cropped, but they exceed 5 m in height on sloping, unmechanised land [13].
Cutting the trunk back to regrow a low tree (2.0-2.2 m), a technique from
Miyazaki, brought the first crop in two years [13]. A new one-year nursery
tree needs at least three, because it is not allowed to fruit for its
first two years [13]. Cut-back trees gave 2.3-2.9 kg per tree in year two
and 5.1-8.3 kg in year three [13]. A Miyazaki trial with artificial
pollination reached 12.7 kg in year two [13]. That compares with the 33.8
kg per tree needed for the 2.7 t per 10 a target [13].

**Finding 5 — production centred on Miyazaki and Kochi.** National
production was 5,386 t in 2005 and 5,714 t in 2010. In 2010, Miyazaki
grew 55% and Kochi 28% [2]. Miyazaki's own figure for 2010 is 3,152.5 t
from 196.6 ha [14]. More recently it ships about 3,000 t a year [15].
For 2021 it reports half of national area and about 60% of national
production [5]. Shizuoka, western Kanagawa, and Ehime also grow it [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial citrus tree; old trees
  exceed 5 m [13]. Foliage looks orange-like [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one crop a year, picked
  November to May across house and open-field crops (Finding 3)
  [10][11][13].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Fruiting starts in year 3 or later
  from a one-year nursery tree, because the first two years are kept
  for canopy growth (Izu practice) [13]. Cut-back trees crop again in year
  two [13].
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate southern Japan: Miyazaki, Kochi, Izu
  Peninsula (Shizuoka), western Kanagawa, Ehime [2][13]. Grown in the
  Riverside collection, California [3]. Cold limit and chill
  requirement: Not sourced. Elevation: Not sourced.
- **Composition:** Japanese food composition tables (8th edition), raw
  fruit, per 100 g edible [12]:
  - **Segments with albedo (07112):** 30% refuse; 46 kcal; water 87.2 g;
    protein 0.6 g; fat 0.1 g; carbohydrate 11.7 g; fibre 2.1 g; potassium
    130 mg; calcium 23 mg; vitamin C 26 mg; folate 16 µg.
  - **Juice sacs (07113):** 55% refuse; 35 kcal; water 90.7 g; carbohydrate
    8.3 g; fibre 0.7 g; potassium 110 mg; calcium 5 mg; vitamin C 21 mg;
    folate 13 µg.

  Squeezing method barely changes juice pH, Brix, sugars, acids, or
  vitamin C. Juice that takes in more peel carries more of the peel
  flavonoids tangeretin and nobiletin and more aroma [16].
- **Edibility:** Raw; thinly peeled and eaten with the albedo (Finding 1)
  [2][4].
- **Pollination:** Required; self-incompatible, cross-pollinated by hand
  or by bees from interplanted varieties (Finding 2) [3][7][8][9][13].
  On the Izu Peninsula, growers rely on open pollination from
  interplanted Kawano natsudaidai, without hand pollination [13].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Izu target 2.7 t per 10 a, 27 t/ha; that is 33.8 kg per tree
  at an assumed 80 trees per 10 a [13]. Miyazaki 2010: 3,152.5 t from
  196.6 ha, about 16 t/ha including young orchards (see Estimates,
  below) [14].
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Grafted. The Fukuoka trial trees were on trifoliate
  orange [8], and the Riverside trees are on Carrizo and C-35 citrange
  [3]. Planting stock is one-year nursery trees [13]. Clonal/viral risk:
  the Izu trial excluded trees showing stem pitting suspected of virus
  infection [13]. No hyuganatsu-specific virus or clean-stock data found.
- **Labor:** Harvesting time was measured in the Izu trial as two workers
  picking 10 kg [13]; the numbers were not extracted. Hand pollination
  is a stated labour burden [7]. Hours per hectare: Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not sourced. Salinity: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Fruit is stored to March on the Izu
  Peninsula [13]. The fruit does not ripen after harvest [5]. Storage
  temperature and humidity: Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** One-year nursery trees, not cropped for two years
  [13]. Densities around 80 trees per 10 a (800/ha) are assumed in the
  yield target [13]. Low trees are kept at 2.0-2.2 m by pruning after
  cutting back [13].
- **Cultivars and sports:**
  - **Muroto konatsu** (Kochi): seedless bud sport, small fruit, 100-120 g
    [2].
  - **Sukumo konatsu** (Kochi): less acid, earlier [2].
  - **Matsuoka konatsu** (Kochi): sport of Sukumo konatsu, more radial
    grooves at the stem end, earlier than Nishiuchi [2].
  - **Nishiuchi konatsu** (Kochi): sport with few seeds and weaker
    self-incompatibility; its tetraploid was used as pollen in Miyazaki
    breeding [2][7].
  - **Shiratori hyūga** (Shizuoka): larger, smoother fruit, few seeds [2].
  - **Ihara hyūga** (Shizuoka): more vigorous, more seeds than Shiratori
    [2].
  - **Early hyuganatsu** and **self-compatible hyuganatsu**: Miyazaki
    lines used as breeding parents [7].
  - **Haruka** (derived hybrid; see Naming and scope): found in 1980 as a
    natural hybrid seedling of hyuganatsu by Ishii Tokuo in Nijō,
    Itoshima, Fukuoka; grafted 1982, first fruit 1986, registered 15
    October 1996 [1][17]. Natsudaidai is inferred as the pollen parent
    from DNA markers [1]. Fruit about 200 g, round to pear-shaped, often
    with a neck at the stem end; yellow rind, hard to peel by hand;
    10-11 segments; moderately seedy; edible sweet albedo [1]. Sugar about
    13 °Brix and low acid [1]. In a Nagasaki comparison of nine late
    citrus (December 2006), haruka measured 10.9 °Brix, 9.46 g total sugar
    and 0.83 g titratable acid per 100 mL. Its organic acids were about
    half citric and half malic, where the others were 85-97% citric [17].
    An earlier Fukuoka study found citric acid falling from 75% of acids
    in September to 40% from February, and linked its clean aftertaste to
    malic acid [17]. Juice is high in hesperidin and narirutin, and the
    peel low in auraptene [17]. The 2009 harvest was about 1,258 t: Ehime
    45%, Nagasaki 25%, Hiroshima 21% [1]. It keeps 1-3 weeks refrigerated
    [1]. In Hiroshima it held over 285 yen/kg for five straight years.
    Seventy percent of surveyed growers named poor rind colour their main
    problem. The cause is shade: fruit inside the canopy or behind a
    windbreak colours less and has lower sugar, so sunny sites suit it
    best [18]. Exported to Singapore, Taiwan and Hong Kong [1].

  The standard fruit weighs about 300 g and has a thick, yellow, smooth
  rind [2]. It is globose to oblong, light-yellow-fleshed, and moderately
  seedy [3].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced for hyuganatsu.
- **Water regime:** No flooded requirement; ordinary orchard crop.
  Irrigation: Not sourced.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Two production systems with separate
  windows: house fruit from late November to February, open-field fruit
  from late February to May. On the Izu Peninsula, stored fruit is picked
  in March and tree-ripened fruit in April-May [10][11][13]. Not
  successive ripening within one tree.
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Arose in Japan [2]; held in the
  California collection, where it is not commercially available [3].
  No invasive listing found in this pass. Japan's domestic
  citrus-greening movement controls apply to citrus plants (see
  `daidai.md`).
- **Controlled environment:** Greenhouse (house) production is
  established in Miyazaki, including seedless production since 1992
  [2][10][11]. No yield or energy figures found.

## Hazards

- **Seedy fruit from stray pollen:** not a safety hazard, but a market
  one; seedless production needs pollen exclusion (Finding 2) [8].
- **Virus:** stem pitting suspected of virus infection occurs in old
  Izu orchards; affected trees were excluded from the trial [13]. No
  outbreak data found.
- **Drug interaction:** not sourced for hyuganatsu. The juice contains
  peel flavonoids (tangeretin, nobiletin) [16]; no furanocoumarin data was
  found. TO BE ADDRESSED with the other citrus drug-interaction material.
- **Fungal contamination of the edible product:** nothing found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fruit → fresh):** harvest → optional storage to March →
  thin peeling of the flavedo → sliced with albedo, often sugared [1][2]
  [13]. Refuse 30% [12].
- **Chain 2 (fruit → juice):** squeezing by pulper, belt press, or in-line
  extractor; pulper juice from whole raw fruit is highest in tangeretin
  and nobiletin; belt pressing gives the most aroma; boiling or peeling
  first strips the aroma, and pasteurisation lowers it [16].
- **Chain 3 (peel → confectionery):** candied peel ("hyuganatsu peel")
  and marmalade [2].
- **Other uses:** ponzu, fruit liqueur, and as a filling in Miyazaki
  dishes (futomaki, gyoza, lettuce rolls) [2]. A water-soluble fraction
  reportedly suppressed bone loss in ovariectomised rats [1]; animal data
  only.

## Estimates and derivations

**Miyazaki average yield, about 16 t/ha.** 3,152.5 t ÷ 196.6 ha [14] =
16.0 t/ha. Area includes non-bearing orchards, so this understates
mature-orchard yield. Against the 27 t/ha Izu target [13], about 60%.
Range about 16-27 t/ha.

**Edible share and fibre gain from eating the albedo.** For a 300 g fruit
[2], segments with albedo give 300 × (1 − 0.30) = 210 g; juice sacs alone
give 300 × (1 − 0.55) = 135 g [12]. 210 ÷ 135 = 1.56, about 56% more
edible fruit. Fibre: 210 g × 2.1 g/100 g = 4.4 g, against 135 g × 0.7
g/100 g = 0.9 g [12], about five times more per fruit (three times per
100 g).

**Miyazaki share in 2010.** 3,152.5 t [14] ÷ 5,714 t [2] = 55%, matching
the encyclopedia's 55% [2].

**Izu target per hectare.** 2.7 t per 10 a [13] × 10 = 27 t/ha; 80 trees
per 10 a [13] = 800 trees/ha.
