# Cherry — Sweet Cherry (*Prunus avium*) and Sour Cherry (*Prunus cerasus*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers two related but distinct species, treated together in
one document because of their shared research base, but understood as
two separate crops: sweet cherry (*Prunus avium*, also called gean or
mazzard), a diploid species that is mostly self-incompatible and eaten
fresh [1][14]; and sour or tart cherry (*Prunus cerasus*), a tetraploid
species thought to have arisen as a natural hybrid between sweet cherry
and a wild steppe cherry relative. Sour cherry is self-fertile and
almost entirely processed rather than eaten fresh, and falls into two
traditional groups: dark-juiced Morello types and light-juiced Amarelle
types [2][3]. The two species differ substantially in pollination
biology, harvest method, perishability, processing use, cold-hardiness,
and orchard lifespan, so this entry presents both species' figures
side by side throughout.

Not covered here as separate crops, but noted as cultivar groups within
sour cherry: Duke cherries (natural and bred sweet × sour hybrids,
pollen-sterile and intermediate in flavor between their parents) and
Marasca (a small, dark, bitter Dalmatian-coast sour-cherry variety grown
specifically for maraschino liqueur production) — both were evaluated
for separate crop-entry status and judged to share essentially all of
sour cherry's growing biology, differing only in product or pollination
quirks. A further related crop, a set of cold-hardy dwarf sour cherries
bred specifically for prairie conditions from sour cherry and a wild
steppe-cherry relative, is treated as its own separate crop entry by
this project given its distinctive own-rooted suckering shrub habit and
mechanized over-the-row harvest.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — rain cracking is the single factor that decides where
sweet cherries can be grown commercially.** Rain, heavy fog, or dew
shortly before harvest cracks the fruit, and for susceptible cultivars in
bad years up to 90% of a crop can crack [19]. This is described as "one
of the primary factors limiting commercial sweet cherry production to
the arid west" of the United States [19]. The mechanism is not simple
whole-fruit water uptake but a cascading failure at the fruit surface
(sometimes called the "zipper model"): the fruit's waxy cuticle layer
stops growing early in development and becomes increasingly strained as
the fruit swells; microscopic cracks then let water into surface cells,
which burst and release an acid that weakens neighboring cell walls,
propagating the crack [12]. Rain shelters over the orchard are the most
effective control, though expensive; cultivars differ meaningfully in
susceptibility, and even low cracking percentages can render an entire
harvest uneconomical because of the grading labor required to sort
cracked fruit [12]. Sour cherries are considerably less susceptible to
this problem [19].

**Finding 2 — the two species have essentially opposite pollination
systems.** Most sweet cherry cultivars are self-incompatible through a
genetic mechanism that prevents pollination between cultivars sharing
certain genetic markers, so compatible pollinizer cultivars with
overlapping bloom timing are required; a handful of self-fertile sweet
cultivars exist and serve as "universal" pollinizers for other cultivars
[14][19]. Commercial growing guidance recommends at least three
different sweet cherry varieties be planted together for reliable
pollination [19]. By contrast, essentially all commercial sour cherry
varieties are self-fertile, and a single variety is sufficient on its
own [2][19].

**Finding 3 — both species must be cooled within hours of harvest, but
by different methods with opposite time constraints.** Sweet cherries
must be cooled below about 5°C within four hours of harvest, with rapid
water-based cooling the fastest method [10]. Mechanically harvested sour
cherries instead go directly into large tanks of cold water; they must
soak for at least four hours but no more than eight, since longer
soaking bleaches their color and causes a quality defect called scald
[11]. The cold soak also firms the fruit enough to make mechanical
pitting possible [11]. This gives sour cherry handling both a minimum
and a maximum processing-start window — an unusual combination compared
with most produce, which typically has only a minimum delay before
quality loss.

**Finding 4 — sweet cherries store for weeks and are not chilling-
sensitive.** Recommended storage is just below freezing (roughly -1 to
0°C) at very high humidity for 2-4 weeks; the fruit should be kept "as
cold as possible without freezing" and produces very little ethylene
[10]. The fruit does not ripen further after picking (non-climacteric)
and shows no chilling-injury sensitivity at all, unlike many other fruit
crops. The practical storage limits are instead shrivel (from a thin
cuticle), stem browning, impact pitting/bruising, and decay [10].

**Finding 5 — frost sensitivity at bloom is unusually well quantified
by growth stage.** A detailed regional study measured bud-kill
temperatures for a standard cultivar at each developmental stage: at
full bloom, 10% of flower buds die around -2.2°C and 90% die around
-3.9°C; at the earliest bud-swelling stage, 90% kill instead requires a
much colder roughly -15°C [13]. Sour cherry blooms later than sweet
cherry (and later than most other stone-fruit species), making it
somewhat less frost-prone overall [19].

**Finding 6 — the orchard represents a roughly 25-year investment, and
rootstock choice sets hard soil-drainage limits.** One detailed regional
cost-study budget assumes full production by the sixth year and a
25-year total productive life (5 establishment years plus 20 years of
full production) [18]. Dwarfing rootstocks can bring trees into bearing
by the third year and full production by the fourth, where standard
vigorous rootstock can take up to four times longer to reach full
production [17]. One widely used drought-tolerant rootstock is
"extremely sensitive to water-logged soils," including brief winter
waterlogging, and suits calcareous soils specifically; every cherry
planting site needs at least about a meter of active, well-drained
rooting depth [17][19]. Sour cherry trees are considerably more
cold-hardy than sweet cherry but shorter-lived [19]. A serious bacterial
canker disease can kill up to 10% of young trees outright and cut
mature-orchard yields by 10-50%, with infection rates of 50-80% reported
in wet regions of the US Pacific Northwest [17][19].

**Finding 7 — rootstock seed is the crop's one genuine seed use.**
Commercial cultivars are budded onto seedling rootstock (most commonly
one of two traditional species-level rootstocks) or onto clonal
dwarfing rootstock, since cultivars do not come true from seed [1][17].
Rootstock seed averages roughly 1,000-1,300 cleaned seeds per kilogram
and needs 90-180 days of cold stratification (duration depending on
whether the stone is removed first); properly dried and refrigerated, it
stores for up to three years. Seedling rootstock trees themselves reach
minimum seed-bearing age at 6-7 years [23].

**Finding 8 — the pits are cyanogenic, but the ripe flesh is not.**
Measured cherry kernel amygdalin content runs several milligrams per
gram (lower than apricot kernels, which run noticeably higher), with one
report of a much higher figure specifically for a Morello-type sour
cherry kernel [24]. All parts of the plant except the ripe fruit contain
cyanogenic glycosides [1]. This places cherry pits in the same broad
toxicity category as several other crops this project has researched
that are cyanogenic in some tissues but safe in the part normally eaten.

**Finding 9 — the processing chains for both species are old,
well-documented, and in some cases precisely quantified.** For sweet
cherries, maraschino-style cherries are traditionally made by brining
whole light-colored cherries in a dilute sulfur-dioxide solution with
hydrated lime for at least 4-6 weeks, then pitting and stemming them, a
24-48 hour running-water leach to remove the sulfur dioxide, dyeing, and
a multi-day sugar-syrup buildup; a candied ("glacé") version continues to
a much higher sugar concentration, is held several weeks, and is then
dried [20]. Dried sweet cherries are dried down to about 80% solids,
yielding roughly 17.5% of fresh weight [21]. For sour cherries, products
include pie filling, concentrated juice, dried sweetened fruit,
preserves, syrups, and a well-known fruit-flavored beer style made by
macerating the fruit in a specific style of fermented beer [2]. Dried
cherry stems are used for a traditional diuretic tea [35]. Cherry
kernels themselves contain a substantial fraction of oil by weight, and
whole fermented cherries (including stones) are traditionally
pot-distilled into a clear cherry brandy [33][34].

**Finding 10 — yields are well documented in the United States, but
much thinner for the rest of the world, especially for sour cherry.** US
national statistics show sweet cherry yields averaging roughly 9 t/ha in
a strong year and around 6 t/ha in a weaker one, with the leading
production state reaching nearly 13 t/ha in a particularly good year
[3]. A world average sweet cherry yield around 6 t/ha can be derived from
global production-and-area figures [4]. US sour cherry yields average
roughly 6.7 t/ha nationally, with one state reaching over 13 t/ha and
another as low as 4 t/ha in different years [3]. No reliable world
per-hectare yield figure was found for sour cherry at all — only total
world production, led by Russia, Poland, Ukraine, and Turkey [2][6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial deciduous fruit trees; sweet cherry
  harvested over 1-3 hand-picking passes, sour cherry typically
  harvested in a single mechanical shake-and-catch pass.
- **Climate:** Sweet cherry is effectively restricted by rain cracking
  to drier climates (arid, semi-arid, and Mediterranean, in addition to
  cool-temperate and temperate zones); sour cherry tolerates a somewhat
  wider, cooler range (cool-temperate and temperate) and is more
  cold-hardy overall (Finding 1, 6).
- **Food status / composition:** Both raw-edible (sour cherry is very
  tart but edible raw); detailed public food-composition sources give
  roughly 630 kcal/kg and 10.6 g protein/kg for sweet cherry, and
  roughly 500 kcal/kg and 10.0 g protein/kg for sour cherry.
- **Pollination:** Sweet cherry requires insect (bee) pollination from a
  genetically compatible pollinizer cultivar; sour cherry does not
  strictly require cross-pollination, since essentially all commercial
  varieties are self-fertile, though bee pollination is still typical
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield:** Well documented for the US for both species; world
  figures are reasonably solid for sweet cherry but largely absent for
  sour cherry beyond total production (Finding 10; see Estimates below
  for derivation detail).
- **Water use:** Adopted from a standard international crop-water-use
  reference's figures for cherries generally (grouped with apples and
  pears rather than other stone fruit in that reference); fruit growth
  is short and water-sensitive throughout, but a sourced postharvest
  deficit-irrigation strategy can safely withhold a meaningful fraction
  of irrigation water after harvest without hurting the current
  season's crop, though a deeper cut lowers next year's fruit set;
  waterlogging tolerance is low, and standing water on ripe fruit
  (rain, dew, or careless overhead irrigation) causes cracking
  regardless of species, though sour cherry is considerably more
  resistant (Finding 1).
- **Propagation:** Entirely by grafting/budding named cultivars onto
  rootstock; two main traditional seedling rootstock species are used
  (one more tolerant of heavy, wet soils; the other notably more
  drought-tolerant but extremely waterlogging-sensitive and suited to
  calcareous soils), alongside a range of newer clonal dwarfing
  rootstocks that bring trees into earlier bearing (Finding 6, 7).
- **Soil:** A fairly narrow, near-neutral pH range is preferred for
  both; well-drained soil with at least about a meter of active rooting
  depth is essential, since waterlogging tolerance is low; one
  drought-tolerant rootstock extends suitability to calcareous soils up
  to a higher pH.
- **Labor:** Harvest is the dominant labor component for both species;
  sweet cherry is hand-picked (labor scales with picker skill and
  training-system efficiency), while sour cherry is typically
  trunk-shaken onto catch frames mechanically, with hand labor
  correspondingly much lower at commercial scale. No detailed sourced
  labor budget exists for most non-harvest operations; those figures
  here are rough estimates by analogy to other deciduous tree-fruit
  crops this project has researched.
- **Storage:** Sweet cherry is cool-stored for 2-4 weeks, not
  chilling-sensitive, and does not ripen further off the tree; sour
  cherry is almost entirely processed within hours of a cold-water soak
  rather than stored fresh at all (Finding 3, 4).
- **Stand life:** A roughly 25-year productive orchard life is
  documented for sweet cherry in one detailed cost-study budget (5
  establishment years, 20 years of full production); sour cherry trees
  are shorter-lived but more cold-hardy. Bacterial canker disease is a
  major cause of early orchard decline (Finding 6).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Bacterial canker (a major disease, see
  Finding 6), birds (which can eliminate an entire unprotected sweet
  cherry crop in a single day), and several viruses carried by
  sensitive clonal rootstocks.
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named sweet cherry cultivars spans
  early-to-late harvest timing (relative to a historic reference
  cultivar), dark and light/blush fruit color, and self-fertile versus
  pollinizer-dependent types; sour cherry cultivars span the Amarelle
  and Morello groups, plus the Duke-hybrid and Marasca cultivar groups
  noted above (cultivar detail below).
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable as a hydroponic or
  fully enclosed crop (a full-size orchard tree); the closest analog to
  protected cultivation is rain-shelter structures built over
  conventional orchards specifically to prevent cracking.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include bacterial canker (a serious,
potentially tree-killing disease), several viruses carried by sensitive
clonal rootstocks, and bird predation on ripening fruit (severe enough
that netting is considered the only effective control in small
plantings).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — cherry kernels (pits) contain cyanogenic
glycosides (amygdalin and a related compound) at levels that, while
lower than some related stone-fruit kernels, are still a genuine
poisoning risk if crushed and eaten in quantity; the ripe flesh itself
does not contain these compounds. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — traditional sulfur-dioxide brining used in
maraschino-style cherry production leaves a substantial sulfite residue
in the brined fruit before the standard multi-day water leaching step
removes most of it; this is a processing-residue consideration rather
than a hazard in the fresh fruit itself. Deferred for the same reason.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** sweet cherry — harvested fruit hydrocooled, sorted, and
  packed for fresh sale; alternatively brined in a sulfite-lime solution,
  pitted and stemmed, leached, and finished into maraschino or candied
  ("glacé") cherries; pitted fruit also dried, and stems dried
  separately for tea. Sour cherry — harvested fruit cold-soaked in tanks
  for 4-8 hours, then pitted; pressed into juice and concentrated, or
  made into preserves, syrups, and sweetened dried fruit. Fermented and
  distilled products: sweet cherries mashed with stones, fermented, and
  pot-distilled into a clear cherry brandy; sour cherries macerated into
  a specific sour-beer style. The Marasca sour-cherry cultivar group
  supports a distinct traditional liqueur-making chain: whole fruit (with
  some leaves and stems) steeped in spirit for up to several years,
  distilled, aged, and sweetened into a clear liqueur, alongside a
  candied-fruit-in-syrup product made the same way.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen and canned cherry
  products in general; kernel-oil extraction and use; timber and gum use
  of the trees.

A long cultural tradition of candied maraschino-style cherries and a
dedicated Dalmatian-coast cherry liqueur tradition both derive
specifically from this crop; the latter uses a dedicated sour-cherry
cultivar grown almost nowhere else commercially.

## Open research questions

- Winter chill-hour requirements for several named cultivars conflict
  between sources by a wide margin (for example, one low-chill cultivar
  is given two quite different chill-hour figures across different
  sources) — not resolved here.
- A cherry-brandy distillation yield figure conflicts moderately between
  two sources, though the two figures are roughly consistent once a
  likely unit difference (pure alcohol versus finished spirit volume) is
  accounted for.
- The fresh-to-dried weight ratio for dried sweet cherries is given
  differently by two sources (roughly 0.175 versus roughly 0.25) — not
  resolved here.
- World sour-cherry yield per hectare could not be sourced at all,
  despite total world production being reasonably well documented;
  national-level production statistics for the main producing countries
  would be needed to derive a reliable figure.
- Nearly all non-harvest labor-hour figures for both species are
  estimated by analogy to other deciduous tree-fruit crops this project
  has researched rather than directly sourced, since no detailed cherry
  labor-cost study covering every operation was found.
- Seasonal water-use figures rest partly on search-snippet-level
  sources and a standard reference work's crop-coefficient table rather
  than a comprehensive direct measurement for either species.
- Whether — and to what degree — a hazard associated with crushed stones
  during traditional brandy-mashing carries meaningful residual
  cyanide risk in the finished spirit was not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (bloom to harvest, roughly 55-100 days for sweet
cherry and a similar or slightly longer window for sour cherry) is
estimated from documented regional bloom dates and national harvest-date
ranges, since detailed cultivar-by-cultivar phenology records were not
available for every cultivar.

World-average sweet-cherry yield (roughly 6,000 kg/ha) is derived from
recent global production-and-harvested-area figures; good, exceptional,
and poor yield tiers are derived directly from documented US national
and state-level average yields converted to a metric per-hectare basis.
Sour-cherry world-average and poor-yield figures are estimated (rather
than derived) from documented US national and state-level figures,
adjusted downward to reflect that the largest world producing countries
likely use lower-input orchard systems than typical US commercial
production; good and exceptional sour-cherry yield figures are derived
directly from documented US national and state-level figures.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index,
root-zone available water) are derived from a standard international
crop-water-use reference's stage-length table for cherries, cross-
checked against a detailed regional water-balance study's seasonal
water-requirement figure, which lines up reasonably well with the
derived figures. Sour cherry is assumed to share sweet cherry's
water-use parameters by analogy, since no dedicated tart-cherry water
study was found. Drought tolerance is judged moderate for both species
(a standard international reference groups cherries with apples and
pears rather than more drought-sensitive stone fruit, and one rootstock
is notably drought-tolerant, though fruit growth itself is
water-sensitive).

Planting density (roughly 200-750 trees/ha in conventional orchards, up
to several thousand per hectare in ultra-high-density dwarfing systems)
is derived from documented standard and dwarfing-rootstock tree-spacing
guidelines.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest are derived from documented hand-
picking productivity rates (which vary by picker skill and training
system) applied to the estimated yield figures above; sour-cherry
mechanical-harvest labor figures are rough estimates, since no
documented hours-per-hectare figure for trunk-shaker harvest crews was
found. Other labor operations (land preparation, pruning, post-harvest
handling) are estimated by analogy to other deciduous tree-fruit crops
this project has researched.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake per tonne of fruit are
derived from a documented regional nutrient-management guideline's
fruit-removal figures, converted to metric units; sour cherry is assumed
to share these figures by analogy, given the shared genus and fruit
type.

Soil pH minimum and root-depth figures are estimated by analogy to other
deciduous tree-fruit crops this project has researched, since the
sources reviewed gave a preferred range and an upper limit but no
explicit lower pH bound.

Storage duration (about 21 days for sweet cherry, estimated at about a
week for the small fresh-market share of sour cherry) is derived or
estimated from documented storage-duration ranges and the fact that
nearly all sour cherries are processed within hours rather than stored
fresh.

Peak daily harvest-yield rate, harvest-window length, and post-ripeness
quality-decay rate are estimated from documented harvest-pass patterns
(sweet cherry picked in 1-3 hand passes; sour cherry's fruit ripening
window) and from each species' differing susceptibility to cracking and
bird predation once ripe.
