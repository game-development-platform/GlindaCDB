## Naming and scope

Cultivated bananas are seedless, parthenocarpic clones, mostly
triploid, derived from two wild diploid ancestor species and their
hybrids [1][3]. This entry treats three related but genuinely distinct
crops together in one document, since they share the same underlying
plant biology, water-use physiology, propagation methods, and most
diseases, while differing meaningfully in product, climate band, and
market:

- **Dessert banana** — sweet bananas eaten raw when ripe; mostly one
  genome group, led by the commercial subgroup that replaced an earlier
  dominant export cultivar after a mid-20th-century outbreak of
  Fusarium wilt (Panama disease) [1][3].
- **Plantain** — true plantains plus other starchy cooking bananas of
  a related but distinct genome composition, cooked green or ripe
  rather than eaten raw [5][7].
- **East African highland banana** (matooke) — a distinct genome group
  grown at meaningful elevation across the African Great Lakes region,
  including both cooking-type and beer-brewing clone sets [4][18].

A related crop, enset (a starchy root/tuber relative in the same
broader plant family), is treated as a separate crop entry elsewhere in
this project. Grounding and water-use comparisons for this entry draw
on two other tropical herbaceous fruit crops with similar ratoon-based
harvest patterns that this project has also researched.

These three crops differ meaningfully in ways that matter for
cultivation modeling: product and processing (sweet raw fruit with
ethylene-ripening infrastructure, versus starchy cooked product
processed into chips and flour, versus steamed matooke alongside a
separate beer-brewing clone set used for juice and spirits); storage
temperature (green dessert fruit tolerates a slightly warmer storage
regime than green plantain, which ripens sooner); climate band (lowland
tropics and subtropics generally, versus specifically humid lowland
tropics for plantain, versus cool highland elevation for the highland
group); dominant disease (a soil fungus and a leaf fungus threaten
dessert banana severely; a bacterial wilt disease is the dominant
yield-limiting factor for the highland group specifically); and typical
yield level, which varies enormously between export-oriented dessert
production and smallholder subsistence production of the other two.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one plant genus, three distinct food economies.** Recent
world production figures show total output in the hundreds of millions
of tonnes, split between dessert bananas and plantains at roughly a
three-to-one ratio [1]. A handful of countries dominate production,
with at least one major producer consuming nearly its entire crop
domestically rather than exporting [1][4]. A substantial share of world
dessert-banana production is exported, dominated by one particular
commercial cultivar subgroup grown across several tropical exporting
regions [15]. Plantains rank among the most important food staples
worldwide, supplying a very large share of daily calories for tens of
millions of people in Africa [5]. In at least one major East African
country, matooke consumption runs to a substantial fraction of a
kilogram per person per day [4].

**Finding 2 — every above-ground stem fruits exactly once, but the
underground plant persists indefinitely.** All above-ground plant parts
grow from an underground corm; the visible "trunk" is not true wood but
a tightly rolled pseudostem of leaf sheaths [1]. Each pseudostem bears
exactly one fruit bunch (a substantial weight, arranged in multiple
"hands" of many individual fruit) and is then cut down after harvest;
the plant itself persists through continuously emerging offshoot
suckers, with the next sucker in line fruiting several months later
[1]. The crop therefore behaves, at the level of a whole field or
planting, as a continuously harvested perennial system rather than a
single discrete annual or even typical perennial crop — bunches are cut
one at a time, year-round, across an entire planting of overlapping
generations. Plantings are eventually replanted once yields decline or
disease pressure builds up [1][7].

**Finding 3 — ripening is deliberately managed as a farm-to-market
process, never allowed to happen on the plant.** Fruit of all three
crops is harvested while still mature but fully green; fruit left to
ripen on the plant splits and develops poor texture, so maturity is
instead judged by bunch age since flower emergence and by direct
measurement of individual fruit "finger" size [3]. Commercial ripening
uses a substantially higher ethylene concentration than the minimum
needed, applied for roughly a day or two at a specific moderate warm
temperature, followed by several more days to reach full ripe color
[3]. Temperature management during ripening is critical in both
directions: above a certain warm threshold the flesh softens while the
peel stubbornly stays green (a recognized defect); well above that, the
ripening process becomes irreversibly blocked entirely. In the other
direction, chilling injury sets in within hours to a few days below a
specific cool-temperature threshold, so green fruit of the dessert crop
and the plantain crop are each held at their own distinct, carefully
controlled cool-but-not-cold storage temperature. Controlled-atmosphere
storage (reduced oxygen combined with elevated carbon dioxide) can
double or triple shelf life [3].

**Finding 4 — among the thirstiest and most drought-sensitive of all
major fruit crops.** A standard international crop-water-use reference
gives notably high crop-coefficient values for this crop alongside an
unusually shallow root zone and a conservative soil-moisture-depletion
allowance [2]. The same reference's yield-response-to-water parameter
for this crop is among the highest documented for any crop in that
reference, indicating that yield loss per unit of water deficit is
unusually severe [2]. A detailed Brazilian irrigation study documented
substantial measured seasonal water use in a first production cycle,
at a notably high daily rate, with a modestly higher crop coefficient
in a second (ratoon) cycle [9]. Growth itself stops entirely at a
specific moderately cool temperature well above actual freezing [14].

**Finding 5 — serious diseases shape where, and which type, can be
grown at all.** A soil-borne fungal wilt disease historically ended
commercial viability of the once-dominant export cultivar, and a newer
strain of the same pathogen now threatens the cultivar subgroup that
replaced it [1][3]. A separate major leaf fungal disease can cut
production by half or more and has been documented to destroy a
region's entire export industry within just a few years [1][3]. A
virus spread by aphids has no cure and no resistant cultivar available,
with the dominant commercial dessert subgroup severely affected [1]. A
bacterial wilt disease affects essentially all cultivars and is
documented as the single main yield-reducing factor on smallholder
farms in at least one major East African highland-banana-growing
country; fertilizer there was found to improve yields only where this
disease was absent, and early removal of the male flower bud is a
documented practice that slows its insect-vectored spread [13]. Across
all three crops, clean planting material — whether laboratory-grown
plantlets or suckers specifically treated against pests before planting
— is the common first line of defense [1][7].

**Finding 6 — detailed labor figures exist from two very different
production systems.** A documented irrigated plantain system in South
America shows a roughly 14-month cycle to first harvest, with
irrigation representing the single largest labor-cost share, followed
distantly by harvest labor and bunch-bagging labor [6]. A documented
export-oriented dessert-banana system shows a very large on-farm
workforce relative to planted area, with roughly two-thirds of workers
in field operations (including harvest) and roughly a quarter in the
packing facility [17].

**Finding 7 — the East African highland group is both a food staple
and the basis of traditional alcoholic beverages.** One specific clone
set within this group is grown specifically for its bitter, astringent
fruit used in brewing, kept entirely separate from the cooking-type
clone sets [4]. A traditional beer-brewing process involves pit-
ripening the fruit for several days, treading it out with a fibrous
grass to extract juice, filtering and diluting it, adding roasted
sorghum, and fermenting the mixture for several more days to produce a
beverage with a meaningfully alcoholic strength — though documented
alcohol-content figures conflict notably between sources [12]. The
unfermented juice itself is also consumed as a mild, essentially non-
alcoholic beverage [12].

**Finding 8 — in B-genome cultivars, part of the clonal-degeneration
risk is built into the plant's own DNA, not carried by an outside
pathogen.** Banana streak virus (BSV) exists in at least four distinct
species as infectious sequences integrated directly into the *Musa
balbisiana* B genome itself, rather than only as an ordinary externally
transmitted infection; this affects plantain (AAB) and other B-genome
cultivars and hybrids [23]. Tissue culture/micropropagation — the
standard clean-stock method used across all three crops to exclude
externally transmitted pathogens (see Propagation, below) — is itself
a documented trigger that can activate this integrated viral sequence
into infectious virus, independent of any external infection source;
one study found natural plantains and a synthetic hybrid equally prone
to this activation for one BSV species, but not for another, and a
separate field-risk study found tissue culture and ordinary field
cultivation trigger activation through distinct pathways, with water-
regime and thermal stress flagged as separate activators under field
conditions [23]. The practical upshot: standard tissue-culture clean-
stock certification, which works well against ordinary externally
transmitted viruses, cannot by itself guarantee virus-free planting
material for B-genome cultivars specifically.

## Agronomic data

**Shared across all three crops** (family-level biology):

- **Life form / season:** A giant perennial herb (not a true tree),
  with the visible "trunk" formed from tightly rolled leaf sheaths
  (a pseudostem) rather than true wood, growing from an underground
  corm [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuously harvested — bunches are cut one at
  a time, year-round, across an entire planting, since each individual
  pseudostem bears exactly one bunch before being cut down and replaced
  by its own continuously emerging offshoot sucker (Finding 2). Fruit
  is always harvested mature but fully green and ripened off the plant
  afterward (Finding 3).
- **Pollination:** Not required for any of the three crops as grown
  commercially — all are sterile, parthenocarpic, seedless clones; true
  seed set is extremely rare and relevant only to formal breeding
  programs, which cross seed-fertile relatives and then select and
  propagate successful clones vegetatively rather than producing true-
  breeding hybrid seed.
- **Propagation:** Exclusively vegetative across all three crops —
  sword suckers dug from established plantings, corm pieces, or
  laboratory-grown (tissue-culture) plantlets; suckers are commonly
  treated (pared and hot-water-treated) against pests before planting.
  Tissue culture reliably excludes ordinary externally transmitted
  pathogens, but B-genome cultivars (plantain and some hybrids) carry a
  distinct clonal-degeneration risk from an infectious virus integrated
  directly into their own genome, which tissue culture itself can
  trigger into activity (Finding 8) [23].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Disputed between sources for flowering:
  some classify banana as day-neutral for floral induction itself, with
  photoperiods under roughly 12 hours only slowing the rate of bunch
  initiation and development rather than blocking flowering outright;
  other work describes a facultative long-day response in which longer
  photoperiods during the mid-vegetative phase (roughly the two to
  three months before bunch formation) hasten flowering, with
  sensitivity reportedly varying by genome group/cultivar. Not
  reconciled between sources [24]. Not applicable to tuberization/
  bulbing, since Musa has no such storage organ.
- **Water use:** An unusually shallow root zone combined with a
  conservative soil-moisture-depletion allowance and a notably high
  yield-sensitivity-to-water-deficit parameter make this one of the
  more drought-sensitive major fruit crops; good drainage is also
  important, since the crop does not tolerate waterlogging well
  despite its high water demand (Finding 4).
- **Cold/heat limits:** Growth slows well above freezing and stops
  entirely at a specific moderately cool temperature; frost kills
  above-ground tissue outright; a specific cold-triggered flowering
  disorder ("choke throat," where leaves and the flower structure fail
  to emerge properly) is documented in cooler growing conditions; at
  the opposite extreme, growth also stops above a specific very high
  heat threshold.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A soil-borne fungal wilt disease, a major
  leaf fungal disease, an incurable aphid-borne virus, and a bacterial
  wilt disease are the dominant disease concerns, with relative
  severity varying by crop and genome group (Finding 5). A separate,
  genome-integrated viral degeneration risk affects B-genome cultivars
  specifically (Finding 8).
- **Soil:** A standard international crop-water-use reference rates
  banana only qualitatively as salinity-"sensitive," with no numeric
  salinity threshold or yield-decline slope given [2][25]. Field-
  irrigation studies do give real figures, though for irrigation-water
  EC rather than the soil-ECe convention FAO's own table uses, so the
  two aren't directly comparable: an Omani trial found yield losses up
  to 50% once irrigation water exceeds roughly 1.0 dS/m EC, and a
  Brazilian study found an roughly 10.1% yield decline per unit
  increase in irrigation-water EC above a 0.6 dS/m baseline, regardless
  of cultivar [25].

**Dessert banana specifically:**

- **Climate:** Tropical and frost-free subtropical lowlands.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible when ripe; notably calorie-dense and
  potassium-rich for a fruit.
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield (derived from an older
  combined banana-and-plantain statistic) sits in the low thousands of
  kg/ha; well-managed export-oriented commercial plantations reach
  figures several times higher (see Estimates below).
- **Labor:** Export-plantation labor is substantial and well
  documented relative to the other two crops, split between field
  operations (including a cableway-assisted harvest and transport
  system at the most intensive production tiers) and packing-house
  labor (Finding 6).
- **Storage:** Green fruit is held at a specific cool-but-not-cold
  temperature for several weeks before deliberate ethylene-triggered
  ripening; once ripe, shelf life is only a few days.

**Plantain specifically:**

- **Climate:** Specifically humid lowland tropics, documented across
  West and Central Africa, the Caribbean, and northern South America.
- **Food status:** Not typically raw-edible — green fruit is starchy
  and always cooked; ripe fruit can technically be eaten raw but is
  usually still cooked.
- **Yield / composition:** Smallholder on-farm yields in West and
  Central Africa are considerably lower than intensively managed
  irrigated systems elsewhere, which can reach yields several times
  higher (see Estimates below).
- **Labor:** A detailed South American cost study shows irrigation as
  the single largest labor-cost share, with harvest and bunch-bagging
  labor also significant (Finding 6).
- **Storage:** Green fruit is held slightly cooler than the dessert
  crop and ripens sooner once removed from cool storage.

**East African highland banana specifically:**

- **Climate:** Cool tropical highland elevations specifically, with
  documented best performance at a fairly narrow elevation band well
  above sea level.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — cooking-type clone sets are always
  steamed green; the separate beer clone set is bitter and astringent
  and used only for brewing.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented median on-farm yields in one
  major growing country are higher than earlier literature reports
  suggested, though a bacterial wilt disease remains the dominant
  yield-limiting factor on affected farms (Finding 5).
- **Stand life:** Grown in permanent home-garden plantings that can
  persist for decades, far longer than typical commercial dessert or
  plantain plantings.
- **Storage:** Cooking-type matooke has a very short green shelf life
  (a matter of days) and is normally cooked promptly after cutting;
  beer-type fruit is deliberately pit-ripened for several days before
  pressing.

## Hazards

A soil-borne fungal wilt disease, a major leaf fungal disease, an
incurable aphid-borne virus, and a bacterial wilt disease are the
dominant disease concerns across all three crops, with relative
severity varying by crop and genome group (see Finding 5 above for
detail).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — people with a natural rubber latex allergy can
experience cross-reactive allergic symptoms from handling or eating
bananas. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — green fruit and peels contain tannins relevant to
their use as livestock feed, and rejected/reject-grade fruit fed to
animals can carry pesticide residue; worker exposure to nematicides and
fungicides in commercial plantations was not researched in depth here.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — home-distilled spirits made from the highland
beer-banana clone set carry a methanol content worth noting from a
food-safety standpoint, consistent with other home-distilled fruit
spirits. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Dessert banana chains:** Bunch cut mature green → dehanded, latex
  drained, crown treated, and packed the same day (with a meaningful
  fraction rejected at grading) → held at a specific cool-but-not-cold
  temperature → deliberate ethylene-triggered ripening at a warmer
  temperature. Green fruit is also peeled and dried/milled into flour,
  or fried into chips; ripe fruit is dried or pureed. Pseudostem yields
  fiber; leaves are used as plates and wrappers; male flower buds are
  eaten as a vegetable.
- **Plantain chains:** Fruit is graded into multiple quality tiers,
  then ripened either naturally or with ethylene; green pulp is
  processed into fried chips and a milled flour product.
- **Highland banana chains:** Cooking-type matooke is peeled and
  steamed, traditionally wrapped in its own leaves; beer-type fruit is
  pit-ripened, then pressed into juice, combined with roasted sorghum,
  fermented into a traditional beer, and can be further distilled into
  a spirit.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned, frozen, and aseptic
  processed products generally; specific regional cooked dishes;
  banana ketchup, wine, and vinegar; bagging, cableway, and controlled-
  atmosphere shipping equipment and technique in detail; silage use of
  byproducts; and wild seeded banana species.

In northern Indian Hindu tradition, whole banana fruit clusters are
required for a specific goddess offering, and banana with rice balls
appears in ancestor-offering rites; in South Indian Tamil wedding
tradition, whole banana plants (pseudostem with leaves) are tied in
pairs to form a ceremonial doorway arch; in Bengali tradition, a banana
plant is one of several plants bound together and ritually worshipped
at a major autumn festival; in parts of southern India, a ritual
"marriage" of two banana plants, and a separate devotional practice of
planting and tending a banana plant until it fruits, are both
documented. In Thai folk belief, a specific spirit is said to inhabit
some banana plants, marked by colored cloth tied around the pseudostem.
In at least one major East African country, matooke is essentially
synonymous with the general concept of "food" and is considered a
national dish.

## Open research questions

- Optimum growing temperature for the dessert crop conflicts between
  sources (a notably cooler figure specific to one documented cultivar
  trial versus warmer figures from other sources) — not reconciled.
- Documented wind-damage thresholds conflict considerably across
  sources (a roughly fourfold range between the lowest and highest
  reported damage thresholds).
- Alcohol content of the traditional highland banana beer conflicts
  substantially between sources (a notably wide range versus a lower
  single figure from a different source).
- Highland-banana on-farm yield figures from one detailed field study
  are explicitly noted by its own authors as higher than earlier
  published literature suggested — not fully reconciled.
- A minor discrepancy exists between a plantain nutritional-composition
  table figure and the same source's descriptive text.
- The world-average dessert-banana yield figure relies on an older,
  combined banana-and-plantain statistic rather than a current banana-
  specific figure.
- Dessert-crop labor-hour figures derived from plantation staffing
  ratios, pulp-to-peel weight shares, and flour/puree conversion
  fractions are each estimates rather than directly sourced figures.
- Growth-cycle duration for all three crops, and nutrient uptake per
  tonne of fruit, were not well sourced and are estimated.
- No standard "tropical giant herb" climate archetype exists for
  modeling purposes; this crop's frost response (killing the entire
  above-ground plant, not just leaves) does not map cleanly onto
  archetypes built from true trees.
- Flowering photoperiod response (day-neutral-with-modulation vs.
  facultative long-day) is not reconciled between sources, and neither
  camp's sourcing was opened beyond search-snippet depth.
- Banana's FAO-56 salinity rating is a bare qualitative label with no
  numeric threshold; the two numeric field-irrigation figures found use
  irrigation-water EC, not FAO's own soil-ECe convention, so they
  can't be directly substituted into FAO-56-style modeling.
- Whether eBSV activation risk (Finding 8) differs meaningfully between
  the plantain and highland-banana B-genome groups specifically was not
  found — the one activation study located tested a natural plantain
  and a synthetic AAAB hybrid, not the highland group.

## Estimates and derivations

Growth-cycle length to first harvest is derived differently for each
of the three crops: for the dessert crop, from a standard international
reference's documented first-year growing-season length combined with
a documented sucker-to-sucker fruiting interval; for plantain, directly
from a documented regional irrigated-production timeline; for the
highland group, estimated as somewhat longer given cooler highland
growing conditions, though not independently sourced.

Yield tiers for all three crops are derived from a combination of
documented regional production statistics (an older combined banana-
and-plantain world figure for the dessert crop's world average; a
detailed South American national industry statistic for its good and
exceptional tiers; West and Central African on-farm averages for
plantain's lower tiers and a modeled higher-input scenario for its
exceptional tier; and a detailed multi-year field study for the
highland group's typical range), with poor-yield tiers in each case
estimated for a disease-affected growing season.

A seasonal water-use-days index and regional seasonal water totals for
the dessert crop are derived by summing a standard international
reference's documented first- and second-year monthly crop-coefficient
tables, cross-checked against a detailed Brazilian irrigation study's
directly measured seasonal water-use figures; the plantain and
highland crops are assumed to share this water-use physiology by
analogy, since they belong to the same genus and growth habit and no
crop-specific water-balance study was found for either.

A drought-buffer estimate (days without water before yield penalty
begins) is derived from standard soil-water-holding-capacity
assumptions combined with this crop's documented shallow root depth and
conservative depletion allowance.

Labor-hour figures are derived from two very different documented
production systems: a detailed South American cost study's labor-cost
breakdown (converted to hours using the same study's documented daily
wage rate) for plantain, and a documented plantation staffing ratio
(workers per hectare, split between field and packing-house roles) for
the dessert export crop; highland-banana labor was not independently
estimated, being grown primarily in home gardens rather than documented
commercial systems.

Peak-yield-per-day figures for all three crops are derived by dividing
each crop's good-yield tier by an assumed or documented harvest-window
length in days, reflecting the crop's continuous, cut-one-bunch-at-a-
time harvest pattern rather than a discrete harvest season.

Pack-out fraction, pulp-to-peel weight share, and dried/flour/puree
conversion fractions for processed products are each estimated from
documented reject-rate and dry-matter figures, since no source read
gave a directly measured pulp-share or conversion figure for any of the
three crops.
