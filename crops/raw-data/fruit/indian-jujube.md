## Naming and scope

*Ziziphus mauritiana* Lam. (Rhamnaceae): Indian jujube, ber, Indian plum,
Indian cherry, Malay jujube; Chinee apple in Queensland; dunks in
Trinidad and tropical Africa; ponsigué in Venezuela; bedara in Malaya;
widara in Indonesia; phutsa in Thailand; táo in Vietnam; kul in
Bangladesh [1][2][3][18]. The old synonym *Z. jujuba* L. and the common
name "Chinese date" cause confusion with the Chinese jujube (*Z. jujuba*
Mill.), a separate, hardier species of mild-temperate climates [1][2]
(see `jujube.md`). The Indian national food composition table still
labels its ber entry "*Zizyphus jujube*" [9]. The two species differ in
fruit, rootstock, climate, dormancy season and uses, so they are separate
entries. The Indian species is distinguished by the dense silky hairs on
the leaf underside [1].

Wild forms of this species and related wild *Ziziphus* species are
rootstock sources and wild plants, not separate crop entries. The
large-fruited "apple ber" / "Thai ber" / "apple kul" group and the
Bangladeshi BARI cultivars are cultivar notes here, not their own rows
[18].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a tree that rests through the hot dry season.** A shrub
1.2-1.8 m tall or a tree 3-12 m, with a fast taproot, zigzag downy
twigs, and paired straight or hooked spines; thornless forms exist [1].
It is evergreen, or leafless for several weeks in hot summers [1]. In
India the leaves fall after fruit ripens, the tree rests through the hot
dry summer, and new growth flushes just before the monsoon. Flowers form
on that new growth, and fruit takes 4 months (early cultivars) to 6
months (late) to develop [2]. Indian cultivars ripen from October through
mid-February to the end of April [1]. In parts of Southeast Asia,
flowering follows wet-season shoot growth and fruit matures in the dry
season [2]. In Egypt's Assiut Governorate there are two crops a year, a
main early-spring crop and a second in autumn [1]. This is the reverse of
the Chinese jujube's winter dormancy (see `jujube.md`).

**Finding 2 — self-incompatible cultivars must be mixed.** The flowers
are bisexual and protandrous [2]. The pollen is thick and heavy, not
airborne. It is carried by honeybees, a yellow wasp (*Polistes hebraeus*)
and the house fly [1]. 'Banarasi Karaka', 'Banarasi Pewandi' and
'Thornless' are self-incompatible, and 'Banarasi Karaka' and 'Thornless'
are also reciprocally cross-incompatible [1]. Some cultivars set fruit
parthenocarpically [2]. Planting a mix of at least three cultivars is
the standard remedy [2].

**Finding 3 — tough enough to be a declared weed.** In India the
minimum shade temperature for survival is 7-13 °C and the maximum 37-48
°C [1]. Mature trees have survived short freezes in northern Florida,
and young trees may freeze to the ground and recover [1]. The rainfall
range is 150-2,250 mm [1], or 125-2,225 mm in another source [5]. The
tree tolerates moderately saline soil, drought and waterlogging [1]. That
toughness makes it invasive:
- **Queensland:** a category 3 restricted invasive plant under the
  Biosecurity Act 2014. Plants and seed may not be given away, sold or
  released [16]. It was first recorded in the Torres Strait in 1863 and
  in Townsville in 1916, and it spread from early-20th-century mining
  areas [16]. Seed is carried by animals and birds. It regrows from cut
  roots, survives moderate fire even as a seedling, and has no biological
  control agent [16].
- **Northern Territory:** declared under the Weeds Management Act 2001
  as Class A (to be eradicated) and Class C (not to be introduced), with
  a statutory management plan [16].
- **Fiji and Australia:** listed as a serious environmental weed [5].
- **Caribbean:** naturalised and forming thickets in Barbados, Jamaica and
  Puerto Rico [1].
- **Israel:** six trees introduced from Malaysia in 1939 bore light crops
  heavily infested with fruit flies, and were destroyed to protect other
  fruit trees [1].

**Finding 4 — grafted onto wild seedlings, sown in place.** Superior
selections are shield- or T-budded onto seedlings of wild types; ring
budding was the older method [1]. Grafted plants are less thorny than
seedlings [1]. Budding near Bombay was practised from about 1835 but kept
secret until 1904 [1]. Rootstock seed comes from stones of tree-ripened
fruit [1]:
- Stones are floated in 17-18% salt solution, and floaters are discarded.
- Sinkers are dipped in 500 ppm thiourea for 4 hours and cracked. The
  seeds then germinate in 7 days, against 21-28 days in uncracked stones.
- Seed stays viable for about 2½ years, with germination declining.
- Spring-sown seedlings are ready to bud in 4 months.

Because of the taproot, rootstocks are often raised in the field and
budded in place [1]. Nursery seedlings may need up to 15 months before
field planting [5]. Seedling and wild trees can be top-worked in June
and bear a year later. The Punjab top-worked 50,000 trees free for growers
in 1935-1939 [1]. Air-layers root with IBA and NAA at 5,000-7,500 ppm plus
100 ppm boron [1]. In one Sahel trial, locally sourced suckers rooted and
performed better as rootstocks than nursery seedlings [3].

**Finding 5 — hard pruning every year, and several picks.** After the
first year's training to one shoot with nothing below 75 cm, and shaping
in years two and three, trees are pruned right after harvest at the start
of dormancy [1]. Each pruning removes 25-50% of the previous year's
growth, sometimes with a lighter second pruning before flowering [1].
Flowering and fruiting are on current-season shoots, so pruning sets the
crop [18]. Fruit ripens unevenly. It is picked 2-3 times by hand from
ladders, at about 50 kg per worker per day, and the rest is shaken down
[1]. Unripe fruit is acrid, and overripe fruit loses colour and crispness
[2]. Slightly underripe fruit is white, crisp and apple-like; fully ripe
fruit is mealy, and overripe fruit turns soft, spongy and musky [1].

**Finding 6 — yields depend on the training system.**
- **Per tree:** seedling trees bear 5,000-10,000 small fruits a year, and
  superior grafted trees up to 30,000 [1]. The best Indian cultivar, at
  about 66 fruit per kg, yields 77 kg a tree [1]. Prime-age trees (10-20
  years) in northern India yield 80-200 kg [5]. In a 1980 Haryana trial
  of 16 cultivars, 'Banarasi Karaka' gave the most, 130 kg per tree [1].
- **High-density Assam practice:** standard spacing is 5 × 3 m, and
  high-density 3 × 2.5 m, for 30-40 t/ha [18]. One farm trial of Thai
  ber, with a specific pruning height and micronutrients, reported 85.18
  t/ha [18]. That is a single trial, well above the guidance range.
- **Bangladesh:** in a twelve-cultivar trial, Dhaka-90 kul yielded 24.25
  t/ha. Apple kul was judged the best commercial cultivar for appearance
  and demand [18].
- **India, 1984-1995:** 0.9 million t on 88,000 ha with improved
  cultivars is reported [5], about 10 t/ha if annual (see Estimates,
  below).
- **Sahel:** rainfed yields followed annual rainfall closely [3].

National area figures conflict widely between sources [12].

**Finding 7 — storage advice that contradicts itself on cold.** At
ambient temperature, slightly underripe fruit ripens and keeps 8 days
under wheat straw, 7 days under leaves, and 4 days with carbide [1]. The
sources disagree on how cold it can be stored:
- Cold-storage studies of 'Umran' used 3-5 °C at 85-90% or 85-95% RH
  [19]. A practitioner guide claims 30-40 days under those conditions
  [19].
- The same guide warns that below 5 °C the fruit browns, loses flavour,
  and breaks down internally [19]. That conflicts with the 3-5 °C regime.
- Another study held 'Umran' 35 days at 10 °C and 79% RH [19].
- Polymer-film packaging stretched shelf life from 4 days to 15 at
  ambient, and to 25 refrigerated [19].
- Earlier snippets gave under a week at ambient and two to three weeks
  cooled [6].

The fruit stands handling, shipping and marketing very well [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial shrub or small tree, evergreen or
  summer-deciduous (Finding 1) [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; uneven ripening picked in 2-3
  or more passes; two crops a year in Assiut, Egypt (Findings 1, 5) [1][2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Top-worked trees bear a year later
  [1]. Clonal trees bear within two years [2]. In a Sahel agroforestry
  study, significant production started around years 5-6, peaked near
  year 10, and lasted about 40 years [4]. Wild plants in northern
  Australia may take 8 years to reach the 1 m height at which they seed
  [5].
- **Climate zones:** Tropical to subtropical, arid to semi-arid; needs a
  fairly dry climate and full sun; unsuited to the wetter lowlands of
  Malaysia [1]. Survival limits 7-13 °C minimum and 37-48 °C maximum in
  India [1]. Australian stands grow at 470-1,200 mm rainfall, best in
  riparian zones in the drier part [5]. *Elevation:* wild trees reach
  1,650 m in China and India; commercial culture stops at 1,000 m [1][5];
  naturalised to 600 m in Fiji [5]. Chill requirement: none found.
- **Composition:** Indian national food composition table (2017), item
  E068, per 100 g edible: water 84.4 g, protein 1.34 g, fat 0.35 g, ash
  0.80 g, total dietary fibre 3.73 g (2.71 insoluble, 1.02 soluble),
  carbohydrate 9.40 g, energy 204 kJ (49 kcal), thiamine 0.01 mg,
  riboflavin 0.02 mg, niacin 0.33 mg, folate 5.99 µg, vitamin C 60.9 mg
  [9]. The older reference values are water 81.6-83.0 g, carbohydrate
  17.0 g, sugars 5.4-10.5 g, fibre 0.60 g, vitamin C 65.8-76.0 mg, and
  pectin 2.2-3.4% (dry) [1]. An encyclopedia gives 58.5 kcal and 70.9 mg
  vitamin C [5]. Secondary sites give about 79 kcal [9]. Fibre differs
  six-fold, 0.6 against 3.73 g, probably from crude-fibre vs
  dietary-fibre methods. That is an inference.
- **Edibility:** Raw, crisp or ripe; unripe fruit eaten with salt in
  Southeast Asia; seed kernels in famine [1].
- **Pollination:** Required for self-incompatible cultivars; insect
  pollinated; mix at least three cultivars (Finding 2) [1][2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Flowering is set by the
  pre-monsoon flush and pruning rather than day length in the sources
  [2][18]. Not applicable to storage organs.
- **Yield:** About 10-40 t/ha across systems, with one 85 t/ha trial
  outlier; 77-200 kg per tree (Finding 6; see Estimates, below) [1][5]
  [18].
- **Water use:** No crop coefficient found. Irrigation in hot weather and
  during fruit development is highly beneficial; water stress drops
  immature fruit; Indian growers have irrigated up to 35 times through
  the winter [1]. The summer rest needs no irrigation [2]. Sahel rainfed
  yields rose close to linearly with rainfall above a threshold [3].
  Drip dosing for a Sahel agroforestry system was documented [4].
- **Propagation:** Budded or grafted onto wild seedling rootstock, often
  in place (Finding 4) [1][2][3]. *Clonal/viral risk:* a witches'-broom
  disease caused by a mycoplasma-like organism, found in 1973 near Pune,
  was shown to transmit by grafting or budding diseased scions [1].
  Clean-scion practice was not found.
- **Labor:** Harvest at about 50 kg per worker-day [1], or about 440
  worker-days per hectare at 22 t/ha (see Estimates, below). Pruning and
  spraying hours: Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Best on neutral or slightly alkaline sandy loam; also
  laterite, well-drained medium black soils, and the gravelly alluvium of
  dry riverbeds [1]. In Australia it grows on cracking clays, solodic
  soils and deep alluvium [5]. Tolerates alkaline soils up to pH 9.2
  [2] (carried from the original pass; not re-opened). Fertiliser practice is 10 kg manure plus 0.5 kg ammonium sulphate
  per tree per year of age up to year 5, or NPK twice a year at about 110
  and 172 kg/ha [1]. Zinc and boron sprays improve gloss [1]. *Salinity:*
  "moderately saline soils are tolerated" [1]. Seedling screening rated
  cv. Tikadi moderately tolerant up to about 2.5 mmhos/cm [20]. A pot
  study of 'Gola' and 'Umran' found dry mass and leaf area falling
  linearly with salinity from a soil of ECe 1.5 dS/m, pH 8.2 and SAR 12.5
  [20]. No yield threshold was found.
- **Storage (postharvest):** See Finding 7: ambient 4-8 days; cold
  regimes and chilling limits conflict [1][6][19].
- **Establishment:** Untrimmed trees at 11-12 m; pruned trees at 7-8 m
  [1]. Assam practice uses 5 × 3 m, or 3 × 2.5 m for high density [18].
  Intercropping is limited to the first 2-3 years [7].
- **Cultivars:** India has 90 or more [1]. Among them:
  - **Umran:** large, 30-89 g; golden turning chocolate-brown; TSS 19%;
    150-200 kg per tree; late; keeps and ships well [1].
  - **Gola:** 14-17 g; TSS 17-19%; 80-100 kg; earliest; high price [1].
  - **Kaithli:** TSS 18%; 100-150 kg per tree; high vitamin C [1].
  - **Katha phal:** about 10 g; TSS 23%; late [1].
  - **Seo ber (Seb):** astringent unless peeled or eaten light brown;
    keeps very well [1].
  - **Nazuk:** poor keeper [1].
  - **Dandan:** non-spiny; keeps well [1].
  - **Gular Bashi:** TSS 18.8% yellow, 22.4% brown [1].
  - **Assam types:** five described, from fence-hedge wild shrubs to the
    thornless 'Bali bogri' and the recommended 'Tenga-mitha-bogri' [1].
  - **Thai / apple ber and apple kul:** large-fruited; high-density
    systems [18].
  - **Bangladesh:** BARI kul 1 and 2, Dhaka-90 [18].

  One cultivar is documented as resistant to fruit flies and borers [7].
  Fruit of wild trees is 1.25-2.5 cm long, and up to 6.25 × 4.5 cm under
  good cultivation [1].
- **Weather response:** Frost: young trees freeze back and recover;
  mature trees survive short freezes [1]. Heat: survives to 37-48 °C [1].
  Drought: tolerated, but water stress drops immature fruit [1].
  Waterlogging: tolerated [1]. Frost as a quality event: not found.
- **Water regime:** No flooded requirement; rainfed or irrigated orchard
  [1][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Crisp slightly-underripe fruit for fresh
  sale and candying; fully ripe fruit for drying and drinks [1]. Two
  seasonal crops in Egypt [1]. Successive ripening within one tree, picked
  in several passes (Finding 5) [1][2].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced. The family includes many nitrogen-fixing
  species, but no nodulation was reported for this one [5].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native from Yunnan to Afghanistan,
  Malaysia and Queensland in one account [1]. Another gives South Asia,
  southern China, Southeast Asia and Central and West Africa [5]. The
  Queensland range is thus "native" in one source and a regulated weed in
  another (Finding 3) [1][16]. Regulated in Queensland and the Northern
  Territory; serious weed in Fiji and Australia; naturalised in the
  Caribbean; destroyed in Israel over fruit flies (Finding 3)
  [1][5][16].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Invasive weed:** legally restricted in Queensland and declared in the
  Northern Territory; forms impenetrable thickets that block stock and
  cut pasture; spread by animals and birds [5][16].
- **Fruit flies** (*Carpomyia vesuviana*, *C. incompleta*): the worst
  pest in India. The flies prefer the largest, sweetest fruit: 100% of a
  susceptible cultivar may be attacked while a small, less sweet type
  nearby loses 2% [1]. Larvae pupate in soil, so ground treatment under
  the tree helps [1].
- **Other pests:** leaf-eating and slug caterpillars, a gall mite on
  twigs, a fruit-boring caterpillar, weevils, and dodder (*Cuscuta*) [1].
- **Diseases:** powdery mildew (defoliation, fruit drop); sooty mould;
  Cercospora and Isariopsis leaf spots; leaf rust from mild to severe on
  all Punjab cultivars; the graft-transmitted witches'-broom [1].
- **Fungal contamination of the edible product:** fruit on the tree is
  attacked by *Alternaria*, *Aspergillus* (including *A. parasiticus*),
  *Phoma* and others. In storage, fruit is spotted by *Alternaria*,
  *Phoma*, *Curvularia* and *Cladosporium* and rotted by *Fusarium*,
  *Nigrospora*, *Epicoccum* and *Glomerella* [1]. *A. parasiticus* is a
  known aflatoxin producer. That is an inference; no aflatoxin data was
  found for ber. TO BE ADDRESSED.
- **Saponin:** fruit is used to stupefy fish in Ethiopia, and leaves
  lather in water [1]. Strong doses of bark or root may be toxic [1].
  No hazard was found for normal fruit or fodder use.
- **Spines:** paired stipular spines on non-thornless types; grafted
  plants have fewer [1].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (crisp fruit → fresh):** hand-picked → ambient or cold store
  (Finding 7) → fresh sale; for long trips in India, wrapped in white
  cloth and packed in 50 kg paper-lined burlap bags [1].
- **Chain 2 (underripe fruit → candied):** prick → salt brine raised from
  2% to 8% → drain → 8% salt with 0.2% potassium metabisulphite → store
  1-3 months → rinse → cook in sugar syrup with citric acid [1].
- **Chain 3 (ripe fruit → dried / powder / drink):** sun-dried, then
  ground to powder for out-of-season use; crushed in water as a cold
  drink; in Africa, dried fermented pulp is pressed into gingerbread-like
  cakes; in Venezuela, a liqueur (*crema de ponsigué*) [1]. Acid types
  are pickled or made into chutney [1].
- **Chain 4 (stones → rootstock):** salt-float selection → thiourea →
  crack → sow → bud at 4 months (Finding 4) [1].
- **Chain 5 (prunings and leaves → fodder, fuel, fencing):** leaves are
  eaten by camels, cattle and goats. Dry-matter analysis: crude protein
  12.9-16.9%, fibre 13.5-17.1%, ash 10.2-11.7%, calcium 1.42-3.74% [1].
  Leaf meal is limited to 10-20% of a sheep's diet [8]. Leaves also feed
  silkworms [1]. Branches serve as firewood and thorny corral walls [1].
- **Chain 6 (lac):** a host for the lac insect *Kerria lacca*, the source
  of shellac. Trees are inoculated with broodlac in October-November and
  the resin harvested in April-May. Systematic pruning supplies the young
  shoots needed [1][13].
- **Other uses:** hard, reddish, durable wood for well linings, tool
  handles, turnery and charcoal; dye for silk in Myanmar and a cinnamon
  bark dye in Kenya; a minor honey plant in India and Queensland [1][5].
  Young leaves are cooked as a vegetable in Indonesia [1].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** canned or frozen products; folk-medicine
  preparations [1].

**Religious and ritual use:**
- In West Bengal, ripe fruit is offered to the goddess Saraswati at her
  winter festival, and devotees do not eat the fruit before the offering
  [5].
- In the Sikh tradition, sacred jujube trees stand beside a revered pool
  at a major temple complex, with a founding legend of devotion; fallen
  fruit is gathered rather than picked [11]. Some sources identify those
  trees as Chinese jujube instead, so the species there is unresolved [11].
- Under Jewish law, the general orlah rule forbids fruit from a tree's
  first three years after planting; it applies to this species as to any
  fruit tree [17].

## Open research questions

- **Composition:** energy 49 kcal [9], 58.5 kcal [5] or about 79 kcal
  [9]; fibre 0.6 g [1] vs 3.73 g [9]. The Indian national table is
  preferred here.
- **Cold storage:** 3-5 °C regimes [19] vs chilling injury below 5 °C
  [19] vs 10 °C for 35 days [19]. Needs a primary postharvest source.
- **National area** for India conflicts widely between sources [12]; the
  1984-1995 figure [5] is ambiguous as to whether it is annual.
- **Ploidy** is reported as diploid, higher-ploidy and octoploid in
  different germplasm, possibly real variation [2][14].
- **Native range in Queensland:** native in one source [1], a regulated
  weed in Queensland law [16].
- **Water use, labour beyond harvest, soil biology, salinity yield
  threshold, photoperiod, controlled environment:** not sourced.
- **Witches'-broom** clean-stock practice was not found.

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield per hectare from spacing.** Pruned trees at 7-8 m [1] give
10,000 ÷ 64 = 156 to 10,000 ÷ 49 = 204 trees/ha. At 80-200 kg per tree
[5], that is 12.5-40.8 t/ha; at the midpoint of 156 trees × 140 kg it is
21.8 t/ha. Untrimmed trees at 11-12 m [1] give 69-83 trees/ha, or
5.5-16.6 t/ha. The high-density 3 × 2.5 m spacing [18] gives 1,333
trees/ha; 30-40 t/ha [18] then implies 22-30 kg per tree. A regional
yield survey would replace these.

**Indian average, about 10 t/ha.** 0.9 million t ÷ 88,000 ha [5] = 10.2
t/ha, if the figure is annual. The source's "1984 to 1995" wording leaves
that unclear; if it were a cumulative total, the yield would be far
lower.

**Fruit count.** The best Indian cultivar's 77 kg at 66 fruit per kg [1]
= about 5,100 fruit per tree, each about 15 g. That is consistent with
seedling trees' 5,000-10,000 small fruit, and well below the 30,000 cited
for superior grafted trees [1].

**Harvest labour, about 440 worker-days per hectare.** 21.8 t/ha (above)
÷ 50 kg per worker-day [1] = 436 worker-days; at 10 t/ha, 200. Range
about 200-800 worker-days per hectare for 10-40 t/ha.

**Energy conversion.** 204 kJ [9] ÷ 4.184 = 48.8 kcal.

**Fertiliser at year 5.** 5 × 0.5 kg ammonium sulphate [1] = 2.5 kg per
tree; at 21% N that is about 0.53 kg N per tree. The 21% N content is
standard for ammonium sulphate, not taken from the source.
