# Indian Jujube / Ber (*Ziziphus mauritiana*)

## Naming and scope

*Ziziphus mauritiana* Lam. (Rhamnaceae): Indian jujube, ber, Indian plum,
Chinee apple, and numerous regional names across South and Southeast Asia
and West Africa [1][2][3]. It is a distinct species from Chinese jujube
(*Z. jujuba*), a separate crop this project has also researched, though
an old taxonomic synonym has historically caused confusion between the
two [2]. The two species share almost nothing in cultivation: different
fruit, different rootstock species, different climate requirements, and
different product uses, so they are treated here as entirely separate
crops, grounded against each other only for a small number of genus-wide
physiological analogies (water and soil nutrient uptake) noted below.

Wild forms of this species and related wild *Ziziphus* species are
rootstock sources and wild/foraged-plant entries, not separate cultivated
crop entries. A large-fruited cultivar group known as "apple ber" and
related Southeast Asian high-density cultivars are treated here as
cultivar-group notes within this entry rather than as their own crop.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a tropical-to-subtropical tree with its own summer
dormancy pattern.** This species is a shrub or small tree reaching
3-12 m (occasionally 15 m), either evergreen or losing its leaves for
several weeks during the hottest part of the dry season, with zigzag,
tomentose twigs bearing paired spines (thornless cultivars also exist)
[1][2]. In India, leaves drop after fruit ripening and the tree rests
through the hot dry summer, then flushes new growth just before the
monsoon; flowers form on this new growth, and fruit takes four months
(early cultivars) to six months (late cultivars) to develop [2]. Ripening
runs from October to late April depending on cultivar [1]. In parts of
Southeast Asia, flowering instead follows wet-season shoot growth, with
fruit maturing in the dry season [2]. In one North African growing
region, two crops a year are produced: a main spring crop and a second
autumn crop [1].

**Finding 2 — self-incompatibility makes cultivar mixing a management
requirement.** Flowers are protandrous (releasing pollen before their
own stigma is receptive) and bisexual; pollen is heavy and not wind-
borne, carried instead by honeybees, wasps, and house flies [1][2].
Several important cultivars are self-incompatible, and at least two of
those are also cross-incompatible with each other, while some cultivars
set fruit parthenocarpically without pollination at all; standard
practice is to plant a mixture of at least three cultivars together to
ensure adequate cross-pollination [1][2].

**Finding 3 — an exceptionally tough, wide-range tree.** This species
survives a very broad climate range: documented survival minimums around
7-13°C and maximums as high as 48-50°C in its main growing region, with
light frost tolerated by mature trees (young trees may freeze to the
ground but recover) [1][2]. It tolerates annual rainfall from about
125 mm up to over 2,200 mm, performing best with 300-500 mm in a rainfed
setting with a clear dry season, and grows from sea level to 1,000 m
commercially (wild populations reach higher elevations) [1][2][5]. It
tolerates deep sandy loam through to moderately saline, alkaline (up to
pH 9.2), drought-prone, and even occasionally waterlogged soils [1][2][5].
Despite this toughness, it is also documented as a serious environmental
weed in at least two island/continental ecosystems outside its native
range [5].

**Finding 4 — grafted trees on wild seedling rootstock, not true seed.**
Commercial trees are budded or grafted onto seedlings of the wild form of
this species or related wild *Ziziphus* species, often sown and budded in
place because of the tree's strong taproot, which resents transplanting
[1][2][3]. Seed for rootstocks is selected by floating stones from fully
ripe fruit in a strong salt solution and discarding floaters; sinkers
germinate faster after a brief thiourea soak and cracking (about a week)
versus leaving them uncracked (three to four weeks); viability declines
over roughly two and a half years [1]. In at least one West African
trial, locally sourced suckers rooted and performed better as rootstock
material than nursery-raised seedlings [3]. Clonal trees bear within two
years and reach a sizeable crop by year four; in one Sahelian agroforestry
study, significant production began around years five to six, peaked
near year ten, and continued for roughly forty years [2][4].

**Finding 5 — heavy post-harvest pruning and multiple uneven pickings.**
Growers prune hard right after harvest, at the start of the dormant
period, removing roughly a quarter to half of the previous year's growth,
sometimes with a lighter second pruning before flowering [1]. Fruit
ripens unevenly on the tree, requiring two to four or more hand-picking
passes from ladders, with the remainder shaken down at the end of the
season; a single picker can gather roughly 50 kg a day [1][2]. Unripe
fruit is notably acrid, while overripe fruit loses both color and
crispness, so timing each pass matters for quality [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A shrub or small tree, evergreen or summer-
  deciduous, perennial bearing.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing with an extended, uneven ripening
  window per season requiring multiple hand-picking passes (Finding 5);
  at least one growing region documented a distinct two-crop-per-year
  pattern (spring and autumn).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, arid, and semi-arid; an
  exceptionally wide tolerance of heat, drought, and marginal soils, with
  light frost tolerated by mature trees but young trees killed by hard
  freezes (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible at the crisp (slightly underripe) stage;
  unripe fruit is acrid and not palatable.
- **Pollination:** Required for self-incompatible cultivars; insect-
  pollinated (honeybees, wasps, house flies), with a recommended mixed
  planting of at least three cultivars to ensure cross-pollination
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Reported per-tree yields range roughly 80-200
  kg depending on cultivar and season, with seedling trees producing many
  more, smaller fruits than superior grafted selections. Documented
  per-hectare yields vary enormously by growing system: a national
  average figure for improved plantations in one major growing country,
  a much higher figure under high-density training in one regional
  program, and a wide range under rainfed, low-input cultivation in one
  West African trial that tracked closely with annual rainfall. Fruit
  composition (energy, protein, vitamin C) varies meaningfully between
  reference sources for the same growing region (see Open research
  questions). National area statistics for this crop conflict very
  widely between sources.
- **Water use:** Mature trees in arid-zone cultivation need a documented
  minimum seasonal water total for optimal production; in a rainfed West
  African trial, yield rose in close to a linear relationship with annual
  rainfall above a documented threshold. Water stress during flowering
  and fruit growth causes immature fruit to drop, while the tree's
  natural leafless summer rest period needs no irrigation at all. No
  crop-specific water-use coefficient was found for this species in the
  sources consulted; water-use physiology here is estimated by analogy to
  its genus-sibling crop, Chinese jujube, which this project has also
  researched, adjusted for this species' different, hot-season (rather
  than cold-season) dormancy pattern.
- **Establishment:** Budded trees are planted (or rootstock seedlings are
  sown in place and later budded), staked, and trained to four to six
  main scaffold branches; intercropping is typically limited to the
  first two to three years while the orchard canopy closes.
- **Labor:** Harvest labor is derived from a documented per-picker daily
  rate; pruning and pest-control labor are rough estimates, since no
  dedicated cost-of-cultivation study specific to this crop was found.
- **Soil:** Tolerates an unusually wide pH range, from moderately acid
  sandy soils (one West African trial) up to strongly alkaline soils
  (pH 9.2); moderately saline and even occasionally waterlogged soils are
  tolerated; soil nutrient-uptake figures are estimated by analogy to its
  genus-sibling crop.
- **Stand life:** Bears within two years on clonal rootstock, builds to a
  sizeable crop by year four, peaks around year ten in at least one
  long-term agroforestry study, and remains productive for roughly forty
  years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fruit flies are the dominant pest, capable of
  attacking up to essentially all fruit on highly preferred, large sweet
  cultivars while largely ignoring small, sour fruit on a neighboring
  tree; fruit borers, caterpillars, gall mites, and chafer beetles are
  also documented, along with powdery mildew, leaf spots, rust, and a
  graft-transmissible witches'-broom disorder (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** A very large number of named cultivars exist in the main
  growing region (estimated in the low hundreds), spanning early to late
  maturity, small to large fruit, and a distinct large-fruited "apple
  ber" cultivar group developed for high-density, intensively managed
  plantings; one cultivar is documented as notably resistant to fruit
  flies and borers.
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked from ladders over multiple uneven passes, then
  the remainder shaken down (Finding 5).
- **Storage:** Shelf life at ambient temperature is short (under a week);
  cooling to a few degrees above freezing extends storage to two to three
  weeks or more, and wax coating in perforated bags extends it further.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include fruit flies (the dominant pest,
capable of near-total infestation of preferred sweet cultivars), fruit
borers, caterpillars, gall mites, chafer beetles, powdery mildew (causing
defoliation and fruitlet drop), leaf spots, rust, and a graft-transmitted
witches'-broom disorder. Paired stipular spines are a physical/handling
hazard on non-thornless cultivars (grafted plants typically have fewer
spines than seedlings). This species is documented as a serious
environmental weed outside its native range in at least two
regions. Fruit and leaves contain a saponin compound historically used to
stupefy fish, and strong doses of bark or root are described as toxic in
folk-medicine use; no food-safety issue was identified for the normal
fruit or leaf-fodder uses of this crop.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Crisp-stage fruit is eaten fresh, cold-stored, or brined
  for one to three months (with a small amount of a preservative salt)
  before being candied in syrup; it is also made into pickles and
  chutneys from more acidic cultivars. Fully ripe fruit is sun-dried and
  ground into powder, or crushed in water (stones removed) as a cold
  drink. Fruit stones are floated in salt solution to select rootstock
  seed, grown into seedlings, and budded with cultivar budwood to produce
  nursery trees. Post-harvest prunings are stripped of leaves for fodder,
  with branches used as firewood or thorn fencing.
- **Fodder use:** Leaves are eaten by camels, cattle, and goats, with
  documented crude-protein content in the low-to-mid teens percent on a
  dry-matter basis; leaf meal is recommended at no more than 10-20
  percent of a sheep's diet without harming growth. Leaves are also used
  as silkworm food.
- **Lac host:** This species is a preferred host for lac insects
  (usable for both commercially farmed strains), with broodlac
  inoculation and harvest timed around the annual pruning cycle, which
  provides the young shoot growth the insects need.
- **Other uses:** The wood is hard, durable, and reddish, used for tool
  handles, well linings, firewood, and charcoal; thorny branches are used
  for corrals; the species also serves as a minor honey plant and a
  traditional dye source.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned or frozen fruit
  products; young leaves cooked as a vegetable (documented in one
  Southeast Asian source); detailed folk-medicine preparations.

**Religious and ritual use:**
- In at least one Hindu tradition (West Bengal), ripe fruit of this
  species is offered to the goddess Saraswati at a winter festival
  associated with the goddess, and devotees refrain from eating the
  fruit before the offering is made.
- In the Sikh tradition, sacred trees of this genus stand beside a
  revered pool at a major Sikh temple complex, associated with a founding
  legend of devotion; fallen fruit is traditionally gathered by devotees
  rather than picked from the tree. Some sources identify these specific
  sacred trees as the Chinese jujube rather than this species, so the
  exact species identity of that particular sacred planting remains an
  open question not resolved here.
- Under Jewish law, the general orlah prohibition on fruit from a tree's
  first three years after planting applies to this species as it does to
  any fruit tree, with years counted from planting.

## Open research questions

- National area under cultivation for this crop is reported wildly
  differently across sources, ranging from roughly 22,000 hectares to
  figures an order of magnitude or more higher — not reconciled here.
- Fruit energy content is reported very differently between two regional
  food-composition sources for the same species (roughly a two-fold
  difference) — not reconciled here.
- Chromosome number/ploidy is reported inconsistently across sources
  (with values suggesting diploid, higher-ploidy, and octoploid
  populations in different germplasm) — not reconciled here, and may
  reflect genuine ploidy variation across regional populations rather
  than a single error.
- No water-use coefficient specific to this species was found; the
  estimate used here leans on its genus-sibling crop, Chinese jujube,
  which has an opposite (winter, not summer) dormancy pattern — a real
  source of uncertainty.
- No dedicated cost-of-cultivation labor budget was found for this crop;
  labor-hour figures here are rough estimates built from a single
  documented harvest picking rate.
- Genome size is estimated from a single germplasm source (described as
  octoploid); cultivated cultivars at other ploidy levels likely differ.

## Estimates and derivations

Composition values (energy, protein) are derived from an average of
conflicting regional food-composition figures, since the two main
regional sources differ by roughly two-fold on energy content; a
national food-composition table for the crop's main growing region
would improve this considerably.

Yield tiers are derived from a national average figure for improved
plantations in the main growing region (used as the "average" baseline),
a calculated figure from a typical orchard spacing combined with a
midpoint per-tree yield (used as "good"), the upper end of a high-density
regional training-system yield range (used as "exceptional"), and the
lowest documented rainfed yield from a multi-year West African trial
during a drought year (used as "poor"). Peak daily yield is derived by
spreading the "good" yield tier across an assumed effective picking
window reflecting the crop's multiple-pass harvest pattern.

Water-use parameters (seasonal water-use coefficient, active-season
length, rooting depth, soil-moisture-depletion allowance, drought-day
tolerance) are estimated by analogy to this species' genus-sibling crop,
Chinese jujube, adjusted for this species' documented active growing
season (from monsoon-triggered flush to harvest, roughly 240 days) and
its hot-season rather than cold-season dormancy. This analogy is treated
as a reasonable but imperfect stand-in, since the two species' dormancy
timing differs in a way that likely affects real seasonal water
demand. A documented linear yield response to rainfall above a threshold
in one rainfed West African trial is carried forward directly as a
stress-response rule rather than estimated.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest are derived directly from a documented
per-picker daily harvest rate applied to the estimated yield tiers;
pruning and pest-control labor are rough, largely unsourced estimates
reflecting the documented annual hard-pruning practice and a typical
regional fruit-fly spray program, since no dedicated regional cost-of-
cultivation budget was found.

Storage duration is derived as a midpoint across several cool-storage
trials reporting a range of roughly one to three weeks depending on
exact temperature and cultivar.

Genome size is derived from a single flow-cytometry measurement on
octoploid germplasm from one regional source; this is noted as
uncertain given known ploidy variation in the species.
