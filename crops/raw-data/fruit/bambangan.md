## Naming and scope

*Mangifera pajang* Kosterm. (Anacardiaceae), described in 1965 [1].
Chromosome number 2n = 40 [1].
- **Names:**
  - **Brunei:** bambangan, mombangan [1]; mambangan [10].
  - **Malaysia:** bambangan, embawang (Sabah); mawang, alim, embang
    (Sarawak) [1][2].
  - **Indonesia:** asem payang (Kutai, East Kalimantan), alim, n'cham pajay
    (Kenyah Dayak), hambawang (West Kalimantan) [1].
  - **English:** Borneo mango [7]; "wild mango" in the encyclopedia [2].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Hambawang":** West Kalimantan's name for this species [1] is close
    to "asem hambawang", South Kalimantan's name for bachang (*M.
    foetida*) [13].
  - **"Bambangan":** one database gives it as an English name for bachang
    [13]. In this doc it means *M. pajang* only.
  - **The condiment:** "bambangan" also names the Kadazan-Dusun pickle
    made from the fruit (Finding 2) [3].
- **Range:** endemic to Borneo: Sarawak, Brunei, Sabah and Kalimantan [1].
  It was first planted in fruit collections outside Borneo only in the
  1980s [1].
- **Existing group doc:** `wet-tropics-mangoes.md` (row
  `wet_tropics_mangoes`, `OUT-OF-DATE`) covers this species with bachang,
  kuini and binjai [13]. This doc supersedes its bambangan material.

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from
`mango.md` [13]. It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a separate species, endemic to one island [1]. Grafted on
  mango, it takes only rarely (Finding 4) [5].
- **Fruit:** the largest in the genus, up to 20 cm across [1], and up to
  2-3 kg [6][9]. Thick brown skin, peeled like a banana [1].
- **Product:** a fermented pickle of flesh and grated seed, listed as
  Malaysian national heritage food (Finding 2) [3].
- **Co-products:** a kernel fat studied as a cocoa butter alternative,
  and a fibre-rich peel (Finding 3) [7][8].
- **Status:** IUCN Vulnerable (1998) [2][4], a status none of the other
  researched mangoes has.
- **Against bachang:** the two hybridize where grown together [1], but
  bambangan is deciduous, has a sweeter, less offensive-smelling fruit, and its
  uses are pickle, juice and peel rather than souring [1][7].

**Relatives in this project:**
- mango (`mango`, `mango.md`): the same genus [1].
- bachang (`bachang`, `bachang.md`): natural hybrids form where the two
  grow together [1].
- kuini (`kuini`, `kuini.md`) and binjai (`binjai`, `binjai.md`): the same
  wet-tropics group [13].
- wet-tropics mangoes (`wet_tropics_mangoes`, `wet-tropics-mangoes.md`):
  the older group doc [13].

**Proposed:** none new.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the genus's largest fruit, sold in season but rarely grown
beyond Borneo.**
- **Fruit:** globose or ovoid, 9.5-12 (-20) cm × 6.5-10 (-17) cm, with a
  rough potato-brown skin about 1 cm thick [1]. The skin is 5-7 mm [2], or
  8-10 mm [4], in other sources.
- **Weight:** up to 2 kg [6]. Sabah market fruit usually weighs 0.5-1 kg,
  and a 3 kg "giant" form was reported at Tamparuli [9].
- **Flesh:** bright deep yellow, coarsely fibrous, sweet-acid and
  aromatic [1]. Its smell is like bachang's but less offensive [1].
  Edible pulp is 50-67% of fruit weight [6], or 60-65% [7].
- **Eating:** after lengthwise cuts through the skin, the fruit is peeled
  like a banana [1].
- **Markets:** commonly sold in Dayak markets in season [1]. In Sabah, it
  is in almost every weekly market (*tamu*) [3], and in shops and
  roadside stalls from early August to the end of October [9].
- **Price:** RM1-5 a fruit in Sabah, and RM15 for the 3 kg fruit [9],
  about RM1-10/kg (see Estimates). A 2018 report calls it still
  expensive because supply is low [12] (snippet).
- **Wild or grown:** PROSEA says it is rarely found in forest but common
  in Dayak orchards [1]. The encyclopedia says it is not grown
  commercially and is found in forests [2]. The two are compatible if
  "orchards" means village and home-garden trees.

**Finding 2 — a fermented pickle that is national heritage food.**
- **Name:** *jeruk bambangan*, or *toonsom*, *noonsom* or *rinaal
  bambangan* in Dusunic dialects [3].
- **Method** [3][7]:
  1. Wash the fruit and wipe it dry, so that no dirt from the skin
     touches the flesh.
  2. Peel thinly and cube the flesh.
  3. Wash the seed and cut it small or blend it.
  4. Mix flesh and seed with salt and bird's-eye chilli.
  5. Pack it airless into a closed jar.
  6. Ferment for 7-10 days at room temperature.
- **Salt:** the recipe the encyclopedia follows gives a 2:1 ratio of salt
  to seed [3], which is unusually high; it needs checking.
- **Microbes:** *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* and *Pediococcus
  pentosaceus* are the most common organisms isolated, with *Candida*
  yeasts [7].
- **Fermentation:** fermenting at 35 °C reached peak antioxidant values
  within six days, against 10 days at 28 °C. Phenolic content rose
  throughout, to 44.69 mg GAE/g [8].
- **Keeping:** the pickle lasts about a year with salt and proper storage
  [3][9]. Pickled fruit sells for more than fresh [9].
- **Use:** with the raw fish salad *hinava*, simmered fish, or rice [3].
  The fruit's strong smell goes once pickled [3].
- **Heritage:** listed as Malaysian intangible heritage (National Heritage
  Food) in 2012, and gazetted among 117 national heritage foods in 2025
  [3].

**Finding 3 — the seed and peel are being studied as co-products.**
- **Kernel fat:** the main triglyceride is SOS (28.7%), with POS 11.6% and
  SOO 11.2%. Stearic, oleic and palmitic acids make up 36.4%, 44.5% and
  8.4% [8]. The authors call it suitable as a cocoa butter alternative
  in confectionery [7][8].
  - **Blends:** 70% fractionated kernel-fat stearin with 30% palm mid
    fraction closely matched cocoa butter [8]. Blends of 20% kernel fat
    or stearin with 80% cocoa butter were also compatible [8].
  - **Extraction:** hexane gave the highest fat yield, 7.67% [8]. The
    basis (whole seed or kernel, wet or dry) is not stated in the
    abstract.
- **Peel:** a fibre-rich powder with 72.3 g total dietary fibre per 100 g,
  nearly half soluble [8]. The dried peel is also used in sambal and in
  curries for acidity [1][4][7].
- **Pulp fibre:** a dried fibre product of the pulp has 88% total dietary
  fibre [8].
- **Juice:** commonly drunk in Sabah [7]. In a 9-week human trial, juice
  powder raised plasma β-carotene, vitamin C and antioxidant status [7].
- **Review view:** a 2019 review argues production and trade could grow
  on these properties [8]. All of this is laboratory work; no
  commercial product was found.

**Finding 4 — partly graft-compatible with mango, and horticulturally
unstudied.**
- **On mango rootstock:** at Fairchild (Florida), *M. pajang* grafts on
  'Turpentine' mango had a low success rate. The successful grafts grew
  well for more than 2 years [5].
- **Interstocks:** in 2007, trials were still under way for an interstock
  that would suit it [5].
- **Own propagation:** by seed [1][3]. Seeds are monoembryonic [1].
- **PROSEA's verdict:** "all horticultural aspects of this crop need
  further investigation" [1].
- **A likely copying error:** in the Pl@ntUse copy of PROSEA, the
  bambangan agronomy paragraph matches the bachang one word for word,
  including a West Java harvest for October-December [1]. Bambangan
  doesn't grow in Java. This doc treats that paragraph's spacing,
  budding and harvest details as unconfirmed for bambangan.

**Finding 5 — rich in carotenes.**
- **Pulp:** 7.96 mg α-carotene and 20.04 mg β-carotene per 100 g; peel
  4.2 and 13.09 mg [6]. Cryptoxanthin is 1.18 mg in pulp and 0.60 mg in
  peel [6]. The paper doesn't make clear whether these figures are on a
  fresh or dry basis.
- **Other compounds:** the pulp contains vitamin C, naringenin and
  hesperidin; the peel and seed have the most mangiferin [7].
- **Isoflavones:** daidzein 8.3-8.7 mg per 100 g, higher than bachang;
  genistein 0.4-0.6 mg [7].
- **Proximate composition** of the fresh pulp (water, energy, sugars):
  Not sourced. A Universiti Malaysia Sabah proximate analysis is cited by
  the encyclopedia but was not read [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** a deciduous tree [1][4]. Height up to 33 m with a bole up to 70 cm, without buttresses
  [1]. Other figures: 15-33 m [4], up to 20 m [6], 15-50 m [2].
  - **Sabah:** flowers April-May, fruits July-September [1]; the market
    season runs early August to late October [9].
  - **East Kalimantan:** fruits December-March [1].
  - **Brunei:** part of the August-October fruit season of the Kedayan
    calendar [10].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing, one season a year per region
  [1][9]. A tree can bear hundreds of fruits [6].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** wet lowland tropics, with abundant rain spread
  evenly through the year [1]. Grown to above 1,000 m [1]. Cold tolerance:
  Not sourced.
- **Composition:** see Finding 5 [6][7].
- **Edibility:** ripe flesh raw [1][2]; flushing shoots are sold as a
  vegetable in Sarawak [1][11]. The seed is eaten in the pickle [3].
- **Pollination:** insects [4]. The panicles are fragrant; only one or two
  stamens are fertile [1]. Self-compatibility: Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. "Up to hundreds" of fruits a tree [6]; at 0.5-1
  kg a fruit [9], that suggests a very rough order of 50-100+ kg a tree
  (see Estimates).
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** by seed [1][3]. Grafting on mango is rarely successful
  [5]. Clonal risk: Not applicable in practice.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** well-drained alluvium and lowland dipterocarp forest; avoids
  swampy sites [1]. pH and salinity: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** fresh fruit: Not sourced. Pickle: about a
  year [3][9].
- **Establishment:** Not sourced for this species alone. PROSEA's
  14-16 m spacing and shade-tolerant seedlings may be copied from bachang
  (Finding 4) [1].
- **Cultivars:** none named. A 3 kg "giant" form is sold at Tamparuli,
  said to taste the same but give more flesh [9].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** rain-fed; avoids swampy sites [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** ripe fruit for eating, pickle and juice
  [1][3][7]. The older group doc mentions half-ripe fruit for pickling
  [13]; the sources read here say ripe [7].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Status:** endemic to Borneo and not naturalized elsewhere [1]. IUCN
    Vulnerable (1998 assessment, version 2.3 criteria), which is now old
    [2][4]. No invasive listing found in this pass.
  - **Pests carried:** the mango weevil, whose larvae feed in the fruit
    [1][11].
  - **Movement:** the US limits *Mangifera* seed imports because of seed
    borer, so Fairchild brought it in as graftwood [5].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. A 33 m deciduous forest tree
  is unlikely to suit protected cropping (our inference).

## Hazards

- **Irritant sap:** the bark sap blisters skin [1][4]. The encyclopedia
  says the peel has a corrosive latex layer that can burn the lips [2].
  The pickle recipe's careful wiping and thin peeling keep skin material
  away from the flesh [3]. Unlike bachang, no source says the ripe
  flesh's juice is irritant.
- **Pickle safety:** a spontaneous fermentation at room temperature with
  high salt [3][7]. No safety study (pathogens, histamine, pH) was found.
- **Pests:** the mango weevil damages fruit [1][11]. PROSEA's trunk-borer
  notes may be copied from bachang (Finding 4) [1].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for bambangan.
- **Diseases:** Not sourced.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the irritant latex has no identified compound or
  case data, as for bachang (`bachang.md`). The pickle's salt level and
  food safety also need a primary source.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (pickle):** ripe fruit → washed and dried → thinly peeled →
  flesh cubed; seed washed and chopped or blended → mixed with salt and
  chilli → packed airless in a jar → 7-10 days' fermentation at room
  temperature → *jeruk bambangan*, keeping about a year [3][7][9].
- **Chain (fresh):** ripe fruit → lengthwise cuts → peeled like a banana
  → eaten fresh [1]. Edible share 50-67% [6][7].
- **Chain (juice):** pulp → juice, or spray-dried juice powder [7].
- **Chain (peel):** peel → sun-dried → stored → sambal [1][4], or curry
  souring [7]. Also → fibre-rich powder (laboratory) [8].
- **Chain (kernel fat):** seed → kernel → solvent extraction (hexane
  best, 7.67%) → fat → fractionated stearin → blended with cocoa butter or
  palm mid fraction → cocoa butter alternative (laboratory only) [8].
- **Vegetable:** flushing shoots sold in Sarawak [1][11].
- **Medicinal food:** used by the Dusun for cancer and high cholesterol
  [11]. Kernel and peel extracts are studied in cell lines [7][11]. No
  clinical evidence.
- **Other:** ornamental in flower [1]; the wood is "reputed to be used"
  [1].
- **Ritual and religious:** none found. Its fruiting marks the fruit
  season in the Kedayan ecological calendar of Brunei [10], and the
  pickle is a cultural-heritage food [3]. Neither is a ritual use.

## Estimates and derivations

**Price per kg, about RM1-10.** RM1-5 a fruit for fruit of 0.5-1 kg [9].
Lowest: RM1 for a 1 kg fruit = RM1/kg. Highest: RM5 for a 0.5 kg fruit =
RM10/kg. The 3 kg fruit at RM15 [9] is RM5/kg, inside the range. The
article is from 2018 [9]. A market price survey would replace it.

**Edible flesh per fruit, about 250-670 g.** Fruit 0.5-1 kg [9] × edible
share 50-67% [6]: 500 × 0.50 = 250 g; 1,000 × 0.67 = 670 g.

**Yield per tree, very rough order of 50-100+ kg.** "Up to hundreds" of
fruits [6], read as 100 or more, × 0.5-1 kg [9] = 50-100 kg for 100
fruits, and more for a heavy crop. This is an order of magnitude only;
"up to hundreds" may be a maximum, not a typical crop. A per-tree count
from Sabah or Kalimantan would replace it.
