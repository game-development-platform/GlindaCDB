## Naming and scope

The myrtle-leaved orange, binomial *Citrus myrtifolia* Raf., also called
chinotto. Historically classified as *Citrus aurantium* var. *myrtifolia*
Ker-Gawl. (1818), and formerly also *Citrus pumila* Marc. (1921) [1].
This project's own `bitter-orange.md` entry proposed this as a candidate
row specifically because of this taxonomic back-and-forth plus its
sharply different end product (a soft drink and confectionery item,
rather than peel/oil/rootstock) — this entry confirms that reasoning
holds up on fuller research.

**Taxonomic status is genuinely unsettled, and deliberately not resolved
here.** Wikipedia and a Spanish-language horticultural source both treat
it as an independent species on the strength of its own distinguishing
traits [1]. The USDA's GRIN germplasm database, however, recorded a
nomenclature change on 2020-03-06 reclassifying it from *Citrus
myrtifolia* Raf. back to *Citrus* × *aurantium* L. var. *aurantium* —
i.e., folding it back into bitter orange as a variety, not even granting
it independent-variety status [1]. This entry keeps chinotto as its own
row regardless of which taxonomic authority eventually wins, because
this project's own distinctiveness standard is about end product and
market identity, not formal rank: a carbonated soft drink, a named
Campari/amari flavoring ingredient, and a specific Ligurian candying
tradition are about as different from bitter orange's rootstock/neroli/
marmalade economy as two citrus products get, independent of whether a
taxonomist eventually calls this a full species or not.

**A further, narrower distinction exists within the species itself, and
is treated here as a cultivar/landrace note, not a further row split.**
"Chinotto di Savona," the specific Ligurian landrace behind the Slow Food
Presidium and the historic candying industry, was long considered simply
*Citrus myrtifolia*, but a 2020 genetic study found DNA markers that
distinguish the two [3]. Since this doesn't change the product or market
identity — both are "chinotto" in the market sense — this stays a
cultivar-level note (Cultivars, below) rather than a fourth proposed row.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the fruit is deliberately bred/selected for near-total
bitterness, and that bitterness is the entire point.** Unlike bitter
orange, where bitterness is simply a trait of fruit nobody eats fresh,
chinotto appears to have been specifically valued and propagated *because*
of its bitterness: the tree itself is compact (about 3 m), thornless, and
myrtle-leaved rather than orange-leaved [1][2], and every documented
historical use (medicinal tonic, aperitif, digestif, candying) explicitly
trades on the bitterness as a feature rather than something to breed
away [4][5]. The soft drink industry only began in the 1950s, well after
the fruit's longer history as a medicinal/digestive bitter [4].

**Finding 2 — the Savona candying tradition was an imported technique
that briefly became a defining regional industry, then collapsed for
purely economic reasons, not agricultural failure.** Candying know-how
came to Liguria in 1877 when a candier relocated from Apt, in
southeastern France, where the technique had been practiced since 1780
[2][5]; within a few years "many local businesses" had adopted it, and a
cooperative was formed by the late 1800s to handle the whole chain —
cultivation, processing, and sale [2]. Production peaked across the
19th-20th century boundary, then declined from the 1920s for a
combination of causes: a long, labor-intensive candying process, small
fruit yields, poor economic returns, "short-sighted economic policies,"
and a documented run of unusual winter frosts that damaged the groves
[2][5]. Today only a few plants remain around Savona, and the Slow Food
Presidium (backed by Savona's municipal government) is actively trying to
revive it, coordinating roughly 15 growers and 3 producer-processors as
of the source consulted [2].

**Finding 3 — peel essential-oil composition shifts measurably with
fruit ripeness, echoing the maturity-stage effects this project has
already documented in sweet orange and bitter orange.** A peer-reviewed
study found chinotto peel essential oil's composition and bioactivity
depend on maturity stage, with half-ripe fruit's oil specifically shown
to reduce inflammatory markers in an in-vitro test [6]. A separate,
commercial source (treated cautiously, not peer-reviewed) describes
limonene content ranging 48-80% depending on maturity, with linalool and
linalyl acetate also significant [6]. This parallels `orange.md`'s own
navel-vs-processing-cultivar and `bitter-orange.md`'s unripe-vs-ripe
findings: once again, which growth stage is harvested changes the
product, not just its intensity.

**Finding 4 — composition data is thin but shows an unusually high
flavonoid concentration relative to vitamin C.** The one specific,
peer-reviewed figure located gives about 42 mg vitamin C and 780 mg
flavonoids per 100 g fresh weight [7] — a flavonoid:vitamin-C ratio far
higher than typical citrus, though this wasn't independently
cross-checked against a second primary source, and no calorie, sugar, or
fiber figures were found at all.

**Finding 5 — the fruit is a genuine multi-product crop whose products
span confectionery, beverage, and fine-fragrance/flavoring markets
simultaneously, all from a single small fruit.** The same chinotto fruit
supplies: a nationally recognized carbonated soft drink and aperitif
category (sold industrially since the 1950s, still a popular
cola-alternative in Italy) [4]; the Savona candying/preserved-fruit
tradition (syrup-boiled or liqueur-preserved, historically in Maraschino)
[2][5]; a flavoring ingredient in most Italian amari and in Campari
specifically [1]; and marmalade and candied-peel use (e.g. in panettone)
[5]. Few fruit entries in this project supply this many distinct,
named commercial product categories from one small, barely-edible fruit.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial shrub/small tree, compact,
  thornless, myrtle-like foliage; about 3 m tall at maturity [1] — notably
  smaller and thornless compared with bitter orange's larger, thorned
  habit (see `bitter-orange.md`).
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing with one documented annual
  harvest window: mid-September through late November, during which the
  fruit changes from green to orange; once harvested it is processed
  for consumption year-round rather than sold fresh [2].
- **Climate zones:** Found growing in Malta, Libya, southern France, and
  Italy (mainly Liguria/Savona, also Tuscany, Sicily, and Calabria) [1].
  The documented Savona growing area is narrowly confined to the coast
  between Varazze and Pietra Ligure, attributed to a specific favorable
  microclimate on that stretch of coast [2][5] — not quantified further
  in the sources reviewed. Notably frost-sensitive: a documented run of
  unusual winter frosts contributed directly to the historical decline
  of the Savona groves (Finding 2) [2][5], a materially worse cold
  tolerance than general garden/nursery claims made for bitter orange
  itself (see `bitter-orange.md`).
- **Composition:** Thin sourcing; see Finding 4. No calorie, sugar,
  protein, or fiber figures located.
- **Edibility:** Not eaten fresh (bitter); always processed (brined,
  candied, juiced, or distilled for oil) before consumption.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for this species specifically.
  By citrus-genus analogy (consistent with this project's other citrus
  entries, e.g. `buddhas-hand.md`): flowering is understood to be
  triggered by cold and water-deficit stress rather than day length,
  with floral induction described in the literature as occurring
  independently of photoperiod [9]. Not applicable to tuberization/
  bulbing — a woody tree crop with no storage organ.
- **Pollination:** Not sourced for this species specifically.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. The sources consistently describe yields as
  small in absolute terms ("small quantities of fruit," a contributing
  cause of the Savona industry's economic decline, Finding 2) but no
  per-hectare or per-tree figure was found.
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Not sourced specifically for this species. General
  citrus practice is grafting onto a rootstock (see `bitter-orange.md`'s
  own rootstock-seed economy, and general citrus-budding guides) [8];
  no chinotto-specific rootstock-compatibility or scion-performance data
  was found. One semi-micrografting research preprint used a variegated
  chinotto scion in disease-related graft-induction trials, but that
  study wasn't a propagation guide and gave no usable agronomic figures
  [8].
- **Labor:** Not sourced in hours, but qualitatively confirmed as a major
  economic factor: the candying process is explicitly described as
  "long" and "labor-intensive," cited as a direct cause of the
  industry's historical decline (Finding 2) [2][5].
- **Soil:** Salinity tolerance not sourced for this species specifically.
  By citrus-genus analogy only (strongly rootstock-dependent, as found
  elsewhere in this project): general citrus yield loss begins around
  1.4-2 dS/m soil salinity, with tolerance varying considerably by
  rootstock; no chinotto-specific rootstock or salinity data was
  found [10].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not sourced beyond the frost-damage history
  already covered (Finding 2); no named disease or pest was found
  specific to this species.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for fresh fruit. Processed forms
  (candied, liqueur-preserved) are implicitly long-lived by nature of the
  preservation method, but no shelf-life figure was found.
- **Establishment:** Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** "Chinotto di Savona," the Ligurian landrace behind the
  historic candying industry and the current Slow Food Presidium, is
  genetically distinguishable from the broader species by DNA markers per
  a 2020 study, despite having long been treated as simply *Citrus
  myrtifolia* [3] (Naming and scope, above). A "foliis variegatis"
  (variegated-leaf) form is also documented, used as a scion in at least
  one grafting study, apparently grown partly for its ornamental
  variegation rather than fruit [8].

## Hazards

No hazards specific to this species were found in this pass (beyond the
fruit's intense, deliberate bitterness, which is a culinary/processing
characteristic rather than a safety issue). Not independently researched
for allergens, drug interactions, or toxic-dose concerns the way bitter
orange's synephrine content was (see `bitter-orange.md`); given the
close taxonomic relationship, it's plausible similar compounds are
present, but this wasn't confirmed and is not asserted here.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fruit → soft drink/aperitif)**: juice → carbonated soft
  drink (the "chinotto" soda category, industrial since the 1950s) or
  used as an aperitif/digestif base; also a flavoring component of most
  Italian amari and of Campari specifically [1][4].
- **Chain 2 (fruit → Savona candied/preserved tradition)**: harvested
  fruit (mid-September to late November) → brined approximately three
  weeks (historically in seawater) → hand-peeled to remove the bitter
  outer layer → returned to brine → boiled through syrups of increasing
  concentration → either preserved in liqueur (preferably Maraschino) or
  fully candied [2][5].
- **Chain 3 (peel → essential oil)**: peel, at a chosen ripeness stage →
  essential oil (limonene-dominant, composition and reported
  anti-inflammatory bioactivity both dependent on maturity, Finding 3)
  [6].
- **Chain 4 (minor/other)**: marmalade; candied peel used in confections
  such as panettone [5].
- **Not otherwise detailed here**: specific liqueur/amaro recipes beyond
  naming Campari and "most Italian amari" as users of the flavor; modern
  cocktail use (e.g. a documented Paloma variant substituting chinotto
  soda) [4].

## Open research questions

- The species-vs-variety taxonomic question (Wikipedia's independent-
  species treatment vs. USDA GRIN's 2020 reclassification back into
  *Citrus* × *aurantium* var. *aurantium*) is not resolved here, and may
  never fully settle — this entry's row is kept on product/market
  grounds regardless (Naming and scope).
- No yield, water-use, soil, labor-hour, or detailed composition figures
  (calories, sugars, fiber, protein) were found for this species.
- No propagation or rootstock-compatibility guidance specific to this
  species was found; general citrus-grafting practice is referenced
  instead, not asserted as this crop's own confirmed figure.
- The documented Savona microclimate (Varazze-Pietra Ligure coast) is
  named but not characterized quantitatively (temperature, rainfall) in
  the sources reviewed.
- Hazard/safety profile (allergens, bioactive-compound dose concerns
  parallel to bitter orange's synephrine) was not researched.
- The single sourced vitamin C/flavonoid figure (Finding 4) comes from
  one 2011 study and was not cross-checked against a second primary
  source.
- Exact historical production volumes for the Savona candying industry
  (at its 19th/20th-century peak) were not found — only qualitative
  "flourished" / "many businesses" language.
- Photoperiod sensitivity and salinity tolerance are both sourced only
  by citrus-genus analogy; no species-specific data on either was
  found for this species.
