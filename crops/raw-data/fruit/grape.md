# Grape (*Vitis vinifera*), American Grape (*V. labrusca* and hybrids), and Muscadine (*V. rotundifolia*)

**Category:** Fruit · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/fruit/grape.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-09

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

This entry covers three related crops side by side in one document,
since they share a genus and much of their basic biology but differ
sharply in climate tolerance, disease resistance, and products — the
same approach used elsewhere in this project for a couple of other
closely related crop pairs.

## Naming and scope

The genus *Vitis* (Vitaceae) [1], covering three distinct crops:

- **Grape** (*V. vinifera* subsp. *vinifera*) — the European or Eurasian
  grape, domesticated roughly six thousand years ago in southwest Asia
  or the South Caucasus. It covers wine, table, and raisin grapes, with
  thousands of named cultivars [1].
- **American grape** — grapes of eastern North America and their
  hybrids: the fox grape (*V. labrusca*) and labrusca-type cultivars
  (including a very widely grown juice-grape cultivar that is actually
  roughly a third vinifera by parentage), plus French-American and
  Minnesota-bred interspecific hybrid wine cultivars, and a separate
  native species used for red wine in parts of the central and eastern
  US. These are grouped together because they share cold hardiness,
  own-rooted planting, and a juice/jelly/hybrid-wine industry distinct
  from vinifera's. A large-berried, mostly-vinifera tetraploid table
  grape developed in East Asia is treated here as a cultivar note within
  this group [36].
- **Muscadine** (*V. rotundifolia*, subgenus *Muscadinia*) — a
  distinctly different subgenus with 40 chromosomes rather than 38,
  generally not cross-compatible with the bunch grapes [9][10].

These three crops are treated as genuinely distinct entries because they
differ sharply in several things that matter for cultivation modeling:
winter hardiness (vinifera ranges from very tender to moderately tender,
American grapes are considerably hardier, and muscadine suffers severe
damage even at modest cold temperatures); susceptibility to phylloxera
(vinifera is highly susceptible and almost universally grafted onto
resistant rootstocks, American grapes show only weak native resistance
despite folk claims otherwise, and muscadine is among the most resistant
of all); susceptibility to Pierce's disease (vinifera and American
grapes are both fully susceptible, while muscadine ranges from tolerant
to resistant); fruit and harvest habit (vinifera and American grapes
form bunches that are cut or machine-harvested, while muscadine bears
single berries or loose small clusters that drop when ripe and are
hand-picked or shaken onto catch frames); pollination (vinifera and
American grapes are self-pollinating hermaphrodites, while many
muscadine cultivars are female and require a self-fertile pollinizer
cultivar planted nearby); storage life (vinifera keeps one to three
months with sulfur dioxide treatment, American grapes four to seven
weeks, and muscadine only one to four weeks); and the products each
supports (vinifera for wine, table use, raisins, and several minor
village products; American grapes for juice, jelly, and a historically
important religious use; muscadine for wine, juice, jelly, a hull-based
dessert use, and fresh eating).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one genus, three climate envelopes.** Winter cold and
disease susceptibility draw the sharpest lines between these three
crops. Vinifera dies back where midwinter temperatures fall below
roughly −20 to −26°C, and in humid subtropical growing regions a
specific bacterial disease (Pierce's disease) kills it outright, making
it uneconomic there; in truly tropical conditions it never goes dormant
at all, and growers instead schedule two to three crops a year through
pruning and a dormancy-breaking chemical treatment [11][12][29][34].
American grapes occupy the cold-winter, humid-summer niche that
vinifera cannot tolerate, with documented maximum cold hardiness
considerably below vinifera's [12]. Muscadines are confined to the hot,
humid subtropical southeastern United States, where most cultivars
suffer below roughly −12°C [9].

**Finding 2 — one plant, many products: the processing chain decides the
farm.** World vineyard area and production are both substantial; of
total available fresh grapes, roughly half is pressed into wine and
juice/must, nearly as much again goes to fresh table use, and a smaller
share is dried into raisins, according to one major international wine
and vine organization's statistics, though a general reference
encyclopedia gives a noticeably different split weighted much more
heavily toward wine — the discrepancy likely reflects different
accounting bases rather than a real contradiction [3][8].

**Finding 3 — wine grapes trade yield for quality; table grapes do not.**
A standard international crop-water-use reference documents an enormous
range of vineyard yields depending on whether the goal is maximum
tonnage or concentrated wine quality, spanning roughly 2 to 50 tonnes
per hectare [2]. Yield holds steady until relative evapotranspiration
falls to about 80% of potential, then declines in close to a straight
line as water stress increases further, and wine grapes respond more
sharply to this deficit than table grapes do [2]. Deliberate regulated
deficit irrigation is a standard practice for red wine grapes: water is
kept ample from budburst through flowering, then the deficit is
increased step by step after the onset of ripening specifically to
concentrate colour and flavour compounds, and some European wine
quality appellations forbid irrigation altogether [2]. The number of
flower clusters set the previous season is the single largest source of
year-to-year yield variation [2].

**Finding 4 — the vine is grafted because of a root-feeding insect
pest.** A late-19th-century outbreak of a root-feeding insect pest
destroyed roughly two-thirds to nine-tenths of European vineyards,
crashing one major wine-producing country's output by more than 70%
within little over a decade [15]. The enduring fix, still standard
practice today, is grafting vinifera scion wood onto resistant American
rootstock species; a small number of regions remain naturally free of
this pest and can still grow vinifera own-rooted, as can plantings on
pure sandy soil, and at least one rootstock bred specifically to combine
resistance with vinifera parentage notably failed when the pest
eventually overcame its resistance within about two decades [15].

**Finding 5 — table grapes are a cold-chain and sulfur-dioxide crop; wine
grapes are crushed the same day.** Table grapes are non-climacteric with
a low respiration rate; they must be cooled and fumigated with sulfur
dioxide within about half a day of picking, then held just below
freezing at high humidity, and can be marketed for up to eight to ten
weeks after harvest under this regime; they are not chilling-sensitive,
and their stems respire many times faster than the berries themselves
and dry out and brown first [5]. American grapes keep roughly four to
seven weeks under similar cold storage, but many cultivars are actually
injured by sulfur dioxide treatment rather than helped by it [5].
Muscadines keep only one to four weeks, or somewhat longer with
specialized sulfur-dioxide-generating storage aids [5].

**Finding 6 — raisins: roughly a four-to-one drying ratio, two to three
weeks in the sun or about six weeks on the vine.** Traditional tray
drying lays whole bunches out between vineyard rows for two to three
weeks and was, at its historical peak in one major raisin-producing
region, extremely labor-intensive, employing tens of thousands of
seasonal workers [19]. A newer "dried on the vine" method instead cuts
the fruiting canes and lets the fruit dry in place on the trellis for
six weeks or more before a single mechanised harvest pass; lots that are
still too moist require additional finish-drying [19]. A specific
potassium-carbonate-and-oil-emulsion dip can speed drying two- to
threefold for a lighter-coloured raisin type, while a separate,
sulfur-treated process produces golden raisins [20]. A mycotoxin
(ochratoxin A) can form during sun drying and subsequent storage, and is
specifically regulated in dried grape products in at least one major
market [37].

**Finding 7 — the leading American-grape cultivar is fundamentally a
juice crop.** In one major juice-grape-growing US state, juice-destined
vineyards substantially outyielded wine-grape vineyards in the same
region in a recent year [4]. Processing a tonne of this cultivar
hot-pressed yields notably more juice per tonne than wine grapes do, and
processors typically harvest at a lower sugar content and then
concentrate the juice considerably for storage and shipping [31]. This
cultivar also has deep historical significance as the basis of
pasteurised, unfermented grape juice, first produced in the 1860s
specifically to serve a religious community that objected to using
alcoholic wine in a sacramental context — a use still continued by some
congregations today [7][30].

**Finding 8 — muscadine: often female vines, single berries, thick
hulls.** Wild muscadine vines are dioecious (separate male and female
plants); many female fruiting cultivars require a self-fertile
pollinizer cultivar planted at a specific ratio and within a fairly
short distance to ensure adequate fruit set, while a smaller number of
self-fertile cultivars exist and dominate processing use [9]. Documented
yields for processing cultivars commonly fall in a range of several
tonnes per acre, with considerably higher figures described as possible
under excellent management [9][10]. Harvest runs over a long season from
late summer into autumn; fresh-market fruit is hand-picked over several
passes as individual berries ripen unevenly, while processing fruit is
typically shaken onto catch frames or machine-harvested [9].

**Finding 9 — hazards span both people and animals.** Grapes and raisins
are documented to cause acute kidney injury in dogs, with the exact
toxic mechanism uncertain but most likely linked to tartaric acid
content [23]. Grape pomace as livestock feed is limited by its tannin
and residual copper content [18], and copper-based fungicide residues
from vineyard spraying have specifically been documented to poison sheep
grazed on sprayed vineyards or fed pomace [25].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** All three crops are perennial woody vines
  (lianas), trained onto a trellis in cultivation; grape and American
  grape typically reach several metres untrained, while fruit is borne
  on current-season shoots arising from buds on one-year-old wood that
  is renewed each winter on permanent cordons or a head, rather than on
  truly permanent fruiting wood.
- **Harvest pattern:** Grape and American grape are harvested in a
  single pass per year in temperate climates (though table grapes are
  picked over several passes as they ripen), with tropical vinifera
  plantings able to carry two to three crops a year through deliberate
  pruning scheduling. Muscadine is harvested over several hand-picking
  passes for fresh fruit, or in a single mechanised or catch-frame pass
  for processing fruit, as individual cultivars ripen over three to six
  weeks.
- **Climate:** Grape favours Mediterranean, warm-temperate, temperate,
  semi-arid, and irrigated-arid climates, broadly within a mid-latitude
  band, with specialized pruning-scheduled cropping possible in
  subtropical and tropical climates. American grape favours cool-
  temperate and temperate continental climates with humid summers.
  Muscadine is restricted to a hot, humid subtropical band in the
  southeastern United States, with some plantings also in a few other
  warm, humid regions worldwide (Finding 1).
- **Food status:** All three are raw-edible fresh fruit crops; grape
  also supports a major dried (raisin) product.
- **Pollination:** Cultivated grape and American grape vines are
  self-pollinating hermaphrodites requiring no external pollinator
  (wild vinifera is dioecious). Muscadine includes genuinely female
  cultivars requiring insect- and wind-assisted cross-pollination from a
  self-fertile pollinizer cultivar planted nearby, alongside self-
  fertile cultivars that need no such companion planting (Finding 8).
- **Yield / composition:** Raw grape fruit is moderately high in
  calories and sugar; raisins concentrate this considerably by removing
  most of the water. American-grape fruit composition is broadly
  similar. Muscadine fruit is somewhat lower in calories but notably
  fibrous given its thick hull. Yield figures vary enormously by
  intended product, since wine grapes are deliberately cropped light for
  quality while table and processing grapes are pushed for maximum
  tonnage (Finding 3, 7, 8).
- **Water use:** Established grape vineyards typically use roughly
  500-800 mm of seasonal water in semi-arid growing regions, with wine
  grapes specifically managed under regulated deficit irrigation for
  quality reasons in many regions, and some traditional European
  appellations forbidding irrigation altogether (Finding 3). American
  grape and muscadine water-use parameters are estimated largely by
  analogy to grape, given less dedicated crop-water-use research; the
  muscadine production guide consulted here does specifically note that
  roughly one inch of water per week is usually adequate in dry periods,
  with overwatering and poor drainage both discouraged.
- **Establishment:** Grape and American grape are established from
  dormant hardwood cuttings or bench grafts (grape, where grafted onto
  phylloxera-resistant rootstock) planted at a density on the order of a
  few thousand vines per hectare on a trellis system, typically cropping
  from the third year onward. Muscadine is established from own-rooted
  cuttings or layered material at a considerably wider spacing, with no
  harvest expected until at least the second year.
- **Labor:** Highly product-dependent. Wine-grape vineyards are
  comparatively labor-light per hectare thanks to mechanisation options
  for pruning and harvest; table-grape and raisin production are far
  more labor-intensive due to hand-picking, packing, and (for tray-dried
  raisins) tray-laying and turning; muscadine fresh-market harvest
  likewise requires repeated hand-picking passes, while processing-
  destined muscadine fruit can be harvested far faster by catch frame or
  machine (Finding 2, 6, 7, 8; see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Grape tolerates a wide pH range depending on rootstock choice
  (certain rootstocks specifically confer lime tolerance), with sandy
  soils notably also conferring some protection against the phylloxera
  pest. Muscadine prefers a narrower, more acidic pH range and is
  sensitive to poorly drained soils with shallow hardpans.
- **Stand life:** Grape vineyards are commonly productive for around
  two to three decades under intensive commercial management, though
  much older vines exist in less intensive systems; muscadine plantings
  reach full cropping within three to five years and have their
  cordons periodically renewed, with at least one famous individual
  muscadine vine documented at roughly four centuries old.
- **Disease/pest profile:** The historic phylloxera root pest (Finding
  4) and Pierce's disease (Finding 1) are the two most consequential
  pest/disease pressures distinguishing the three crops' growing ranges;
  grey mould, several storage rots, and a leafroll virus spread by
  mealybugs and scale insects are also documented for grape, while
  muscadine faces several region-specific fungal diseases and a
  root-boring insect pest (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** An enormous range of named cultivars exists across all
  three crops, spanning wine, table, raisin, juice, and fresh-eating
  uses, with hardiness, disease resistance, and ripening season as the
  key differentiating traits (see cultivar notes woven through the
  Findings above).
- **Harvest:** Grape has distinct harvest-maturity stages for unripe
  (village/verjuice) use, ripe table use, and ripe wine use, each with
  its own maturity indicator and handling method, plus a specialized
  frozen-on-the-vine stage for icewine production in sufficiently cold
  winters. American grape has a simpler ripe/frozen-for-icewine
  distinction. Muscadine is harvested at a single ripe stage, though
  timing and method differ sharply between fresh-market and processing
  use (Finding 5, 7, 8).
- **Storage:** Storage life and handling requirements differ sharply
  between the three crops and by intended product, detailed in Finding
  5 above.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — grapes and raisins (and by extension related
dried products such as sultanas and currants) cause acute kidney injury
in dogs; the exact toxic mechanism is not fully confirmed but is
most likely linked to tartaric acid content. Juice, jelly, leaves, seed
oil, and wine made from grapes are not implicated in this toxicity.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of animal/health-
safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — copper-based fungicide residues, commonly applied
in vineyard spray programs, have been documented to poison sheep grazed
on sprayed vineyards or fed grape pomace as feed; grape pomace fed to
livestock more generally is also limited by its tannin and residual
ethanol content. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind
of livestock-health data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — sulfur dioxide and sulfite residues (used in
fumigated table grapes, golden raisins, and as a wine additive) are a
known food-additive sensitivity concern for some people. Deferred until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — ochratoxin A, a mycotoxin that can form during sun
drying and storage of raisins and during winemaking, is specifically
regulated with maximum residue limits in at least one major market for
both dried vine fruit and wine. Deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — methanol is a known hazard in grape-derived
spirits distilled from wine or pomace. Deferred until this project has
a place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the chemical used to break winter dormancy on
vines grown in tropical, non-dormant climates (hydrogen cyanamide)
presents a worker-exposure hazard, including a documented adverse
interaction with alcohol consumption. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of occupational-health data.

Documented plant pests and diseases include the historic phylloxera root
pest and Pierce's disease (Finding 1, 4), grey mould and several storage
rot fungi, a leafroll virus spread by mealybugs and scale insects, and
powdery and downy mildew (managed historically with copper sprays,
which is itself the source of the livestock copper-residue hazard noted
above) for grape and American grape; and several fungal fruit rots, a
canker/dieback complex following cold injury or pruning, crown gall,
and a root-boring insect pest for muscadine.

## Uses and processing

- **Grape chains:** Wine grapes are crushed and destemmed the same day
  they are picked; white wine grapes are pressed before fermentation,
  while red wine grapes ferment on their skins before pressing, with
  wine and pomace both potentially going on to distillation into
  brandy-type spirits. Table grapes are picked, packed, forced-air
  cooled, and sulfur-dioxide treated before cold storage. Raisins are
  produced by tray drying, drying on the vine, a speed-drying dip
  treatment, or sulfuring (for golden raisins), then cleaned and
  destemmed. Juice is concentrated, or in some culinary traditions
  boiled down into a thick grape-must syrup. A tart juice is also
  pressed from unripe fruit thinnings (verjuice). Icewine is pressed
  from grapes that froze naturally on the vine. Grape leaves are
  brined for culinary use in some traditions. Pomace is dried for
  livestock feed, with seeds further separated out for seed oil and
  oilcake. Prunings supply scion wood and cuttings for new plantings.
- **American-grape chains:** The leading juice cultivar is hot-pressed
  into juice, concentrate, and jelly; fresh slip-skin table fruit is
  cold-stored for several weeks; certain hybrid cultivars make dry
  "hybrid" wine or specialized icewine; sweet kosher and communion wine
  products are also made from this group.
- **Muscadine chains:** Fruit is crushed and pressed into wine, juice,
  and jelly; fresh fruit is packed in ventilated clamshell containers;
  the thick hulls and pulp are also used in a traditional hull-based pie
  filling; dried pomace is used in dietary supplements.
- Not covered by this research in detail: wine vinegar production; cream
  of tartar recovery from winemaking byproducts; canned juice, frozen
  grapes, and canned fruit cocktail; plant-growth-regulator treatments
  (gibberellin, girdling, ethephon) used in table-grape production;
  fortified, sparkling, and aromatised wine styles; several Middle
  Eastern and Central Asian grape-must confections; sulfur-dioxide pad
  technology and quarantine fumigation in detail; the candy and
  soft-drink flavour use of the "foxy" aroma compound found in American
  grapes; wild *Vitis* species as a foraged resource.

Grape wine has deep and detailed religious significance across multiple
traditions. In Catholic canon law, sacramental wine must be made from
grapes alone with no added juices, sugars, flavourings, acids,
colourings, or preservatives (sulfites as antioxidants are allowed), a
small addition of grape spirit during fermentation is permitted within
a historical limit, and wine made from raisins is acceptable, while
grape juice itself is explicitly not acceptable for this use. In
Judaism, kosher wine production requires specific equipment-cleaning and
handling rules (including restrictions on who may handle it unless it
has been flash-heated), and separate religious-law provisions govern
fruit from a vine's first three years (forbidden) and its fourth year
(treated as holy within the Land of Israel), as well as a prohibition on
planting grain or vegetables within or near a vineyard, and an annual
tithing practice. In Islam, wine itself is forbidden, though fresh
grapes, raisins, and grape molasses are not affected by this
prohibition. American grape's leading juice cultivar has its own
distinct religious history: a Methodist layperson pasteurised its juice
in 1869 specifically to provide an unfermented alternative to wine for
Protestant communion services influenced by the temperance movement,
and that same cultivar also supplies sweet kosher sacramental wine and
Catholic altar wine in some traditions today. No comparable religious or
ritual use was identified for muscadine.

## Open research questions

- The share of world grape production going to wine versus fresh table
  use versus drying differs substantially between a major international
  wine-and-vine organization's statistics and a general reference
  encyclopedia's figures — likely reflecting different accounting bases
  (available fresh-fruit tonnage versus total harvested tonnage) rather
  than a genuine contradiction, but not fully reconciled here.
- American-grape phylloxera resistance is described inconsistently: one
  source calls the native species strongly resistant to both mildew and
  phylloxera, while a more specifically focused source on phylloxera
  itself describes it as only weakly resistant and notes its poor
  suitability as a rootstock for this reason — not reconciled here.
  Readers should treat "strong native resistance" claims about American
  grape with some caution.
- Muscadine's chilling requirement is described very inconsistently
  across sources, from a low informal estimate up to a figure several
  times higher for at least one specific cultivar — not reconciled here.
- Cold tolerance of at least one specific muscadine cultivar is
  described inconsistently even within a single detailed production
  guide (descriptive text calls it relatively cold-tolerant while the
  guide's own cultivar table marks it as not cold-hardy).
- Nutrient removal per tonne of harvested grapes varies considerably
  across sources (nitrogen removal estimates in particular span more
  than a two-fold range).
- A specific respiration-rate figure for muscadine fruit at one
  temperature band was only partially legible in the source material
  used and has been interpreted as the more plausible of two possible
  readings; this should be treated as uncertain.
- Detailed per-window yield shares and the typical cycle length from
  pruning to harvest for tropical, multi-crop-per-year vinifera
  production were not found in the sources consulted and are assumed
  equal across windows purely for modeling convenience.
- Labor-hour estimates for table-grape harvest and packing, for raisin
  production, and for American-grape cultivation in general all rest on
  fairly thin or indirect source material (an assumed box weight, a
  converted cost-share figure, or no cost study at all) and should be
  treated as considerably less certain than the wine-grape labor
  figures, which are drawn from a detailed, directly sourced regional
  cost-of-production budget.
- Soil pH optimum for grape and American grape is estimated rather than
  directly sourced (muscadine's pH guidance, by contrast, is directly
  sourced from a dedicated production guide).
- A dedicated "vine" growth archetype does not yet exist in this
  project's modeling framework; all three crops here are modeled using
  a general deciduous-shrub-with-renewed-canes archetype as the closest
  available fit, since fruit is borne on one-year wood renewed each
  winter on an otherwise permanent trunk and cordon structure — a future
  dedicated vine archetype would be a better fit for all three crops
  here and for a couple of other vining fruit crops this project has
  also researched.
- A specific Islamic-law question about grape products beyond the core
  prohibition on wine itself was not independently researched in this
  pass and is recorded here only as a flag for future research.

## Estimates and derivations

Time from budburst to harvest (roughly 140-200 days for grape, 140-170
for American grape, and 140-190 for muscadine) is derived from a
combination of a standard international crop-water-use reference's
documented grape growing-season lengths, published grape ripening-group
classifications spanning early to late cultivars, and, for American
grape and muscadine, assumed budburst timing combined with documented
harvest-season timing, since direct budburst dates for these two crops
were not found in the sources consulted.

Yield tiers for all three crops are derived from a combination of
national/international production statistics, published commercial
per-acre or per-hectare yield figures by product type and region, and (for
grape's "exceptional" and "poor" tiers specifically) published low- and
high-yielding-vineyard figures from a standard international crop-
water-use reference; all unit conversions use the standard short-ton-
per-acre-to-kilograms-per-hectare conversion factor. Grape's documented
yield ramp to full bearing (roughly 43% of full yield in year three,
86% in year four, and full bearing from year five) is derived directly
from a detailed regional wine-grape establishment cost budget.

Water-use parameters for grape (crop coefficient curves and stage
lengths for different product types, rooting depth, soil-moisture-
depletion allowance, and drought-day tolerance) are derived from a
standard international crop-water-use reference's grape-specific tables
and stage-length data, cross-checked against documented seasonal water-
use figures from several specific growing regions; the resulting
drought-day tolerance before yield penalty begins is calculated from
standard soil-water-holding assumptions combined with the sourced
rooting depth and soil-moisture-depletion allowance. American-grape and
muscadine water-use parameters are estimated largely by analogy to
grape's wine-type crop coefficient curve, on the reasoning that they
share the same genus and general vine growth habit, adjusted
qualitatively for muscadine's documented explicit "roughly an inch a
week" guidance and its specific sensitivity to waterlogging.

Labor-hour estimates differ considerably in how directly they are
sourced. Wine-grape cultural-labor estimates are derived directly from a
detailed regional establishment-and-production cost budget's reported
hours per operation; wine-grape harvest labor is instead estimated from
an assumed hand-picking rate (since the source budget pays harvest by
weight rather than recording hours) and cross-checked against a
separate estimate for mechanised harvest crews. Table-grape harvest and
packing labor is estimated from a measured boxes-per-worker-hour range
from an unrelated regional study, combined with an assumed, unsourced
box weight — this is one of the weaker labor estimates in this entry.
Raisin-production labor is similarly estimated rather than directly
sourced, informed only by a historical total seasonal workforce figure
for one major raisin-producing region with no corresponding hours-per-
hectare figure. American-grape labor is estimated by converting a
regional cost-survey's reported labor cost share into hours using an
assumed (unsourced) wage rate. Muscadine labor estimates are derived
fairly directly from a dedicated regional production guide's own
reported per-acre cost and hour figures (reconstructed, in one case,
by cross-checking garbled footnote figures against the same guide's
reported labor costs at an assumed hourly wage), making them somewhat
more reliable than the table-grape and raisin figures above.

Processing yield fractions (stems as a share of fresh weight, wine
volume per unit of juice, brandy yield from wine, grape-seed-oil
recovery, juice concentration ratios, a grape-must syrup conversion
ratio, verjuice and icewine juice yields, and muscadine pomace share)
are derived from a mix of directly sourced industry conversion factors,
composition data from livestock-feed reference sources, and, in several
cases (verjuice yield, icewine juice yield), reasoned but explicitly
unsourced assumptions about how much lower these specialized products'
extraction rates should be relative to normal ripe-grape juice
extraction.

Yield-profile parameters (time to first harvest, ramp length, peak daily
yield, maximum days fruit can remain on the plant, and daily post-
maturity quality decay rate) are derived from the yield tiers and
harvest-window lengths described above for each of the three crops, with
several specific figures (particularly maximum standing days and daily
quality decay rate) estimated as reasoned placeholders given the general
absence of a dedicated field study measuring exactly these parameters
for any of the three crops.
