## Naming and scope

*Prunus persica* (L.) Batsch (Rosaceae), the fuzzy-skinned peach [1][2].
- **Names and taxonomy:** Linnaeus named it *Amygdalus persica* in 1753;
  Batsch's *Prunus persica* dates from 1801, though one checklist credits
  Stokes (1812) [2]. More than 200 synonyms are recorded [2]. Its subgenus
  or section placement within *Prunus* is disputed [2].
- **Origin:** domesticated in China, with peach stones from the Kuahuqiao
  site near Hangzhou pointing to about 6000 BCE in Zhejiang [2]. "Persica"
  reflects its spread through Persia, not its origin [1][2]. The closest
  wild relatives are *P. kansuensis*, *P. davidiana* and *P. mira*; almond
  is more distant [2].
- **Genome:** 2n = 16; the 2013 reference (doubled-haploid 'Lovell') is
  227 Mb in eight pseudomolecules with 27,852 predicted protein-coding
  genes [2][3].
- **Scope:** this cropID covers the **high-chill** (temperate) fresh and
  canning peaches. Freestone, clingstone, white and yellow flesh, and flat
  peaches are cultivar groups within it [1][2][4].
- **Gate check against the two sibling rows (re-confirmed 2026-11-24):**
  - **`nectarine`** stays separate. It is the same species with a single
    recessive smooth-skin gene but a different market fruit, lower yields,
    and different pest and storage behaviour (see `nectarine.md`). Peach
    cultivars are more prone to internal breakdown than nectarines [5][6].
  - **`peach_low_chill`** stays separate. Low-chill cultivars need far less
    chill, some under 100 h, and are grown in the subtropics and at high
    tropical altitudes [2], a different farming system.

**Relatives in this project:**
- nectarine (`nectarine`, `nectarine.md`);
- low-chill peach (`peach_low_chill`, `peach-low-chill.md`);
- apricot (`apricot`), plum (`plum`) and almond (`almond`, nut category),
  as fellow *Prunus* stone fruits.

**Proposed:** none.
- **Flat peach** (var. *platycarpa*, "pan tao", "Saturn" or "doughnut"
  peach) is a fruit-shape cultivar group, kept under Cultivars [4].
- **Ornamental double-flowered peaches** are a use, not a crop row [2].
- The wild relatives (*P. davidiana* and the others) were not sourced as
  cultivated crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a crop dominated by China, and by California within the
US.**
- **World:** over 1.5 million ha of peach and nectarine in 2009, averaging
  13.0 t/ha [1]. Output in 2024 was 27.9 Mt, of which China grew 17.6 Mt
  (63%); next were Spain (1.4), Turkey (1.2), Italy (1.1), the US (0.8) and
  Iran (0.6) [2]. China's output rose from 380,000 t at 3.6 t/ha in 1970 to
  over 10 Mt at 14.4 t/ha in 2009 [1]. The US had the highest yields, near
  20 t/ha [1].
- **US 2022-2024:** 73,800-76,100 bearing acres; 8.50, 7.99 and 9.61 short
  tons/acre; 647,030, 589,540 and 709,200 tons in total [7]. California
  grew 492,000, 480,000 and 529,000 tons of that, about 75-81% (see
  Estimates) [7].
- **Fresh versus processed:** in 2024 the US crop went 383,430 tons fresh
  and 312,650 tons processed [7]. California clingstone is almost all
  processed (228,250 of 229,870 tons in 2024) [7]. California grows 99% of
  US processing cling peaches [8].
- **Price gap:** in 2024 California clingstone fetched $628/ton against
  $1,500/ton for freestone [7].

**Finding 2 — chill and frost box the crop into a narrow climate band.**
- **Two-sided limit:** the tree blooms early and its flowers are
  frost-tender, yet it needs winter chill that frost-free subtropical areas
  cannot supply [1]. Most cultivars need 600-1,000 h at 4.4-10 °C (40-50
  °F); temperatures below −1.1 °C (30 °F) do not count [2]. California
  canning peaches need about 800-900 h below 7.2 °C (45 °F) [8].
  - FAO's chapter says chill is "computed during dormancy as hours above
    7 ºC" [1]. Every other source counts hours below that level, so this
    looks like a slip (see Open research questions).
- **Bud and flower cold limits disagree by source:**
  - **Dormant flower buds:** resistant above −7 to −10 °C [1], against bud
    death beginning at −15 to −25 °C depending on cultivar and timing [2].
    The tree itself survives to about −26 to −30 °C, though flower buds
    are usually killed there [2].
  - **Open flowers and young fruit:** damaged below −2 to −3 °C [1], or
    below about −1.1 °C [2].
- **Bud-stage table** ('Elberta'-type values, 30-minute exposure) [9]:

  | Stage | 10% kill °F (°C) | 90% kill °F (°C) |
  |---|---|---|
  | First swelling | 18 (−7.8) | 1 (−17.2) |
  | Calyx green | 21 (−6.1) | 5 (−15.0) |
  | Calyx red | 23 (−5.0) | 9 (−12.8) |
  | First pink | 25 (−3.9) | 15 (−9.4) |
  | First bloom | 26 (−3.3) | 21 (−6.1) |
  | Full bloom | 27 (−2.8) | 24 (−4.4) |
  | Post bloom | 28 (−2.2) | 25 (−3.9) |

  Prolonged cool weather before a cold night raises early-stage hardiness
  [9]. (Celsius values are converted; see Estimates.)
- **Other weather limits:** flowers suffer above 22-25 °C by day [2]. Hot,
  dry bloom weather shortens bloom and cuts the crop, and wet bloom weather
  reduces set and invites blossom disease [8]. Climates with heavy winter
  rain below 16 °C are unsuitable because of leaf curl [2]. Spring frost
  matters less for late-blooming processing peaches than for fresh-market
  peaches or almonds [8].
- **A real crop failure:** Georgia's yield fell from 3.30 tons/acre (2022)
  to 0.64 (2023) and recovered to 4.90 (2024); production fell from 24,100
  to 4,930 tons [7]. News reports (snippet only) blame a warm, low-chill
  winter that brought bloom early, followed by freezes on 11-23 March 2023
  [10]. South Carolina fell from 4.10 to 2.50 tons/acre the same year [7].

**Finding 3 — fruit grows in three stages, and crop load sets fruit size.**
- **Double sigmoid:** Stage I is cell division up to pit hardening, at
  about 20-25% of final size. Stage II is slow fruit growth while shoots
  grow, from barely detectable in early cultivars to 40-60 days or more in
  late ones. Stage III is a near-exponential swell to harvest [1].
  Cultivars of different season differ mainly in the length of Stage II
  [1].
- **Crop load:** fewer fruit per tree give bigger fruit, while more fruit
  give more total yield. Early hand thinning gives larger fruit [1]. The
  profitable aim is "moderate crops of well-sized fruit", reached with
  pruning plus costly hand thinning [8].
- **Buds:** the terminal bud is always vegetative. Lateral nodes carry zero
  to three buds, often a leaf bud flanked by flower buds. Ideal fruiting
  shoots are 30-50 cm without secondary shoots [1]. Fruit is borne on
  one-year-old wood, so heavy annual pruning is standard [1][8].
- **Next year's buds:** they are initiated after harvest, usually around
  August, so post-harvest care matters for next year's crop [1].
- **Alternate bearing:** there is a slight tendency when crops are not
  regulated by pruning or thinning [8].

**Finding 4 — one of the best-studied tree crops for deficit irrigation.**
- **Stage II tolerance:** a deficit in Stage II mainly slows shoots and
  trunk growth, and fruit recover fully once full water returns in Stage
  III. It does not cut final yield and can improve firmness, colour and
  soluble solids. This is the classic window for regulated deficit
  irrigation (RDI) [1].
- **Stage III sensitivity:** deficit then cut fruit water content by 25%
  in a medium-season cultivar [1].
- **Post-harvest stress:** water stress in August (bud differentiation)
  raises the share of doubled and deep-sutured fruit the next year. The
  rise is sharp once midday stem water potential falls below −2.0 MPa
  [1]. Next year's fruit set falls too, most strongly in late cultivars,
  and severe loss cannot be thinned away [1].
- **Savings:** with optimal Stage II RDI, ETc can be cut 15-20% without
  yield loss. Applied water in general can be cut 10-20% below maximum
  [1].
- **Indicators:** trunk-diameter fluctuation is the most sensitive
  indicator, followed by stem water potential [1]. Photosynthesis and
  transpiration hold until over 50% of available root-zone water is used
  [1].
- **Suggested limits:** for late-season cultivars, ETc fraction is 100%
  (Stage I), 65% or 35% (Stage II, shallow or deep soil), 100% (Stage III)
  and 80% or 50% (post-harvest). Midday stem water potential limits are
  −0.9, −1.8, −1.1 and −1.8 MPa [1].

**Finding 5 — cold storage hides a delayed injury that is worse just above
freezing than at it.**
- **Regime:** −1 to 0 °C, 90-95% RH. The freezing point is −3 to −1.5 °C,
  depending on soluble solids [5].
- **Danger band:** internal breakdown (mealiness, woolliness,
  leatheriness, flesh or pit-cavity browning, "bleeding") develops faster
  and more severely at 2-7 °C than at 0 °C or below [5]. Symptoms show in
  1-2 weeks at 2-5 °C against 3 weeks or more at 0 °C [6]. They appear only
  as the fruit ripens after storage, so customers find them [6]. Flavour
  is lost before visible symptoms [5].
- **Who is at risk:** most mid- and late-season peaches [5]; melting-flesh
  more than non-melting cultivars; peaches more than nectarines [5][6].
  Early yellow-fleshed cultivars are less susceptible [6].
- **Mitigation:**
  - **Controlled delayed cooling:** a 24-48 h delay at 20 °C raised minimum
    market life by up to 2 weeks in California and Chilean cultivars [6].
    Preripening is used commercially in California [5].
  - **Controlled atmosphere:** 6% O2 + 17% CO2 at 0 °C, with results
    depending on cultivar, fruit size and shipping time [5].
  - **Other:** intermittent warming has also been studied [6].
- **Shipping risk:** exposure to 5 °C in transit significantly shortens
  postharvest life of susceptible cultivars [5].

**Finding 6 — "peach tree short life" and the rootstock that fixed it in
the Southeast.**
- **The syndrome:** peach tree short life (PTSL) is a sudden spring
  collapse of trees usually 3-7 years old. It was the leading cause of tree
  death in the southeastern US, costing about $10 million a year (1998)
  [11]. Ring nematodes are thought to predispose trees to winter cold injury
  and bacterial canker [11].
- **Rootstocks:** 'Lovell' tolerates PTSL, and 'Nemaguard' resists
  root-knot nematode [11] but is susceptible to ring nematode [8].
  'Guardian' (BY520-9, USDA-ARS and Clemson, released 1993) combines both
  [11][12].
- **Trial:** in a 1989-1996 trial, PTSL killed 97% of 'Lovell' and 95% of
  'Nemaguard' trees in South Carolina, against none on 'Guardian'. Under
  worst-case Georgia management the losses were 40%, 80% and 20% [11]. A
  later Sandhill trial gave over 90% 'Guardian' survival after eight years
  [12].
- **Uptake:** an estimated 90% of peach trees now planted in South
  Carolina and Georgia are on 'Guardian' [12]. It is seed-propagated under
  US Plant Variety Protection No. 9400013, the first granted to a
  seed-propagated tree, and sold only through licensed nurseries [11][12].
- **Cultural controls:** keep soil pH above 6, prune just before bloom
  (February-March) rather than in autumn, and fumigate against ring
  nematodes [11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** deciduous perennial tree, vigorous and over 5 m
  tall unpruned [1]; trained to open-vase, trellis or V systems [1].
  Typically lives up to about 20 years [2]. Stand life: California cling
  orchards are replanted every 15-18 years [8]; lifespan is given as about
  15 years (UC Davis) and 7 years (Maine) [2]; the Ontario budget implies
  about 19 years (see Estimates) [13]. PTSL kills trees at 3-7 years on
  susceptible rootstocks (Finding 6) [11].
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing. California picks clingstone from
  10 July to 15 September and freestone from 20 April to 10 October.
  Georgia and South Carolina pick from May to August, and other states
  from July to September [7]. San Joaquin Valley fresh harvest runs from
  mid-May to early October across more than 155 cultivars [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **California cling peaches:** first harvest in year 3 in conventional
    orchards (about 6 tons/acre). High-density orchards give 2-3 tons/acre
    in their second season [8].
  - **Ontario fresh peaches:** 33% of full yield in year 3 and 66% in year
    4 [13].
  - **Generally:** fruit 2-3 years from sprouting [2], typically third-year
    bearing [2].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** dry continental or temperate climates are easiest; high
    humidity raises pest and disease pressure [2].
  - **Chill:** 600-1,000 h (general) and 800-900 h (California cling)
    (Finding 2) [2][8].
  - **Elevation:** only relevant in the tropics and subtropics, where
    low-chill cultivars are grown at high altitude [2]; that is the
    `peach_low_chill` row. Not otherwise sourced.
- **Composition (raw yellow peach per 100 g, FoodData Central SR Legacy
  169928) [14]:**
  - energy 39 kcal; water 88.87 g; protein 0.91 g; fat 0.25 g;
  - carbohydrate 9.54 g; fibre 1.5 g; total sugars 8.39 g (sucrose 4.76,
    glucose 1.95, fructose 1.53);
  - vitamin C 6.6 mg; vitamin A 16 µg RAE; β-carotene 162 µg; potassium
    190 mg.
  - Total polyphenols are 16-113 mg/100 g by flesh type [2].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible. The fruit is climacteric: harvested after
  minimum maturity and ripened before eating [5]. Consumer acceptance needs
  about 11% soluble solids with acidity at or below 0.7% (mid-season fruit)
  [5]. The kernel is cyanogenic (see Hazards).
- **Pollination:**
  - **Self-fertile:** flowers are self-fertile, and bees are not needed to
    improve set (California cling) [8]. Flowers outcross at about 5% [2].
  - **Exception:** some fruiting cultivars produce no pollen [2]; those
    would need a pollinizer, but none is named.
  - **Bee forage:** peach is a poor nectar and pollen source for honey
    bees [2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** flowering is not reported as
  photoperiodic. Growth cessation and bud set respond to short days. In
  the non-dormant *evergrowing* (evg) mutant, six tandem DAM genes are
  candidates for the trait. DAM1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 respond to a long-to-short
  day shift, while DAM3 does not and may follow chilling [15].
- **Yield:**
  - **US mean 2022-2024:** 7.99-9.61 tons/acre, about 17.9-21.5 t/ha [7].
  - **California 2022-2024:** clingstone 14.70-16.60 tons/acre (33.0-37.2
    t/ha) and freestone 10.80-11.90 (24.2-26.7 t/ha) [7].
  - **Mature California cling orchard:** about 20 tons/acre (44.8 t/ha) in
    year 7 and after, rising from 6, 12, 15 and 18 tons/acre in years 3-6
    [8].
  - **Ontario mature fresh orchard:** 10 tons/acre [13] (22.4 t/ha if
    short tons; see Estimates).
  - **Other US states:** 0.64-6.50 tons/acre in 2022-2024 [7].
  - **World mean:** 13.0 t/ha (2009, with nectarine) [1].
  - All per-hectare conversions are in Estimates.
- **Water use:**
  - **FAO-66 half-monthly Kc (mature midseason cultivar, Lleida, Spain)
    [1]:**
    - March 0.25-0.30 (bloom);
    - April 0.45-0.60; May 0.70-0.80 (end of Stage I);
    - June 0.90-0.95 (start of Stage III);
    - July to mid-August 1.05 (harvest);
    - late August to September 1.00;
    - October 0.75-0.55; early November 0.45.
    - Post-harvest values may be lowered by deficit irrigation.
    - Lysimeter Kc = 0.082 + 1.59 × midday light interception [1].
  - **FAO-56 stone fruit [16]:**
    - no ground cover: 0.45-0.55 / 0.90 / 0.65 (ini / mid / end);
    - active ground cover: 0.50-0.80 / 1.15 / 0.85-0.90;
    - tree height 3 m.
  - **California:** mature orchards with vegetated middles use about 40
    acre-inches a year including rain (about 1,016 mm; see Estimates),
    peaking at about 0.3 in (7.6 mm) a day. Systems should deliver 0.5 in
    a day [8].
- **Propagation:** budded on seedling rootstocks: 'Lovell' and 'Nemaguard'
  in California [8], 'Guardian' in the Southeast (Finding 6) [11][12], and
  wild peach seedlings in China [17].
  - **Clean stock:** plum pox virus (PPV) spreads by aphids over short
    distances (200-300 m) and by moving infected material. Infected trees
    cannot be cured and must be destroyed. Certified nursery stock is a
    named control [18].
- **Labor (Ontario 2023, mature year, 10-ton yield, per acre) [13]:**
  - pruning 45 h, thinning 50 h, picking 85 h, brush raking 3 h: 183 h of
    hand labour;
  - plus 34.7 h of machine-operator time;
  - total about 538 h/ha (see Estimates).
  - In the third and fourth years, hand labour is 60 and 119 h/acre [13].
  - Pruning, thinning, pest management and harvest are the largest
    California cling costs [8].
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture:** most productive on well-drained, uniform sandy loams; sand
    to loam is optimal. Clay loams make fruit size hard to reach [8].
    Trees are vulnerable to disease in wet soils [2].
  - **pH:** above 6 on PTSL sites [11]. No full optimum range was sourced
    this pass.
  - **Nutrients:** 50-100 lb N/acre a year (56-112 kg/ha; see Estimates).
    Zinc is often deficient on sand. Peach is a heavy potassium user [8].
  - **Salinity:** sensitive, with ECe threshold 1.7 dS/m and a 21%/dS/m
    slope for shoot growth and fruit yield [19]. Yield halves at about 4.1
    dS/m (see Estimates). Boron, sodium and chloride toxicity should be
    checked before planting [8].
- **Storage (postharvest):** −1 to 0 °C, 90-95% RH, air at about 50 ft³/min
  (Finding 5) [5].
  - **Precooling:** to 5-10 °C in bins if packing is next day, otherwise
    to near 0 °C. Susceptible cultivars should be cooled within 8 h. Fruit
    is recooled after packing [5].
  - **Respiration:** 4-6 mg CO2/kg·h at 0 °C, 64-110 at 20 °C [5].
  - **Fresh-cut slices:** 2-8 days at 5 °C [5].
  - **Storage life:** a fixed figure in weeks was not stated by HB66 this
    pass.
- **Establishment:**
  - **Densities:** 400-1,000 trees/ha, highest for processing orchards
    [1].
  - **California spacings:** conventional is 16-20 ft in the row and 18-22
    ft between rows. High density is 6-8 ft in the row and 16-18 ft
    between rows, trending toward shorter, ladder-free trees [8].
  - **Ontario:** 242 trees/acre (about 598/ha), with 1% replacement in
    year 2 [13].
  - **Site preparation:** at least a year before planting. This covers
    irrigation, levelling, ripping and fumigation according to nematode
    samples [8].
  - **Support:** ropes or wire around scaffolds keep heavy crops from
    breaking limbs [8].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Groups:** freestone (fresh), clingstone (canning) and semifree;
    flesh from white to deep yellow to red [2]. China recognises 495
    cultivars [2].
  - **California processing maturity groups:** extra early, early, late
    and extra late. They are spread to even out peak labour [8].
  - **Thinning-response cultivars:** 'May Crest', 'June Lady', 'Elegant
    Lady' and 'O'Henry' [1]. Large 'Elegant Lady' and 'O'Henry' benefit
    from CA storage [5].
  - **Rootstock line:** 'Lovell', the cultivar behind the reference genome
    [2][3].
  - **Flat peach** (var. *platycarpa*): oblate, less fuzzy, white-fleshed
    and usually sweeter. It reached the US from China in 1869 and is
    harvested from late spring to the end of summer [4].
  - **Dwarf cultivars:** bred, but with little commercial success [1].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** see the bud-stage table (Finding 2) [9]. Frost is not a
    quality improver for this crop in any source found.
  - **Heat:** flowers suffer above 22-25 °C [2]. Heat plus water stress
    after harvest raises abnormal fruit the next year [1].
  - **Rain:** winter rain drives leaf curl [2]; wet bloom drives brown rot
    [8].
  - **Waterlogging:** wet soils bring disease [2]. No duration was sourced.
- **Water regime:** ordinary irrigation (drip, berm-and-flood, rarely
  sprinklers) [8]. No flooded requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one fresh stage, set by skin ground colour
  changing from green to yellow, with firmness used where red colour masks
  it [5]. California uses two maturity tiers: "U.S. Mature" (minimum) and
  "Well-Mature" or "Tree Ripe" [5].
  - **Successive ripening:** fruit does not ripen uniformly, so cling
    peaches need two or three colour pickings, sometimes followed by
    machine harvest [8].
- **Soil biology:** replant ground and sandy soils carry nematodes. Old
  roots are removed, sites backhoed, and soil fumigated before planting
  [8]. Ring nematode predisposes trees to PTSL (Finding 6) [11].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** eastern Asia (northern China) [20]; domestication in
    eastern China [2].
  - **Australia:** widely naturalised in southern and eastern Australia.
    Rated an environmental weed in New South Wales and a minor one in
    Victoria and South Australia [20].
  - **Africa:** escaped in Libya, Ethiopia, Kenya, South Africa and Cape
    Verde [2].
  - **Plum pox:** first found in North America in 1999 (Adams County,
    Pennsylvania), handled by quarantine and tree destruction [18]. The
    M strain is more aggressive in peach and spreads faster, and it has
    been reported as seed-transmitted [18].
  - **Export quarantine:** California exports to Taiwan need a
    phytosanitary certificate covering peach twig borer, plum curculio,
    codling moth, fire blight, apple maggot, Pacific spider mite and
    medfly. British Columbia and Mexico have their own systems approaches.
    Imports are sometimes fumigated with methyl bromide [5].
- **Controlled environment:** protected peach and nectarine covered nearly
  16,000 ha in China in 2012, about 20% of it peach (roughly 3,200 ha; see
  Estimates) [17]. It uses unheated solar lean-to houses and high tunnels
  with forced early chilling, and advances harvest by up to 90 days [17].
  No closed-system (CEA) work on peach was sourced.

## Hazards

- **Kernel cyanogens:** amygdalin breaks down to hydrogen cyanide,
  benzaldehyde and sugar [2]. A UK survey measured 6.8 mg/g in peach seed
  (Argentine fruit), against 0.1 mg/g in nectarine. Earlier reports range
  from 0.02 to 43.3 mg/g, with 'Redhaven' at 11.1 mg/g [21]. Canned peach
  slices held 0.06 mg/g [21]. Kernels make the cheap marzipan substitute
  persipan [2].
- **Allergy:** peach allergy ranges from oral allergy syndrome to
  anaphylaxis. Peeled or canned fruit may be tolerated [2].
- **Peach leaf curl:** the most serious fungal disease, controlled in the
  dormant season with copper, ziram, chlorothalonil or dodine [2][8].
- **Brown rot (*Monilinia fructicola*):** the most important postharvest
  disease. It infects at bloom and is controlled by sanitation, fungicides
  and prompt cooling [5]. Rhizopus rot is controlled below 5 °C, and gray
  mold by avoiding wounds [5].
- **Tree diseases:** PTSL and bacterial canker (Finding 6); Armillaria and
  Phytophthora root and crown rots; shot hole, powdery mildew and rust [8].
- **Insects:** peach twig borer and oriental fruit moth need annual
  treatment in California [8]. Peachtree borers, plum curculio, green peach
  aphid, white peach scale and San Jose scale are serious elsewhere [2].
- **Plum pox virus:** incurable; infected trees are destroyed [18].
- **Pesticide residue:** more than 1% of European peaches exceeded legal
  limits in 2013, reflecting heavy fungicide use in wet climates [2].
- **Inking:** a cosmetic skin blackening from abrasion plus heavy-metal
  contamination. It is reduced by gentle handling and by avoiding foliar
  nutrient sprays within 15 days of harvest [5].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported in this pass.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — kernel cyanide, peach allergy, and livestock
poisoning from wilted *Prunus* foliage (not re-sourced this pass) are
deferred until this project has a place for health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** hand-picked into bags, baskets or totes → bins →
  cooled (forced air or hydrocooling) → packinghouse dump, clean, sort,
  size → packed (yellow flesh mostly in 2-layer trays, white and tree-ripe
  fruit in 1-layer trays) → recooled → stored at −1 to 0 °C → ripened
  before sale [5].
- **Chain 2 (processing cling):** contracted to a processor → picked by
  hand or machine (early morning, cool fruit) → graded under
  processor-grower quality standards → canned, frozen, juice or baby food
  [8].
- **Chain 3 (fresh-cut):** fruit at 13-27 N firmness → sliced → ascorbate
  and calcium lactate dips or modified-atmosphere packaging → 2-8 days at
  5 °C [5].
- **Kernel:** persipan [2]; see Hazards.
- **Ornamental:** double and semi-double flowered trees [2].
- **Cultural:** a symbol of longevity in China [2].
- **Not detailed here:** dried peaches, jam, purée and cull handling;
  peach wood uses.

## Open research questions

- **Chill counting:** FAO's "hours above 7 ºC" [1] against hours below
  7.2 °C (45 °F) [8] or between 4.4 and 10 °C [2]. Probably a slip in [1].
- **Dormant-bud hardiness:** −7 to −10 °C [1] against bud death beginning
  at −15 to −25 °C [2]. The FAO figure may refer to late-dormancy buds. It
  sits close to the first-swelling 10% kill in [9].
- **Open-flower frost:** −2 to −3 °C [1], −1.1 °C [2], or −2.8 °C for 10%
  kill at full bloom [9].
- **Bees and set:** "bees are not needed to improve sets" [8] against the
  old doc's "bees improve fruit set" (no longer sourced). Pollen-less
  cultivars [2] would need a pollinizer.
- **Ontario ton:** the report does not define "ton" on the page read; the
  t/ha conversion assumes short tons.
- **Georgia 2023 cause:** chill shortfall plus March freeze, from news
  snippets only [10].
- **Not sourced:** full pH range; rooting depth; elevation range outside
  the tropics; waterlogging duration; a storage life in weeks; peach-
  specific virus certification schemes other than PPV; livestock foliage
  toxicity; world area for a recent year.

## Estimates and derivations

**Short tons per acre to t/ha:** 1 short ton/acre = 0.907185 t ÷ 0.404686
ha = 2.2417 t/ha.
- US means [7]: 7.99 × 2.2417 = 17.9; 9.61 × 2.2417 = 21.5 t/ha.
- California cling [7]: 14.70 × 2.2417 = 33.0; 16.60 × 2.2417 = 37.2 t/ha.
- Freestone [7]: 10.80 × 2.2417 = 24.2; 11.90 × 2.2417 = 26.7 t/ha.
- Mature cling orchard [8]: 20 × 2.2417 = 44.8 t/ha.
- Ontario [13]: 10 × 2.2417 = 22.4 t/ha if short tons. If metric tonnes,
  10 ÷ 0.404686 = 24.7 t/ha. Range 22.4-24.7 t/ha.

**California share** [7]: 492,000 ÷ 647,030 = 76.0% (2022); 480,000 ÷
589,540 = 81.4% (2023); 529,000 ÷ 709,200 = 74.6% (2024).

**China share** [2]: 17.6 ÷ 27.9 = 63%.

**Georgia 2023 drop** [7]: 4,930 ÷ 24,100 = 0.20, so 80% below 2022.

**Celsius bud temperatures** [9]: °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8. For example, 27 °F
→ −2.8 °C and 1 °F → −17.2 °C.

**Water** [8]: 40 acre-inches × 25.4 mm = 1,016 mm a year; 0.3 in × 25.4 =
7.6 mm a day; 0.5 in = 12.7 mm a day.

**Labour per hectare** [13]: (183 + 34.7) h/acre = 217.7 ÷ 0.404686 = 538
h/ha. Hand labour alone is 183 ÷ 0.404686 = 452 h/ha. Picking is 85 ÷
0.404686 = 210 h/ha.

**Nitrogen** [8]: 50 lb/acre × 0.4536 ÷ 0.404686 = 56 kg/ha; 100 lb/acre =
112 kg/ha.

**Tree density** [13]: 242 ÷ 0.404686 = 598 trees/ha. California
conventional [8]: 43,560 ft² ÷ (20 × 22) = 99 to 43,560 ÷ (16 × 18) = 151
trees/acre (245-374/ha). High density: 43,560 ÷ (8 × 18) = 303 to 43,560 ÷
(6 × 16) = 454 trees/acre (748-1,121/ha). FAO's 400-1,000 trees/ha [1]
spans most of this.

**Ontario orchard life** [13]: establishment through year 4, plus costs
amortised "over remaining 15 years", gives about 19 years in total.

**Salinity** [19]: relative yield = 100 − 21 × (ECe − 1.7). 50% at 1.7 + 50
÷ 21 = 4.1 dS/m; zero at 1.7 + 100 ÷ 21 = 6.5 dS/m.

**Chinese protected peach area** [17]: 16,000 ha × (1 − 0.80) = 3,200 ha.

**Energy check** [14]: 0.91 × 4 + 0.25 × 9 + (9.54 − 1.5) × 4 = 3.6 + 2.3
+ 32.2 = 38 kcal, close to the listed 39 kcal.
