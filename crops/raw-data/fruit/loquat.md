# Loquat (*Eriobotrya japonica*)

## Naming and scope

*Eriobotrya japonica* (Thunb.) Lindl. (Rosaceae, tribe Maleae), sometimes
placed in a different genus, also known as Japanese medlar [2][3]. This
is a single cultivated species with over 800 named cultivars across
Asia; white-fleshed and orange-fleshed types form distinct cultivar
groups [3]. This entry does not cover the true European medlar, an
unrelated bletted autumn fruit that also shares one of this crop's
common Spanish names; the European medlar is treated as its own
separate crop entry.

This crop is grounded against another crop this project has also
researched in the same taxonomic tribe (which shares a rootstock
connection with this crop), and against two other subtropical evergreen
fruit crops that share a water-stress flower-induction mechanism with
this one.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an evergreen that flowers in autumn and fruits in
spring.** This crop blooms in autumn, develops its fruit through winter,
and ripens in early spring, reaching market before almost any other
spring fruit; prices documented in one major growing region are highest
early in the season and fall sharply once stone fruit and cherries
arrive on the market [5]. In that same region, sales are heavily
concentrated in two particular months out of a roughly four-month
marketing window [4]. Flower clusters form at the tips of new shoots
only after shoot growth stops during a summer rest period [6]. This
means the crop's phenology runs opposite to the usual temperate
calendar: frost risk falls on flowers and young fruit in winter rather
than spring, and earliness to market is the single biggest lever on
crop value.

**Finding 2 — post-harvest water stress moves the entire next crop
earlier.** Deliberately withholding irrigation after harvest advances
the following bloom and harvest. Under a regulated-deficit-irrigation
regime cutting water substantially from late spring through summer,
full bloom arrived meaningfully earlier than in a fully irrigated
control [5]. A roughly six-to-nine-week withholding period timed to
mid-summer advanced bloom further the harsher the induced stress was,
with one specific stress-intensity threshold (measured via a specific
internal plant-water-status indicator) documented as working best,
saving a substantial fraction of total water use with no yield loss
[6][7]. Deficits applied later in summer had little effect, or actually
delayed bloom [7]. This is functionally the same kind of water-stress
flower-induction lever documented for two other subtropical fruit crops
this project has also researched.

**Finding 3 — a delicate, labor-heavy fresh fruit.** Individual fruits
are small and bruise or scratch very easily, with damaged areas turning
brown or black [1]. In one major Mediterranean growing region, labor is
documented as roughly three-quarters of total production cost — the
highest labor share of any horticultural crop studied there — with
thinning and harvest together accounting for most of it; flower- and
fruitlet-thinning alone makes up roughly a quarter to a third of total
labor [4][9]. A distinctive skin disorder (a purple discoloration spot)
can affect a substantial minority of fruit in bad years and cut its
market value sharply; heavier thinning worsens the disorder, while
calcium-salt sprays applied before color change and protective bagging
of fruit clusters (common practice in at least one major Asian growing
country) both reduce it, though bagging adds further labor cost [4][9].

**Finding 4 — subtropical, but genuinely frost-limited.** Mature trees
survive down to roughly -11°C, but flower buds, open flowers, and young
developing fruit are each killed at progressively milder cold
thresholds, with open flowers and young fruit especially vulnerable to
a fairly mild winter frost [2][3]. The crop also grows poorly in
climates that are too fully tropical; in genuinely tropical conditions
it can bloom two or three times a year, with fruit maturing in a much
shorter window after bloom than in subtropical climates [3]. It is
native to cooler hill country in south-central China at moderate
elevation [2][3].

**Finding 5 — one country dominates production; a different country
dominates exports.** A single East Asian country accounts for a large
majority of world production by area and tonnage, with one specific
province recently overtaking another as the leading producer within
that country [8][9]. A separate Mediterranean country is the world's
leading exporter despite much smaller total production, and this crop
is notably absent from at least one standard international agricultural
statistics database [4].

**Finding 6 — cyanogenic compounds in seeds and young leaves.** Seeds
and young leaves (and, per at least one source, possibly the peel)
contain cyanogenic glycosides including amygdalin, the same class of
compound documented for stone-fruit kernels elsewhere in this project's
hazard research; the seeds are deliberately used to flavor a traditional
Italian liqueur [2][3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial tree, often kept
  pruned to a modest height; blooms in autumn, fruit develops through
  winter, and ripens in early spring — an inverted phenology relative to
  most temperate tree fruit (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single spring harvest spread over several
  pickings in subtropical growing regions; two to three crops a year are
  possible in genuinely tropical climates (Finding 1, 4).
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean, and warm-temperate climates;
  the crop does poorly where conditions are too fully tropical and is
  genuinely frost-limited during its winter flowering and fruit-
  development period (Finding 4).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (bees), with some
  cultivars self-fertile and others benefiting from dedicated pollinizer
  cultivars planted alongside them.
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yields are modest; well-
  managed high-density orchards in the dominant producing country reach
  considerably higher figures (Finding 5; see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-coefficient reference
  exists for this crop; a dated regional study gives a four-stage crop-
  coefficient curve. The crop tolerates a deliberate post-harvest dry-
  down well (used to advance the next bloom, Finding 2) but needs full
  irrigation from flowering through fruit growth and ripening. Root
  depth is fairly shallow on a commonly used fruit-tree rootstock also
  used for this crop, with most roots in the top meter of soil.
  Waterlogging tolerance is low — seedling rootstock is notably prone to
  "root choking," though an alternative clonal rootstock resists this
  better.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by grafting a selected cultivar onto a
  rootstock seedling (the great majority of orchards in at least one
  major growing region) or, less commonly, onto a clonal rootstock also
  used for another related fruit crop this project has researched; the
  alternative rootstock gives earlier bearing and larger fruit but a
  shorter overall tree lifespan.
- **Labor:** Documented as the single largest cost share of any
  horticultural crop studied in at least one major Mediterranean growing
  region, dominated by thinning and careful hand-harvest of delicate
  fruit; no direct labor-hour study was found, so figures here are
  estimated from documented labor-cost shares (see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Tolerates lime well, particularly on seedling rootstock; a
  documented commercial orchard soil runs moderately alkaline.
- **Stand life:** First crop roughly three years after grafting onto
  seedling rootstock (longer on the alternative clonal rootstock, but
  with earlier bearing in absolute terms); productive life is described
  as very long on seedling rootstock but considerably shorter (around
  two decades) on the alternative clonal rootstock, due to a graft-
  compatibility limitation (see Estimates below).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal scab disease requiring
  several sprays a season, several soil-borne fungal pathogens causing a
  small but real rate of tree death, thrips, and mites are documented
  (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** A dominant Spanish export cultivar accounts for the
  great majority of production in that country's main growing region;
  numerous other cultivars from several countries differ in fruit size,
  season, flesh color, sweetness, and self-fertility, with over 800
  cultivars documented across Asia overall (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked carefully over several weeks in spring, given
  the fruit's extreme susceptibility to bruising and scratching (Finding
  3).
- **Storage:** A few weeks under refrigeration just above freezing; the
  fruit is not chilling-sensitive and is a low ethylene producer, but
  slowly loses acidity in storage and is highly susceptible to
  mechanical damage (Finding 3; see Sources).

## Hazards

Seeds and young leaves of this crop contain cyanogenic glycosides,
including a compound also found in stone-fruit kernels; at least one
source also reports this compound in the peel. The edible flesh itself
is considered safe. Seeds are deliberately used, rather than avoided,
to flavor a traditional Italian liqueur, which implies some tolerance
for the compound at the doses involved, but seeds are not meant to be
eaten directly.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the cyanogenic-glycoside hazard from seeds and
young leaves is deferred in fuller detail until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit is graded, precooled within about a day of picking,
  and cold-stored for a few weeks. Seeded flesh (which makes up a
  majority of fruit weight) is made into jam or syrup. Seeds flavor a
  traditional Italian liqueur, or are used as rootstock-seedling source
  material. Mature leaves are dried into a traditional herbal tea.
- Not covered by this research: canned loquat in processing detail;
  protective fruit-cluster bagging technique; shade-net orchard
  construction; monofloral loquat honey; loquat wine; calcium and
  gibberellin spray formulations used against the purple-spot disorder.

## Open research questions

- Caloric content is reported very differently between two sources (a
  notably lower versus a notably higher figure per 100 g), likely
  reflecting a different measurement basis — not reconciled.
- Storage duration conflicts across three sources (a few weeks, a
  somewhat longer figure, and a much longer "cool storage" figure) — not
  reconciled.
- Number of flowers per flower cluster is reported very differently
  between two sources — not reconciled.
- Area and production figures for the dominant producing country
  conflict somewhat between two sources from different years — not
  reconciled.
- A labor-hour figure specific to this crop was not found in either
  major source document reached; current labor estimates rely on
  documented cost-share percentages rather than direct hour counts.
- Soil pH optimum and tolerance range are rough estimates rather than
  directly sourced figures.
- Salinity-tolerance classification was not researched in detail, beyond
  a qualitative note that one rootstock option is more salt-tolerant
  than the other.
- Heat-tolerance ceiling is not well established beyond a general
  qualitative note that the crop performs poorly in fully tropical
  conditions.

## Estimates and derivations

World-average yield (roughly 5,100 kg/ha) is derived by dividing a
documented total production figure across 13 countries by the
corresponding total harvested area from a roughly mid-2000s source; this
average is pulled down by a large, lower-yielding producing country and
small-scale plantings in at least one other country. A poor-yield tier
(roughly 3,200 kg/ha) is derived from a specific national yield figure
from a particular year. A good-yield tier (roughly 24,900 kg/ha) is
derived directly from a Mediterranean cost-study orchard's documented
yield. An exceptional tier (roughly 32,500 kg/ha) is the midpoint of a
documented range for well-managed, high-density orchards in the
dominant producing country.

Days from planting to first harvest (roughly 1,100-1,800 days) are
derived from documented timelines — a shorter figure for grafting onto
seedling rootstock, a longer figure representing an upper bound for
grafted trees generally. The ramp-up period to full production (roughly
four years) is estimated from a documented yield trajectory for
dwarf high-density trees in one growing region, which showed yield
roughly tripling between year three and year seven.

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days (and the resulting water-use "legacy"
figures) are derived from a dated regional study's four-stage crop
coefficient curve, combined with phenology-based stage-length estimates
(a long post-harvest shoot-growth-and-rest period, a shorter bloom-and-
set period, fruit growth, and a ripening-and-harvest period); the
resulting total is cross-checked against that same study's measured
seasonal water use and found to run somewhat higher, which is noted as
an acceptable approximation rather than a precise match.

Root-zone depth (set at 1.0 m) is derived from a documented observation
that most roots of trees on a specific clonal rootstock lie within the
top meter of soil at one research site. Soil-moisture-depletion
allowance is estimated by analogy to a closely related fruit-tree group
(no loquat-specific figure exists in the standard international
reference checked). Drought tolerance is rated medium (reflecting
documented post-harvest stress tolerance, balanced against the need for
full irrigation during flowering and fruit growth) and waterlogging
tolerance is rated low (reflecting documented "root choking" on
seedling rootstock). Days-without-water-before-penalty is derived from
a documented soil-moisture-holding capacity at one research site divided
by an assumed peak-season water-use rate.

A growth-stage curve is estimated judgementally as a mostly flat,
evergreen pattern (no leafless stage) reflecting the crop's year-round
canopy with a brief summer-rest dip.

Planting density per hectare is derived arithmetically from several
documented spacing configurations across different growing regions,
ranging from fairly widely spaced traditional orchards to much denser
dwarf high-density plantings; one source's own stated density-and-
spacing figures appear internally inconsistent and are simply recorded
as printed rather than corrected.

Labor-hour figures across establishment, pruning, thinning-plus-bagging,
harvesting, and post-harvest handling are rough estimates, since no
direct labor-hour study was found in either major source reached; the
harvest-tier figure is derived by dividing a documented good-yield-tier
figure by an assumed careful hand-harvest productivity rate appropriate
for small, easily bruised fruit, with thinning-plus-bagging set at
roughly half the harvest figure to stay consistent with documented
labor cost-share percentages.

Soil pH optimum and range (roughly 7.0 optimum, tolerance 6.0-8.0) are
rough estimates informed by seedling rootstock's documented lime
tolerance and one research orchard's documented moderately alkaline
soil; no acid-tolerance limit was found.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (peak daily harvest rate, harvest
plateau length, maximum days fruit can stand before quality decline, and
daily decay rate) are derived from the good-yield tier divided across an
estimated one-month effective harvest plateau (informed by documented
monthly sales-share concentration), with standing-time and decay
figures treated as placeholder estimates given the fruit's well-
documented tendency to soften and bruise quickly once ripe.
