## Naming and scope

*Eriobotrya japonica* (Thunb.) Lindl. (Rosaceae, tribe Maleae) [1][11].
- **Synonyms:** *Mespilus japonica* Thunb., *Crataegus bibas* Lour. and
  *Photinia japonica* (Thunb.) Benth. & Hook. f. ex Asch. & Schweinf.
  [2][11]. The encyclopedia's sister species database files it as
  *Rhaphiolepis bibas* [11].
- **Names:** Japanese plum, Japanese medlar; *pípá* (Chinese); *nespola
  giapponese* (Italy); *néflier du Japon* or *bibassier* (French);
  *níspero* or *níspero japonés* (Spanish); *ameixa amarella* (Portuguese)
  [2][11]. The English name comes from Cantonese *lou4 gwat1* [11].
- **Origin:** the Dadu River Valley in western Sichuan is now taken as the
  centre of origin of the genus, with wild *E. japonica* there [1].
  Morton calls it native to south-eastern China and possibly southern
  Japan [2].
- **Cultivar groups:** over 800 varieties in Asia [2][11]. Morton splits
  them into a "Chinese" group (orange flesh, thick skin, small numerous
  seeds, good keeping) and a "Japanese" group (pale flesh, very juicy,
  large seeds, fair to poor keeping) [2]. The Chinese region groups in
  Lin et al. include a south-subtropical group of large, early,
  cold-sensitive cultivars such as 'Jiefangzhong' [1].
- **Not covered:** the European medlar (*Mespilus germanica*), which
  shares the Spanish name *níspero*. It is its own row, `medlar`
  (`medlar.md`).

**Relatives in this project:**
- medlar (`medlar`, `medlar.md`): also tribe Maleae;
- quince (`quince`, `quince.md`): a dwarfing loquat rootstock (Finding
  5) [1][2];
- pear (`pear`, `pear.md`): shares fire blight ("pear blight") and is a
  rejected rootstock [1][2].

**Proposed:** none. No other cultivated *Eriobotrya* was found. *E.
deflexa* and *Photinia serrulata* appear only as Chinese rootstocks [1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an inverted calendar, where earliness sets the price.**
- **Phenology:** it "blooms at fall, develops its fruits during winter,
  and ripens them at early spring," which lets it reach market "before any
  other spring fruit" [4].
- **Spain:** prices are "very high at the beginning of the season (March
  and April), but sharply fall on May and June" when cherries, peaches
  and plums arrive [4].
- **Japan:** marketing runs from January to July, with more than 50% of
  fruit sold in June. April sales are rising as protected culture spreads
  [1].
- **China:** sold in May-June at prices above citrus and banana, below
  longan and litchi, and similar to apple and pear [1].
- **Flowering sequence (Zhejiang):** main panicle axes differentiate in
  early August, and anthesis comes in November, three months later [1].
  Flowering lasts 1.5-2.5 months [1]; it spans three months in Israel [1].
- **Tropics:** it may flower 2-3 times a year from July, setting fruit
  mainly from the second flowering [2]. In Guatemala it shifts to forming
  panicles in the spring dry season [5].

**Finding 2 — summer water stress moves the next bloom earlier.**
- **Site:** 'Algerie' on 'Provence' quince at 5 × 5 m, Almería, with 231
  mm rain and 1,922 mm class A pan evaporation a year, on sandy loam (FC
  13.4%, WP 5.1%, pH 7.8) [4][5].
- **Control:** about 40% of pan evaporation, roughly 7,100 m³/ha [4]; or
  about 6,800 m³/ha, 990 of it in the six-week trial window [5].
- **Six-week cuts (mid-June to end of July):** [4]
  | Water applied | Full bloom advanced |
  |---|---|
  | 50% | 13 and 10 days |
  | 25% | 27 and 21 days |
  | 0% | 25 and 27 days |
  | 25% for 11 weeks (to end of August) | 12-13 and 18 days |
- **Effects:** earlier, more concentrated bloom, then earlier harvest
  "without detrimental effects on fruit quality and productivity" [4].
  Flowers were lighter but fruit set was not affected [4].
- **Timing:** a 66% cut in July advanced bloom 13 days, June had no
  effect, and August delayed full bloom 8 days [6].
- **Water saved:** about 1,450 m³/ha a year, with water productivity up by
  over 40% and no yield or quality loss (snippet) [13].
- **Mechanism:** the earlier bloom comes from earlier flower induction,
  not faster development [5].

**Finding 3 — a delicate, hand-intensive fresh fruit.**
- **Spain:** labour is 66% of total costs, "the highest relative amount of
  all horticultural crops in Spain" [8]. The old doc's "about
  three-quarters" figure came from the unreachable CIHEAM paper (Open
  research questions).
- **Thinning:** Japan leaves 1-4 fruit per panicle [1], one for 'Tanaka'
  and two for 'Mogi' [2]. China leaves 4-6 [1]. Putian growers remove up
  to 30% of whole panicles [1]. Almería leaves 4-5 on main shoots and 1-2
  on laterals [4]. 'Akko 1' sets 20-30 fruit per cluster and is thinned to
  about 6 [2].
- **Bagging:** "indispensable" in Japan for appearance and to keep the
  pubescence on [1]. One labourer bags 1,000-1,500 clusters a day, and 1
  ha can need 150,000 bags [2] (see Estimates).
- **Harvest:** the stalk does not separate cleanly, so clusters are cut
  and each fruit is clipped [1][2]. "Almost all" grading and packing in
  Japan is manual, which "limits the area of loquat production for each
  grower" [1].
- **Callosa d'en Sarrià:** 500-1,000 day labourers are needed each
  campaign, and the crop "cannot be mechanised" (snippet) [13]. Producer
  costs rose 28-29% in 2023, with labour named among the causes [12].
- **Dwarfing:** quince-rooted dwarf trees "greatly reduce" pruning,
  thinning, bagging and harvest labour [1][2].

**Finding 4 — hardy wood, tender fruit.**
- **Wood:** cold-hardy to -10 °C [1]; established trees tolerate -11.1 °C
  [2]; -12 °C is the critical wood temperature [1].
- **Flowers and fruit:**
  - flower bud killed at -7 °C and the mature flower at -3 °C in Japan;
    the seed is killed at -3.9 °C, so the fruit falls [2];
  - ovules in young fruit die after brief exposure to -4 °C [1];
  - Korean cold injury to young fruit: 10% at -2 °C, 40-49% at -3 °C and
    95-100% at -4 °C. Immature 'Mogi' fruit freezes at -1.4 to -1.8 °C,
    and smaller fruit are more susceptible [1].
- **Field events:** undamaged at -6.7 °C for 12-14 hours on three nights
  in Florida in 1977, and at -6 °C in France in 1985 [1].
- **Limit for regular cropping:** -3 °C for young fruit limits cropping in
  Florida and much of California [1]. In Florida it does not fruit north
  of Jacksonville [2]. In temperate climates "the fruits seldom ripen to
  an edible state" [11].
- **Heat:** summer temperature should not exceed 35 °C [1]. Extreme heat
  hurts the crop and dry, hot winds scorch leaves [2]. Where it is too
  warm and moist, the tree is ornamental only [2].

**Finding 5 — quince rootstock for dwarf, labour-saving orchards.**
- **Default:** loquat seedlings are preferred over apple, pear, quince or
  pyracantha rootstocks "under most conditions" (Turkey) [1][2].
- **Quince:**
  - **Dwarfing:** quince and pyracantha can cause "extreme dwarfing", to
    under 2.5 m [2].
  - **Uptake:** quince has driven Israeli expansion since 1960 [1][2]. It
    is widely used in Egypt because it tolerates heavier, wetter soils
    [1][2], and in Almería [4][5].
  - **Drawbacks:** it puts out numerous suckers [2]. Most quince roots
    stay within 1 m in Almería [5].
- **Israeli dwarf yields:** 7 t/ha at 3 years and 25 t/ha at 7 years [2].

**Finding 6 — China dominates production; Spain dominates exports.**
- **China:**
  - **1949-1994:** 1,700 ha in 1949 [1], rising to 25,900 ha and 102,000
    t (Fujisaki 1994), led by Fujian and Zhejiang [1].
  - **Later figures:** about 42,000 ha and 200,000 t, of which about
    2,000 t is exported (snippet) [13]. Others give about 130,000 ha and
    over 650,000 t, and 1.2 million t in 2010 (snippets) [13]; the
    encyclopedia gives about 120,000 ha and 400,000 t [11].
  - **Range:** grown in 19 provinces, from the Yangtze to Hainan [1].
- **Japan:**
  - **Area:** 4,162 ha in 1934, displaced by citrus after the war [1].
  - **Output:** 2,800 ha and 13,000 t (Fujisaki 1994) [1]; 17,000 t
    (Morton) [2].
  - **Cultivars:** 'Mogi' is 60% of the crop and 'Tanaka' 35% [2].
- **Spain:**
  - **Area and output:** 2,914 ha, 41,487 t and 19,400 t exported
    (CIHEAM 2003 table, snippet) [13]; the encyclopedia also gives
    41,487 t [11].
  - **Trade:** exports are 36-47% of production, and over 80% of them go
    to Italy, Portugal and France [8].
  - **Regions and cultivar:** Andalusia is the second region, with 39% of
    production. 'Algerie' and its mutations are 98% of south-east coast
    production [8].
  - **Valencia:** 1,079 ha in 2022, 892 of them in the Callosa d'en
    Sarrià PDO (snippet) [13]. Callosa expected 12,000-13,000 t in 2023,
    about 80% exported, mainly to Italy [12].
- **Statistics gap:** FAO held no loquat statistics (snippet) [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree, 6-9 m [2] or 5-10 m and often
  3-4 m [11], with a rounded crown [2]. It grows in flushes; spring and
  summer shoots carry the autumn panicles [1]. Panicles hold 30-100
  flowers [2], or 3-10 [11] (Open research questions).
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial; one spring crop in the subtropics,
  picked by hand in several passes. Each Japanese district harvests for
  only 7-10 days [1]. Seasons: Florida from February, California from
  April, Israel March-May, Brazil May-October [2]; China May-June [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Grafted trees:** bear in 5 years or less [2]. Dwarf Israeli trees
    give 7 t/ha at 3 years [2].
  - **Seedlings:** take 8-10 years [2].
  - **Lifespan:** orchards over 30 years old stay productive [1].
  - **Ramp-up:** 'Wolfe' gives 45 kg per tree at 5 years and 136 kg at
    15-20 years [2].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** subtropical to mild-temperate [2]; latitudes 20-35°, up
    to 45° in maritime climates [1].
  - **Elevation:** wild in China at 914-2,100 m [2]. In the tropics it
    fruits well at 900-1,200 m but little or not at all lower down
    [1][2]; it is adapted at 900-2,100 m [1]. India up to 1,500 m [2].
    China and India grow it up to 2,000 m [1].
  - **Temperature limits:** winter should stay above -3 °C and summer
    below 35 °C [1]; full detail is in Finding 4.
  - **Rainfall:** 1,000-1,200 mm a year [1].
  - **Hardiness zones:** USDA zone 8 and up; poor above 10a [11].
  - **Chill:** no chilling requirement was sourced. Winter is its
    fruit-growth season (Finding 1).
- **Composition (per 100 g):**
  - **USDA SR Legacy 169908, raw:** 47 kcal; water 86.7 g; protein 0.43
    g; fat 0.2 g; carbohydrate 12.1 g; fibre 1.7 g; potassium 266 mg;
    phosphorus 27 mg; calcium 16 mg; magnesium 13 mg; iron 0.28 mg;
    vitamin A 76 µg RAE (1,530 IU); vitamin C 1.0 mg; folate 14 µg [7].
  - **Church et al. 1935, three cultivars:** moisture 84.7-86.1%;
    soluble solids 11.5-12.7%; total acid 0.4-0.7%; total sugars
    8.5-10.1%; protein 0.4% [1].
  - **Morton's USDA-ARS table:** 168 calories; protein 1.4 g;
    carbohydrate 43.3 g; potassium 1,216 mg; vitamin A 2,340 IU (Open
    research questions) [2].
  - **Carotenoids:** total 17.6 µg/g in Brazilian fruit, 44% β-carotene
    and 27% β-cryptoxanthin, for 175 RE/100 g [1]. The peel is about 5
    times richer than the pulp [2].
  - **Sugars and acids:** sucrose dominates in ripe fruit and sorbitol
    during development; malic acid is the main acid [1].
  - **Quality:** high-quality fruit has SSC above 12% and TA of 0.3-0.6%
    [3]. Fruit weighs 20-80 g [3]. Waste is "30% or more, due to the
    seed size" [11]. Seeds are 1-10 per fruit, usually 3-5 [2].
  - **Seeds:** 20% starch [1].
- **Edibility:** Raw-edible flesh, peeled. The seeds and young leaves are
  not eaten (Hazards) [2][11].
- **Pollination:**
  - **Pollinators:** bees [2]; *Apis dorsata* was the main visitor in
    Punjab. Bagged flowers set 15% less fruit [1].
  - **Self-fertility:** most cultivars are self-fertile [1]. 'Advance',
    'Champagne', 'Ahmar' and 'Saint Michel' are self-infertile, and
    'Akko 13' needs cross-pollination [2].
  - **Cross-pollination:** gives 10-17% more crop in India. 'Advance'
    pollen nearly doubles 'Tanaka' yield, while 'Pale Yellow' pollen
    lowers it [2].
  - **Flush quality:** early and late flowering flushes have little
    viable pollen [1][2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Per tree:** 50 kg in Brazil at 200 trees/ha, giving 10 t/ha [2].
    'Golden Ziad' 23.5 kg and 'Maamora Golden Yellow' 20 kg in Egypt [2].
    'Précoce de Itaquera' bears 1,500-2,000 fruit per tree [2] (see
    Estimates).
  - **Per hectare:** dwarf Israeli trees give 25 t/ha at 7 years [2].
    Japanese fertiliser rates are set for an expected 10 t/ha [1].
  - **National averages** (derived): China about 3.9 t/ha, Japan about
    4.6 t/ha and Spain about 14.2 t/ha (see Estimates) [1][13].
- **Water use:**
  - **Rainfed:** "In general, loquat does not require irrigation" in Asia.
    Sprinklers are used at maturation against sunburn [1].
  - **Drought tolerance:** hardier than orange but not as hardy as fig
    [1].
  - **Watering times:** at blossom swelling and 2-3 times at harvest [2].
  - **Irrigated (Almería):** about 6,800-7,100 m³/ha a year at about 40%
    of class A pan [4][5]; Finding 2.
  - **Crop coefficient:** Kc 0.50 May-October and 0.70 for the rest of
    the year, on class A pan ETo (Spain, snippet) [13].
  - **Rooting:** shallow [2]. Most quince roots lie within 1 m [5].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** seed is only for rootstocks or ornamentals, because it
    segregates [1][2]. It stays viable for 6 months, and 5 °C is best for
    storage. Seedlings are topworked at 1.25 cm stem diameter [1][2].
  - **Grafting:**
    - **Young-stock cleft graft (Fujian):** over 80% survival, and over
      a million plants a year in one county [1].
    - **Other grafts:** veneer grafting in Japan and Pakistan [1][2].
      Chip, patch and T budding in Turkey, with March best [1].
  - **Cuttings:** under 50% rooting in Japan; 65% in Egypt with 4,000 ppm
    IBA; 70% in China [1].
  - **Air layers:** 20% rooting, or 80-100% with 3% NAA [2].
  - **Micropropagation:** shoot tips multiply 4.25-fold a month, with 96%
    survival after transplant [1].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** no loquat virus was reported. Fire blight
    (*Erwinia amylovora*) is listed as severe in China and the US [1], and
    is the "major enemy" in California [2]. Fire blight spreads through
    infected nursery stock, which is general pome-fruit knowledge rather
    than a loquat source.
- **Labor:** see Finding 3. No labour-hour study was found. The only
  per-day rate sourced is for bagging [2] (see Estimates).
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture:** deep, well-drained sandy or clay loams with organic
    matter [1]; light sandy loam to heavy clay and oolitic limestone, but
    it needs drainage [2].
  - **pH:** 5.0-8.0, with 6.0 optimum [1]. The Almería orchard is at pH
    7.8 [5].
  - **Calcium:** a black fruit pit disorder linked to low soil Ca was
    corrected with 100 kg/ha Ca [1].
  - **Salinity:** Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - **Precooling:** to below 5 °C within 20 hours of harvest [3].
  - **Conditions:** 0-5 °C at over 90% RH. Fruit keeps 3-4 weeks at 0 °C
    and 2 weeks at 10 °C [3].
  - **Older figures:** 10 days at ordinary temperatures and 4 weeks to 60
    days in cool storage, with a 3-5 day shelf life afterwards [1][2].
  - **CA:** over 40 days at 3 °C with low O2 [1]. The USDA chapter says
    "no information is available" (Open research questions) [3].
  - **Chilling and ethylene:** not chilling-sensitive. It produces low
    ethylene and is non-climacteric [1][3].
  - **Respiration (day 4):** 11.2, 12.4, 30.6 and 80.0 mg CO2/kg/h at 1,
    5, 10 and 20 °C [3].
  - **Packaging:** polyethylene bags slow weight loss [3], but also
    promote internal browning and fungi and alter flavour [1][2].
  - **Disorders:** bruises and scratches turn brown or black, and long or
    high-CO2 storage causes internal browning [3].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Density by region:**
    - **General:** 500-600 trees/ha, or about 450 for vigorous cultivars
      (5 × 4 m) [1].
    - **Japan:** 5-7 m apart [1].
    - **Brazil:** 7 × 7 m on flat land, 8 × 5-6 m on slopes [1][2].
    - **Normal trees:** 7.5-9 m apart, about 200/ha [2].
    - **Dwarf Japanese trees:** 4 × 2 m, which Morton gives as 500/ha
      (see Estimates) [2].
    - **Almería:** 5 × 5 m [4].
  - **Fertiliser (N-P-K, kg/ha a year):**
    - **China:** 195-165-210 on poor orchards and 150-94-112 on fertile
      ones [1];
    - **Japan:** 170-115-125 for 10-year 'Mogi', and 240-190-190 for a
      10 t/ha target [1];
    - **Almería:** 160 N, 120 P2O5 and 120 K2O units [4].
  - **Training:** open-centre or modified leader, pruned in autumn and
    winter, and renovated a week after harvest [1]. Taiwan keeps
    mini-dwarfs under 0.9 m against typhoons [2].
  - **Gibberellin:** 60 ppm at full bloom improves set and size, while
    300 ppm gives small seedless fruit [2].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Spain:** 'Algerie' (Finding 6); the new 'Xirlero' has low purple
    spot [12]; 'Peluche' is about 70 g and 16 °Brix [11].
  - **Japan:** 'Mogi' is 40-50 g, cold-sensitive and self-fertile.
    'Tanaka' is 70-80 g, late and highly cold-tolerant [2]. 'Mizuho' is
    70-120 g [2].
  - **Israel:** 'Akko 13' is 50-70% of plantings [2].
  - **Brazil:** 'Mizuho' 65% and 'Précoce de Itaquera' 35% [2].
  - **China:** 'Jiefangzhong' gives large fruit and is cold-sensitive
    [1].
  - **Florida:** 'Wolfe', released in 1966 [2].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** Finding 4.
  - **Wind:** the tree grows tall and upright when untrained, which leads
    to wind damage. Newly planted trees need support poles [1].
  - **Sun:** sunburn, or "purple spot", affects fruit in hot climates and
    is reduced by bagging [1][2].
  - **Rain and humidity:** high humidity at flowering favours grey mould
    (*Botrytis*) [1].
- **Water regime:** Rainfed in Asia [1]; drip-irrigated with a summer
  deficit in Spain (Finding 2) [4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Bloom to harvest:** 150-200 days [1]; about 150 days in China and
    Japan [1]; 90 days [2]; 90 days in the tropics [11] (Open research
    questions).
  - **Ripening:** the fruit is non-climacteric and does not abscise; it
    shrinks on the tree [1]. Picking is by skin colour, because unripe
    fruit is "excessively acid" [1][2]. Commercial fruit is picked
    eating-ripe [3].
  - **Grades ('Tanaka'):** over 60, 50-59, 40-49 and 30-39 g [3].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced. Orchards in Asia are kept in sod with the
  clippings used as mulch [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Hawaii WRA:** score 7, high risk (2017). Naturalized on Kauaʻi,
    Oʻahu, Lānaʻi, Maui and Hawaiʻi Island, with fruit "readily dispersed
    by birds and other animals" [9].
  - **GISD:** can be invasive in natural environments in various Pacific
    islands and La Réunion. Its alien range includes Bermuda, French
    Polynesia, New Caledonia and Saint Helena [10].
  - **Elsewhere:** naturalized in India, "as it volunteers readily from
    seed" [2]. A UF/IFAS caution status in central and south Florida, a
    Queensland invasive listing and South African weed records come from
    snippets only [13].
  - **Quarantine pests:** Caribbean fruit fly in Florida, Medfly in
    Tunisia and *Bactrocera* (*Dacus*) *dorsalis* in India [2]. The USDA
    chapter lists no quarantine issues [3].
- **Controlled environment:**
  - **Japan:** plastic greenhouses began in Tarumizu against volcanic
    ash, then against cold. They pay through earlier marketing, steadier
    crops and spread labour, using 'Mogi' and 'Nagasaki-wase' [1].
    Nights should stay at or below 15 °C and days at or below 25 °C [1].
  - **Spain:** an EVA cover from budbreak to ripening advanced harvest 6
    days with no yield change [1].

## Hazards

- **Cyanogenic glycosides:**
  - **Where:** seeds contain amygdalin, which converts to HCN, and the
    peel has some too [2]. Seeds and young leaves carry "small amounts of
    cyanogenic glycosides (including amygdalin)" [11]. Loquat is a
    cyanogenic plant [1].
  - **Cases:** poultry have been poisoned by seeds [2]. A 5-year-old who
    ate four unripe fruits was dazed for about 2 hours [2].
- **Leaves:** contain triterpenes, tannin and "traces of arsenic"; young
  leaves have saponin [2].
- **Flowers:** some people get headaches near a tree in bloom [2].
- **Diseases:**
  - **Fire blight:** Finding 5 and Propagation [1][2].
  - **Root and crown rots:** *Rosellinia necatrix* root rot is the most
    severe disease in China [1]. *Phytophthora* crown rot and
    *Diplodia natalensis* collar rot are also recorded [2].
  - **Leaf and fruit spots:** black spot and scab (*Spilocaea
    eriobotryae*) in Italy and Australia, and *Entomosporium* leaf spot
    [1][2].
- **Physiological disorders:** purple spot is "the main limiting factor" in
  Spain [8]; black fruit pit (Soil) [1].
- **Pests:** fruit flies, scales, aphids, thrips, codling moth and birds;
  Israel uses wire netting against birds [2].
- **Postharvest rots:** *Diplodia natalensis*, *Pestalotia* and
  *Aspergillus niger* [2].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported. *A. niger* is recorded only as a
  rot, with no toxin data [2].

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the cyanogenic-glycoside hazard of seeds, peel and
young leaves (including seed liqueur use) is deferred in fuller detail
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** clusters cut → fruit clipped with stalk → graded
  by size and colour → soft packs of 300-500 g bags or 1-2 kg cartons in
  Japan → precooled below 5 °C → 0-5 °C [1][3]. Whole clusters are sold
  in Spain [1].
- **Chain 2 (canned):** peeled, seeded fruit canned in syrup, exported
  from Taiwan and increasingly made in Fujian and Zhejiang [1][2].
- **Chain 3 (preserves):** jam; jelly from slightly underripe fruit, which
  has enough pectin; juice, syrup and candied fruit [1][2]. Poor grades go
  to jam in India [1].
- **Chain 4 (alcohol):**
  - **Fruit wine:** made from the fruit [1][11].
  - **Seed wine:** seeds (20% starch) have been used for wine [1].
  - **Nespolino:** an Italian seed liqueur [11] (Hazards).
- **Chain 5 (dried):** dried fruit "has good flavor" [1].
- **Leaves:** a Hong Kong cough product, and leaf infusions for diarrhoea
  and hangover [1][2]. Leaf triterpenes show anti-inflammatory and
  short-lived hypoglycaemic effects in animals [1].
- **Honey:** "highly nectariferous, with... high honey potential" [1].
- **Wood:** pink and hard, used in place of pear wood for rulers [1][2].
- **Fodder:** young branches are lopped for animal feed [2].
- **Perfume:** flower-oil trials in France and Spain in the 1950s had a
  very small yield [2].
- **Ritual and religious:** No religious use sourced. In China a loquat is
  placed in ornamental fruit bowls to symbolise the Five Prosperities
  (*wurui*) [11].

## Open research questions

- **Labour share:** 66% of cost [8] against the old doc's "about
  three-quarters" from the unreachable CIHEAM paper. The labour-by-
  operation split was not re-found.
- **Purple spot:** incidence, value loss and the calcium-spray results are
  not re-sourced. Only "main limiting factor" [8] and bagging [1][2]
  remain.
- **Bloom to harvest:** 150-200 days [1] against 90 days [2].
- **Morton's composition table:** 168 calories and 43.3 g carbohydrate,
  about 3.6 times USDA's fresh values, which suggests a dry or
  concentrated basis (see Estimates) [2][7].
- **Panicle size:** 30-100 flowers [2] against 3-10 [11].
- **CA storage:** a Chinese low-O2 study [1] against "no information" [3].
- **Elevations:** Morton gives Guatemala as "3,000 and 6,900 ft (900-1,200
  m)", but 6,900 ft is about 2,100 m [2].
- **Dwarf density:** 4 × 2 m gives 1,250 trees/ha, not Morton's 500 (see
  Estimates) [2].
- **Chinese area and production:** figures span 25,900-130,000 ha and
  102,000 t to 1.2 million t across sources and years [1][11][13]. No
  FAOSTAT series exists [13].
- **Invasive listings:** Florida, Queensland and South Africa are snippet
  level only [13].
- **Not sourced:** salinity threshold, labour hours, soil biology and
  chill requirement.

## Estimates and derivations

**National yields** [1] (Fujisaki 1994 table):
- China: 102,000 t ÷ 25,900 ha = 3.94 t/ha.
- Japan: 13,000 ÷ 2,800 = 4.64 t/ha.
- Taiwan: 13,000 ÷ 2,500 = 5.2 t/ha.
- Fujian: 35,000 ÷ 11,900 = 2.94 t/ha; Zhejiang: 35,000 ÷ 9,100 = 3.85
  t/ha; Jiangsu: 5,000 ÷ 600 = 8.3 t/ha.

**Later yield checks** [11][13]:
- China (snippet): 200,000 ÷ 42,000 = 4.76 t/ha; 650,000 ÷ 130,000 = 5.0
  t/ha.
- China (encyclopedia): 400,000 ÷ 120,000 = 3.33 t/ha.
- Spain (CIHEAM table): 41,487 ÷ 2,914 = 14.2 t/ha. Exports 19,400 ÷
  41,487 = 47%, the top of Soler's 36-47% range [8].
- China exports: 2,000 ÷ 200,000 = 1% of production.

**Valencian yield** [12][13] (rough, since the 2023 Callosa figure is
compared with 2022 regional area): 12,000-13,000 t ÷ 1,079 ha = 11.1-12.0
t/ha.

**Yield tiers** (for this project):
- Poor: about 3 t/ha (Fujian 2.94, China 3.3-3.9).
- Typical: about 5 t/ha (Japan 4.6, China 4.8-5.0).
- Good: 10-14 t/ha (Brazil 10 [2], Valencia 11-12, Spain 14.2).
- Exceptional: 25 t/ha (dwarf Israeli trees at 7 years [2]).

**Per-tree to per-hectare** [2]: Brazil 50 kg × 200 trees = 10 t/ha.
'Précoce de Itaquera': 1,500-2,000 fruit × 25.3-29.1 g = 38-58 kg per
tree.

**Bagging labour** [2]: 150,000 bags/ha ÷ 1,000-1,500 bags per day =
100-150 person-days/ha. At 8 hours that is 800-1,200 hours/ha for
bagging alone. This is an upper bound for intensive Japanese-style
culture; Spanish practice was not sourced.

**Densities** [1][2][4]:
- 5 × 4 m = 500/ha (Lin's "about 450" is lower than its own spacing).
- 5 × 5 m = 400/ha; 7 × 7 m = 204/ha; 8 × 5 m = 250/ha; 8 × 6 m =
  208/ha.
- 7.5-9 m square = 123-178/ha (Morton's "about 200").
- 4 × 2 m = 1,250/ha, against Morton's 500/ha.

**Almería water** [4][5]:
- 0.40 × 1,922 mm = 769 mm ≈ 7,690 m³/ha, close to the stated ~7,100.
- The 1,450 m³/ha saving ÷ 7,100 = 20% of seasonal water [13].
- Six-week window: W50% 450 and W25% 259 m³/ha against 990 for the
  control [5].
- Available water: (13.4 - 5.1)% = 8.3% by volume ≈ 83 mm per metre of
  soil, if the percentages are volumetric (not stated) [4].

**Root-zone depth:** 1.0 m, from most quince roots lying within 1 m [5].

**Vitamin A check** [1][7]: 175 RE ÷ 2 ≈ 88 µg RAE (for food provitamin A
carotenoids, 1 RAE is about 2 RE), close to USDA's 76 µg RAE.

**Morton's table basis** [2][7]: 168 ÷ 47 = 3.6; 43.3 ÷ 12.1 = 3.6;
1,216 ÷ 266 = 4.6. A consistent factor of about 3.6 points to a
different basis rather than fresh fruit.

**Bloom date** [4]: a 27-day advance shifts harvest from the May-June
price drop back towards March-April.

**Cold thresholds** [2]: 12 °F = -11.1 °C; 19.4 °F = -7 °C (Morton
prints "7°C"); 26.6 °F = -3 °C; 25 °F = -3.9 °C.
