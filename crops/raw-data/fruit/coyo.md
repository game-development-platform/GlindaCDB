## Naming and scope

*Persea schiedeana* Nees, a forest tree native from southern Mexico to
Colombia, and possibly the closest living relative of a much
better-known cultivated crop in the same genus that this project has
also researched [1][2][3]. Local names include coyo or coya in
Guatemala, chinene or chinini in Mexico, chucte or supte in Honduras,
and yas in Costa Rica. Excluded from this entry: a probable natural
hybrid between this species and its better-known avocado-type relative,
used purely as a rootstock selection and treated under that other crop's
rootstock material rather than as its own crop; and a different wild
Lauraceae-family fruit tree found in similar forest habitats, which is
a distinct species from this one.

This is a genuinely separate crop from its better-known relative, not
merely a minor variant, despite drawing heavily on that crop's biology
for analogy in places this species itself has not been separately
studied. It differs in several agronomically important ways: it is
grown as a seed-sown forest and shade-coffee tree rather than in
orchards, has a markedly shorter postharvest life, bears a much
oil-richer fruit, and is itself sometimes used as rootstock material for
its more commercially developed relative.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an incipiently domesticated agroforestry tree, not an
orchard crop.** A field survey of 124 trees across six villages in a
mountainous region of Oaxaca, Mexico found that the species grows mostly
as shade and food trees within traditional shade-coffee plantings, with
a smaller share found in forest fragments, at a mean elevation of around
730 m [2]. Surveyed trees averaged 32 years old (ranging 8-100) and 21 m
tall (ranging 5-45). The large majority of growers had planted the tree
from seed sown directly where it was wanted, and most actively selected
seed from their best-performing trees for flavor, pulp content, and
fruit size — though trees also self-sow readily, with seed dispersed by
several native mammals. Deliberately planted trees bore measurably
larger, better-flavored fruit than self-sown ones, a clear selection
effect. The dominant management practice (reported by the large
majority of growers) was simply clearing competing trees and vines away
from the tree, rather than any more intensive orchard-style care; a
smaller share of growers also tipped young saplings to encourage
branching, or watered young trees. In season, villagers reported eating
several fruits a day, and a meaningful minority sold fruit, mostly
within their own village.

**Finding 2 — an avocado-like fruit, but more fibrous, creamier-fleshed,
and strikingly oil-rich.** Surveyed fruit ranged widely in size (8-28 cm
long, 90-1,000 g, averaging around 300 g), with flesh that was mostly
smooth and creamy in texture (described as such for over three-quarters
of surveyed trees), low in fiber for most trees, and shaped long-necked,
pear-shaped, or round depending on the individual tree [2]. The skin is
thick and pliable, peeling readily; the fruit has milky juice and
distinctively pink cotyledons, and the seed is notably more perishable
than that of its better-known cultivated relative [1][3]. Most
strikingly, flesh oil content measured 25-36%, dramatically higher than
the 4-7% documented for a comparable avocado type grown at similar
elevations — making this fruit a genuine potential oil crop in its own
right, with the oil itself high in oleic acid and low in linolenic acid
[2][5]. In the surveyed region, the fruiting season usefully fills a
seasonal food-security gap, ripening after stored grain runs low and
before the main local avocado-relative harvest.

**Finding 3 — phenology and propagation are documented regionally but
inconsistently between source regions.** In the Oaxaca survey region,
flowering and new leaf growth occur January-March, old leaves drop
February-March, fruit matures May-August, and a second leaf flush with
sporadic reflowering follows August-December [2]. A Veracruz source
instead gives flowering May-June and fruiting July-August, and one
search-snippet source states fruit reaches physiological maturity
roughly 91-97 days after fruit set [6][7]. Grafting is reported to work
best in December-January, with seed sown the previous June-July reaching
graftable size by then [2]. Seed is recalcitrant (cannot be
dried or stored) and is handled by soaking 24 hours, then sowing broad
end down at roughly three-quarters depth, avoiding overwatering [7]. The
source surveying growers' practice explicitly notes that formal grafting
research on this species is "nearly non-existent," framing improved,
grafted selections as a clear future opportunity rather than an
established practice.

**Finding 4 — roughly eighty years of intermittent interest as a
rootstock for its better-known relative, with genuinely conflicting
results.** Trials in Honduras in the 1940s found seedlings strong,
thick-stemmed, and multi-rooted, and documented survival of two weeks of
flooding on heavy clay soil in 1947 — but avocado-type trees budded onto
this rootstock later grew poorly, an unexplained "lack of congeniality"
[4]. Glasshouse testing in California found most seed collections of
this species susceptible to a major soilborne root-rot pathogen, and
fruit from topworked trees was judged inferior [3][4]. By contrast,
trials in a South African growing region beginning in 1952 found two
named avocado-type cultivars grafted onto this rootstock grew very
vigorously with sound graft unions, reaching substantial size within
6-8 years and cropping well [3]. A probable natural hybrid between this
species and its cultivated relative was collected from a Guatemalan
market in 1975 and released in 1984 as a named rootstock selection, with
moderate resistance to the major root-rot pathogen and some salt
tolerance, though more frost-sensitive than standard Mexican-race
rootstocks [4]. Frost tolerance itself is contested between sources: one
1947 source states the species is simply "not frost-resistant," while a
1952 source describes "some degree of frost resistance compared to the
surrounding flora" — not reconciled here.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A large evergreen-to-briefly-deciduous forest
  tree, 15-45 m tall; a single annual fruiting season, strongly biennial
  in bearing pattern.
- **Climate:** Tropical highlands and humid lowlands, roughly 100-2,800
  m elevation (mostly 250-1,400 m in the core surveyed range);
  frost-sensitive, with conflicting detail on degree (Finding 4).
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible (ripe flesh). Caloric and
  protein content are carried over by analogy from its better-known
  cultivated relative, since no direct proximate analysis of fresh flesh
  was found; this analogy is treated as uncertain given the fruit's much
  higher measured oil content, which may put its true energy density
  well above the analogy figure if that oil content is expressed on a
  fresh-weight basis (an open question — see below).
- **Pollination:** Not directly researched for this species; presumed
  insect-pollinated by analogy by its better-known cultivated relative,
  which displays a two-day flower-opening pattern across individual
  trees; this species' own flowering mechanism was not studied.
- **Yield:** No per-tree or per-hectare yield figures have been
  published for this species. All yield tiers here are rough estimates
  derived from an assumed average on-year fruit count and weight,
  adjusted for the species' strongly biennial bearing pattern and an
  assumed orchard-equivalent planting density (see Estimates below);
  actual agroforestry plantings have far lower effective per-hectare
  yield, since trees are scattered as coffee shade rather than densely
  planted.
- **Water use:** Crop-coefficient curve and related water-use parameters
  are carried over by analogy from its better-known cultivated relative,
  since no species-specific water-use study was found. The species is
  documented growing in humid to medium-moist sites, preferring moist,
  organic soils; one collection site received around 1,700 mm annual
  rainfall at a mean temperature of 18°C. Drought tolerance is judged
  low, given its preference for consistently moist sites, while
  waterlogging tolerance is judged moderate — notably higher than its
  cultivated relative — based on documented survival of two weeks of
  flooding on heavy clay soil, and an observation of healthy trees
  growing in a permanently saturated ravine bottom.
- **Propagation:** Mainly by direct seeding of selected superior-tree
  seed, sown where the tree is wanted; grafting has been proposed and
  demonstrated as feasible but remains essentially unresearched as a
  commercial practice. Seed is recalcitrant and cannot be stored.
- **Soil:** Documented growing in dark, organic-rich soils in its core
  surveyed region, and tolerant of heavy clay soils; pH tolerance is
  carried over by analogy from its cultivated relative in the absence of
  direct data. Seedlings form several spreading main roots rather than a
  single taproot.
- **Labor:** No labor-cost study was found for this species. Harvest
  labor is a rough estimate based on an assumed hand-harvest rate by
  climbing and cutting or gathering fallen fruit; establishment and
  cultural-care labor (chiefly clearing competing vegetation) are
  likewise rough estimates by analogy to low-input tree-crop
  establishment.
- **Storage:** Very short — fruit ripens two to three days after
  harvest and spoils within about a week, markedly shorter than its
  cultivated relative, which can be held on the tree for months before
  harvest.
- **Stand life:** Trees to 100 years have been recorded; juvenility
  (seed to first bearing) is documented at roughly 9 years, plus or
  minus about 4 years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific weevil species that bores into
  young fruit from fruit-set onward, documented destroying up to 80% of
  a crop in affected trees; the same major root-rot pathogen that
  affects its cultivated relative has also been recovered from old trees
  of this species, with most tested collections found susceptible.
  Gritty "stone cells" and excess fiber are noted as quality defects in
  some individual trees.
- **Cultivars:** No named commercial cultivars exist. Documented types
  include the average self-sown baseline tree, farmer-selected planted
  seedlings (better flavor and pulp content but also more fiber), trees
  specifically chosen at regional fruit fairs as grafting-improvement
  candidates, a long-necked light-green type documented in Guatemala,
  two numbered rootstock-selection lines with possible significant
  root-rot resistance, and the named natural hybrid rootstock selection
  discussed in Finding 4.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable — this is a forest
  tree reaching up to 45 m in height.

## Hazards

No hazards to people were found in the sources reviewed. Documented
pests and diseases include a specific fruit-boring weevil species
(destroying up to 80% of a crop in affected trees) and the same major
root-rot pathogen affecting its better-known cultivated relative.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — given how closely related this species is to a
cultivated relative whose leaves, seed, and fruit skin are documented to
contain a compound toxic to livestock and birds, the same hazard may
plausibly apply here, though no direct data for this species was found.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data, and flagged as an open question for that related crop's own
hazard documentation as well.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit cut by climbing, or gathered when fallen → sorted
  (weevil-damaged fruit culled) → ripened at ambient temperature for 2-3
  days → eaten fresh within about a week; opened fruit → flesh (roughly
  70% of whole fruit weight including skin) → pressed for flesh oil;
  seed → sown fresh (cannot be stored) → seedlings used either for the
  crop's own propagation or as rootstock material for its cultivated
  relative; superior seedlings → proposed grafting with selected scion
  material (not yet established commercial practice).
- Not covered in detail by this research: timber and honey byproducts of
  the tree; laboratory-scale biodiesel production from waste oil; use
  as rootstock material for its cultivated relative (treated under that
  other crop's own documentation); canning or freezing (no evidence
  either is practiced).

No religious or ceremonial use was found in the sources reviewed for
this species.

## Open research questions

- Whether the documented 25-36% flesh oil content is measured on a
  fresh-weight or dry-weight basis is unclear from the sources reviewed;
  this materially affects the fruit's true caloric density, which the
  current composition estimate (carried over by analogy from a
  cultivated relative) does not reflect if the oil figure is in fact
  fresh-weight.
- No per-tree or per-hectare yield figures have been published for this
  species; every yield tier in this entry is a rough estimate.
- The frost-tolerance conflict between an early source ("not
  frost-resistant") and a later source ("some degree of frost
  resistance compared to the surrounding flora") is not reconciled.
- Rootstock performance is genuinely contradictory across trial
  locations and eras: seedlings were initially promising but later
  "uncongenial" with a budded cultivated relative in one Honduran trial,
  yet vigorous and productive with sound graft unions in a separate
  South African trial — not reconciled here.
- A detailed regional study of fruit morphology and biochemistry (cited
  by the main source but not independently opened for this research)
  likely holds additional detail on oil basis and composition.
- Detailed tree-count-based yield surveys exist in the primary field
  study but were not reported numerically by that study's authors,
  leaving per-tree yield entirely unquantified.
- One source lists this species as IUCN Endangered, raising a possible
  wild-population conservation consideration for any dual-mode (wild
  versus cultivated) treatment of this crop, which was not otherwise
  explored here.

## Estimates and derivations

Time to first harvest (roughly 3,285 days, about 9 years) is taken
directly from the documented juvenility figure of 9 years, plus or minus
about 4 years; grafted trees would likely bear sooner, but no sourced
figure exists for grafted bearing age.

Caloric and protein composition figures are carried over by analogy from
a well-documented, closely related cultivated species, since no direct
proximate analysis of fresh flesh for this species was found. This
analogy is treated with real uncertainty: if this species' much higher
documented oil content (25-36%, versus roughly 4-7% in the comparable
cultivated-relative type) is expressed on a fresh-weight rather than
dry-weight basis, true energy density could substantially exceed the
analogy figure used here.

Pollination mechanism is assumed by analogy to a closely related
cultivated species' flower-timing pattern, since this species' own
flowering mechanism was not directly studied.

Yield tiers are rough estimates built from an assumed on-year fruit
count and weight per tree (roughly 200 fruit averaging 0.3 kg, i.e.,
about 60 kg per on-year tree), halved to account for the species'
strongly biennial bearing pattern, then scaled by an assumed
orchard-equivalent planting density of about 100 trees/ha (comparable to
large, widely spaced seedling trees of its cultivated relative). The
poor-yield tier additionally reflects the documented potential for up to
80% crop loss to the fruit-boring weevil. These figures should be
treated as having a very wide error range, and do not reflect actual
agroforestry conditions, where trees are scattered as coffee shade
rather than planted at any meaningful density.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index)
are adopted from a closely related cultivated species' documented
figures, since no species-specific water-use study was found; drought
tolerance is judged low given this species' documented preference for
consistently moist sites, while waterlogging tolerance is judged
moderate (notably higher than the cultivated relative) based on
documented flooding survival.

Soil pH tolerance is carried over by analogy from the same closely
related cultivated species, since no direct pH-tolerance study for this
species was found.

Growth-stage curve and photosynthesis-pathway parameters are rough
estimates: essentially no growth-stage progress is assumed during the
roughly 9-year juvenile period, followed by a rise to full bearing
capacity around 5 years after first fruiting, consistent with the
documented tree lifespan of up to 100 years.

Labor-hour estimates for establishment and cultural care are carried
over by analogy from a closely related cultivated species' lower
labor-intensity tiers (reflecting direct seeding and minimal
intervention), since no species-specific labor-cost study was found;
harvest labor is a rough estimate based on an assumed hand-harvest rate
by climbing and cutting or fallen-fruit gathering.

Peak harvest rate and standing-crop decay parameters are derived from
the documented roughly 90-day fruiting season length and the
world-average yield estimate above, combined with the documented rapid
ripening and short (about one-week) postharvest life of fallen or
harvested fruit.
