## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus heterophyllus* Lam. (Moraceae); synonyms *A. integrifolius*
auct. non L. f. and *A. integra* Merr. [1][4]. Names include jak, jaca and
jaqueira (Portuguese); nangka in Malaysia and the Philippines; khanun in
Thailand; khnor in Cambodia; mak mi in Laos; mit in Vietnam [1][4]. It is
probably native to the rainforests of India's Western Ghats [1][3]. It is
the national fruit of Bangladesh and Sri Lanka, and the state fruit of
Kerala and Tamil Nadu [2].

**Naming trap:** the epithet *integer* / *integra* has been applied to
both this species and champedak (*A. integer*), and the names are often
wrongly used as synonyms [1][3]. Champedak is its own entry (see
`champedak.md`).

Two texture types run through every growing region: a soft, sweet,
fibrous type broken open by hand, and a firm, crisp type cut with a knife
[1][3]. They are treated as cultivar groups here, not separate crops
(Cultivars).

**Siblings in this project:** `breadfruit.md` (*A. altilis*) and
`champedak.md`. Jackfruit differs from breadfruit in being grown from
true seed or grafted, with a deep taproot. It bears hundreds of edible
seeds per fruit, tolerates a 2-4 month dry season and light frost, and
carries an allergen risk (Hazards) [1][3]. Jackfruit is hardier than
breadfruit [1].

**Proposed rows.** Three other fruit trees in the genus are described in
the sources as culturally important [1][3]:
- **Lakoocha** or monkey jack (*A. lakoocha*): a deciduous tree 6-9 m
  tall from the humid sub-Himalaya up to 1,200 m. Its sweet-sour fruit
  is mostly made into curries or chutney, and the male spike is pickled.
  The timber is teak-like, and the bark has 8.5% tannin [1]. Proposed as
  `lakoocha`; it passed its gate check on 2026-11-19 and is researched in
  `lakoocha.md`.
- **Kwai muk** (possibly *A. lingnanensis*): native from Guangdong to
  Hong Kong. It is a small, red-fleshed fruit eaten raw. Young trees are
  injured at -2 to -1 °C, and mature trees have endured -4 to -3 °C [1].
  Proposed as `kwai_muk`; rejected at its gate check (below).
- **Marang** (*A. odoratissimus*): named as an important fruit tree of
  Southeast Asia [3]. Not researched here. Proposed as `marang`.

**Gate check for `kwai_muk` (2026-11-19): failed.** Kwai muk
(*Artocarpus hypargyreus* Hance ex Benth., or possibly *A. lingnanensis*
Merr.; 白桂木) was checked as a candidate crop row:
- **Taxonomy:** a distinct *Artocarpus* species, so it would qualify on
  taxonomy alone, but its identity is unsettled. Florida trees were
  introduced as *A. hypargyreus* but may be *A. lingnanensis* [1][19].
  Chinese sources also confuse 白桂木 with 桂木 (*A. nitidus* subsp.
  *lingnanensis*) [20].
- **Range:** a tree of broad-leaved evergreen forest at 100-1,700 m in
  Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, southern Hunan, Jiangxi and
  south-east Yunnan [19]. A Chinese seed-bank study calls it endangered
  [19]. It is dispersed by civets and macaques in Hong Kong [19].
- **Farming system:** none found. It is "cultivated, but not
  domesticated" [19] and "occasionally cultivated in Southeast Asia but
  rarely elsewhere" [19]. Florida has grown it experimentally since 1927,
  but only as a few widely separated trees [1][21]. All Florida
  propagation has been by seed; no cultivar selection or vegetative
  propagation work has been done [21]. No yield, area, production,
  spacing, price or market figure was found in English or Chinese [20].
- **Harvest and uses:** fruit 2.5-5 cm, orange-red pulp, 0-7 seeds,
  ripening August-October in Florida [1][21]. Eaten fresh, dried, or
  preserved in salt or sugar syrup [19][21]; in Guangdong, folk cooks
  steam it with fermented bean paste [20]. A mature tree can bear up to
  2,000 small fruit [21].
- **Other traits:** slow-growing, 6-15 m (10-25 m in the wild) [1][19];
  self-pollinating or mainly outcrossing, insect-pollinated [19][21];
  fruit begins 4-6 years after planting, per a nursery claim [19]; needs
  regular watering; chlorosis on limestone soils [21]. A 2025 Hawaii weed
  risk assessment scored it 0.0, low risk [19].

It is a dooryard and wild-gathered fruit with no documented crop system,
the same reason `ichang_papeda` failed. The cold-tolerance and fruit
notes above are kept here; no separate doc is written.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one tree, four foods, and a glut that is mostly wasted.**
- **Young fruit:** green fruit 1-3 months old is cooked as a vegetable
  [4]. Full-grown but unripe fruit is boiled in chunks, and it has no
  odour at this stage [1]. Unripe flesh is also pickled, dried, or canned
  in brine or curry [1].
- **Ripe bulbs:** the bulbs are the sweet edible perianths. They make up
  25-40% of fruit weight [1], 35-40% in another source [4], and 29-50%
  across Florida cultivars [4].
- **Seeds:** 100-500 per fruit [1][3], or 30-500 [4]. Fruit size does
  not predict seed count [3]. Seeds are boiled or roasted, and raw seeds
  are indigestible (Hazards) [1].
- **Male flower spikes and young leaves:** cooked as vegetables [1].
- **Waste:** in Bangladesh about 45% of the harvest, roughly 500,000 t,
  is reported wasted each year for lack of storage and processing [6]. The
  tonnage matches the national statistics bureau's output figure, not the
  extension department's (see Estimates, below).

**Finding 2 — must mature on the tree, then ripens in days.** Time from
flowering to maturity is reported three ways:
- 3-8 months [1][3];
- 90-180 days [3];
- 150-180 days [4].

Flowering date alone is not a reliable guide [3]. The maturity signs are:
- the skin turns from green to yellowish or brownish;
- the spines spread and flatten, and the skin yields slightly;
- the last leaf on the stalk yellows;
- the fruit sounds hollow when tapped [3][4].

Fruit picked even a few days early will not ripen to its best quality
[3]. Once picked, mature fruit ripens in 3-7 days [3], or 3-10 days at
24-27 °C [4]. Below 16 °C ripening is delayed [4]. Fruit for market
should be cut 3-5 days before ripening [3]. Jackfruit is climacteric and
produces significant ethylene [12].

**Finding 3 — drought-hardy, flood-intolerant, low-risk weed in Hawaii
but invasive in Brazil.**
- **Drought:** "cannot tolerate drought" in one account [1]. It tolerates
  3-4 months of drought in another, though it does best with even
  moisture [3]. It is "moderately drought tolerant" in a third [4].
- **Flooding:** trees decline or die after 2-3 days of wet soil [4], or
  after a day or two of waterlogging [3]. If the roots touch standing
  water, the tree bears no fruit or dies [1][3]. A 1992 seedling trial
  found 95% of jackfruit seedlings survived 3-72 hours of flooding (see
  `champedak.md`, Finding 7). That is short flooding of seedlings, not
  sustained waterlogging of trees.
- **Cold:** leaves are damaged at 0 °C, branches at -1 °C, and branches
  or whole trees die at -2 °C [3][4]. The minimum tolerated is -3 to 0 °C
  [3]. A young Florida tree was killed to the ground by the 1886 freeze
  [1].
- **Wind:** trees survive hurricane-force winds, losing leaves and small
  limbs [3][4].
- **Invasiveness:**
  - **Hawaii:** the Hawaii-Pacific Weed Risk Assessment (2003) scores it
    1, "low risk". It is naturalised on O'ahu and Hawai'i and is spread
    by animals and people. It has short-lived seeds, no seed bank, no
    thickets and no vegetative spread [15].
  - **Pacific profile:** "not considered invasive; naturalization in new
    environments is unusual" [3].
  - **Brazil:** introduced in colonial times [16], and also in the
    mid-19th century per another account [1]. It is invasive in the
    Atlantic Forest: in Tijuca National Park in Rio de Janeiro, and on Ilha
    Grande, where its abundance follows past human occupation [16]. Its
    spread from the second half of the 20th century has suppressed the
    recruitment of native trees in one Rio fragment [16]. A 2024 national
    agricultural research agency review lists it among the most-recorded
    invasive plants of the Atlantic Forest [16]. The opossum *Didelphis
    aurita* is its main seed disperser on Ilha Grande, and the agouti eats
    its seeds in Tijuca [16].
  - **Legal restrictions:** none found.

**Finding 4 — a birch-pollen allergen, and rare anaphylaxis linked to
latex allergy.**
- **Birch-pollen allergen:** a Bet v 1-related allergen was shown by RAST
  and immunoblot inhibition. Double-blind placebo-controlled challenges
  confirmed the allergy in two jackfruit-allergic patients [8]. In an open
  challenge, all five patients with birch-pollen-related apple allergy had
  oral-allergy-like symptoms [8]. An earlier 1997 case of oral allergy
  syndrome was not explained by Bet v 1 or profilin [8].
- **Latex-linked anaphylaxis:** case reports describe anaphylaxis after
  dried jackfruit in a 34-year-old Thai nurse (2015) [9]. They also
  describe it in a 21-year-old US man who was not a healthcare worker and
  was latex-sensitised from infant surgery (2021) [9]. Further latex-fruit
  cases were reported in 2023 [9]. One non-latex case involved fresh fruit
  [9]. The authors call the reaction rare, and the latex
  cross-reactivity mechanism is unconfirmed [9].

**Finding 5 — per-tree yields conflict tenfold.**
- **Typical:** mature trees yield 70-100 kg a year [3]. In Florida the
  range is 18-114 kg, and 68 kg or more counts as a good producer [4].
- **Fruit count:** in India a good yield is 150 large fruits, some trees
  bear 250, and a fully mature tree may bear 500, probably small ones [1].
  At even 5 kg a fruit, 150 fruits is 750 kg (see Estimates, below), far
  above the 70-100 kg range.
- **Germplasm and trials:** a Western Ghats germplasm survey gave
  148-1,177 kg per tree [7]. Eight-year-old West Bengal seedling trees
  peaked at 76.3 kg in a nutrient trial [7].
- **'PLR 1':** the Tamil Nadu agricultural university gives about 80
  fruits weighing about 900 kg a year, and elsewhere on its pages 29 fruits
  weighing 114 kg [11].

The sources do not reconcile these figures. The most likely reasons are
seedling vs grafted trees, tree age and fruit size.

**Finding 6 — national figures conflict twofold in Bangladesh.**
- **Extension department:** 1.893 million t on 62,273 ha in FY2022, at
  30.4 t/ha. FY2021 was 1.869 million t on 65,364 ha [6].
- **Statistics bureau:** about 1.02 million t [6].
- **Encyclopedia (2024):** Bangladesh 0.926 million t; India 1.436
  million t; Thailand 0.392 million t; Indonesia 0.340 million t [2].
- **Area basis:** Morton's 1980s figure for India was 26,000 ha [1], and
  much of India's crop grows as backyard and shade trees in betel nut,
  coffee, pepper and cardamom plantations [1]. Per-hectare national
  figures may not count those trees.

**Finding 7 — cold storage sits on a chilling knife-edge.** Sources
disagree on the safe temperature:
- **11.1-12.8 °C** at 85-95% RH: ripe fruit keeps 3-6 weeks [1].
- **13 ± 1 °C** at 85-95% RH: 2-4 weeks, with chilling injury below 12
  °C. Symptoms are skin discoloration, pulp browning and off-flavour [5].
  Morton's range lies across this threshold.
- **12 °C:** about 2 weeks, in a Malaysian standard procedure [5].
- **10 °C** at 85-90% RH: about 2 weeks [5].
- **19 °C:** postharvest life is extended by 5 days. In the same study,
  fruit at 13 °C developed chilling injury [5].
- **13-18 °C:** the handbook's compatibility group for mixed storage
  [12].
- **Unknown:** proper storage temperatures "have not been determined"
  [4].

Separated bulbs and processed products last longer (Uses and processing).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree 9-21 m [1], 9-12 m in Florida
  [4], or 8-25 m [3]. It has a taproot and white latex in all parts [1].
  It grows 1.5 m a year when young, slowing to 36-60 cm [3]. It reached
  17.5 m in 20 years in Sri Lanka [1][3]. It can live 100 years, but Thai
  plantations replace trees at 20 years, planting alternate rows every 10
  years [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial, with a main season and off-season fruit
  (Harvest stages).
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - Seedlings: 4-14 years [1][3].
  - Precocious cultivars: 2½-3½ years [1].
  - 'Singapore' / 'Ceylon' jack: 18-30 months after transplanting
    [1][3].
  - Florida seedlings: often in year 3-4 [4].
  - An Indian national guide: 6-8 years after planting [7].
  - Travancore bundled seedlings: 6-7 years [1].
- **Climate zones:** Humid tropics, and frost-free subtropical maritime
  climates [1][3][4]. It bears up to 30° latitude, with good crops within
  ±24° [3].
  - **Rainfall:** 1,000-2,400 mm, preferably uniform. Tolerates a dry
    season of 2-4 months under 40 mm [3].
  - **Temperature:** mean annual 24-28 °C; hottest-month mean maximum
    32-35 °C; coldest-month mean minimum 16-20 °C [3].
  - **Elevation:** sea level to 1,500 m in southern India; fruit grown
    above 1,200 m is poor and fit only for cooking; to 244 m in Guangdong
    [1]; 1-1,600 m overall [3].
  - **Chill requirement:** none found.
- **Composition:** per 100 g edible portion.
  - **Ripe bulbs, Indian national table (2017) [13]:**
    - water 78.6 g; protein 2.74 g; fat 0.15 g; ash 0.91 g;
    - dietary fibre 3.62 g (2.21 insoluble, 1.41 soluble);
    - available carbohydrate 14.0 g;
    - energy 302 kJ (72 kcal);
    - thiamine 0.05 mg; niacin 0.42 mg; vitamin B6 0.22 mg; folate 32 µg;
      vitamin C 6.7 mg.

    The mineral row aligns as roughly magnesium 31.8 mg, phosphorus 23 mg
    and potassium 279 mg. That column alignment is inferred from the
    extracted text.
  - **US national database figures [14]:** 95 kcal; water 73.5 g;
    protein 1.72 g; fat 0.64 g; carbohydrate 23.25 g (sugars 19.08 g);
    fibre 1.5 g; potassium 448 mg; vitamin C 13.7-13.8 mg [2][14].
  - **Florida guide [4]:** 94 kcal; water 73%; carbohydrate 24 g; fibre
    1.6 g; potassium 303 mg; magnesium 37 mg; vitamin C 6.7 mg.
  - **Morton [1]:** 98 kcal; water 72.0-77.2 g; protein 1.3-1.9 g;
    carbohydrate 18.9-25.4 g; fibre 1.0-1.1 g; potassium 407 mg; vitamin
    A 540 IU; vitamin C 8-10 mg.

  The Indian table's ripe fruit carries about 10 g less carbohydrate and
  23 kcal less than the US figure. Its fibre is about twice as high,
  probably from the dietary-fibre method; that is an inference.
  - **Unripe fruit (Indian table) [13]:** water 85.5 g; protein 1.98 g;
    dietary fibre 7.69 g; carbohydrate 3.48 g; 110 kJ (26 kcal); vitamin
    C 17.5 mg.
  - **Mature seed (Indian table) [13]:** water 72.3 g; protein 5.79 g;
    fat 0.44 g; dietary fibre 8.63 g; carbohydrate 11.8 g; 322 kJ (77
    kcal).
  - **Fresh seed (Morton) [1]:** water 51.6-57.8 g; protein 6.6 g;
    carbohydrate 38.4 g. The water content conflicts with the Indian
    table.
- **Edibility:** Ripe bulbs are eaten raw. Young fruit is astringent raw
  and is cooked. Seeds must be boiled or baked to destroy a trypsin
  inhibitor [1].
- **Pollination:** Monoecious, with male and female heads on short twigs
  from the trunk and main limbs [1][4]. It is wind- and insect-pollinated,
  and cross-pollination between cultivars or seedlings improves set and
  quality [4]. Hand pollination gives more fully developed bulbs than wind
  pollination [1]. Seedlings are cross-bred and not true to type [3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Florida guidance is to apply
  no nitrogen after August, because late growth reduces spring flowering
  [4]. That is a nutrition effect, not day length. Not applicable to
  storage organs.
- **Yield:** 18-114 kg per tree, typically 70-100 kg; fruit-count and
  germplasm figures are far higher (Finding 5) [1][3][4][7][11]. Per
  hectare:
  - 10-20 t/ha is claimed for commercial plantations at 100 trees/ha, from
    an unverified secondary source [7];
  - 7-16 t/ha is derived from spacing (see Estimates, below);
  - Bangladesh reports 30.4 t/ha nationally, but the figures conflict
    (Finding 6) [6].
- **Water use:** No crop coefficient found. In Bangalore in 1993,
  irrigated trees transpired 1,054.3 mm and rainfed trees 701.3 mm over
  the observation period. Irrigation delayed fruit maturity by 20-30 days
  [18]. Irrigation is critical from bloom through fruit development [4].
  Timed lawn sprinklers that overwater cause decline from root rot [4].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** loses viability within about a month [1][3][4]. Seeds keep
    a few weeks moist in a refrigerator, but germinate more slowly [3].
    There are 50-90 seeds per kg [3].
  - **Germination:** 3-8 weeks, shortened by soaking in water for 24 hours
    [1]. Fresh seed germinates at about 90% within 1-3 weeks; stored seed
    takes up to 6 weeks [3]. A 10% gibberellic acid soak is reported to
    give 100% germination [1].
  - **Seedlings:** sown in place, or moved before more than four leaves
    appear [1]. Nursery seedlings reach target size in 3-4 months [3].
    Pot-bound trees establish poorly, and deep 45-61 cm pots are advised
    [4].
  - **Grafting:** mainly side-veneer grafting onto pencil-thick seedlings
    of 'NS1' or 'Black Gold' [4]. Chip budding, cleft and approach
    grafting, and the modified Forkert bud, are also used [1][4]. Either
    jackfruit or champedak seedlings serve as rootstocks [1].
  - **Vegetative:**
    - Air-layers are distributed in India [1].
    - Semi-hardwood cuttings in 5,000-10,000 ppm IBA root in 60-70 days
      under mist [4].
    - Etiolated shoots root with 5,000 mg/L IBA [1].
    - Tissue culture has been tried [1].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** none found.
- **Labor:** Not sourced. Harvest is from ladders or by climbing, with
  heavy fruit lowered on a rope [3]. No hours were found for picking,
  pruning, bagging or processing.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained, light to medium soils: sands, sandy loams,
  loams and sandy clay loams [3]. It also grows on gravelly and laterite
  soils and shallow limestone, though there more slowly and shorter [1].
  It tolerates the high-pH calcareous rockland of southern Florida [4].
  - **pH:** 5.0-7.5 [3].
  - **Nutrient disorders:** manganese deficiency is reported in India [1].
    Florida programs use foliar micronutrient sprays and iron chelate soil
    drenches [4].
  - **Fertiliser:**
    - **Pacific profile [3]:** 100-150 g ammonium sulphate per tree in
      year 1; then 0.5-1.0 kg of 14-14-14; 2-3 kg for trees 15-20 years
      old.
    - **Florida [4]:** 0.7-1.4 kg of 6-6-6 a year in year 1, rising to
      8.2-9.1 kg by year 8.
    - **Tamil Nadu [11]:** 10 kg manure with 150 g N, 80 g P and 100 g K
      in year 1, rising each year to 50 kg manure with 750 g N, 400 g P
      and 500 g K from year 6.
  - **Salinity:**
    - **Tolerance claims:** tolerates slightly saline soils, with moderate
      salt-spray tolerance [3]. Another source says it is "probably not
      tolerant" [4].
    - **Trials:** six-month-old seedlings showed leaf injury within a week
      at 200 mM NaCl and above, and slight leaf burn at 100 mM after 6
      weeks; growth fell as NaCl rose [17]. In Brazilian seedling trials,
      irrigation water at 1.32-1.70 dS/m increased leaf number without
      nitrogen. Chlorophyll fell most at 4.0 dS/m without nitrogen [17]. A
      2019 sea-sand trial found significant growth and biomass losses
      under salt stress [17].
    - **Reference tables:** the international drainage reference lists no
      entry for the genus [10]. No yield threshold was found.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Whole fruit at 10-13 °C for 2-6 weeks, with a
  disputed chilling threshold (Finding 7) [1][4][5][12]. Frozen ripe bulbs
  keep a year in syrup with citric acid [1].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Fruit spacing:** 9-12 m [1], or 7.5-12 m; compact grafted clones
    4.6-7.6 m in the row and 6.1-7.6 m between rows [3]. Tamil Nadu uses
    8 × 8 m, in 1 m pits planted June-December [11]. An Indian secondary
    source gives 10 × 10 m traditional and 5 × 5 m high-density spacing
    [7].
  - **Timber spacing:** 2 × 3 m or 3 × 3 m [3].
  - **Shade for coffee and similar crops:** 15 × 15 m [3].
  - **Young trees:** need protection from sunscald and grazing; rats eat
    sown seed [1]. Seedlings do best in 30-50% sunlight, rising to full sun
    as they mature [3].
  - **Training:** topped at 3-5 m at 2-3 years, after which topping
    continues for life [3]. Florida home trees are kept at 2.4-4.3 m [4].
    Young trees carry one fruit per limb, because heavy loads kill limbs
    and stunt trees [4].
  - **Intercropping:** legumes, vegetables and banana in the early years;
    coconut, durian, mango, citrus and coffee as long-term partners [3].
- **Cultivars:** Two texture types (Naming and scope) [1][3]:
  - **Soft type:** *koozha chakka*, *barka* or *berka* in South India;
    *kha-nun lamoud* in Thailand; *vela* in Sri Lanka [1].
  - **Firm type:** *koozha pazham* / *varika*, *kapa* or *kapiya* in South
    India; *kha-nun nang* in Thailand; *varaka* in Sri Lanka [1].

  Fruit weighs 4.5-20 kg, rarely 50 kg [1][2]. Florida gives 4.5-27.3 kg,
  with small cultivars at 1.4-4.5 kg [4]. Most cultivars are 10-30 kg,
  within a known range of 2-36 kg [3]. Named cultivars, with weights and
  yields converted from pounds:
  - **'Singapore' / 'Ceylon':** medium, very sweet, early bearing. A
    summer crop in June-July and a second in October-December. Planted
    widely in India from 1949 [1][3].
  - **'Peniwaraka' (honey jak):** sweet, and claimed by some to be the
    best [1].
  - **Indian research releases:** 'T Nagar Jack' was chosen from 54 clones
    at Burliar. Kallar bred a 'Singapore' × 'Velipala' hybrid [1]. Tamil
    Nadu lists 'Burliar 1', 'PLR 1', 'PLR (J) 2' and 'PPI 1' [11].
  - **'Black Gold' (Australia):** about 10 kg; 54-91 kg per tree; late,
    September-October; 35% flesh; 192 seeds [4].
  - **'Cheena':** a hybrid with champedak; 2.3-4.5 kg; 33% flesh; 38 seeds
    [4]. See `champedak.md`.
  - **'Dang Rasimi' (Thailand):** 8-9 kg; 75-125 kg per tree; vigorous
    [3][4].
  - **'Gold Nugget':** 3.2-5.4 kg; 60-80 kg per tree; early, May-June;
    41% flesh; may split after rain [4].
  - **'Kun Wi Chan':** 15-18 kg; about 110 kg per tree; 29% flesh;
    mediocre quality [4].
  - **'NS1' (Malaysia):** 4-5.5 kg; about 91 kg per tree; early; 34%
    flesh; 63 seeds [3][4].
  - **'J-31':** often fruits off season [4].
  - **'Cochin':** 0.9-2.3 kg; 35-50% flesh; the rag is sometimes edible
    [4].
- **Weather response:** Frost kills at -2 °C; drought is tolerated for 3-4
  months; flooding kills in 1-3 days; trees survive hurricanes (Finding 3)
  [1][3][4]. Frost as a quality event: none found.
- **Water regime:** Free-draining orchard only. No flooded requirement;
  waterlogging is fatal (Finding 3) [1][3][4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Immature green fruit** at 1-3 months, for a vegetable [4].
  - **Mature fruit,** picked 3-5 days before ripening [3].
  - **Seeds** from ripe fruit [1].
  - **Male flower spikes** [1].
  - **Season:**
    - **Asia:** March-June, April-September or June-August by region, with
      off-season fruit September-December [1].
    - **Hawaii:** May-August and November-January [3].
    - **Florida:** summer and autumn [4].
  - **Timing of day:** picking between mid-morning and late afternoon
    reduces latex flow [3].
  - **Ripening:** in Jamaica an "X" is cut in the fruit apex to speed
    ripening [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced here. Indonesian nursery inoculation work
  on jackfruit is noted in `champedak.md` (Soil biology).
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native to the Western Ghats [1][3];
  introduced across the tropics. Low risk in Hawaii; invasive in Brazil's
  Atlantic Forest; no legal restriction found (Finding 3) [3][15][16].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. Root restriction in pots
  harms establishment [4].

## Hazards

- **Allergens:** a Bet v 1-related allergen (birch-pollen cross-reaction);
  rare anaphylaxis linked to latex allergy (Finding 4) [8][9].
- **Raw seeds** contain a powerful trypsin inhibitor, destroyed by boiling
  or baking [1].
- **Digestive effects:** ripe fruit is laxative in excess, and raw unripe
  fruit is astringent and indigestible [1].
- **Latex:** sticky, and stains clothing permanently. Oiling hands, knives
  and work surfaces is standard [1][3][4].
- **Odour:** the unopened ripe fruit smells of decayed onions, so it is
  opened outdoors [1][3].
- **Pests:**
  - **India:** shoot borer (*Diaphania caesalis*); mealybugs; spittle bug;
    jack scale; the bark borers *Indarbela tetraonis* and *Batocera
    rufomaculata*, the most destructive; stem and fruit borer; brown bud
    weevil [1].
  - **Southern China:** longicorn beetle larvae damage the fruit stem [1].
  - **Florida:** wood borers attacking wounded wood, and several scales
    [4].

  Young fruit is bagged in paper sacks [1].
- **Diseases:**
  - pink disease;
  - *Rhizopus artocarpi* rot of stem, fruit and male flowers;
  - leaf spots;
  - grey blight, charcoal rot, collar rot and rust [1];
  - *Botrytis* grey mould [4];
  - root rots (*Pythium*, *Phytophthora*, *Fusarium*, *Rhizoctonia*),
    especially after flooding [4].
- **Fungal contamination of the product:** fruit rots, as above. No
  mycotoxin finding.
- **Physical:** heavy fruit loads break limbs, especially on topped trees
  [3][4]. Falling fruit is an obvious risk, but no injury data was found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (immature fruit → vegetable):**
  - cut into chunks with oiled knives → boiled in salted water → flesh
    and seeds served [1];
  - or canned in brine or curry; dried, keeping a year in tins; or pickled
    [1];
  - canned young fruit is marketed as a meat substitute [2].
- **Chain 2 (ripe fruit → bulbs):**
  - **Opening:** ripened 3-10 days → opened outdoors → core removed → bulbs
    separated from rag and seeds [1][4].
  - **Fresh and simple products:** eaten raw; made into ice cream,
    chutney, jam, jelly, paste, fruit leather and *papad* [1].
  - **Canned:** crisp types are preferred. Canned fruit keeps 63 weeks at
    24-27 °C with 3% β-carotene loss, and 2 years if frozen [1].
  - **Frozen:** in syrup with citric acid, for 1 year [1].
  - **Drinks and dried products:** nectar, concentrate and powder; fried
    salted chips; a custard from bulbs boiled in milk [1].
  - **Liquor:** fermented and distilled [1].
- **Chain 3 (seeds):** boiled or roasted like chestnuts; preserved in
  syrup; canned in brine, curry or tomato sauce; or roasted, dried and
  milled into flour to blend with wheat flour [1].
- **Chain 4 (rind, rag, core → by-products):** jelly, pectin, and a syrup
  used in tobacco curing [1]. Also feed: rind and fallen fruit for cattle,
  and trees planted in pastures [1].
- **Chain 5 (leaves → fodder and wrappers):** young leaves fatten cattle.
  Leaves are also used as food wrappers and stitched into plates [1].
- **Chain 6 (latex):**
  - birdlime;
  - heated as a cement for china and to caulk boats;
  - resins (82.6-86.4%) of possible use in varnish;
  - bacteriolytic activity equal to papaya latex [1].
- **Chain 7 (wood → timber, dye):** termite-proof and decay-resistant,
  with 75-80% of teak's strength. Used for furniture, construction,
  turnery, oars and instruments [1], including the veena [2]. Old roots
  are carved [1]. Sawdust or heartwood boiled with alum gives a yellow dye
  (morin) [1]. Bark has only 3.3% tannin [1].
- **Other:** shade tree for coffee, pepper, betel nut and cardamom [1][3];
  erosion control and windbreaks [3].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** folk-medicine preparations [1].

**Religious and ritual use:**
- **Malabar:** dried branches make fire by friction in religious
  ceremonies [1].
- **Hindu homa:** dry twigs are used as ritual fuel [2].
- **Buddhist use:** the yellow dye colours Buddhist monks' robes [1][2],
  and jackwood is carved into Buddha statues [2].
- **Restricted wood:** in Indochina the limited supply of jackwood was
  once reserved for temples. Palaces in Bali and Makassar were built of it
  [1].

## Open research questions

- **Per-tree yield:** 70-100 kg [3] against fruit counts implying several
  hundred kilograms [1][7][11], and PLR 1 at 900 kg or 114 kg [11].
- **Bangladesh production:** 1.89 million t from the extension department
  against about 1.02 million t from the statistics bureau [6], and 0.93
  million t in an encyclopedia figure [2].
- **Chilling threshold:** storage at 11-13 °C [1][5] against chilling at
  12-13 °C [5]. A primary postharvest study is needed.
- **Composition:** 72 kcal and 14 g carbohydrate [13] against about 95
  kcal and 23 g [1][4][14]. Seed water content also conflicts [1][13].
- **Drought tolerance:** "cannot tolerate" [1] against 3-4 months [3].
  Possibly young trees against mature trees.
- **Weed risk:** low risk in Hawaii [3][15] against invasive in Brazil
  [16].
- **Not sourced:** water-use coefficient, labour, soil biology, salinity
  threshold, photoperiod, controlled environment.

## Estimates and derivations

**Trees per hectare.**
- 7.5 m spacing: 10,000 ÷ 56.25 = 178 trees/ha.
- 12 m spacing: 10,000 ÷ 144 = 69 trees/ha.
- Tamil Nadu 8 × 8 m [11]: 10,000 ÷ 64 = 156 trees/ha.
- 10 × 10 m and 5 × 5 m [7]: 100 and 400 trees/ha.

**Yield per hectare, about 7-16 t/ha.**
- At 70-100 kg per tree [3] and 100 trees/ha: 7-10 t/ha.
- At 156 trees/ha: 10.9-15.6 t/ha.
- At Florida's "good producer" 68 kg per tree [4] and 156 trees/ha: 10.6
  t/ha.

That brackets the secondary 10-20 t/ha claim [7]. It sits well below
Bangladesh's reported 30.4 t/ha [6], which would need about 195 kg per
tree at 156 trees/ha, or 304 kg at 100 trees/ha.

**Fruit count against tree yield.** 150 fruits [1] × 5 kg (the small end
of most cultivars' 10-30 kg [3]) = 750 kg. That is 7.5-10.7 times the
70-100 kg range [3]. At 150 fruits, 70-100 kg means fruit of only
0.5-0.7 kg, smaller than any cultivar's range. The two figures cannot
describe the same trees.

**Bangladesh yields.**
- Extension department FY2022: 1,893,000 t ÷ 62,273 ha = 30.4 t/ha, as
  stated [6].
- FY2021: 1,869,000 ÷ 65,364 = 28.6 t/ha.
- Statistics bureau: 1.02 million t on the same area would be 16.4 t/ha.
  The bureau's area was not seen, so this is illustrative only.

**Waste basis.** 45% × 1.02 million t = 0.46 million t, close to the
reported 500,000 t [6]. 45% of the extension department's 1.89 million t
would be 0.85 million t. So the waste figure appears to rest on the
bureau's lower output.

**Energy conversions** (÷ 4.184).
- Ripe pulp: 302 kJ = 72.2 kcal.
- Unripe pulp: 110 kJ = 26.3 kcal.
- Seed: 322 kJ = 77.0 kcal [13].

**Water use per tree.** 1,054.3 mm transpired [18] is 10,543 m³/ha over
the observation period. At 8 × 8 m spacing (64 m²), that is about 67,500
L per tree. Rainfed trees transpired 701.3 mm, so irrigated trees used
1.50 times as much. The observation period's length was not seen, so this
is not an annual figure.

**Nutrient removal per tonne of fruit** (pulp and seed only).
- **Nitrogen in pulp:** protein 2.74 g/100 g [13] ÷ 6.25 = 0.44 g N/100
  g, or 4.4 kg N per t of pulp. Pulp is 25-40% of the fruit [1], giving
  1.1-1.75 kg N per t of fruit.
- **Nitrogen in seed:** 5.79 g protein/100 g [13] = 9.3 kg N per t of
  seed. Seed is 5-18% of the fruit [4], giving 0.46-1.67 kg N per t of
  fruit.
- **Potassium in pulp:** 279 mg/100 g (column alignment inferred) [13] =
  2.79 kg K per t of pulp, or 0.70-1.12 kg K per t of fruit.

Rind, rag and core, the majority of the fruit, are not counted, so true
removal is higher. The 6.25 protein factor is a standard convention, not
taken from the source.
