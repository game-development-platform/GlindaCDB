## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus heterophyllus* Lam. (Moraceae), known by numerous regional
names across South and Southeast Asia, probably native to rainforests of
India's Western Ghats [1]. This fruit is the largest borne on any tree,
up to 50 kg [2]. Two texture types are treated here as cultivar groups
rather than separate crops: a soft, sweet-bulb type and a firm, crisp-
bulb type [1].

Two related genus-mates are explicitly out of scope: a smaller, strongly
and distinctively scented relative popular in parts of Malaysia (worth
considering as its own future crop entry, given its own market identity
and eating experience) and a couple of other, more minor related species
treated only as open questions, not researched in depth here.

This entry is grounded against its closest sibling in this genus — a
related latex-bearing tree crop this project has also researched, also
monoecious and also eaten both as a starchy vegetable when unripe and as
a sweet fruit when ripe. That sibling crop differs in key ways that
justify treating this fruit separately: it is grown from true seed or by
grafting with a deep taproot (versus the sibling's seedless, shallow-
rooted clonal propagation), the fruit is enormous with hundreds of edible
seeds, it tolerates a multi-month dry season and light frost, and it
carries a distinct allergen risk (see Hazards below).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one tree, three harvests, but a highly perishable glut.**
Young green fruit is boiled or curried as a starchy vegetable, pickled,
dried, or canned [1][2][3]. Ripe fruit yields sweet yellow bulbs making
up roughly a quarter to half of the fruit by weight, depending on
cultivar [1][4]. The remaining hundreds of seeds per fruit are a protein
food once cooked, though raw seeds are indigestible due to a trypsin
inhibitor (an anti-nutrient neutralized by ordinary cooking) [1]. The
rind, unfertilized flower remnants, and core feed cattle and pigs, and
the leaves are fodder [1][3]. In at least one major South Asian growing
country, where this fruit makes up a large share of national fruit
production, an estimated 40-50 percent of the crop is wasted each year,
reflecting its perishability and the glut nature of a single, concentrated
harvest season [6].

**Finding 2 — must mature fully on the tree, then ripens off it within
days.** Fruit takes roughly three to eight months from flowering to
maturity, varying by source and growing region [1][3][4]. Maturity signs
include the surface spines flattening and spreading, skin color shifting
from green to yellowish-brown, the last leaf on the fruit stalk
yellowing, and the fruit producing a hollow sound when tapped; picked
even a few days too early, the fruit will not ripen to its best quality
[3]. Once cut, fruit ripens within roughly three to ten days at warm
room temperature, and is typically opened outdoors because of its strong
smell [1][3]. Whole fruit keeps two to four weeks around 13°C, with
chilling injury appearing below about 12°C; separated bulbs keep roughly
two weeks refrigerated, longer if vacuum-packed [1][5].

**Finding 3 — sturdier than its sibling crop in some ways, but cannot
tolerate wet feet.** Leaves are damaged at freezing temperatures, branches
at slightly below freezing, and the tree dies at a few degrees below
freezing; it bears good crops within roughly 24 degrees of latitude of
the equator [3][4]. It tolerates three to four months of drought thanks
to a strong taproot, in contrast to its shallow-rooted sibling crop, but
does best with consistently even soil moisture [3]. Critically, the tree
declines and dies if its roots remain waterlogged for more than a day or
two, and fails to fruit at all in standing water [1][3]. It survives
hurricane-force winds with little fruit damage, since the fruit grows
directly on the trunk and major limbs [3].

**Finding 4 — a documented birch-pollen allergen cross-reactivity, and a
rare latex-allergy link.** A specific allergen related to a well-known
birch-pollen allergen protein family was confirmed in this fruit by
controlled clinical challenge; all birch-pollen/apple-allergic patients
tested in one study reacted to it [8]. Anaphylaxis from this fruit is
rare, but documented cases include one linked to latex allergy and
earlier cases in healthcare workers (who have elevated rates of latex
sensitization) [9]. TO BE ADDRESSED: this fruit's birch-pollen-related
and latex-cross-reactive allergy risk is a genuine human health hazard
that this project has not yet built a dedicated allergen/health-hazard
data structure for; it should be flagged for a future pass once such a
structure exists, alongside other plant-food allergen entries.

**Finding 5 — high per-hectare yields, but the underlying data are thin
and conflicting.** Typical per-tree yields run roughly 70-100 kg, with
good producers substantially higher and up to several hundred smaller
fruits possible per tree [1][3][4]. Documented per-hectare yields vary
widely: a national average figure for one major South Asian growing
country, and a considerably wider, higher range reported in regional
snippets from another major growing country [6][7]. Production totals for
that same South Asian country conflict by roughly a factor of two between
two different reference sources — not reconciled here. No dedicated
agricultural labor-cost study was found for this crop; labor figures here
are estimates.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A large evergreen tree (roughly 8-25 m), dense
  shade canopy, strong taproot, latex present in all parts; perennial
  bearing.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing with a main harvest season
  concentrated over a few months (varying by growing region), and
  off-season cropping documented in some areas and cultivars.
- **Climate:** Tropical and frost-free subtropical/maritime climates;
  bears well within roughly 24 degrees of latitude, tolerates up to 30
  degrees; dies at a few degrees below freezing (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible at the ripe-bulb stage; young fruit is
  astringent and not palatable raw, and seeds are indigestible raw due to
  a trypsin inhibitor neutralized by cooking.
- **Pollination:** Required; wind- and insect-pollinated, monoecious with
  separate male and female flower clusters on the trunk and branches;
  cross-pollination between cultivars improves fruit set, and hand
  pollination produces more fully developed bulbs than wind pollination
  alone.
- **Yield / composition:** Raw ripe bulbs are notably calorie- and
  protein-dense compared to many other tropical fruits, though composition
  figures conflict somewhat between two reference sources (see Open
  research questions). Documented yields vary enormously between a
  calculated per-tree-times-density estimate, a national average for one
  major growing country, and a considerably higher regional range from
  another; national production figures for at least one major growing
  country also conflict by roughly two-fold between sources.
- **Water use:** No dedicated crop-water-use coefficient study was found
  for this crop; water-use parameters here are estimated by analogy to
  its genus-sibling crop (also an evergreen, densely shaded tropical
  tree), adjusted downward somewhat to reflect this crop's stronger
  drought tolerance from its deep taproot. Irrigation is described as
  critical from flowering through fruit growth, while mature trees
  otherwise tolerate three to four months of dry season; waterlogging
  of any duration beyond a day or two is seriously damaging (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded in place (two to three seeds per
  planting site, best seedling retained) to avoid taproot damage, or
  raised as nursery seedlings under partial shade and outplanted before
  becoming root-bound; young trees need protection from sun-scald and
  grazing, and are typically topped at a few meters height within the
  first few years to manage eventual tree size.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-cost study was found; harvest, pruning,
  and post-harvest labor-hour figures here are rough estimates built from
  a documented average fruit-handling rate.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained, moderately fertile soils preferred, but
  the species tolerates shallow limestone, sandy, rocky, and lateritic
  soils; moderate salinity and salt-spray tolerance documented; a
  nutrient-deficiency disorder (manganese) has been observed in at least
  one major growing region.
- **Stand life:** Time to first fruiting varies hugely by propagation
  route: a particular precocious cultivar can bear in under three years
  from seed, most grafted trees fruit in three to four years, while
  ordinary seedling trees can take four to fourteen years; mature trees
  can live roughly a century, though at least one regional plantation
  system replaces trees after about twenty years as productivity
  declines, replanting in alternating rows on a rolling cycle.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Bark and shoot borers are described as the
  most destructive pest, alongside mealybugs, scale insects, spittlebugs,
  bud weevils, and fruit flies (fruit is sometimes bagged in paper sacks
  as protection); documented diseases include a fruit-rot fungus, a
  flower-spike rot, leaf spots, and several soil-borne collar and root
  rot pathogens.
- **Cultivars:** A range of named cultivars exists, spanning a roughly
  forty-fold range in individual fruit size, soft versus firm bulb
  texture, flesh percentage, precocity, and yield per tree; one
  documented cultivar is a deliberate cross with the related smaller-
  fruited genus-mate mentioned under Naming and scope.
- **Harvest:** Fruit is checked for maturity signs, then cut and lowered
  by rope from a ladder, since mature fruit is heavy and grows directly on
  the trunk and major limbs (Finding 2, 3); harvesting is best done in
  mid-morning to late afternoon to reduce latex flow, and oiled knives
  are used when opening fruit to manage the sticky latex.
- **Storage:** Whole mature fruit keeps several weeks around a moderate
  cool temperature; chilling injury appears at colder temperatures
  (darkened skin and pulp, off-flavor, increased decay); cut fruit ripens
  quickly at room temperature and separated bulbs keep roughly two weeks
  refrigerated.

## Hazards

Documented pests include bark and shoot borers (the most destructive),
mealybugs, scale insects, spittlebugs, bud weevils, and fruit flies.
Documented diseases include a fungal fruit and flower-spike rot, leaf
spots, and several soil-borne collar and root rots. Overeating ripe fruit
has a laxative effect, and raw young fruit is astringent and
indigestible. Raw seeds contain a trypsin-inhibiting anti-nutrient
neutralized by ordinary cooking (boiling or baking). TO BE ADDRESSED:
this fruit carries a documented allergen cross-reactive with a major
birch-pollen allergen protein, with rare but real anaphylaxis cases
including at least one linked to latex allergy — a genuine human health
hazard not yet covered by a dedicated allergen data structure in this
project (Finding 4). Falling fruit weighing up to several tens of
kilograms presents a physical/occupational hazard, though no specific
injury statistic was found; livestock must be fenced away from young
trees to prevent grazing damage.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Mature fruit is cut and lowered by rope, then either
  ripened over several days at warm room temperature or held cool, then
  opened with oiled knives to separate bulbs, seeds, and rags. Bulbs are
  chilled, dried, made into jam and preserves, or fermented and distilled
  into liquor. Seeds are boiled or roasted, or dried and milled into
  flour. Young (unripe) fruit has its rind and core cut away before being
  cooked, pickled, or dried. Rags, leaves, and fallen fruit feed
  livestock. Latex is used as glue, caulk, birdlime, or gum. Male flower
  spikes are eaten with salt and vinegar, or cooked and pickled. Pruned
  wood is used as fuel.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned young fruit, bulbs, and
  seeds, and frozen bulbs and pulp; chips and snack products; heartwood
  yellow dye; timber and musical-instrument use; pectin extraction.

No religious or ritual food use was identified for this crop in the
sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- National production figures for one major South Asian growing country
  conflict by roughly two-fold between two reference sources — not
  reconciled here.
- Per-hectare yield figures conflict substantially between a national
  average source, a range of regional snippet sources, and a calculated
  estimate from per-tree yield and planting density — not reconciled
  here; national figures may count fruit numbers or bearing area
  differently between sources.
- Storage duration at a similar cool temperature is reported somewhat
  differently between two sources (three to six weeks versus two to four
  weeks) — not reconciled here.
- Time from flowering to fruit maturity is reported as ninety to one
  hundred eighty days in one source versus a narrower one hundred fifty
  to one hundred eighty days in another — not fully reconciled here.
- Edible bulb fraction of total fruit weight is reported somewhat
  differently between two sources (a quarter to two-fifths versus
  closer to a half) — not reconciled here.
- Drought tolerance is described inconsistently: one source says the
  species "cannot tolerate drought," while another documents three to
  four months of tolerance in mature trees — likely reflecting a real
  difference between young and mature tree drought tolerance, not fully
  reconciled here.
- No dedicated crop-water-use study or agricultural labor-cost budget was
  found for this crop; several key agronomic parameters here are
  estimated by analogy to its genus-sibling crop or built from rough,
  unsourced assumptions.

## Estimates and derivations

Time to first harvest and the overall growing-season-length range are
derived from documented bearing-age figures across different propagation
routes (a precocious cultivar from seed, ordinary grafted trees, and
slower ordinary seedlings), taking a value in the grafted-tree range as a
representative planning figure given that it reflects the most common
commercial propagation method.

Yield tiers are derived primarily from a typical per-tree yield figure
combined with a standard planting density (used for the "average" tier,
cross-checked against a separately documented fruits-per-hectare figure
from one growing region), a national average yield figure from one major
growing country (used as "good"), an upper regional yield figure from
snippet sources in another growing country (used as "exceptional"), and a
calculated low figure from low-yielding trees at wide spacing (used as
"poor").

Water-use parameters (seasonal water-use coefficient, rooting depth,
soil-moisture-depletion allowance, drought-day tolerance) are estimated
by analogy to this crop's genus-sibling crop — another evergreen,
densely shaded tropical tree this project has also researched — adjusted
for this crop's deeper taproot and documented multi-month drought
tolerance, which together suggest a somewhat deeper effective root zone
and longer dry-spell tolerance than its sibling crop.

Labor-hour estimates (harvest, pruning and maintenance, post-harvest
handling) are built from an assumed per-fruit handling rate applied to
the estimated yield, since no dedicated regional cost-of-cultivation
budget was found for this crop; the resulting total is noted as somewhat
lower than a sourced figure for its genus-sibling crop, reflecting that
this crop's fruit is picked in fewer, much larger units.

Soil nutrient-removal rates (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium per tonne of
fruit) are derived from the raw-pulp composition figures, converting
protein content to a nitrogen-equivalent and reading phosphorus and
potassium directly from composition data; this is explicitly a pulp-only
basis and likely understates true whole-fruit nutrient removal, since
seeds (high in protein) and rags/rind (a majority of total fruit weight)
are not accounted for.

Yield-profile parameters (ramp-up period to full bearing, peak daily
yield, standing time before quality decline) are estimated by analogy to
the multi-year ramp-up pattern documented for this crop's genus-sibling
crop, combined with this crop's own documented harvest-season length.
