## Naming and scope

*Citrus medica* L., one of the handful of original, non-hybrid citrus
species and a parent species of lemon, lime, and many other cultivated
citrus hybrids [2]. This entry covers the candying citrons (named
cultivars grown for their thick peel), and the ritual "etrog" strains
grown specifically for Jewish religious use. The fingered citron
(Buddha's hand, a distinctive many-fingered, pulpless botanical variety)
is treated as a separate crop entry by this project, since it differs
enough in products, production systems, and market to warrant its own
treatment, even though it is technically the same species. Citron
hybrids such as a well-known thick-skinned lemon cultivar are excluded
from this entry; a separate, minor citron-hybrid group (pear-shaped,
low-juice "curiosity" fruits of little commercial importance) is noted
only in passing and not treated as its own crop. Several European
common names for this fruit all refer to the same species.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — primarily a peel crop, not a juice crop.** The fruit
consists mostly of thick, fleshy rind with very little juice [1][2][3].
Commercial fruit intended for candying is picked green, at a specific
target length, and processed through a long traditional chain: the peel
is halved and the pulp removed, then fermented for roughly a month in
seawater or salt brine (with the brine changed periodically), after
which it is desalted, boiled soft, and candied by gradually building up
the sugar concentration of a surrounding syrup over several days [1].
World output of candied or dried citron peel is a modest niche crop —
on the order of a few thousand tonnes a year from roughly a thousand
hectares worldwide, concentrated in Puerto Rico and the Mediterranean,
with one Italian growing region alone accounting for the overwhelming
majority of that country's production [4][10].

**Finding 2 — the ritual "etrog" market operates under entirely
different rules from the peel trade.** This fruit is one of the "four
species" used in a major Jewish autumn festival, based on a biblical
commandment [2][3]. A perfect ritual specimen can sell for well over
$100, while blemished fruit is instead sold for ordinary culinary use
[3]. Critically, trees intended for ritual fruit must not be grafted —
they are grown only from rooted cuttings or seed, and inspectors
specifically check for a smooth, unbudded trunk as proof [1][9]. This
matters because at least one official geographic-indication growing
region's rules permit grafting onto other citrus rootstock, meaning that
regional/quality certification does not by itself guarantee a fruit's
ritual suitability [10]. Ritual fruit is cut over a narrow window
(roughly late summer to early autumn), giving a market window of under
two months [3][5]. Growers of ritual fruit stake fruiting branches and
may trim thorns specifically to keep the rind free of scarring, since
rind perfection is central to ritual fruit value [1].

**Finding 3 — the most cold-tender citrus species.** This species is
described as "highly sensitive to frost," failing to harden off for
winter as early as other citrus species, with both foliage and fruit
damaged by intense heat and drought [1]. It has been called "the
tenderest of all Citrus species" — growth stops below about 13°C and
above about 35°C, and fruit is killed outright at around -2°C [6]. A
documented frost event in a major Italian growing region in January
2017 reportedly killed a large majority of trees or crop (reports vary
on exactly which), with ungrafted (own-rooted, ritual-type) trees among
the worst affected; growers in that region now cover trees with plastic
over winter as a precaution [9][10].

**Finding 4 — easy to propagate vegetatively, but relatively short-
lived.** Cuttings taken from 2-4-year-old wood root readily [1][6].
Seeds are monoembryonic, and the species is self-pollinating and highly
homozygous as a result of its breeding history [1][2]. Trees begin
bearing at 2-3 years and reach peak production between roughly 8 and 15
years; commercial orchards are typically kept in production for only
about 12-14 years before yields decline, and trees generally die at
around 25-30 years of age [1][6][10]. Average per-tree yield runs
roughly 30-40 kg, with exceptional individual trees reaching up to
around 100 kg [1][6].

**Finding 5 — phototoxic peel oil, shared with other citrus.** Citron
peel oil contains the same photosensitizing furanocoumarin compounds
found in lemon and lime peel oil, a documented skin-contact hazard when
combined with sunlight exposure [11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A thorny, evergreen shrub or small tree
  (roughly 2.4-4.5 m), blooming several times a year with no dormant
  season.
- **Climate:** Subtropical and frost-free Mediterranean coastal climates,
  and tropical conditions below roughly 1,300 m elevation — the
  narrowest climate tolerance of any citrus species researched for this
  project (Finding 3).
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible (pith and zest; pulp is
  often minimal and acidic). Detailed caloric and protein composition
  figures were not confidently sourced; one source's moisture and
  vitamin-C figures were flagged by the source itself as needing
  rechecking.
- **Pollination:** Not required; the species is self-pollinating, though
  insects do visit the flowers.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 7,000 kg/ha) is derived
  from a documented total peel-crop output and growing area; good and
  exceptional tiers are derived from per-tree yield figures and an
  assumed planting density, and should be treated as having a wide error
  range (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No citron-specific water-use study was found; water-use
  parameters are carried over by analogy from standard citrus figures
  (a related citrus crop this project has also researched provided the
  detailed citrus water-use discussion these figures are drawn from).
  Drought tolerance is judged low, consistent with documented heat/
  drought damage to foliage and fruit, and waterlogging tolerance is
  also low, since the species needs good soil aeration.
- **Propagation:** Mainly by rooted cuttings of 2-4-year-old wood, or by
  budding for non-ritual peel orchards; budding/grafting is explicitly
  forbidden for trees intended to produce ritual fruit. Seed propagation
  is also an accepted route for ritual trees, since seedlings grow
  true-to-type due to the species' high homozygosity.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained loam with good aeration is preferred; a
  near-neutral to mildly alkaline pH range is tolerated.
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour budget exists for this crop; rough
  estimates suggest harvest and a substantial pruning/maintenance burden
  (staking fruiting branches, thorn trimming, water-sprout removal, and
  in colder growing regions, erecting winter plastic covers) as the main
  labor components. Ritual-fruit orchards are likely considerably more
  labor-intensive per kilogram than peel orchards, given the
  fruit-by-fruit inspection and grading required, though this was not
  quantified in any source.
- **Storage:** Fresh fruit keeps best cool (around 10°C) for one to two
  weeks; at room temperature it loses weight and yellows quickly. Ritual
  fruit can be held for several months under refrigeration with specific
  plant-hormone and fungicide dips, a specialized storage protocol
  distinct from ordinary peel-crop storage.
- **Stand life:** Bearing begins at 2-3 years, peak production is
  reached between roughly 8 and 15 years, commercial orchards are
  typically kept productive for about 12-14 years, and trees generally
  die around 25-30 years of age (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Citrus bud mite, rust mite, and scale insects;
  gummosis and dieback; a branch-knot fungal disease in at least one
  growing region.
- **Cultivars:** Spans acidic peel-crop types (several named regional
  cultivars), non-acidic peel-crop types, and several named ritual
  ("etrog") strains tied to different Jewish communities' traditional
  preferences, each with slightly different fruit shape, aroma, and
  seediness.
- **Harvest:** Green fruit for candying is picked at a specific target
  length; ritual fruit is cut over a narrow late-summer-to-early-autumn
  window and inspected fruit by fruit; fully ripe yellow fruit (used for
  zest, culinary use, and essential-oil extraction) follows later in the
  season.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Own-rooted trees grow well in
  containers and greenhouses, as is also documented for the related
  fingered-citron crop this project has also researched; whether
  container or soilless cultivation affects a fruit's ritual
  acceptability was not researched in any source reviewed.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include citrus bud mite, rust mite, scale
insects, gummosis, dieback, and a branch-knot fungal disease in at least
one growing region. Thorns on the plant are also a handling hazard,
particularly relevant since scarring from thorns can ruin fruit intended
for the ritual market.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other citrus, the peel oil is phototoxic (the
same furanocoumarin compounds found in lemon and lime peel oil),
creating a skin-contact photosensitization hazard when peel oil contacts
skin followed by sunlight exposure. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** green fruit → halved and depulped → brined for roughly a
  month → desalted, boiled, and candied into succade; ritual fruit →
  graded fruit by fruit → stored under refrigeration (with specialized
  hormone/fungicide dips for long-term festival storage); yellow,
  fully-ripe fruit → zest, salads, peel oil, and a traditional citron
  liqueur; prunings → an essential oil distilled from the leaves and
  twigs, and rooted cuttings for propagation.
- Not covered in detail by this research: canned or frozen products;
  winter protective structures; thorn-trimming practice in detail;
  rabbinical ritual-inspection practice; a citron-flavored soft drink;
  moth-repellent use of the fruit.

A long-standing Jewish ritual tradition treats whole, unblemished fruit
of this species as one of the "four species" used during a major autumn
festival, with strict production rules (no grafting, smooth unbudded
trunk) and fruit-by-fruit rabbinical quality inspection governing which
fruit qualifies; demand is highly seasonal and geographically
international, with one major producing country's output largely
destined for export to dozens of countries.

## Open research questions

- Cold-hardiness figures conflict between sources: one gives a
  relatively cold leaf-damage threshold, which sits oddly against this
  species' description elsewhere as "the tenderest of all citrus
  species" — not reconciled here.
- Growing-region area figures for at least one major production region
  conflict substantially between sources.
- Fermentation/brining duration for the candying process is given
  somewhat differently across sources (roughly 30-40 days versus a more
  specific "about 40 days, changed every two weeks").
- A detailed food-composition figure from one source was flagged by that
  source itself as needing to be rechecked.
- Caloric content, peel-to-whole-fruit weight ratio, candied-peel yield
  ratio, essential-oil yield, salinity tolerance, and detailed irrigation
  practice were not found in the sources reviewed.
- Planting density (trees per hectare) is an estimate based on assumed
  spacing rather than a sourced orchard-planning figure.
- Whether container or soilless cultivation of a tree intended for
  ritual fruit affects that fruit's ritual acceptability was not
  addressed in any source reviewed.

## Estimates and derivations

Time to first harvest (roughly 730-1,100 days, i.e., 2-3 years) and a
multi-year ramp to full bearing (reaching a plateau around year 8) are
derived from documented bearing-age and peak-production-age figures,
cross-checked against the documented commercial orchard lifespan of
roughly 12-14 years.

Yield tiers are derived from a combination of sources: a world-average
figure (roughly 7,000 kg/ha) from a documented total peel-crop output
divided by growing area; good and exceptional tiers from documented
per-tree yield figures (roughly 30 kg and up to 68 kg per tree
respectively) combined with an assumed planting density of about 500
trees/ha; and a poor-yield figure derived from one major growing
region's documented total production and area. These should be treated
as having a fairly wide error range, since the underlying area and
per-tree figures come from search-snippet-level sources rather than
official statistics opened directly.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient curve, seasonal water-use index,
root-zone depth) are adopted from a standard international crop-water-
use reference's citrus figures for a tree with roughly half canopy cover
and no ground cover, since citron trees are typically small and open;
this is treated as a reasonable approximation rather than a
species-specific measurement, since citron may transpire somewhat more
than other citrus under heat stress (a documented sensitivity not
otherwise quantified).

Planting density (roughly 500 trees/ha) is estimated from the species'
documented shrubby growth habit and typical mature size, absent any
sourced orchard-spacing guideline.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest, pruning/maintenance, and post-harvest
handling are rough estimates based on assumed per-fruit handling rates
and the documented labor-intensive maintenance practices (staking,
thorn-trimming, winter protection), since no detailed cost study was
found for this crop.

Storage duration (about two weeks under refrigeration for ordinary
fruit) is derived from a documented short-term cold-storage trial;
long-term, months-long storage for ritual fruit specifically relies on
a specialized hormone/fungicide dip protocol documented separately and
is not interchangeable with ordinary fresh-fruit storage guidance.
