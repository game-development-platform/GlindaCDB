## Naming and scope

*Prunus cerasifera*, known as cherry plum or myrobalan generally, alycha
in Eastern Europe, tkemali in Georgia, and can erik in Turkey [1]. This
entry covers the cherry plum as a fruit crop, including both the
green-picked Turkish types grown specifically for an early, tart green
fruit, and the Russian "hybrid alycha" cultivars bred by crossing cherry
plum with Chinese plum [5][6][9].

Not covered here: ornamental purple-leaf cultivars bred purely for
foliage color, and a related ornamental hybrid involving sand cherry
[1]. This species is closely related to — and may be the sole wild
ancestor of — European plum, and is also distinct from Japanese plum,
both of which this project treats as separate crop entries; values from
those related crops are used here only as explicitly flagged analogies
where no cherry-plum-specific data was found.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one tree, two distinct harvestable crops.** The green
stage is a major seasonal product in Turkey: unripe fruit, picked from
hazelnut-size up to about 3 cm, is traditionally eaten dipped in salt.
A 2015 trial of two named Turkish green-plum cultivars picked fruit five
times at 12-day intervals across a roughly six-week window, finding
green fruit rich in both sugars and phenolic compounds, with one
cultivar notably higher in both than the other [5]. A separate, larger,
juicier, and less sour green-plum type is also grown commercially.
Green fruit is also cooked into stews and stuffed vine leaves, or
pickled [6]. The ripe stage follows later in the season: fruit 2-3 cm
across, colored yellow, red, or dark purple, ripening from midsummer
into early autumn, eaten fresh when sweet or made into jam when sour
[1].

**Finding 2 — two distinctive fermented/cooked condiment products.** A
"pungently tart" sauce made from red or green cherry plums, cooked down
with garlic, a mint-family herb, coriander, dill, chili, and salt, is a
major national condiment in Georgia, bottled commercially by numerous
producers and eaten with grilled meat, poultry, and potatoes [7]. A
companion product is a tart, dried fruit-leather made the same way.
Cherry plums are also traditionally used to sour a well-known Georgian
soup and a walnut-and-herb stew [1][7].

**Finding 3 — the species is also the dominant plum rootstock in at
least one major producing country.** Seedling-origin myrobalan rootstock
is the most widely used plum rootstock in Turkey, though nurseries'
preference for it is tempered by grower complaints about suckering,
slow bearing, excessive vigor, and occasional graft incompatibility [8].
A specific clonal selection, introduced in California in 1920 from
French seed stock, roots at a very high rate (92%) from hardwood
cuttings and tolerates both waterlogging and root-knot nematode; it is
compatible as rootstock for apricot, plum, and prune, but performs
poorly with peach and is partly incompatible with almond [10]. Seed
propagation generally needs 2-3 months of cold stratification, though
germination itself can take up to 18 months [4].

**Finding 4 — a documented Russian hybrid-alycha breeding program.** One
named cultivar (a cross between a Chinese plum cultivar and a cherry
plum cultivar) was officially released in 1987 for several cold Russian
growing regions. It is a weak-growing tree bearing large red fruit with
a documented sugar and acid content, rated reasonably well for both
fresh eating and processing (compote, jam, juice) [9]. It shows high
winter hardiness, is partly self-fertile, and propagates readily from
both green and hardwood cuttings; less formal sources report
cold-hardiness to around -30°C, first fruiting in year 2-3, and average
per-tree yields around 10 kg (up to 50 kg for the best trees) [9][11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous tree or large shrub, wild forms
  reaching 8-12 m and often spiny and suckering; hybrid cultivars are
  much smaller (roughly 2.5-3 m). One of the first European trees to
  flower each year (as early as mid-February in parts of its naturalized
  range), carrying real frost risk at bloom.
- **Climate:** Temperate, Mediterranean, continental, and semi-arid
  climates across its native range (the Balkans through Turkey, the
  Caucasus, Iran, and Central Asia); tolerant of drought, strong wind,
  and frost.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible at both harvest stages
  (green fruit eaten with salt; ripe fruit eaten out of hand). Caloric
  and protein content are carried over by analogy from the related plum
  crop this project has also researched, since no dedicated composition
  analysis for this species was found.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not directly sourced for this species.
  By analogy with deciduous *Prunus* generally (sweet cherry, apricot,
  almond), dormancy release and flowering timing are driven by
  cumulative winter chilling followed by post-dormancy heat
  accumulation, not by day length; a commercial nursery source gives a
  rough 300-500 chill-hour estimate for this species specifically,
  unverified against peer-reviewed data [12]. Not applicable to
  tuberization/bulbing — a tree crop with no storage organ.
- **Pollination:** Sources conflict on self-fertility — one source
  describes the species as "self-fertile but cross-pollinated," while
  another describes it as an outbreeding diploid; the Russian hybrid
  alycha cultivars are documented as partly self-fertile. Insect (bee)
  pollinated regardless, and notable as one of the first spring nectar
  sources available to pollinators.
- **Yield:** No per-hectare production statistics exist for this species
  specifically, since international agricultural statistics typically
  lump it together with plums and sloes as a combined category; yield
  tiers here are derived from per-tree figures and an assumed planting
  density (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from
  the related plum crop this project has also researched, likely
  overstating this species' actual needs given its documented strong
  drought tolerance; waterlogging tolerance is at least moderate, since
  the main rootstock selection specifically tolerates waterlogged soil.
- **Propagation:** By seedling (variable, since the species is an
  outbreeder), by named cultivars budded onto seedling or clonal
  rootstock, or by rooted hardwood or green cuttings; seed needs 2-3
  months of cold stratification and can take up to 18 months to
  germinate.
- **Soil:** Tolerates light, medium, and heavy soils, with well-drained
  soil preferred; pH range is carried over by analogy from the related
  plum crop, since no direct figure was found for this species.
  Salinity tolerance is directly sourced at the species level but
  inconsistent: in vitro trials on the Myrobalan 29C and Myrobolan B
  clonal rootstock selections found only limited to moderate salt
  tolerance (worse under severe NaCl stress), while a *P. cerasifera* ×
  *P. munsoniana* hybrid rootstock (Mariana 2624) tested as the most
  salt-tolerant of seven *Prunus* rootstocks compared, and a 2023 trial
  of eight *P. cerasifera* genotypes found tolerance varying widely
  within the species — suggesting genotype matters more than species
  identity here, so no single figure can be cited as representative
  [13].
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour budget exists for this crop; rough
  estimates are carried over by analogy from the related plum crop,
  adjusted for smaller fruit size, with an added allowance for the
  extra selective hand-picking passes needed for the green-fruit market.
- **Storage:** No dedicated storage study was found for this species;
  green fruit is eaten crisp within about a week of picking, while ripe
  fruit softens quickly and is generally sold, cooked into condiments or
  jam, or (when overripe) mashed for brandy, rather than stored for any
  length of time.
- **Stand life:** Not well researched; one source notes that large
  crops occur only "every four years or so" under wild/naturalized
  conditions in at least one cool-climate region, suggesting irregular
  bearing outside intensive cultivation.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific plum-virus disease is a
  documented threat to wild populations; rootstock seedlings are prone
  to vigorous suckering; one named cultivar shows reduced fruit size
  under unusually heavy crop loads.
- **Cultivars:** Includes a wild baseline type, several named Turkish
  green-plum cultivars (differing in sugar and phenolic content at the
  green stage), at least one named Russian hybrid-alycha cultivar bred
  with Chinese plum, a specific clonal rootstock selection, and several
  purely ornamental purple-leaf forms (noted here only because their
  fruit can be used for intensely colored jam).
- **Harvest stages:** Two harvest stages from the same tree — green
  (picked over several passes in spring, used fresh with salt, in
  cooking, or pickled) and ripe (picked later in the season, used
  fresh, for jam, or for the Georgian condiment products).
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not researched in depth; one
  source notes ornamental bonsai use of the species.

## Hazards

Documented disease pressure includes a specific plum-virus disease that
threatens wild populations; rootstock seedlings commonly sucker
vigorously, a management nuisance rather than a toxicological hazard.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other stone fruit in the genus *Prunus*, the
seeds (kernels) and leaves are cyanogenic; the fruit flesh itself is not.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** green fruit → eaten fresh with salt, or cooked into stews
  and pickles; ripe fruit → eaten fresh or made into jam; ripe fruit →
  puréed and sieved → cooked down with garlic and herbs into the
  Georgian tart condiment sauce, or dried into a sour fruit leather;
  ripe fruit → juice and compote; overripe fruit → mashed → fermented
  and distilled into a fruit brandy; stones → stratified → grown on as
  seedling rootstock trees (for the crop itself or for other stone-fruit
  rootstock use); prunings → hardwood cuttings → clonal rootstock.
- Not covered in detail by this research: canned and frozen fruit;
  purely ornamental purple-leaf cultivation; a reported traditional
  flower-remedy use.

## Open research questions

- No reliable per-hectare yield or production-area statistics exist for
  this species specifically, since it is typically lumped with plums
  and sloes in international agricultural statistics; a dedicated
  country-level statistic (from Turkey, Georgia, or Russia, the main
  producing regions implied by the sources) would improve on the
  per-tree-derived estimate used here.
- Self-fertility is directly disputed between sources (self-fertile but
  cross-pollinated, versus an outbreeding diploid) — not resolved here.
- No dedicated postharvest storage study was found for this species;
  the storage estimate used here is a judgment call based on the fruit's
  softness relative to the related plum crop.
- Stand life and bearing regularity are not well researched; one source's
  note on irregular, infrequent heavy-crop years under less intensive
  cultivation was not further quantified.
- Soil pH tolerance is carried over by analogy from the related plum
  crop rather than directly sourced for this species.
- Salinity tolerance is directly sourced but conflicting at the
  genotype level (clonal rootstock selections read as only moderately
  tolerant, while a hybrid rootstock and at least one of eight tested
  genotypes read as strongly tolerant) — not reconciled into a single
  representative figure here.
- No peer-reviewed, species-specific chilling-requirement study was
  found; the ~300-500 chill-hour figure used here comes from a
  commercial nursery source only.
- Spacing and planting-density figures used to derive per-hectare yield
  tiers are assumed by analogy to typical plum-orchard spacing, not
  directly sourced for this species.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein content are carried over by analogy from the related
plum crop this project has also researched, since no dedicated
composition analysis was found for this species; green fruit is known to
be lower in sugar than ripe fruit, though this was not quantified in any
source reviewed.

Yield tiers (roughly 2,000-25,000 kg/ha across poor-to-exceptional
production) are derived from documented hybrid-cultivar per-tree yield
figures (roughly 10 kg average, up to 50 kg for the best trees) combined
with an assumed planting density similar to typical plum-orchard
spacing (roughly 650 trees/ha); wild seedling trees are larger but bear
much more irregularly, so this estimate likely best represents
cultivated hybrid orchards rather than wild or semi-wild stands.

Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from the related plum
crop's documented crop-coefficient and stage-length figures, likely
overstating this species' true water need given its well-documented
drought tolerance.

Time to first harvest (roughly 900 days, about 2.5 years) is derived
from the documented bearing age of hybrid cultivars; seedling-origin
trees are expected to take longer, though this was not directly sourced.

Labor-hour estimates are carried over by analogy from the related plum
crop, scaled down somewhat for this species' smaller fruit size, with
an unquantified addition for the extra selective hand-picking passes
required by the green-fruit market.

Soil pH range is carried over by analogy from the related plum crop,
with the upper bound raised slightly to reflect this species' documented
wide soil adaptability (light, medium, and heavy soils).

Storage duration (about one week) is estimated from the fact that no
direct storage study was found for this species, reasoning that its
generally softer, smaller fruit likely keeps for less time than the
related plum crop's documented several-week storage life.

Peak harvest-yield rate and standing-quality decay are estimated by
assuming the ripe crop is harvested over roughly a three-week window,
consistent with a source noting that fruit can remain on the tree into
early autumn.
