## Naming and scope

*Prunus salicina* Lindl. (Rosaceae): Japanese plum, Chinese plum, Asian
plum, willow-leaf cherry; *li* (李) in Mandarin; *sumomo* in Japan. The
epithet refers to the willow-like leaves [1]. It is native to China,
Taiwan, Myanmar, Laos and Vietnam, and was domesticated in the Yangtze
basin of southwestern China [1]. It was domesticated in Japan about 400
years ago and brought to California from Japan in 1870 by John Kelsey.
Luther Burbank imported about 12 seeds in 1885 and bred many cultivars
from them [3].

Most modern "Japanese" plum cultivars are interspecific hybrids [16].
Burbank crossed *P. salicina* with *P. simonii* and with native American
plums (*P. americana*, *P. hortulana*, *P. munsoniana*) [1]. They are
grown and handled as one crop, so they are treated as one entry here.
Plum × apricot hybrids are also a cultivar group within this entry
(Cultivars) [24][25][26].

**Separate entries:**
- **European plum** (*P. domestica*): see `plum.md`. It blooms later,
  needs more chill, and is mostly dried as prunes [3][18]. As a general
  rule the two do not pollinate each other [23].
- **Ume** (*P. mume*): see `ume.md`. Ume is the source of most salted and
  dried "Chinese plum" snacks [17].
- **Cherry plum** (*P. cerasifera*): see `cherry-plum.md`. Russian
  "hybrid alycha" cultivars cross it with this species.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — China grows most of the world's plums, at a low national
yield that hides very productive farms.**
- **China:** 2.11 million ha and 7.0 million t, 55.6% of world output in
  2019 [20]. That is about 3.3 t/ha (see Estimates, below). Its plum
  yield per area rose 102.8% over thirty years, mainly from synthetic
  fertiliser [20].
- **Fujian survey:** a 2021 survey of 254 plum farms in Zhao'an county
  found an average of 20.1 t/ha, ranging from 1.7 to 97.5 t/ha [20]. The
  best-managed group ("high yield, low impact") averaged about 42-43 t/ha
  [20].
- **World, both species:** over 2.5 million ha in 2009, averaging 4.3
  t/ha [18].
- **California, 2023-2025:** 9,600-9,800 bearing acres yielding 8.62-9.51
  short tons per acre, or 19.3-21.3 t/ha [5].
- **Modelled orchard:** a California cost study assumes 900 boxes of 28
  lb per acre at maturity, or 28.2 t/ha (see Estimates, below) [4].

The low Chinese national average is therefore not a ceiling. A probable
reason is that the national area includes young, extensive or
low-input plantings; that is an inference. The original pass also
reported weak postharvest infrastructure in China [2]:
- pre-cooling reaches under a quarter of the crop;
- cold-chain penetration is under half;
- domestic cultivars keep 5-7 days at ambient, against 20 days for a
  long-storing imported cultivar.

Trade-press accounts add that some southern Chinese plums keep only 1-2
days at room temperature [28]. Pre-cooling within 2 hours of picking
extends that from under 24 hours to over 72 hours [28]. Chemical soaking
to extend shelf life has caused a food-safety scandal [28].

**Finding 2 — self-incompatible, and paired only with its own kind.**
Most fresh-market Japanese plums are self-unfruitful and need
cross-pollination, so plantings carry two or more cultivars [4][18].
- **Pollinizer distance:** within about a hundred yards, with overlapping
  bloom [8].
- **European plum:** as a general rule, Japanese and European plums do not
  pollinate each other. Japanese plums bloom about a week earlier [23].
- **Exceptions:** some cultivars are self-compatible [1], for example
  'Methley' and 'Beauty' [21].
- **Bees:** California orchards place one hive per acre, and up to two
  for hard-setting cultivars. 'Friar' is easy-setting [4].
- **Thinning:** trees bloom profusely. 'Black-Gold' sets 10-40% of its
  flowers in Mediterranean conditions, but commercial crops need only
  5-10%, so fruit is thinned by hand, chemically or mechanically [18].

**Finding 3 — low and very cultivar-specific chill.**
- **Spain:** in a 21-cultivar study at two sites, chilling requirements
  ranged 277-851 chill hours, 412-1,030 chill units and 26-51 chill
  portions. Heat requirements ranged 4,343-9,525 growing degree hours
  [16]. 'Earliqueen' needed the least (292 CH, 30 CP), the only
  low-chill cultivar. 'Golden Globe' needed the most (725 CH, 50 CP)
  [16]. Thirteen cultivars, including 'Santa Rosa', 'Fortune' and
  'Angeleno', were medium (31-40 CP) [16]. An earlier eight-cultivar study
  gave 6,130-7,635 GDH [6].
- **'Sanyueli' (China):** a low-chill cultivar whose flower buds needed
  28 hours at or below 7.2 °C in one report and about 50 hours in its
  genome study [7]. A high-chill cultivar, 'Furongli', expresses the
  dormancy gene *PsDAM6* more strongly [7].
- **'Santa Rosa':** 300 chill hours per a university database and a
  nursery, 500-600 per a wholesale nursery, and 700 per another nursery
  [21].
- **'Methley':** 200-450 chill hours across sources [21].

Japanese plums grow better than European plums in warmer temperate
regions because they need less chill [18]. But they bloom early, which
exposes the blossoms to late frost (Finding 6) [8][23].

**Finding 4 — the plum cold-storage trap: 0 °C good, 5 °C worse.**
- **Standard storage:** -1.1 to 0 °C at 90-95% RH. The freezing point is
  -2 to -1 °C, depending on soluble solids [3].
- **Chilling injury:** most cultivars suffer it at 5 °C. 'Blackamber',
  'Fortune' and 'Angeleno' kept over 5 weeks at 0 °C. 'Show Time', 'Friar'
  and 'Howard Sun' developed chilling injury within 4 weeks even at 0 °C.
  Every cultivar lasted much longer at 0 °C than at 5 °C [3].
- **Symptoms:** flesh translucency and browning, plus loss of juiciness
  in late cultivars. They appear on ripening after storage at 2-8 °C
  [3].
- **Ethylene:** fruit produces ethylene at 0.01-5 µL/kg/h at 0 °C, rising
  to 0.1-200 at 20 °C [3]. Most fruit picked "California Well-Mature"
  ripens without added ethylene. Slow-ripening cultivars need 100 µL/L
  for at least 24 hours at 20 °C [3].
- **Controlled atmosphere:** 1-2% O₂ with 3-5% CO₂ reduces decay, but has
  limited use beyond a month [3].
- **Conflict:** an encyclopedia gives only 3-4 days at room temperature
  and 1-2 weeks cold [1], against more than 5 weeks at 0 °C for
  good-keeping cultivars [3].

**Finding 5 — labour is dominated by hand thinning, and picking may be
piece-rate.** A 2004 California cost study (furrow-irrigated 'Friar')
gives mature-orchard hours per acre [4]:

| Task | Hours per acre | Hours per hectare |
|---|---|---|
| Hand pruning (December-January) | 64 | about 158 |
| Hand fruit thinning (April) | 143 | about 353 |
| Hand picking | 74.2 | about 183 |
| All non-machine labour | 293.45 | about 725 |

See Estimates, below, for the per-hectare conversions. Picking cost $832
an acre, about $11.21 an hour. That is above the study's $9.52 general
labour rate, which suggests a piece-rate premium; that is an inference
(see Estimates, below).

The original pass used a 2016 drip-irrigated study with lower figures:
roughly 24, 30 and 32 hours per acre [4]. That study was not re-found, so
the two cannot be reconciled here.

**Finding 6 — plum pox virus is the defining clonal risk.**
- **Spread:** plum pox virus (sharka) has spread over long distances
  mainly through infected planting material, then within orchards by
  aphids. It is not transmitted by seed or pollen, and pruning is not
  thought to spread it [14].
- **Susceptibility:** Japanese plum appears highly susceptible. Every
  cultivar assayed in a 2024 Czech trial showed symptoms, to differing
  degrees [14]. In Spain an average of 667 virus-carrying aphids visited
  each vigorous Japanese plum tree in May [14].
- **Losses:** a 2006 estimate put Japanese plum losses at 18,000 t a year,
  at only 5% loss of fruit from infected trees, or 126 million euros [14].
  Worldwide sharka management since the 1970s has cost over 10,000
  million euros [14].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous tree to 9-12 m [1]. Flowers about 2
  cm across, in threes, around April [1]. Flower buds form the previous
  season, on spurs and terminal shoots [18].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial, with successive ripening. Harvest date
  is set by each cultivar's skin colour change, so trees are picked by
  colour [3]. The number of passes is not sourced. The California season
  runs from 15 May to 20 October across cultivars [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - California orchards bear an economic crop in year 3 and reach full
    yield in year 7 [4].
  - Standard trees bear 3-5 years after planting [8].
  - 'Burbank' bears in 2-3 years; 'Methley' in 2-4 seasons [1].
  - Productive life is 5-35 years [18]. The cost study assumes a 20-year
    orchard [4].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** temperate-warmer regions, because chill needs are lower
    than European plum's [18].
  - **Hardiness:** many cultivars are rated to USDA zone 5 [8].
    'Shiro' is rated zones 5-8 and 'Satsuma' 4-9 [8]; 'Methley' 5-9
    [22].
  - **Chill:** 28-851 hours depending on cultivar (Finding 3) [7][16].
  - **Elevation:** 200-2,600 m in China [1].
  - **Rainfall:** early cultivars can be grown without irrigation on 300
    mm in the season; midseason cultivars need at least 400-500 mm [18].
- **Composition:** Per 100 g, ranges across Japanese plum cultivars [1]:
  water 80.7-89.4 g; carbohydrate 10-17 g; glucose 4.4-10.3 g, fructose
  1.8-4.8 g, sucrose 0.7-4.2 g; protein 0.38-0.96 g; fibre under 1.5 g;
  energy 183-331 kJ (44-79 kcal); phenolics 95-202 mg. Generic US "plums,
  raw" figures cited by third-party sites are 46 kcal, 0.7 g protein and
  11.4 g carbohydrate [9]. That US entry does not separate species.
  Fruit is about 4-7 cm across; wild types are 3.5-5 cm [1].
- **Edibility:** Raw. Fruit at about 10 N flesh firmness is ready to eat
  [3]. Kernels contain amygdalin (Hazards).
- **Pollination:** Required for most cultivars; bee-pollinated; only
  Japanese or Japanese-hybrid pollinizers work (Finding 2) [4][8][23].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** 19-21 t/ha in California, and 28 t/ha in a modelled mature
  orchard. In China, 3.3 t/ha nationally but 20 t/ha on average on
  surveyed Fujian farms (Finding 1) [4][5][20].
- **Water use:**
  - **Crop coefficient:** FAO advises using peach values for plum [18].
    The peach curve rises from 0.25 in early March to 1.05 in July and
    early August, holds at 1.00 after harvest, and falls to 0.45 by
    November [19].
  - **Canopy:** trees on a Tatura trellis at full cover used 92% of
    class-A pan evaporation in midsummer; vase or palmette trees used 82%
    [18].
  - **California:** seasonal consumptive use is 36 acre-inches (914 mm).
    At 82% irrigation efficiency, 44 acre-inches (1,118 mm) are applied,
    with June the peak month [4].
  - **Deficit irrigation:**
    - Final-stage water stress shrinks fruit, speeds ripening and raises
      sugar [18].
    - Fruit weight falls linearly with midday stem water potential, but
      the slope differs by site and season [18].
    - Only about 10% less water is admissible without yield loss in
      'Black-Gold' and 'Black Amber', and 20-25% less in 'Fortune' in
      Italy's wetter Po Valley [18].
    - After harvest, 'Black Amber' reached -3.3 MPa without defoliating,
      and half-rate irrigation cost no yield over 3 years [18].
    - A complete postharvest cutoff partly defoliated an early cultivar
      and cut the next year's crop [18].
    - Four seasons of deficit on young trees cut yield 10% by keeping
      trees small [18].
  - **Monitoring:** midday stem water potential is the best indicator,
    and trunk contraction is a secondary one [18].
- **Propagation:** Budded or grafted onto rootstocks, used to cope with
  waterlogging, iron chlorosis and salinity [18]. Myrobalan (cherry plum)
  is a major plum rootstock (see `cherry-plum.md`). Rootstocks for this
  species specifically were not re-sourced. *Clonal/viral risk:* plum pox
  virus spreads through infected planting material (Finding 6) [14].
- **Labor:** About 158 h/ha pruning, 353 h/ha thinning and 183 h/ha
  picking, about 725 h/ha of hand labour in all (Finding 5; see Estimates,
  below) [4].
- **Soil:**
  - **Site:** deep, well-drained class I or II soil, ripped and slip-plowed
    to 1.2-1.5 m before planting [4].
  - **pH:** 5.5-6.5 [8].
  - **Sensitivities:** waterlogging, iron chlorosis and salinity [18].
  - **Nitrogen:** 125 lb N per acre (140 kg/ha) a year in California,
    split between March and September, plus zinc [4]. Surveyed Fujian
    farms averaged 187 kg N, 82 kg P and 155 kg K per hectare, with N
    ranging 7-450 kg [20].
  - **Salinity:** threshold ECe 2.6 dS/m, with yield falling 31% per dS/m
    above it; rated moderately sensitive [11]. The FAO table files this
    under *P. domestica* "plum; prune". A US source says the 2.6/31 figure
    came from studies of 'Santa Rosa', a Japanese plum [11], so it
    applies here directly. The older figure for plum was 1.5 dS/m and 18%
    [11].
- **Storage (postharvest):** -1.1 to 0 °C at 90-95% RH; over 5 weeks for
  good keepers; chilling injury at 2-8 °C (Finding 4) [3]. Fruit is
  forced-air cooled to near 0 °C after packing [3]. Fresh-cut slices keep
  2-5 days at 0 °C [3].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** 4.3 × 5.5 m (14 × 18 ft), or 172 trees per acre (425 per
    hectare) [4]. Home plantings set standard trees about 6 m apart and
    semi-dwarfs about 3 m [8].
  - **Site preparation:** ground previously in orchard or field crops is
    fumigated before planting, and dead trees are replaced in year 2 [4].
  - **Training:** open-centre training is standard; Japanese trees are
    more spreading than European ones [8].
- **Cultivars:** Over 155 new Japanese plum cultivars were registered in
  the EU between 1995 and 2021 [1]. Among those found:
  - **Burbank selections:** 'Santa Rosa', 'Beauty', 'Eldorado',
    'Formosa', 'Gaviota', 'Shiro', 'Wickson' [1].
  - **'Santa Rosa':** a common pollinizer; chill is disputed at 300-700
    hours (Finding 3) [21].
  - **'Methley':** self-fruitful; a "universal pollinizer"; hardy to zone
    5 [21][22].
  - **'Friar':** about 11% of the area and 16% of the crop in the southern
    San Joaquin Valley in 2004; easy-setting [4]; prone to chilling
    injury [3].
  - **Long keepers:** 'Blackamber', 'Fortune' and 'Angeleno' keep over 5
    weeks at 0 °C [3]. 'Angeleno', 'Casselman', 'Santa Rosa', 'Laroda' and
    'Queen Ann' are kept in controlled atmosphere [3].
  - **Slow ripeners:** 'Black Beaut', 'Casselman', 'Late Santa Rosa',
    'Kelsey', 'Nubiana', 'Queen Ann', 'Roysum' [3].
  - **'Sanyueli':** very low chill, with a sequenced genome [7].
  - **Spanish set:** 'Earliqueen' (lowest chill) and 'Golden Globe'
    (highest), among 21 cultivars [16].
  - **Plum × apricot hybrids:** interspecific plums, mostly of plum
    parentage, with smooth plum-like skin and high sugar. Their common
    commercial names are trademarks, so they are described generically
    here [24][25][26].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** early bloom raises late-frost risk [8][23]. The standard US
    critical bud temperatures are for European prune plum only; for
    'Santa Rosa' they are "unavailable" [12]. Those prune values run from
    -10 °C (10% kill) at first swell to -2 °C at full bloom and after
    [12].
  - **Heat and drought:** see Water use.
  - **Waterlogging:** sensitive [18].
  - **Frost as a quality event:** none found.
- **Water regime:** Irrigated or rainfed orchard; no flooded requirement;
  waterlogging-sensitive [18].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **California fresh fruit:** a two-tier maturity system, "U.S. Mature"
    (minimum) and "California Well-Mature" [3].
  - **Firmness:** a maximum-maturity firmness index marks fruit that can
    still be handled without bruising [3].
  - **Liqueur:** half-ripe fruit is used for Japanese *sumomo shu* [1].
  - **Season:** cultivars ripen in succession from May to October in
    California [5]. Imported Chilean fruit reaches China in January-March
    and Xinjiang fruit in August-September [28].
- **Soil biology:** Plums form endomycorrhizal associations. Inoculation
  with the ectomycorrhizal fungus *Hebeloma hiemale* increased growth of
  a *P. cerasifera* × *salicina* hybrid [1]. Replant disease was not
  sourced, although California sites are fumigated before replanting [4].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Status:** native to China and mainland Southeast Asia; introduced to
    Korea, Japan, the US and Australia [1]. New Zealand records it as
    exotic and cultivated [15]. IUCN status is Least Concern [1].
  - **Listings:** none found [15].
  - **Quarantine:** California plums need phytosanitary certificates for
    Taiwan, British Columbia and Mexico. They must be certified free of
    named pests such as plum curculio, codling moth and oriental fruit
    moth [3].
  - **Contrast:** cherry plum is a recorded environmental weed (see
    `cherry-plum.md`).
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Kernel amygdalin:** pits and leaves contain amygdalin, which breaks
  down to hydrogen cyanide. Crushed pits are the main concern [1]. A UK
  market survey found plum seed amygdalin of 0.44-17.5 mg/g, varying with
  plum type [13]:
  - green 17.5 mg/g;
  - black 10;
  - purple 2.16;
  - yellow 1.54;
  - red 0.44.

  The samples were named by skin colour, not species or cultivar, so how
  much applies to this species is not known [13]. Stone-fruit seeds could
  release 0.01-1.1 mg cyanide per g [13]. Processed fruit products were
  very low in amygdalin [13].

  TO BE ADDRESSED: this project has no cyanogenic-glycoside hazard data
  structure yet. Ripe flesh carries no such hazard.
- **Plum pox virus** (Finding 6) [14].
- **Postharvest rots:**
  - brown rot (*Monilinia fructicola*), the most important postharvest
    disease of stone fruit, which infects at flowering;
  - grey mould (*Botrytis cinerea*);
  - *Rhizopus* rot at 20-25 °C [3].

  *Monilia mumecola* brown rot was first reported on this species in
  Fujian in 2023 [27]. Fruit cracking makes late cultivars more prone to
  decay [3].
- **Fungal contamination of the product:** rots as above. No mycotoxin
  finding.
- **Orchard pests (California):** peach twig borer, San Jose scale,
  katydids, codling moth, mites and aphids. Brown rot blossom blight,
  jacket rot and powdery mildew are treated at bloom [4]. Black knot and
  plum curculio are major problems in the US Northeast [8].
- **Chemical residues:** chemical soaking to extend shelf life caused a
  food-safety scandal in China [28]. Pest control in China was reported
  as overwhelmingly chemical [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh fruit):** picked by colour into bags → bins →
  packinghouse: washed, sorted, sized → packed in 12.6 kg (28 lb)
  containers → forced-air cooled to about 0 °C → stored at -1.1 to 0 °C →
  ethylene-conditioned if slow-ripening → retail on cold or dry tables by
  firmness [3].
- **Chain 2 (processed):** canned, frozen, jam and jelly [1][3]; fresh-cut
  slices at 0 °C [3].
- **Chain 3 (drinks):** plum wine; Japanese *sumomo shu* liqueur from
  half-ripe fruit; Taiwanese *wumeijiu*, a smoked plum liquor blending
  *P. salicina* and *P. mume* liquors with oolong tea liquor [1].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** Chinese salted, sugared and
  licorice-scented preserved plums, documented as mostly made from ume
  [17] (see `ume.md`).

No religious or ritual use was found in the sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- **Chill for 'Santa Rosa':** 300-700 hours across sources [21]; Spain
  rates it medium [16]. The low-chill 'Sanyueli' needs 28 or about 50
  hours [7].
- **Labour hours:** 2004 (64 / 143 / 74 h per acre) against the original
  pass's 2016 figures (about 24 / 30 / 32) [4]. Whether picking is paid
  piece-rate is unresolved.
- **China's yield gap:** a national 3.3 t/ha against a surveyed 20 t/ha
  (Finding 1) [20]. The national area basis is unclear.
- **Shelf life:** 3-4 days at room temperature and 1-2 weeks cold [1],
  against over 5 weeks at 0 °C [3], and 1-2 days for some southern
  Chinese fruit [28].
- **Kernel amygdalin** has not been measured by named *P. salicina*
  cultivar [13].
- **Japanese-plum critical frost temperatures** are unavailable [12].
- **Not sourced:** photoperiod, replant disease, controlled environment,
  rootstock choice for this species, number of picking passes.

## Estimates and derivations

**Unit conversions.**
- 1 short ton per acre = 2.2417 t/ha. California's 8.62-9.51 tons per
  acre [5] is therefore 19.3-21.3 t/ha.
- 9,800 acres = 3,966 ha.

**Modelled mature yield, 28.2 t/ha.** 900 boxes × 28 lb = 25,200 lb =
11,430 kg per acre [4]. ÷ 0.4047 ha per acre = 28.2 t/ha. The study's
county averages of 7.04-7.87 tons per acre (1999-2003) [4] are 15.8-17.6
t/ha.

**Yield per tree.** 28.2 t/ha ÷ 425 trees/ha [4] = about 66 kg per tree.
At California's current 20 t/ha [5], it would be about 47 kg per tree if
the same spacing applied. That spacing is an assumption.

**China's national yield, about 3.3 t/ha.** 7.0 million t ÷ 2.11
million ha [20] = 3.32 t/ha. That is about a sixth of the Fujian survey
mean of 20.1 t/ha [20].

**Labour per hectare** (× 2.471 acres per ha) [4]:
- pruning: 64 h/acre = 158 h/ha;
- thinning: 143 h/acre = 353 h/ha;
- picking: 74.2 h/acre = 183 h/ha;
- all non-machine labour: 293.45 h/acre = 725 h/ha;
- machine labour: 21.27 h/acre = 53 h/ha.

**Picking rate.** 11,430 kg per acre ÷ 74.2 h = about 154 kg per
picker-hour. The cost per hour, $832 ÷ 74.2 h = $11.21, is about 18% above
the study's $9.52 general labour rate [4]. A piece-rate or harvest
premium is the likeliest explanation; that is an inference.

**Water.**
- 36 acre-inches × 25.4 mm = 914 mm consumptive use [4], or about 9,140
  m³/ha.
- 44 acre-inches = 1,118 mm applied [4].
- At 425 trees/ha, 9,140 m³/ha is about 21.5 m³ per tree per season.

**Nitrogen.** 125 lb per acre × 0.4536 kg ÷ 0.4047 ha = 140 kg N/ha [4].
That is about 0.33 kg N per tree at 425 trees/ha.

**Bee hives.** 1-2 hives per acre [4] = 2.5-4.9 hives per hectare.

**Critical bud temperatures** (European prune values, °F to °C) [12]:
- 14 °F = -10.0 °C;
- 28 °F = -2.2 °C.
