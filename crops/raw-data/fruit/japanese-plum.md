## Naming and scope

The Chinese or Japanese plum, native to China, Taiwan, and Southeast
Asia, domesticated in southwestern China, and later developed further in
Japan before being introduced to California by early 20th-century
breeders [1][3]. Many widely grown "Japanese" cultivars are actually
complex selections that also include a related wild American plum
species and another East Asian plum species in their parentage; all are
treated here as one crop [1].

Explicitly not covered by this entry: a different, smaller East Asian
stone fruit sometimes also loosely called "Chinese plum" (the source of
umeboshi, umeshu, and most Chinese salted/dried plum snacks — a
genuinely distinct species this project treats separately); interspecific
plum-apricot hybrid types (treated below as a cultivar group within this
entry, since they are grown, stored, and handled identically); and the
European plum, a closely related stone-fruit crop this project has also
researched, which does not cross-pollinate with this species and differs
in chilling requirement and cold-hardiness (Finding 3).

This entry is grounded against that closely related European stone-fruit
sibling crop for most shared physiological fields (storage physiology,
salinity tolerance, frost-damage thresholds, kernel toxin profile), and
secondarily against peach (another stone-fruit crop this project has
researched) for water-use-by-canopy-light-interception modeling.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one country grows roughly three-quarters of the world's
plum-growing area at comparatively low yield.** One major East Asian
growing country accounts for the large majority of world plum-growing
area but a smaller majority of world output, reflecting a meaningfully
lower average yield per hectare than other major producing regions; that
national average yield has stayed roughly flat over a recent three-year
period [2]. By contrast, California's fresh-market plum yields run
several times higher, and a well-run commercial orchard in California's
San Joaquin Valley produces roughly 28 tonnes per hectare of packable
fruit [4][5]. The gap reflects management and post-harvest
infrastructure as much as genetics: in the lower-yielding country,
pre-cooling reaches under a quarter of the crop and cold-chain
penetration is under half, with domestic cultivars keeping only five to
seven days at ambient temperature versus twenty days for a long-storing
imported cultivar that dominates that country's import market [2]. This
crop spans an unusually wide yield range across growing regions as a
result.

**Finding 2 — mostly self-incompatible, so orchards carry dedicated
non-harvested pollinizer trees.** Most fresh-market cultivars of this
species are self-unfruitful and require cross-pollination; one
California cost study assumes roughly one-tenth of an orchard's trees
are pollinizer trees that are planted but never harvested, alongside
standard beehive placement (doubled for harder-setting cultivars) [4].
This species and its closely related European plum sibling crop do not
pollinate each other [8].

**Finding 3 — among the lowest chilling requirements of any temperate
stone fruit researched so far.** A study of eight cultivars of this
species in Spain found chilling requirements considerably lower than
typical for most temperate stone fruit, with most Chinese cultivars of
this species needing a moderate chilling total and the European plum
sibling crop often needing substantially more [6][7]. At least one named
low-chill Chinese cultivar (with a fully sequenced genome) and at least
one widely grown Western low-chill cultivar are documented at the low
end of this range [7][16]. This low chilling requirement allows the crop
to be grown successfully in subtropical growing regions where the
higher-chill European plum sibling crop cannot reliably produce a crop
[2].

**Finding 4 — the same cold-storage trap as its European sibling crop,
but sharper and more cultivar-dependent.** Standard storage is just below
freezing at high humidity; some cultivars keep well over five weeks
under these conditions, while others develop internal chilling-injury
browning within four weeks even at the same near-freezing storage
temperature — all cultivars keep meaningfully longer near freezing than
at a few degrees warmer [3]. The fruit is climacteric and ripens further
after harvest, with ethylene treatment used to speed ripening of
slow-to-ripen cultivars [3].

**Finding 5 — a rare case where detailed orchard labor-hour data was
available.** A California cost-of-production study gives dollar figures
for dormant hand pruning, hand fruit thinning, and hand picking per acre
at full crop, from which hourly labor figures can be derived (roughly 24,
30, and 32 hours per acre respectively, or proportionally more per
hectare) [4]. The picking figure implies an unusually high per-worker-hour
harvest rate, high enough that it may actually reflect a piece-rate
payment structure rather than a direct hourly wage, meaning the true
labor-hour figure could be somewhat higher than this derivation suggests.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous perennial fruit tree; perennial
  bearing, harvested in several hand passes by fruit color.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; several hand-picking passes
  per season sorted by color/ripeness stage.
- **Climate:** Subtropical through cool-temperate and Mediterranean
  climates; an unusually low chilling requirement among temperate stone
  fruit allows cultivation into subtropical growing regions where its
  higher-chill European sibling crop cannot reliably crop (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Required; insect/bee-pollinated. Most commercial
  cultivars are self-incompatible, requiring dedicated pollinizer trees
  (a meaningful fraction of an orchard, never harvested) and standard
  beehive placement; this species does not cross-pollinate with its
  European plum sibling crop (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Raw fruit is moderate in calories and protein.
  Yield varies enormously by growing region and management
  infrastructure: a much lower national average in the largest-growing-
  area country versus several-fold higher yields in a well-managed
  California orchard (Finding 1).
- **Water use:** A detailed water-use-response dataset exists for this
  crop. Stage II (pit hardening) is identified as the best window to
  concentrate any deliberate deficit irrigation for fresh-market
  production, since it is relatively insensitive, though it is short in
  early-maturing cultivars and risks deficit stress spilling into the
  more sensitive final fruit-growth stage. Final-stage water stress
  significantly reduces fruit size (with a site- and season-specific
  linear relationship to plant water status) while advancing ripening and
  raising sugar content. Post-harvest water status should be kept
  moderately high; one cultivar tolerated severe post-harvest water
  stress without defoliation in a controlled trial, but a complete
  post-harvest irrigation cutoff in an early-maturing cultivar caused
  partial defoliation and reduced the following year's crop. Only
  roughly 10 percent less water than full crop demand is typically
  admissible without yield loss in most studied cultivars, somewhat more
  (20-25 percent) for at least one cultivar in a humid growing region.
  Midday stem water potential is identified as the most useful
  field-measurable water-stress indicator for this crop, best related to
  fruit size during the final growth phase; trunk shrinkage and
  canopy-air temperature difference are documented secondary indicators.
- **Establishment:** Grafted/budded trees are planted; commercial
  California orchards commonly use a peach rootstock or another
  specialized rootstock, while other rootstocks are used in other growing
  regions.
- **Labor:** Detailed per-acre labor-dollar figures exist for pruning,
  thinning, and picking from one California cost study, converted here
  to hours (Finding 5); the harvest figure in particular may understate
  true labor hours if it reflects piece-rate pay.
- **Soil:** Moderate salinity sensitivity (shared threshold with the
  European plum sibling crop); well-drained soil preferred; rootstock
  choice affects waterlogging tolerance, with a peach-derived rootstock
  implying less tolerance of wet soils than some alternative rootstocks
  used elsewhere.
- **Stand life:** First harvest around year three, reaching full mature
  yield by years five to seven; productive orchard life documented at
  eighteen years or more in at least one commercial setting.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A serious virus disease (spread through
  propagation material, managed via certified clean planting stock) is
  among the main threats in the largest-growing-area country, alongside
  bacterial shot hole and brown rot; pest control in that country is
  reported as overwhelmingly chemical rather than biological.
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named cultivars spans early to late
  season, red/purple to yellow to black skin color, and a range of
  chilling requirements from very low (a named low-chill Chinese
  cultivar with a sequenced genome, and at least one widely grown
  low-chill Western cultivar) to moderate. A distinct cultivar group of
  interspecific plum-apricot hybrids (predominantly plum parentage,
  smooth plum-like skin, notably high sugar content) is grown, stored,
  and handled the same as standard cultivars of this species and is
  treated here as a cultivar group rather than a separate crop; the
  specific commercial trademark names used for these hybrids are not
  used here since they are registered trademarks, with the fruit instead
  described generically as an interspecific plum or plumcot type.
- **Harvest:** Hand-picked in several passes sorted by fruit color, since
  ripening within a planting is uneven; a documented harvest window
  length of under two weeks per pass in a commercial California setting.
- **Storage:** Just below freezing at high humidity is standard; shelf
  life ranges from under four weeks (chilling-injury-prone cultivars) to
  over five weeks (long-storing cultivars) under these conditions;
  holding at intermediate refrigerator temperatures (roughly 2-8°C) is
  specifically to be avoided, since it falls within the chilling-injury
  zone for this fruit. Controlled-atmosphere storage is used for
  extended storage of some cultivars.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases in the largest-growing-area country
include a serious virus disease (managed through certified clean
planting stock), bacterial shot hole, and brown rot. Pest control in
that growing region is reported as overwhelmingly chemical rather than
biological, though this is a general agricultural-practice note rather
than a food-safety hazard specific to the fruit itself. TO BE ADDRESSED:
kernels (inside the pit) of this species contain amygdalin, a cyanogenic
compound, at levels documented to vary by roughly twenty-fold across
different cultivars in one analytical study; this is a genuine deferred
human and animal health-safety hazard (both for kernel consumption and
for livestock exposure to foliage) that this project has not yet built a
dedicated cyanogenic-glycoside hazard data structure for, and should be
flagged for a future pass alongside the same hazard in other stone-fruit
crops this project has researched. Ripe flesh itself carries no such
hazard; mitigation is simply to avoid eating the kernels, whether whole
or crushed.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Harvested fruit is cooled and sorted/packed for fresh
  sale. Pitted fruit is made into jam. Fruit is also fermented into plum
  wine.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned and frozen product
  forms; Chinese-style salted, sugared, or licorice-scented preserved
  plum snacks (these are documented as made predominantly from the
  distinct related species excluded from this entry's scope, rather than
  from this species).

No religious or ritual food use was identified for this crop in the
sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- Chilling-requirement figures conflict somewhat across three different
  source studies (a Spanish multi-cultivar trial, a general figure for
  Chinese cultivars, and a specific figure for one widely grown Western
  early cultivar) — not fully reconciled here, though all agree this
  species sits at the low end of stone-fruit chilling needs.
- The derived harvest labor-hour figure (Finding 5) may understate true
  labor input if the underlying cost-study figure reflects piece-rate
  pay rather than a direct hourly wage — not resolved here.
- Several agronomic parameters (water-use-by-canopy-light-interception
  modeling, some labor-hour tiers, several soil-nutrient-uptake figures)
  are estimated by analogy to this crop's closely related European plum
  sibling crop or to peach, rather than sourced directly for this
  species — a real source of uncertainty given this species' documented
  differences in chilling requirement and climate range.
- Waterlogging tolerance is estimated from rootstock choice (a
  peach-derived rootstock implying lower tolerance) rather than directly
  measured for this species; actual tolerance is likely rootstock-
  dependent and varies by growing region.

## Estimates and derivations

The growing-season-length range (bloom to harvest) is derived from a
documented California bloom timing and a documented harvest-date range
across cultivars in that growing region.

Yield tiers are derived from a national average figure for the largest-
growing-area country (used as the average/baseline, since that country
accounts for the majority of world production), county-average yield
figures from a multi-year California dataset (used as "good"), a
calculated packable-yield figure from a well-run California study
orchard (used as "exceptional"), and an estimate below the national
average reflecting a frost or poor-fruit-set year (used as "poor").

Water-use parameters (seasonal crop coefficient, stage lengths, rooting
depth, soil-moisture-depletion allowance) are derived from a standard
international crop-evapotranspiration reference's general stone-fruit
coefficients, cross-checked against documented consumptive-use and
applied-irrigation figures from a California orchard-cost study; several
stress-response and water-management details are drawn directly from a
dedicated water-yield-response reference for this specific crop. Where
no crop-specific figure exists (carbon-uptake rate, growth-stage curve,
drought-day tolerance, waterlogging tolerance, several soil-nutrient-
uptake figures), values are estimated by analogy to the European plum
sibling crop, reflecting their shared genus, canopy structure, and fruit
type.

Labor-hour estimates for pruning/thinning and harvest are derived
directly from a California cost study's per-acre labor-dollar figures at
a stated hourly wage rate, converted to hours and then to a per-hectare
basis; post-harvest handling labor is estimated by analogy to the
European plum sibling crop, since packing is contracted out to a
separate facility in the California study and no grower-side hours were
given for that operation.

Storage duration is derived as a safe middle figure between the
documented under-four-week range for chilling-injury-prone cultivars and
the over-five-week range for better-storing cultivars.

Yield-profile parameters (peak daily yield, standing time before
quality decline) are derived from the "good" yield tier spread across a
short effective harvest window, and from a qualitative comparison to the
shorter ambient shelf life documented for Chinese-grown cultivars versus
a long-storing imported cultivar.
