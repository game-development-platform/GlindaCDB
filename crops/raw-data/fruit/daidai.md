# Daidai (*Citrus* × *aurantium*, daidai group)

## Naming and scope

Daidai (橙, also written 代々; Chinese 代代 / 酸橙 / 回青橙) is the East
Asian branch of bitter orange: the lineage that spread east from the
species' area of origin and differentiated in China into its own cultivar
group, as distinct from the lineage that went west and became the
Mediterranean sour/Seville orange [1]. This entry covers cropID `daidai`
and both of the old Japanese cultivars that are jointly sold under that
name, **kabusu** (臭橙, *shūtō*) and **kaiseitō** (回青橙, also
*za-daidai* or *hon-daidai*), plus the Chinese flower-producing strain of
kaiseitō (*hana-daidai*, the 代代花 of Chinese tea and medicine) [1].

**The scientific name is unsettled, and is not resolved here.** At least
five treatments are in current use: *Citrus* × *daidai* Siebold ex Hayata
(a general encyclopedia's article title, flagged there as an unresolved
name) [2]; a synonym of *Citrus* × *aurantium* f. *aurantium* (Kew's
Plants of the World Online, i.e. no separate rank at all) [2]; *Citrus
aurantium* L. var. *daidai* Makino (the form used for the Japanese
Pharmacopoeia crude drugs) [8]; *Citrus aurantium* L. var. *cyathifera*
Y. Tanaka (used in the peel-oil chemistry literature) [13]; and *Citrus
aurantium* 'Daidai', equated with *C. aurantium* var. *amara* Engl., in
Chinese sources [11]. One germplasm collection files its "Daidai double
calyx sour orange" accession under yet another name, *Citrus taiwanica*
[14]. The status-table row uses the species name plus "daidai group"
rather than picking a variety name.

**Why this is its own row (significance check, 2026-11-16).**
`bitter-orange.md` proposed daidai as a candidate on the strength of "its
own distinct cultural/medicinal use pattern." Fuller research confirms and
strengthens that reasoning on four independent grounds, the same
product-and-market standard this project applied to `chinotto`:

1. *A different product economy.* The main commercial product is an
   intact, leaf-on ripe fruit sold as a New Year ornament, with juice for
   ponzu as the outlet for whatever the ornament trade rejects (Finding
   2). Mainland bitter orange's economy is rootstock seed, neroli, and
   marmalade peel; none of those is daidai's primary market.
2. *A different defining trait.* Ripe fruit does not drop, hangs for two
   to three years, and regreens every summer. That trait is the source of
   the name, the symbolism, and the ornament market (Finding 1).
3. *Measurably different fruit and tree.* A Japanese fruit-industry
   profile describes daidai and European sour orange side by side as
   separate types: daidai fruit is larger (about 200 g vs. 150 g), seedier,
   more acid, and the tree is said to be hardier (Finding 3) [1].
4. *Its own measurable industry and regulatory identity.* It is tracked
   as a separate fruit in Japanese production statistics (about 68 ha and
   585 t in 2023) [4], has its own line in the national food composition
   tables [12], and is the named botanical source of two Japanese
   Pharmacopoeia crude drugs [8]. In China a strain of it is a dedicated
   flower-bud crop for scented tea and medicine (Finding 5).

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Mediterranean sour/Seville orange** and the species-level material
  (rootstock history, citrus tristeza epidemics, neroli economics,
  synephrine supplement safety): `bitter_orange`, see `bitter-orange.md`.
  The neroli cultivar 'Bouquet' ('Bouquet de Fleurs'), described alongside
  daidai in [1], is a European sour-orange cultivar and belongs there as
  a cultivar note; it is summarized under Cultivars below only for
  contrast.
- **Natsudaidai / natsumikan** (*Citrus natsudaidai*), a separate Japanese
  citrus whose name merely contains "daidai" and which turned up
  repeatedly in this research (as a marmalade comparison [7], and as a
  close genetic relative of kabusu in one study [16]). It has no row in
  this project yet. Proposed as its own candidate row.
- **Hetsuka daidai**, a local citrus of Kagoshima that one study found to
  carry pomelo-derived cytoplasm and to be closely related to kabusu,
  natsudaidai and kimikan, with its own flavonoid/coumarin/synephrine
  profile and lower fruit weight and juice acidity than natsudaidai [16].
  Despite the name it appears to be a distinct local variety rather than a
  daidai cultivar. Proposed as its own candidate row.
- **Chinotto** (`chinotto`) and **laraha** (still `PROPOSED`), the other
  two variety-level candidates raised in `bitter-orange.md`.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — ripe fruit stays on the tree for years and regreens every
summer, and this single trait created both the name and the market.**
Mature fruit does not drop; a tree carries fruit of the current and
previous seasons at once, which is the stated origin of the name (代々,
"generation after generation") [1][2][3]. Sources put the persistence at
two to three years [3][10][17]. Rind color starts in early November, is
essentially complete by late December, keeps reddening until about
February, then turns green again and recolors the following autumn [1].
Sources disagree on when the regreening shows: "spring" [2][5][7], "the
next summer" [3], or becoming conspicuous from about June [1]. The same
phenomenon is documented in late-held Valencia orange, where a 2023
review attributes it to chromoplasts reverting to chloroplast-like
plastids, with chlorophyll-synthesis genes up-regulated and carotenoid
genes down-regulated, promoted by gibberellin, high temperature and blue
light [15]; that review does not discuss daidai specifically, so applying
its mechanism here is an inference. The cultivar name kaiseitō (回青橙,
"returning-green orange") records the trait directly [1][4]. The fruit is
not usable indefinitely, though: juice declines from February and the
pulp dries out (granulates) in spring (Finding 4), so a two-year-old
fruit is an ornament, not a juice source.

**Finding 2 — the primary product is a decoration, and food use is
largely the salvage stream.** For most of its history in Japan daidai was
a dooryard tree with "almost no commercial value" beyond New Year
ornaments, where it tops the *kagami mochi* and hangs on *shimekazari* as
a symbol of family continuity [1][2]. Ornament fruit is cut in early to
mid December, timed to wreath-making, on a 5-7 cm twig carrying 3-5
leaves [1]; about 74% of one year's volume at the Tokyo central wholesale
market moves in December [5]. The ornament trade drives orchard practice
in ways that run against normal fruit-growing logic: large fruit is
*unwanted*, so heavy thinning is counterproductive, and where two fruits
set side by side one is removed so the other can be cut with its leaves
[1]. Juice processing for ponzu takes fruit that is off-grade for
ornaments or that the ornament trade cannot absorb, and the fresh
"vinegar citrus" trade to restaurants takes mainly the oversized fruit
from late October, restaurants preferring it still uncolored [1]. With
ornament demand falling, more of the crop now goes to ponzu [3]. In parts
of Japan (Miyagi is named) fruit dropping off the twig is considered
unlucky, so the fruit is pinned on with a skewer [1].

**Finding 3 — measured side by side, daidai is not simply "Japanese
bitter orange."** The fruit-industry profile describes four types within
the group [1]:

| Type | Fruit weight | Rind | Acid | Seeds | Calyx |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kabusu | ~200 g | 6-7 mm, medium texture | ~5% | ~30 | normal |
| Kaiseitō | ~200 g | 7-8 mm, rougher | "high" | ~20 | thickened |
| European sour orange | ~150 g | 6-7 mm, smoother | 4.5-5% | fewer | normal |
| 'Bouquet' | ~130 g | 4-5 mm, smooth | ~4% | ~6 | — |

All four are polyembryonic [1]. European sour orange is described as more
upright and *less* cold-hardy than kabusu [1]. Kaiseitō's thickened
calyx, which keeps swelling on the tree even if the young fruit drops, is
the mark that separates it from kabusu and the source of the name
*za-daidai* ("daidai on a seat") [1][2]; a germplasm collection's daidai
accession likewise notes the calyx remaining on the tree after fruit fall
[14]. Kaiseitō's weight is disputed: about 200 g [1], 130-180 g [4], or
20-150 g in one consumer source [4]. Kabusu colors earlier and has a
smoother rind, making it the better ornament and marmalade fruit, but in
practice the two are grown intermixed (including as seedling trees) and
shipped mixed, and ornament makers reportedly do not object [1].

**Finding 4 — juice content has a sharp seasonal peak that does not
coincide with the main harvest for the main product.** Fruit enlarges
rapidly through summer and autumn and nearly stops in December, with a
second growth phase after April [1]. Juice starts accumulating in August,
reaches about 20% of fruit weight by late October, and peaks from late
November through January; it then declines from February, followed by
drying-out of the pulp. Acid is about 5% during the winter peak and falls
quickly as the pulp dries in spring [1]. So the restaurant trade's
late-October green fruit is cut at roughly 20% juice, well before the
peak, and the December ornament harvest happens to fall at peak juice,
which is what makes diverting ornament rejects to ponzu workable.

**Finding 5 — in China a strain of the same cultivar is a flower crop,
not a fruit crop.** The Chinese perfumery/tea daidai (*hana-daidai*) is
identified as a strain of kaiseitō, and its flowers are used to scent tea
[1]. Chinese sources treat 代代花 as the dried flower *buds* of *C.
aurantium* 'Daidai', a "medicine-food homologous" material [11], picked
in batches in May-June before opening and dried promptly, by one account
over a fierce fire to 70-80% dry and then a slow one [11]. Producing
areas named are Suzhou and Yangzhou (Jiangsu), Jinhua (Zhejiang) and
Fujian [9][11]. For scented tea the fresh flower is used: its petals are
thick and release fragrance only when warm, so scenting involves repeated
gentle heating, unlike jasmine [9]; one general account gives 20-40 kg of
fresh flowers per 100 kg of base tea for daidai and similar flowers [18].
Outside its southern growing areas it is a pot plant, moved indoors above
0 °C for winter [9][10]. No tonnage, area or per-tree flower yield was
found.

**Finding 6 — a wartime supply gap once made this an export crop, and
the industry never really grew again.** When the First World War cut off
European sour orange from North American marmalade makers, Japan began
exporting daidai to the US and Canada in 1914, several thousand 40-pound
boxes a year, and considerable new planting followed in the mid-Taishō
years; by the late 1930s it had lost competitiveness, lost the market,
and declined [1]. Area was a little under 100 ha from the mid-1960s to
about 1980, 96 ha in 2003 with almost no new planting, and about 68 ha in
2023 [1][4]. In 2003 nearly half of the recorded area was scattered trees
rather than orchard [1].

**Finding 7 — it is the named source of two Japanese pharmacopoeial
drugs, taken from the same tree at opposite ends of fruit development.**
*Tōhi* (橙皮) is the peel of the ripe fruit, an aromatic bitter stomachic
and the raw material of bitter tincture, orange-peel tincture and
orange-peel syrup [1][3][8]. *Kijitsu* (枳実) is the unripe fruit, whole
or halved crosswise, used as a stomachic, expectorant and laxative
[2][3][8]. This mirrors the Chinese *zhi shi* / *zhi ke* split documented
in `bitter-orange.md`, but here the pharmacopoeial source is named at the
variety level [8]. One Chinese entry describes the halved unripe fruit as
3-4 cm across, harvested in July-August [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree, thorny, 4-5 m as a
  seedling, vigorous and upright, becoming near-cylindrical with age; on
  trifoliate orange rootstock it is slow-growing, spreading and compact
  [1][3]. Chinese pot-culture sources describe a 2-5 m shrub [10].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; one crop per year, cut at
  different times according to destination (Harvest stages, below).
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 3 years after grafting, and
  likewise about 3 years after transplanting a rooted cutting, in Chinese
  pot-culture guidance [10]. Not sourced for orchard trees or seedlings;
  the Japanese profile says only that trifoliate-rootstock trees are slow
  to fill their space, so large nursery trees and planned high-density
  planting are advised to shorten the unproductive period [1].
- **Climate zones:** Grown scattered across Japan's satsuma belt and into
  warmer districts [1]; hardiness zones 9-11 in one general reference
  [3]. Main Japanese districts are the Izu Peninsula (Atami, Shizuoka)
  and Tanabe (Wakayama) [3][6]. In China, outdoors in Jiangsu/Zhejiang/
  Fujian and pot-grown further north [9][10]. Chill requirement: none
  sourced, and none expected for an evergreen citrus. Elevation: Not
  sourced; the one district described is seaside hillside [6].
- **Composition:** Juice only, from Japan's national food composition
  tables (current edition, item 07083, read at search-snippet depth): 35
  kcal, water 91.2 g, protein 0.3 g, fat 0.2 g, carbohydrate 8.0 g,
  vitamin C 35 mg, potassium 190 mg, magnesium 10 mg, folate 13 µg per
  100 g [12]. Sites still reproducing an older edition give 24 kcal [12];
  the difference is between editions of the same table, not between
  independent measurements. Acid about 5% in winter juice [1]. Whole-fruit
  and peel nutrition: Not sourced. Peel oil is about 92.5-94.7% limonene
  [13].
- **Edibility:** Not eaten fresh (too sour, peel bitter) [1][2]. Juice is
  used raw as a vinegar; peel is cooked into marmalade or dried as a drug
  [1][3].
- **Pollination:** Pollen is fertile and the tree self-compatible; fruit
  are normally all seeded and the type is thought to have no
  parthenocarpy [1]. A species-level gap in `bitter-orange.md` that this
  source fills for the daidai group.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for daidai. By citrus-genus
  analogy (see `bitter-orange.md` and `chinotto.md`), flowering is driven
  by cool temperature and water deficit rather than day length. Not
  applicable to tuberization/bulbing.
- **Yield:** Not sourced per tree or per hectare. National totals: about
  585 t from about 68 ha in 2023, and about 514 t shipped in 2024 [4],
  which implies roughly 8.6 t/ha (see Estimates, below). Alternate
  bearing is comparatively weak [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Described as resistant to both drought and
  excess moisture [1], while Chinese pot-culture guidance says it likes
  moisture but dislikes waterlogging [9][10]; the two are not reconciled
  (orchard trees vs. containers may explain it).
- **Propagation:** Grafted on trifoliate orange (*Poncirus trifoliata*)
  in both Japan and China [1][10]; in China also on sour orange or daidai
  seedlings, grafted late April to early May on 2-3-year-old stock, or
  raised from cuttings of 1-2-year-old wood taken June to early July,
  rooting in about two months in 60% loam / 40% sand under shade [10].
  Seed is polyembryonic and seedling trees are common in Japanese
  districts, often planted as windbreaks around satsuma orchards [1].
  Clonal/viral risk: susceptible to citrus tristeza virus [1][3]; one
  germplasm accession was found in 1985 to carry exocortis and seedling
  yellows, both removed by shoot-tip grafting [14], the standard route to
  clean stock. Daidai was itself identified in 1928 as "one of the most
  commonly used Japanese stocks" [14], and one produce-trade source
  calls it the primary rootstock for satsuma [17]; that is not what the
  Japanese profile describes today (trifoliate is the general stock) [1],
  so the rootstock role looks historical.
- **Labor:** Not sourced in hours. No dedicated spray calendar or
  fertilizer standard exists; growers spray it along with neighboring
  satsuma or amanatsu and fertilize as for mid/late-season citrus [1].
- **Soil:** Well-drained, organic-rich, pH 6.0-7.5 in one cultivation
  guide [5]; slightly acid sandy loam in Chinese guidance [9][10].
  Salinity tolerance: Not sourced for daidai (the species-level rootstock
  salinity data is in `bitter-orange.md`; trees here are mostly on
  trifoliate stock, so it would not transfer).
- **Storage (postharvest):** Keeps comparatively well because of the
  thick rind, but flavor falls as it dries; bag it against drying and
  hold cool and dark or refrigerated [4]. No temperature/humidity regime
  or storage-life figure found. On the tree, juice quality ends with
  spring drying-out (Finding 4). Peel oil held warm loses germacrene D,
  myrcene, linalyl acetate and limonene [13].
- **Establishment:** Plant March-April or September-October, spring in
  colder districts [5] (one nursery advises spring wherever winter minima
  go below -5 °C [19]); hole about 50 × 50 cm; pots of 30-40 cm or more
  [5]. Train on trifoliate stock to an open-centre form with three main
  limbs and prune as for satsuma, opening the canopy because fruit sets
  on the outside [1]. Orchard spacing: Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** Kabusu and kaiseitō (Finding 3). Variegated chimeras of
  each, 'fuiri-shūtō' (from kabusu) and 'shima-daidai' (from kaiseitō),
  are grown as ornamentals [1]; a "kiku-daidai" form is also listed [4].
  *Hana-daidai*, the Chinese flower strain, is a line of kaiseitō
  (Finding 5) [1]. 'Bouquet', by contrast, is a European sour-orange
  cultivar: weak, spreading, thornless, about 3 m, densely flowering,
  grown for neroli and orange-flower water, introduced to Japan from the
  US in 1903 and not grown commercially there [1].
- **Weather response:** Comparatively tolerant of both cold and heat [1];
  tolerates about 0 °C, with prolonged cold slowing growth and lowering
  fruit quality [5]; hardier than jasmine or *Michelia* in Chinese pot
  culture, overwintered above 0 °C [9][10]. Fruit cold damage is not a
  practical concern because the crop is off the tree by year-end [1].
  Wind is the real weather hazard: rind scarring ruins ornament and fresh
  grades, and strong wind snaps the fruit stalk [1]. Frost as a quality
  event: Not sourced. (Rind color develops through the cold months and
  reverses with summer heat, Finding 1, but no source ties quality to
  frost itself.)
- **Water regime:** Ordinary rain-fed/irrigated orchard crop; no flooded
  requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Three destination-specific windows from
  one crop: late October onward for fresh "vinegar citrus" (large fruit,
  preferably uncolored); early to mid December for ornaments (leaf-on
  twigs); fully colored fruit for marmalade [1]. One district reports
  harvest beginning in late November with full ripeness around January
  [6]. For the drugs, unripe fruit in summer (July-August in one Chinese
  account) [9]; for the Chinese flower crop, buds in May-June [11].
  Flowering is May-June, at about the same time as early satsuma [1][3].
  Because unpicked fruit persists, a tree carries more than one season's
  fruit at once; this is persistence rather than successive ripening
  within a season.
- **Thinning:** Poorly defined; a leaf-to-fruit ratio of about 80-100
  leaves per fruit is used, with damaged, misshapen and undersized fruit
  removed first, and restraint advised because ornament buyers do not
  want large fruit (Finding 2) [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** An introduced plant throughout its
  cultivated range in Japan (it arrived from China) [1]. No invasive
  listing was found for daidai itself in this pass. At species level,
  bitter orange has escaped cultivation into forests and old homesites
  in peninsular Florida, where one university assessment of non-native
  plants rates it a "caution" species (manage to prevent escape) while
  noting no documented negative impacts, and a global invasive-species
  database is reported as holding no reference listing it as invasive
  in North America [20]. Seedling trees are common in Japanese districts
  [1], but no account of spontaneous spread there was found. Movement
  of planting material is restricted in part of Japan: citrus nursery
  stock (including trifoliate orange and kumquat) may leave the areas
  regulated for citrus greening disease and its psyllid vector (Okinawa,
  the Amami and Tokara islands, and the Ogasawara islands are the
  regulated areas named) only after passing a plant-protection
  inspection, which an older notice says can take a year or more; seed
  and fruit are excluded from that restriction [21]. The current list of
  regulated islands has changed over time (one island was declared free
  of the pathogen in 2024) and was not checked against the regulation
  text [21].
- **Controlled environment:** Long-established as a container plant in
  China, where it is grown in pots throughout the north and the middle
  and lower Yangtze and wintered indoors [9][10]; potting mix of garden
  soil or leaf mould, yellow clay and rice-husk ash at 3:1:1, repotted
  every one to two years [9]. No hydroponic or closed-system work found.

## Hazards

- **Citrus tristeza virus:** susceptible [1][3]; see `bitter-orange.md`
  Finding 2 for the species-level history. Resistant to citrus scab and
  fairly resistant to canker [1].
- **Pests and other diseases:** melanose, citrus red mite, rust mite and
  scale insects are the spray targets in Japan [1]; leaf spot and cottony
  cushion scale (with sooty mould) in Chinese pot culture, both worst in
  hot, humid, poorly ventilated conditions [9]. No outbreak-severity
  figures found.
- **Synephrine:** peel and fruit contain synephrine [3]; the contested
  safety of concentrated extracts is covered in `bitter-orange.md`
  Finding 6 and applies to the drug/supplement uses here, not to ponzu or
  marmalade. No daidai-specific synephrine content figure was found.
- **Fungal contamination:** nothing found in this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (ripe fruit → New Year ornament):** fully colored fruit cut
  early to mid December on a 5-7 cm twig with 3-5 leaves → wreath and
  *kagami mochi* makers → retail at year-end [1]. Byproduct: off-grade
  and surplus fruit, which feeds Chain 2.
- **Chain 2 (fruit → juice → ponzu):** off-grade/surplus ornament fruit,
  or fruit grown for juice (color and sugar matter little, so shadier
  sites will do) → pressed juice, used as a vinegar and blended with soy
  sauce as ponzu [1][3][7]. Home-made ponzu is rested several days under
  refrigeration and keeps some months [5]. Juice yield from pressing: Not
  sourced beyond the roughly 20% juice content in late October and the
  late-November-to-January peak (Finding 4).
- **Chain 3 (fresh "vinegar citrus"):** large, uncolored fruit from late
  October → restaurants, for hot-pot dishes [1].
- **Chain 4 (ripe peel → marmalade; historic export):** fully colored
  fruit → marmalade [1]; the basis of the 1914-1930s export trade to
  North America (Finding 6).
- **Chain 5 (ripe peel → *tōhi*):** peel dried → aromatic bitter
  stomachic; bitter tincture, peel tincture, peel syrup [1][3]. Folk use:
  1-2 g of powdered peel after meals [3].
- **Chain 6 (unripe fruit → *kijitsu*):** unripe fruit whole or halved
  crosswise → dried [8][9].
- **Chain 7 (flower buds → dried 代代花; fresh flowers → scented tea):**
  buds picked in batches May-June and dried [11]; fresh flowers scent tea
  with repeated gentle heating [9]. Hydrosols and essential oil are also
  made from the flowers [11].
- **Chain 8 (peel → cold-pressed oil):** limonene-dominant oil [13]; one
  Japanese district markets oil from fruit, leaves and flowers in an
  aroma product line [6].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** local processed goods (dressing,
  miso, pepper seasoning, sweets, liqueur) [6]; raw juice on chapped skin
  [3]; rind once smoked as a mosquito repellent, a proposed origin of the
  name kabusu [1]; windbreak, street and garden tree [1]; variegated
  forms as ornamentals [1].

## Open research questions

- **Production shares conflict.** One source ranks 2023 harvest as
  Shizuoka about 212 t (36%), Wakayama about 136 t (23%), Hiroshima about
  115 t (20%) [4]; another calls Wakayama the largest at about 37%, with
  Shizuoka and Kagoshima next [5]. The 2003 area split (Shizuoka 38%,
  Wakayama 23%, then Fukuoka, Hiroshima, Ehime) [1] agrees with the
  first. The government survey tables themselves were located but not
  opened.
- **No yield figure** per tree or per hectare; the one in Estimates is a
  national average over an area that includes scattered trees.
- **Kaiseitō fruit weight** (about 200 g vs. 130-180 g vs. 20-150 g) and
  **regreening onset** (spring vs. June vs. summer) are not reconciled.
- **Origin** is given as Assam/northeast India [1][7], the Himalayas
  [2][3], or (one Chinese entry) Zhejiang [9]; date of arrival in Japan is
  unknown, with a legendary attribution and a first clear record put
  either in 1631 [4] or in the late Muromachi spread of the name [1].
- **Genetics.** A marker study lists kabusu simply as sour orange of
  unknown Japanese origin [16]; no study was found testing whether kabusu
  and kaiseitō are clonal variants of one another or of European sour
  orange.
- **Chinese flower crop:** no area, tonnage, per-tree flower yield, or
  oil yield; the tea-scenting ratio [18] is a general figure for a class
  of flowers, not a daidai-specific standard.
- **Pharmacopoeia wording** was read only through secondary material; in
  particular, whether *kijitsu* also admits other species as sources was
  not confirmed.
- **The rootstock claim** in a produce-trade source (primary satsuma
  rootstock) [17] conflicts with the Japanese profile [1] and is probably
  historical; not resolved.
- Invasiveness is sourced only at species level and only for Florida;
  the Japanese movement restriction was read from summaries, not the
  regulation.
- Photoperiod, salinity, elevation, water use, labor hours, orchard
  spacing, storage regime, pressed-juice yield, and whole-fruit and peel
  composition were all not sourced.
- Whether `natsudaidai` and `hetsuka_daidai` warrant rows (Naming and
  scope) needs a decision.

## Estimates and derivations

**Average national yield, about 8.6 t/ha.** 585 t harvested from 68 ha in
2023 [4]: 585 ÷ 68 = 8.6 t/ha. This is a floor for a managed orchard, not
an orchard yield: in 2003 nearly half the recorded area was scattered
trees [1], many are unmanaged seedling windbreaks [1], and fruit is
deliberately not thinned for size (Finding 2). If only half the area were
producing orchard and it carried most of the crop, orchard yield would
approach 17 t/ha; a plausible range is therefore roughly 8-17 t/ha. A
prefectural experiment-station yield trial or a growers' cooperative
standard would replace this.

**Approximate fruit count, about 2.9 million a year.** 585 t [4] ÷ 0.2 kg
per fruit [1] = 2.9 million fruit. Using the lighter kaiseitō figures
(0.13-0.18 kg) [4] gives 3.3-4.5 million. Not all are sold as ornaments;
the split between ornament, juice and fresh use was not sourced.
