## Naming and scope

*Chaenomeles japonica* (Thunb.) Lindl. (Rosaceae, subfamily Maloideae):
Japanese quince, dwarf Japanese quince, Maule's quince [1]. It is a dwarf
shrub of 0.6-1.2 m, native to central and southern Japan at 100-2,100 m,
on hillsides, riverbanks and lakeshores [1]. The genus is most closely
related to *Cydonia* (quince), *Docynia*, *Malus* (apple) and *Pyrus*
(pear) [1].

**The genus:** four species are recognised [1]:
- *C. japonica* (this entry);
- *C. speciosa*, flowering quince, a 2-5 m shrub with fruit up to 140 g;
- *C. cathayensis*, a shrub or small tree to 6 m with fruit of about 180
  g, occasionally 600 g;
- *C. thibetica*, a 1.5-3 m shrub.

Four ornamental hybrid groups also exist, including *C.* × *superba*
(*japonica* × *speciosa*) [1]. More than 500 ornamental cultivars have been
bred [1].

**Why *C. japonica* alone:** it is the species best adapted to the North
European climate [4], and the only one bred as a fruit crop in the Baltic
region [1][4]. Its fruit ripens earliest in the genus, at the end of
August in the Baltic. The fruit of *C. speciosa*, and especially *C.
cathayensis*, does not normally ripen there [1].

**Separate entries:**
- **Quince** (*Cydonia oblonga*): see `quince.md`. It is a different
  genus: a self-fertile tree crop of warmer climates, with fruit that can
  soften.
- **Mugua** (*C. speciosa*): listed in `medicinal-status.md`. Fruit
  collected from wild Chinese *C. speciosa*, and possibly *C. cathayensis*
  and *C. thibetica*, has long been used medicinally in China [1][6].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a Baltic processing crop that collapsed and then revived.**
- **Start:** Latvian research began in 1951, and the first large
  plantations were set up in the 1970s [1].
- **1993 peak:** Latvia had about 300 ha, with an average yield of 12-15
  t/ha and a maximum of 20-30 t/ha [1]. Latvia and Lithuania together had
  more than 400 ha [1].
- **Collapse:** seed-grown plantations were very variable, which made
  management difficult and products poor. With cheap imported juice
  concentrates after independence, "the interest in growing Japanese
  quince dropped drastically" [1].
- **Breeding:** a joint Latvian-Lithuanian-Swedish breeding programme
  began in 1992 and linked with a Finnish project in 1998 [1]. Its
  EU-funded project (FAIR CT97-3894) produced the 2003 report used
  heavily here [4][15].
- **Revival:** the Latvian area reached about 650 ha in 2020 and more
  than 700 ha by 2023 [18]. Another source gives 723 ha in 2022 [18]. The
  original pass also reported a recent roughly fourfold expansion, a
  meaningful organic share, and spread into Poland, Lithuania, Estonia
  and Sweden [3][5].
- **Current research:** a 2017-2020 Latvian EU-funded project developed
  environment-friendly growing, plant protection, storage and waste-free
  processing methods for the crop [21].

**Finding 2 — strongly self-incompatible, with low fruit set.**
- **Self-incompatibility:** a strong gametophytic system. In
  self-pollinations, pollen tubes grow slowly and often stop in the style
  [8].
- **Cross-pollination:** in compatible crosses, pollen tubes reach the
  ovary in 2-5 days and fertilisation follows 4-8 days after pollination.
  The effective pollination period is 3-5 days [8].
- **Fruit set:** initial set averaged 21.1% (range 0-83.3%) and final set
  10.4% (range 0-37.9%) [8].
- **Defective flowers:** 55-65% of flowers had visibly defective pistils
  on average, and over 70% in one genotype [8].
- **Pollinators:** honeybees and bumblebees, drawn to nectar-rich but
  scentless flowers [1].
- **Planting rule:** at least three varieties per orchard. Either plant a
  pollinator in every fifth row, or plant 2-4 rows of each variety in turn
  [4].
- **Self-fertile selections:** breeders were reported to have two
  promising self-fertile candidates [5].

**Finding 3 — a very acid juice fruit, and two anomalies in its
analysis.** Juice from *C. japonica* across five sites [12]:
- **Acidity:** pH 2.5-2.7; titratable acidity 3.2-4.0% as citric acid.
  Lemon juice is 5-9% and apple juice 0.2-0.7% [12].
- **Malic acid:** 3.06-5.09 g/100 ml, against about 0.5 in apple juice
  [12].
- **Soluble solids:** 7.0-9.0 °Brix, similar to lemon juice [12].
- **Sugars:** fructose up to 2.3 g/100 ml, glucose up to 1.1 and sorbitol
  up to 0.5, across the genus [12].
- **Vitamin C:** 45-79 mg/100 ml in *C. japonica*. HPLC found no ascorbic
  acid itself, suggesting it is all in the dehydroascorbic form [12].
- **Phenols:** 210-460 mg/100 ml [12].
- **Potassium:** 153-241 mg/100 ml, similar to citrus [12].

The two anomalies:
- **Vitamin C conflict:** the processing trials report 102 mg/100 ml in
  fresh juice [2], and a review cites 114-169 mg/100 g from a Lithuanian
  study [19]. These are higher than the 45-79 above.
- **Fluoride:** the juice chapter reports 43-139 mg/100 ml. Orange juice
  contains 0.11-0.19 mg/100 ml [12]. That is several hundred times higher
  and is not discussed in the source. It may be an analytical artefact,
  but that is an inference (Hazards).

**Finding 4 — seeds are a tenth of the fruit and are cyanogenic.**
- **Seed share:** seeds make up up to 10% of fresh fruit weight [2], or
  about 10% by the nutrient analysis [4]. The original pass gave 4-10%
  [2][3].
- **Off-taste:** crushed seeds give juice an off-taste, so fruit is halved
  and deseeded before pressing [2].
- **Cyanogenic compounds:** intact seeds contain amygdalin, with total
  cyanogenic compounds equal to 690 mg HCN per kg of seed [17]. A review
  cites figures of 0.3 and 0.7 mg/g [19].
- **Seed oil:** neither solvent-extracted nor pressed oil contained
  amygdalin or its breakdown products [17]. Deoiled seed meal still held
  310-590 mg HCN-equivalent/kg, depending on method [17].
- **Oil content:** 6.1-16.8% of seed dry weight, averaging 8.2%. Seeds
  hold 40-46% moisture [13]. The oil is 89% unsaturated, mainly linoleic
  (up to 53%) and oleic acid [13]. The original pass reported a different
  oil share and rich tocopherol, carotenoid, sterol and squalene contents
  from a Latvian yearbook article [3]; that was not re-opened.

**Finding 5 — hardy and early, protected by snow and a long bloom.**
- **Dormancy:** plants have a short dormancy and start growing very early
  [4].
- **Bloom:** at Dobele, Latvia, flowering began between 28 April and 30
  May across 1999-2001, and lasted 15-22 days [8]. A frost of -3.5 °C on
  2 May 2000 damaged some pistils, but "only slightly decreased the total
  number of functional flowers" because flowering is long [8].
- **Winter:** young plants are vulnerable in cold winters below -20 °C
  with unstable snow cover, so spring planting is recommended there [4].
  The original pass gave about -25 °C hardiness when dormant [7].
- **Snow cover:** Baltic material is not perfectly adapted. Growers have
  compensated by selecting spreading plants that snow cover protects.
  Cold winters with little snow defeat this [16].
- **Ripening:** starts in mid-August and peaks in mid-September in the
  Baltic and southern Scandinavia [16]. Early varieties are preferred
  where autumn frost comes early [4].

**Finding 6 — hand picking sets the economics.** A 2003 Swedish cost
model for organic, plastic-mulched, irrigated, fenced orchards gives the
following [15]:
- **Planting:** 3 m between rows, with 2,222, 3,333 or 4,444 plants/ha.
- **Yield build-up:** first significant yield in season 3 (1 kg/plant),
  rising 1 kg a year to 6 kg in season 8, then 4-6 kg/plant.
- **Hand picking:** at 50 kg per person-hour, break-even needed a fruit
  price of 6 SEK/kg (about €0.67) at normal density.
- **Machine harvest:** an assumed harvester at 5,000 kg/hour would lower
  break-even to 2-3 SEK/kg (see Estimates, below).

The conclusion was that the fruit suits jam, syrup, aroma extracts and
liqueur. It is not competitive as a pectin source [15]. Mechanical
harvest remains a breeding goal; it needs erect plants whose fruit drops
easily [16].

**Finding 7 — a comparatively healthy plant, but grey mould is
everywhere.**
- **Grey mould:** *Botrytis cinerea* was very common in unsprayed Swedish
  fields. It appeared on shoots, flowers, fruit at all stages and stored
  fruit, and can infect unwounded shoots [9].
- **Fruit spots:** mainly *Septoria cydoniae*, *Phlyctema vagabunda*,
  *Phoma exigua* and *Entomosporium mespili* [9].
- **Resistance:** the genus seems not susceptible to scab or powdery
  mildew [1].
- **Recorded elsewhere:** fire blight (*Erwinia amylovora*) and apple
  chlorotic leaf spot virus [1].
- **Severity:** none of the fungi is "currently considered severe, unless
  fruits are stored for a longer period" [4]. Skin damage is tolerable
  because the fruit is processed [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Dwarf, spreading, thorny, suckering deciduous
  shrub, 0.6-1.2 m [1]. Flower buds form in late summer and autumn, in
  clusters of 1-6 on wood two years old or older [1]. Suckering is
  abundant in many genotypes, but is independent of yield [16].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial, one harvest season a year, from
  mid-August to a mid-September peak in the Baltic [16]. The number of
  passes is not sourced.
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - A small yield comes in season 2 and the first significant yield in
    season 3 [15].
  - Full yield arrives by season 8, after which plants are pruned and
    yield dips in season 9 [15].
  - Potted plants bear earlier than bare-root plants [4].
  - Orchard life is 15-20 years [4][15].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** cool-temperate and coastal in origin [1].
  - **Hardiness:** grown commercially in Latvia and Lithuania [1][18].
    Young plants are at risk below -20 °C without snow [4]. US extension
    sources rate the species USDA zones 5a-9b; another plant database
    gives zone 2 [20].
  - **Elevation:** 100-2,100 m in Japan [1].
  - **Chill:** not sourced; dormancy is short [4].
- **Composition:**
  - **Fruit:** about 4 cm and under 50 g, with up to 80 seeds [1].
    Breeding-trial averages ranged 27-211 g across the genus and hybrids
    [12].
  - **Juice:** see Finding 3 [12].
  - **Fibre:** 28-36 g per 100 g dry matter across genotypes, mostly
    pectic and cellulosic [11]. A review gives 3.9 g per 100 g fresh fruit
    [19].
  - **Dry matter:** about 11-12.5% (see Estimates, below) [11][19].
  - **Pectin:** 100 g of dry fruit (800 g fresh) holds 11 g pectins, 3 g
    hemicelluloses and 18 g cellulosic residue [11].
  - **Energy and protein:** not sourced. An estimate is about 30-40 kcal
    per 100 g (see Estimates, below).
- **Edibility:** Not eaten raw. The fruit does not soften and is too acid
  to eat fresh, so it is always processed [1][2]. It resists oxidative
  browning during processing [1]. Seeds are cyanogenic (Finding 4) [17].
- **Pollination:** Required; bee-pollinated; strongly self-incompatible;
  needs three varieties per orchard (Finding 2) [1][4][8].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Historic Latvia:** 12-15 t/ha on average, 20-30 t/ha maximum
    (1993) [1].
  - **Cost model:** 4-6 kg/plant, or about 8.9-26.7 t/ha (see Estimates,
    below) [15].
  - **Promising genotypes:** 8-10 kg/plant in breeding populations [15].
  - **Selection floor:** 3 kg/plant in season 4 and 4 kg in season 5
    [16].
  - **Poland:** 'Darius' averaged 1.94 kg/plant in 2022-2023 trials [18].
- **Water use:**
  - **Crop-specific study:** none found.
  - **Roots and drought:** the root system is widespread and deep, so
    plants grow in rather dry conditions [4].
  - **Planting:** late spring planting without irrigation can reduce
    establishment [4].
  - **Cost model:** assumes automatic drip irrigation and fertigation
    [15].
  - **Crop coefficient:** not sourced. The original pass borrowed a
    general berry coefficient; that has been dropped here as unsupported.
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** germinates 95-100% if not dried and stratified about a
    month at 2-4 °C. Commercial practice uses 2-3 months [1]. Seedlings
    are too variable for orchards [4].
  - **Softwood cuttings:** clones are propagated from 20 cm top cuttings
    soaked in 30 mg/l IBA for 18 hours [10].
  - **Hardwood cuttings:** 20 cm base cuttings soaked in 100 mg/l IBA for
    24 hours also root well [10].
  - **Micropropagation:** proliferation about 3-4 and rooting over 90%
    [10]. Rooting ability depends on genotype [10].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** apple chlorotic leaf spot virus has been
    recorded [1]. Stored nursery plants are very susceptible to grey mould
    and should be kept at -1 °C [4].
- **Labor:**
  - **Picking:** 40-50 kg per person-hour [15], or about 330-530 h/ha at
    model yields (see Estimates, below).
  - **Weeding:** mechanical weeding works poorly on dwarf, spreading
    plants, so hand weeding is needed for the first three years [4].
  - **Hours for other tasks:** not sourced.
- **Soil:**
  - **Type:** well-drained, acid mineral soil with some organic matter
    and some clay [4].
  - **pH:** below 6. Plants are prone to chlorosis, worse in dry alkaline
    soil. Sulphur or iron sulphate can acidify before planting [4].
  - **Before planting:** 60-100 t/ha of organic material on sandy or poor
    soils. P and K at 150-300 kg/ha, with potassium sulphate rather than
    chloride [4].
  - **Annual fertiliser:** 100-180 kg N and 140-260 kg K per hectare.
    Fruit removes about 8 kg N, 1.4 kg P and 12 kg K per tonne; the
    nitrogen requirement is 2-3 times apple's, because of the seeds [4].
  - **Magnesium:** deficiency appears below pH 5.5 on sand, or with excess
    potassium [4].
  - **Salinity:** not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** The skin bruises easily despite the firm flesh
  [4].
  - **At 1 °C:** fruit in 12-15 kg boxes keeps 2 months at 1 °C and at
    least 85% RH [4], or at most 9 weeks without significant quality loss
    [14]. After 3 months, juice yield falls and the skin browns [14].
  - **At 5 °C:** storage at 5 °C and 80% RH cuts this to 4 weeks [14].
  - **Ozone:** under study for storage, per the original pass [7].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** usually 2.5-3 m between rows and 0.6-0.9 m within rows
    [4], or about 3,700-6,700 plants/ha (see Estimates, below).
  - **Planting season:** spring or autumn. Autumn plantings need at least
    10 cm of peat, wood-chip or sawdust mulch [4].
  - **Weeds and ground cover:** perennial weeds must be removed before
    planting. Grass between rows is sown from year 2 [4].
  - **Mulch:** black or woven plastic mulch gave the best weed control,
    growth and yield, and keeps fruit off the soil [4].
  - **Fencing:** fencing against hares, rabbits and deer is assumed [4][15].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Registered in Latvia:** 'Rasa', from Latvian material; 'Darius' and
    'Rondo', from the EU programme [18].
  - **New:** 'Jānis' and 'Silvija' were submitted for registration in 2023
    [18].
  - **Rankings conflict:** an Estonian trial and Latvian reports rank
    'Darius' and 'Rondo' best for hardiness and yield. Another Latvian
    report gives 'Rasa' the largest fruit and highest yield [18].
  - **Ideotype:** erect, thornless, few suckers, early-ripening, with
    fruit that drops easily [16].
  - **Thorns:** mass selection had already reduced thorny plants to about
    4% [1].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Spring frost:** partly buffered by the long bloom (Finding 5) [4][8].
    A gentle sunny slope reduces frost risk [4].
  - **Winter cold:** without snow, cold harms young plants [4][16].
  - **Autumn frost:** favours early cultivars [4].
  - **Erosion:** the plant has been recommended for erosion-prone slopes
    [4].
- **Water regime:** Rainfed or drip-irrigated; well-drained soil required;
  no flooded requirement [4][15].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Ripeness signs:** no precise criteria exist. The skin turns yellow
    and sticky, seed coats turn dark brown, and the fruit becomes
    fragrant [4].
  - **During ripening:** density, soluble solids, acidity and vitamin C
    rise [14].
  - **Late cultivars:** they may not form a functional abscission layer,
    which hinders shake harvest [16].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Suckering:** freely suckering, forming thickets; in New Zealand it
    persists in abandoned gardens and suckers into pavements [20].
  - **Naturalisation:** New Zealand says it is not common as a fully
    naturalised plant. A gardening source says flowering quinces have
    naturalised in the eastern United States [20].
  - **Listings:** no official weed listing was found [20].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. Seedlings in a greenhouse were
  killed by a severe grey mould attack [1].

## Hazards

- **Cyanogenic seeds:** 690 mg HCN-equivalent per kg of seed (Finding 4)
  [17]. Deseeding before pressing already keeps seeds out of juice [2].
  Seed oil was free of amygdalin; seed meal was not [17].

  TO BE ADDRESSED: this project has no cyanogenic-glycoside hazard data
  structure yet. Flesh and juice carry no reported cyanogenic hazard.
- **Fluoride in juice:** a reported 43-139 mg/100 ml (Finding 3) [12].
  That would be far above drinking-water norms, but it has not been
  corroborated. It is flagged rather than modelled.
- **Thorns:** they make handling difficult and can damage fruit [16].
  Modern cultivars are selected thornless [16][18].
- **Plant-health:** grey mould, fruit-spot fungi, fire blight and apple
  chlorotic leaf spot virus [1][9]. Recorded pests include:
  - leaf weevils, *Yponomeuta* and *Operophtera* larvae, *Orgyia antiqua*
    and red spider mites in Sweden [1];
  - *Grapholita dimorpha*, pear slug, and root-knot nematode in China [1].
- **Fungal contamination of the product:** storage rots (*Botrytis*,
  *Phlyctema*, *Penicillium expansum*) [4]. No mycotoxin finding.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (juice):**
  1. Fruit is halved and deseeded.
  2. The flesh is crushed and pressed or centrifuged, for a juice yield of
     40-60% [2].
  3. Overnight pectolytic enzyme treatment raises the yield to 70%, but
     cuts vitamin C from 102 to 63 mg/100 ml [2].
  4. Juice withstands 90 °C for 10 minutes unchanged [2].
  5. It can be concentrated ninefold to 70 °Brix [2].
- **Chain 2 (syrup and liqueur):** 1 cm deseeded slices are covered with
  an equal weight of sugar and kept refrigerated for one day. The syrup
  flavours ice cream, soft drinks, lemonade and liqueur [2].
- **Chain 3 (preserves):**
  - **Candied slices:** 60 °Brix syrup for 2 hours at 60 °C under vacuum
    [2]. The original pass reported a patented Latvian candying process
    licensed to over a dozen companies [3].
  - **Canned halves:** in 20 °Brix juice-sugar with calcium; they kept 10
    months [2].
  - **Others:** a quince-paste-style conserve (1:1 sugar), jam, curd, and
    fruit in brine [2].
- **Chain 4 (by-products):**
  - **Seeds:** pressed for oil, leaving the cake [3][13].
  - **Pectin:** purified pectin at 0.5% of flour weight raised bread
    volume 7% [2].
  - **Aroma:** recovered by sugar extraction or pervaporation [2].
  - **Cores:** used in sauces [3].
- **Market products:** syrup, liqueur, carbonated soft drinks, marmalades
  and candies have been sold in Latvia and Lithuania [1]. A sparkling
  drink is exported, per the original pass [3].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** the Chinese medicinal dried fruit,
  from *C. speciosa* (see `mugua`) [6].

No religious or ritual use was found in the sources consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

**Plant density.** The 10,000 m² per hectare is divided by the space per
plant [4]:
- 3 × 0.9 m = 2.7 m² → 3,704 plants/ha;
- 2.5 × 0.6 m = 1.5 m² → 6,667 plants/ha.

The cost model's 2,222, 3,333 and 4,444 plants/ha correspond to 3 m rows
at 1.5, 1.0 and 0.75 m spacing [15].

**Yield per hectare** [15]:
- low: 4 kg × 2,222 = 8.9 t/ha;
- normal: 5 kg × 3,333 = 16.7 t/ha;
- high: 6 kg × 4,444 = 26.7 t/ha.

These bracket Latvia's 1993 average of 12-15 t/ha and maximum of 20-30
t/ha [1].

**Picking labour** [15]:
- 16.7 t/ha ÷ 50 kg/h = 333 h/ha;
- 16.7 t/ha ÷ 40 kg/h = 417 h/ha;
- 26.7 t/ha ÷ 50 kg/h = 533 h/ha.

A 5,000 kg/h harvester would need about 3-5 machine-hours per hectare.

**Break-even prices.** At 1 EUR = 9 SEK [15]:
- 6 SEK/kg = €0.67/kg;
- 2-3 SEK/kg = €0.22-0.33/kg.

**Nutrient removal per tonne** (flesh 90%, seeds 10%) [4]:
- N: 0.9 × 5.0 + 0.1 × 35.0 = 8.0 kg/t. That matches the chapter's 80 kg
  N per 10 t, which supports reading the seed average of 35 kg/t as
  correct.
- K: 0.9 × 12.5 + 0.1 × 8.6 = 12.1 kg/t.

**Dry matter.**
- 3.9 g fibre per 100 g fresh ÷ 35 g per 100 g dry [19] = about 11% dry
  matter.
- 100 g dry per 800 g fresh [11] = 12.5%.

**Energy (rough).** These figures mix juice-based and fruit-based values
and assume standard energy factors; the result is an estimate, not a
measurement.
- sugars, about 3.6-4 g [12][19] × 4 kcal = 14-16 kcal;
- malic acid, 3-5 g [12] × 2.4 kcal = 7-12 kcal;
- fibre, 3.9 g [19] × 2 kcal = 8 kcal.

That gives about 30-36 kcal per 100 g fresh fruit.

**Cyanide per kilogram of fruit.** 10% seeds [2][4] × 690 mg HCN/kg seed
[17] = about 69 mg HCN-equivalent per kg of whole fruit if seeds were
crushed in. The HCN-to-amygdalin mass ratio is 457.4/27.03 = 16.9. So
690 mg HCN per kg corresponds to about 11.7 g amygdalin per kg of seed,
or 11.7 mg/g. That is an inference; the seed moisture basis is not
stated.

**Juice volume.** A 40-60% juice yield [2] at a density of about 1.03
g/ml [2][12] gives about 390-580 L of juice per tonne of fruit.
