# Lemon (*Citrus × limon*)

**Researched:** 2026-09-29

## Naming and scope

The true lemon, a hybrid of citron and bitter orange [1]. This entry
covers the commercial acid lemons — Eureka, Lisbon, Femminello, Verna,
Fino, Interdonato, and similar types [1][15]. Not covered here, though
also sometimes called "lemons": a sweeter, less-acid citron × mandarin/
pomelo hybrid often treated as its own crop (a closely related crop this
project has also researched); a citron × lemon cross grown mostly as an
ornamental; rough lemon and Volkamer lemon, which appear here only as
rootstocks; and true sweet lemons (limetta types), which this project
treats as a separate crop entry [1][15].

No other citrus crop this project has researched was available as a
direct cross-reference at the time of writing. Citrus-wide water-use
data cited throughout come from a standard international reference's
citrus chapter, which is based mostly on research into a different
citrus crop (sweet orange); each such figure is flagged where it
applies.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an evergreen that never stops flowering, so harvests are
picks spread over most of the year rather than a single season.** In
cool coastal climates, citrus flowers essentially year-round, though
most heavily in spring; commercial Eureka fruits "throughout the year"
[1][2][15]. Italian growers of one major cultivar group manage their
groves to produce four distinct crops a year, each with its own market
name tied to the season it is picked [15]. A detailed California cost
study assumes three picks a season, with the largest in winter, and
lemons are picked by size rather than color [4]. Lemons need no winter
chill at all, but active growth requires temperatures of roughly 7°C or
warmer [1].

**Finding 2 — water stress is deliberately used as a management tool.**
In a distinctive Sicilian practice (the "forzatura", producing the
so-called Verdelli effect), growers deliberately withhold irrigation for
about nine weeks starting in June, until the trees reach a specific,
fairly severe internal water-stress threshold. When watering resumes,
the trees produce an off-season bloom and a genuine summer crop; one
documented trial got meaningful yields from both the resulting summer
harvest and the following winter harvest [2]. Too little induced stress
produces no off-season bloom at all, while too much drops leaves and
flowers and harms the following winter crop [2]. This means that any
irrigation-management model for this crop needs to support deliberately
induced stress as a feature, not just avoidance of stress.

**Finding 3 — among the longest-storing fruits researched so far for
this project, but notably chilling-sensitive.** Lemons picked while
still dark green keep for up to roughly six months under refrigeration
at high humidity, with color, juice content, and flavor actually
improving during storage [3]. Citrus in general is described as storing
"up to six months or more for lemons" [2]. Long storage below a specific
threshold temperature causes rind pitting, internal membrane staining,
and a reddish internal blotch disorder; the same source handbook gives
somewhat conflicting guidance on the exact lowest safe storage
temperature, which is recorded here as an unresolved internal
inconsistency rather than reconciled [3].

**Finding 4 — one fruit, several distinct product streams.** Beyond
fresh fruit, this crop yields juice (with a specific, fairly high citric
acid concentration), cold-pressed peel oil, pectin extracted from the
peel, salt-preserved whole fruit, marmalade, and a well-known zest-based
liqueur [1][17][18]. Industry practice generally recovers the peel oil
first — breaking open the oil-bearing rind cells and flushing the oil
out with a water spray — before juicing the same fruit [1]. Peel-oil
recovery rate is the point of sharpest disagreement across sources
found, with figures spanning roughly an order of magnitude or more
depending on the source [20].

**Finding 5 — high yields documented in California; the world figure is
mixed with a closely related citrus fruit.** A major California growing
region averaged a specific yield figure across a recent season [5], and
a detailed university model orchard at full production gives a notably
higher figure [4]. A standard international agricultural-statistics
source reports this crop combined with limes as a single category, so
no lemon-only world yield figure could be isolated from the sources
reached [1][23].

**Finding 6 — rootstock and scion compatibility is a hard agronomic
constraint.** The dominant commercial cultivar is flatly incompatible
with several widely used citrus rootstock types [13]. Lemons are mostly
grown budded onto a handful of specific vigorous rootstock species or
hybrids; these lemon-type rootstocks tend to be freeze-susceptible and
to give coarser fruit with less juice than some alternatives [11][13]
[14]. In Mediterranean growing regions, a serious vascular wilt disease
attacks lemon above all other citrus types, and the common lemon
rootstocks are highly susceptible to it [19].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial tree with no true
  dormant season; flowering occurs nearly year-round in mild coastal
  climates (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Effectively continuous harvest via repeated picks
  (commonly around three per season in a major production region, or
  as many as four named seasonal crops under intensive Italian
  management), picked by fruit size rather than color (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Mediterranean, subtropical, and irrigated semiarid
  climates; the crop needs no winter chill at all but is notably
  frost-sensitive, and does poorly in true tropical climates where fruit
  stays green and has a short market life.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (juice and zest; very sour).
- **Pollination:** Not required for fruit set; like most citrus, this
  crop is largely self-compatible, and the common commercial cultivars
  are low-seeded to essentially seedless.
- **Yield / composition:** A major California growing region yields in
  the low 40s t/ha on average in recent seasons, with a university model
  orchard at full production reaching considerably higher; a world
  average figure is hard to establish cleanly since major statistical
  sources combine this crop with limes (Finding 5; see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Citrus crop-coefficient curves vary with canopy cover
  and ground cover; this crop's own single crop coefficient (sourced
  specifically to it, from California research) is notably lower than
  the citrus-general curve, implying somewhat lower baseline water
  demand than other citrus — except that irrigation is also used as a
  deliberate stress tool during a specific summer window (Finding 2).
  Root-zone depth is substantial, with a fairly generous soil-moisture-
  depletion allowance before stress onset, consistent with resilience
  under drought (see Estimates below). Flowering and fruit set are the
  most water-sensitive growth stage.
- **Establishment:** Propagated exclusively by budding a selected scion
  onto a rootstock seedling — the crop is not grown from seed itself.
  Several distinct rootstock options exist, each with sharply different
  compatibility, vigor, disease resistance, and salinity/soil-pH
  tolerance (Finding 6).
- **Labor:** A detailed California cost study gives land-preparation,
  pruning, and harvest labor figures; harvest is handled substantially
  through contract piece-rate picking rather than direct hourly labor,
  complicating precise labor-hour estimation (see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** A moderately acidic to neutral-to-slightly-alkaline pH range
  is tolerated, with specific rootstocks chosen deliberately for
  high-pH, high-salinity soils; the crop is rated salt-sensitive overall.
- **Stand life:** First harvest around three years after planting,
  reaching full production by around year eight at standard planting
  density, with a productive life of 40 years or more; a defined,
  gradually increasing yield-fraction curve applies in the early years
  (see Estimates below).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A serious vascular wilt disease specific to
  this crop (most severe in Mediterranean regions, especially on
  susceptible rootstocks), plus citrus greening disease vectored by an
  invasive psyllid insect (actively managed with area-wide spraying in
  at least one major California growing region), several postharvest
  fungal decay organisms, and quarantine-regulated fruit fly pests (see
  Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named acid-lemon cultivars exist,
  differing in season of maturity, cold-hardiness, seediness, rind
  texture, and suitability for fresh use versus juicing or zest-based
  products; distinct non-true "lemons" also exist outside this entry's
  scope (see Naming and scope, and Sources).
- **Harvest:** Hand-clipped using size gauges into field bins, generally
  across three or more picks per season rather than a single harvest
  window (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Among the longest-storing fruits this project has
  researched — up to roughly six months under refrigeration at high
  humidity if picked while still green — but notably chilling-sensitive,
  with several internal quality disorders triggered by storage that is
  too cold (Finding 3).

## Hazards

Cold-pressed lemon peel oil contains phototoxic compounds
(furanocoumarins) that are subject to a specific international
fragrance-industry safety limit in leave-on skin products; the plain
juice and flesh are not implicated. Postharvest fungicide residues
(applied to control green mold, blue mold, and sour rot, three
documented decay organisms affecting packed fruit) may be present on the
peel of commercially packed fruit, which is particularly relevant for
zest-based uses like marmalade, limoncello-style liqueur, or preserved
lemons made from packed rather than unpacked fruit. Several fruit-fly
species are quarantine pests of regulatory concern for this crop, as is
a specific citrus leaf-spot disease.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — citrus peel oil is associated with a skin-
phototoxicity (phytophotodermatitis) hazard beyond the fragrance-
industry context noted above; deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit is cured in storage for months, then packed, with
  roughly four-fifths of picked fruit meeting fresh-market pack
  standards and the remainder going to juice or being culled. Peel oil
  is recovered first, by rasping or pressing the rind with a water
  spray, before the same fruit is juiced. Juicing the whole fruit leaves
  behind a substantial residue fraction (roughly half the fruit's mass)
  usable for cattle feed and pectin extraction. Whole fruit can also be
  salt-preserved over weeks to months, made into marmalade, or used to
  produce a well-known zest-based liqueur (zest steeped in a neutral
  high-proof spirit, then sweetened with syrup).
- Not covered by this research: pectin extraction and citric-acid
  production in processing detail; candied or dried peel; lemon-leaf
  tea; seed oil.

## Open research questions

- Peel-oil recovery rate varies by roughly an order of magnitude or more
  across sources found — not reconciled.
- Lowest safe storage temperature for chilling sensitivity is given
  inconsistently within a single source handbook (a "not below a given
  temperature" rule alongside a separate, somewhat lower tolerated
  temperature for several weeks) — recorded as an internal
  inconsistency, not resolved.
- Rootstock seed storage behavior is described inconsistently across
  sources: one rootstock species is reported to store well under cold,
  dry conditions, while this crop's own seed is separately described as
  desiccation-sensitive — not reconciled.
- Cultivar market-share figures for a major Spanish growing region
  conflict meaningfully between two sources (a notably different share
  for the same cultivar) — not reconciled.
- Whether fruit set occurs without any insect pollination at all was not
  directly sourced; it is inferred from generally low seed counts in
  commercial cultivars, which is treated here as a reasonable but
  unverified assumption.
- A full, properly sourced labor-hours figure for harvest is hard to
  obtain because commercial harvest is paid through a contract
  piece-rate system rather than tracked hourly — the labor estimate used
  here is a rough upper-bound conversion from piece-rate cost data (see
  Estimates below).
- Phosphorus, potassium, and soil-pH optimum/limits for this crop
  specifically were not well sourced; current figures here are largely
  estimated.
- A world yield figure specific to this crop (rather than combined with
  a closely related citrus fruit) was not found in the sources reached.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from bloom to harvest (roughly 180-330 days) are estimated from
the fact that harvests occur essentially year-round, with the specific
Sicilian off-season-bloom practice implying roughly 300-330 days from a
forced late-summer bloom to the following summer's harvest, versus
roughly 180-210 days from an ordinary spring bloom to an autumn harvest.

Climate-zone suitability (Mediterranean, subtropical, irrigated
semiarid) is derived from the combination of documented growing regions,
the crop's minimum active-growth temperature, and its well-documented
frost sensitivity; fully tropical and temperate/cool-temperate zones are
excluded on this basis.

A world-average yield figure of roughly 16,000 kg/ha is derived by
dividing a recent combined global production figure (for this crop plus
a closely related citrus fruit) by the corresponding combined harvested
area — this necessarily includes the related fruit and would be
improved by a crop-specific statistical pull.

Good and exceptional yield tiers (roughly 42,900 and 60,800 kg/ha
respectively) are derived directly from, respectively, a recent major
California growing-region average and a detailed university model
orchard's full-production yield figure, both converted from
customary-unit box/acre figures; these are field (pre-pack) weights,
before any packout-fraction loss. A poor-yield tier (roughly 13,500
kg/ha) is derived similarly from a lower-yielding growing region's
recent season and a young orchard's early-year yield.

Water-use parameters (seasonal crop-coefficient-days, root-available-
water depth, seasonal water totals) are derived from a standard
international reference's citrus stage-length and crop-coefficient
tables, cross-checked against this crop's own documented single crop
coefficient from California research; the resulting seasonal water
total sits consistent with regionally measured irrigation volumes for
drier inland growing regions, though a coastal growing region's
documented applied-water figure is noticeably lower, suggesting real
regional variation in actual practice beyond what the standard
reference alone implies.

Drought tolerance (rated medium) and waterlogging tolerance (rated low)
are estimated judgementally: the crop shows few visible drought-stress
symptoms even under fairly severe internal water stress and recovers
well from it, arguing for at least medium drought tolerance; but a
commonly used lemon rootstock and a major root-rot disease make wet
soils genuinely risky, arguing for low waterlogging tolerance.

Peak carbon-uptake rate and within-season growth-stage stress
multipliers are estimated judgementally, set somewhat below figures
estimated for a deciduous stone-fruit crop this project has also
researched, reflecting this crop's comparatively low stomatal
conductance relative to crops like soybean or almond, but its notable
tolerance of summer water stress.

Labor-hour figures across operations are mostly estimated by analogy to
a deciduous stone-fruit crop this project has also researched, with
land-preparation figures raised to reflect the deeper, heavier orchard-
establishment groundwork documented for this crop specifically; a
pruning-maintenance figure is derived directly from a documented
per-tree pruning time in a California cost study; a harvest-labor figure
is derived as a rough upper bound by converting a documented contract
piece-rate cost into equivalent hours at the same study's manual wage
rate, then discounted downward to account for the piece rate also
covering hauling and contractor margin — a true picker-productivity
figure would meaningfully improve this estimate.

Nitrogen application rate per tonne of fruit (roughly 3.4 kg N/t) is
derived from a documented per-hectare nitrogen application rate in a
California cost study divided by that same study's full-production
yield figure; this is an application rate rather than a true crop
nitrogen-removal rate. Phosphorus, potassium, and soil-pH figures are
not well sourced and are treated as rough estimates, with the upper pH
limit reflecting the fact that at least two specific rootstocks are
deliberately selected for high-pH soils.

Typical cool, humid storage life (roughly 120 days at the "normal"
tier) is derived by discounting the documented six-month maximum
storage life downward to account for fruit picked less fully green,
which the same source material says must be marketed more quickly.

Early-years yield-fraction curve (gradually increasing across roughly
the first six years after planting) is derived directly from a
documented sequence of year-by-year yields in a university model
orchard, expressed as fractions of that same orchard's full-production
yield.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (days to first harvest, ramp-up
period, plateau length, decline period, peak daily harvest rate,
maximum days fruit can stand ready on the tree, and daily post-maturity
quality decay rate) are estimated or derived from the combination of the
growDays estimate above, the documented roughly nine-to-ten-month spread
of harvests across the year, and the good-yield-tier figure divided
across an estimated effective harvest-season length.

Fruit-set-without-pollination is assumed true (that is, pollination is
not required) based on this crop's general self-compatibility and low
seed counts in commercial cultivars, though no source was found testing
this directly for this crop.

A frost-damage threshold of roughly -2°C is derived by converting a
search-snippet-level Fahrenheit range ("high 20s°F") into Celsius.
