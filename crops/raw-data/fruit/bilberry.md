## Naming and scope

*Vaccinium myrtillus* L. (Ericaceae), section *Myrtillus* [3]; syn. *V.
oreophilum* [3].
- **Names:** bilberry, common bilberry, blue whortleberry, whortleberry
  [3][4]; European blueberry (EB) in the cultivation literature [1].
  - **Regional (British Isles):** blaeberry (Scotland), urts or hurts
    (Cornwall, Devon), whinberry, wimberry, fraughan (Ireland) [4].
  - **Nordic:** *mustikka* (Finnish) [5]; *blåbær* (Norwegian), the same
    word Norway's food table uses for highbush blueberry [6].
  - **Canada:** myrtille [4].
- **Name clashes:**
  - **"Bilberry"** is also used for the genus *Vaccinium* as a whole [4].
  - **"Huckleberry"** usually means *Gaylussacia*, but is sometimes used
    for this species [4].
  - **"Blueberry"** in Nordic food and trade statistics (*mustikka*,
    *blåbær*) means this species, not the highbush crop [5][6]. Norway's
    food table lists highbush separately as "American blueberries" [6].
- **Range:** Holarctic. Native to almost every country in Europe, north
  and central Asia, Japan and Greenland, and to western Canada and the
  western United States [4]. In North America it runs from British
  Columbia to central Oregon and through the Rocky Mountains to northern
  New Mexico [3].
  - **Conflict:** `lowbush-blueberry.md` says it does not occur in North
    America apart from a small disjunct population [13], against the two
    sources here [3][4]. The western plants may be a variety (*V.
    myrtillus* var. *oreophilum*, the FEIS synonym) [3]; the point needs a
    taxonomic source.
- **Hybrids:** natural hybrids with lingonberry (*V. × intermedium*) occur
  in northern Europe; fruit set in them is rare [3].

**Gate check (2026-11-22): passes.** The row was proposed from
`lingonberry.md`, as the other main Nordic wild *Vaccinium* berry [13].
It is distinct enough for its own row on every count:
- **Taxonomy:** a separate species from both blueberry rows, in its own
  section [3].
- **Fruit:** the anthocyanins are in the flesh as well as the skin [4], at
  roughly twice highbush levels (275-364 against 145 mg per 100 g) [1].
- **Farming system:** none. It is harvested only from wild stands and has
  never been domesticated (Finding 1) [1][4].
- **Harvest regime:** set by public-access rights and by permit rules for
  migrant pickers, not by land ownership (Finding 2) [5][10][11].
- **Uses:** an EU herbal monograph and a large standardized-extract trade
  with a known adulteration problem (Finding 4) [7][8].

**Wild-managed resource:** yes. Supply is set by public-access picking
rights, labour permits and wild-stand yield, with no viable cultivation
yet [1][2][5]. See `crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/bilberry.md`.

**Relatives in this project:**
- lowbush blueberry (`lowbush_blueberry`, `lowbush-blueberry.md`): the
  managed wild-stand model the cultivation review proposes copying [1].
- blueberry (`blueberry`, `blueberry.md`): highbush [1][6].
- lingonberry (`lingonberry`, `lingonberry.md`): shares the forests,
  pickers, buyers and statistics [2][5]; hybridizes naturally [3].
- cranberry (`cranberry`, `cranberry.md`): the same genus.

**Proposed:** none new. Bog bilberry (*V. uliginosum*, *juolukka*) was
last sold commercially in Finland in 2000 [5]; not proposed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a large crop that nobody farms.**
- **No domestication:** to the review authors' knowledge, no attempt had
  been made to domesticate it; it is "still harvested in forest fields
  without any cultivation" [1].
- **Potential:** wild-stand yield is very variable, with a potential
  "probably close to 2 tons per hectare" [1].
- **Measured wild yields** [1]:
  - **Central Finland:** 0-130 kg/ha over three years.
  - **Sweden:** 0-450 kg/ha over 15 years, by site and year.
  - **Arkhangelsk, Russia:** 171-341 kg/ha in a typical good year, which
    came about one year in four.
  - **Kirov, Russia:** a long-term mean of about 215 kg/ha (aspen forest)
    to 415 kg/ha (spruce forest) (see Estimates).
- **National crops** [2]:
  - **Finland:** about 208 million kg a year (2021 model), with 92-312
    million kg between poor and abundant years.
  - **Sweden:** a mean of 336 million kg in 2015-2019, ranging from 142 to
    565 million kg.
- **Semi-cultivation trials (Norway):** fertilizing native stands raised
  shoot growth but not yield, and the heaviest dose (N60P40) cut the
  four-year mean yield. Cutting shoots to the ground cut yield until the
  third year. Seedlings planted on farmland first fruited in their third
  field year [12] (snippet).
- **The review's suggestion:** manage wild stands the way lowbush
  blueberry is managed, with fertilizer, irrigation, tree cutting and weed
  control [1].

**Finding 2 — the harvest depends on public access and migrant pickers.**
- **Public access (Sweden):** anyone may pick wild berries. There is no
  ban on commercially organized large-scale picking, as long as it causes
  the landowner no loss or nuisance beyond the berries themselves [11].
  Protected areas may restrict it [11].
- **Share picked:** Finland collected about 5-6% of its bilberry crop in
  1997-1999 [2].
- **Foreign pickers, Finland:** in 2025, seasonal pickers picked 75% of
  the wild berries sold, against 36% in 2024 [5]. There were about 3,000
  of them, against about 1,000 in 2024, mostly from Thailand, with others
  from Vietnam and India [5]. The buyers' survey says that without them,
  commercial picking would be next to nothing, since domestic pickers are
  ageing [5].
- **Permit rules, Finland:** since 17 February 2025, picking wild natural
  products falls under the Seasonal Workers Act, and employed pickers
  apply for a seasonal work visa or permit [5]. Tourist visas for picking
  stopped after summer 2023, and about 2,600 seasonal visas were issued
  for 2025 [12] (snippet).
- **Permit rules, Sweden:** since 1 June 2026, no work permit can be
  issued for forest berry picking. Pickers can still get a seasonal work
  permit with an employer established in Sweden [10]. Reported Thai
  picker numbers fell from over 5,000 to 89 in 2025 [12] (snippet).
- **Swings:** Finnish commercial purchases of bilberry ran 1.16-9.36
  million kg a year in 2015-2025: 4.02 in 2023, 1.16 in 2024 and 6.83 in
  2025 [5]. The low 2024 figure came during the change in visa rules [5]
  [12]; the survey ties volumes to foreign pickers but doesn't single out
  2024's cause.

**Finding 3 — frost at bloom, and snow cover, set the yield.**
- **Bloom frost:** in the Swedish 15-year study, the near-zero years all
  had frost during bloom [1].
- **Biennial pattern:** in a ten-year period it flowered heavily every
  second year, partly from frost and partly from biennial bearing [1].
- **Snow:** fruit production is generally poor when winter snow is under
  20 cm deep. Without snow, plants can be killed at -19.5 °C, and flower
  buds can be badly reduced at January temperatures of -32 to -34 °C
  [3].
- **Warm winters:** an exceptionally warm winter (5 °C above normal) in
  northeast Sweden in 1991-1992 caused lethal injury [1]. Spring frost
  injury to flowers has become more frequent with milder winters and an
  earlier start of growth [1].
- **Good years:** profuse flowering in warm weather with showers after the
  last frost, then enough moisture in July and August [1].
- **Forestry:** clearcutting cut bilberry cover in Colorado, while
  thinning raised it within five years [3]. In Scandinavia, berry
  production may peak at stand ages of 20-70 years [3].

**Finding 4 — a medicinal extract trade that attracts fraud.**
- **EU monographs:**
  - **Dried fruit:** traditional use for mild diarrhoea and minor mouth
    inflammation, in adults and children over 12. The tannins' astringent
    action is the plausible mechanism; there are no clinical studies
    [7].
  - **Fresh-fruit extract:** a dry extract with 36% anthocyanosides
    (25% anthocyanidins), drug-extract ratio 76-153:1, in 70% methanol.
    Traditional use for heavy legs from minor venous problems and for
    capillary fragility. Single dose 80-180 mg, up to 160-540 mg a day,
    for 4 weeks; not under 18 [7].
- **Raw material:** 100 kg of hand-picked fruit makes 1 kg of a 100:1
  extract. In 2011 the fruit cost €2.5 to a "near record" €4.6 a kilo,
  so raw material alone was $325-600 per kg of extract [8].
- **Supply:** the whole global supply is wild-harvested in Scandinavia
  and Eastern Europe [8]. Bilberry was the 15th best-selling US
  supplement in 2011 [8].
- **Adulteration:**
  - **2012:** the market was "rife with economic adulteration". Fillers
    included amaranth dye, charcoal, black soybean hull, black rice hull
    and cheaper blueberries [8].
  - **2014:** about half of 14 extracts differed from reference bilberry,
    mostly cut with mulberry and chokeberry [8].
  - **2021:** 45% of supplements tested were unacceptable, including some
    with no bilberry at all; 91% of dried berries were good [8].
- **Health claims:** the encyclopedia, citing the US NIH, says there is no
  evidence that bilberry supplements affect health or disease [4].

**Finding 5 — a small, very dark, very acid berry.**
- **Size:** about 0.35 g per berry on average in the Finnish yield model
  [2]. Norwegian trial berries averaged 457 mg; the literature average is
  328 mg; highbush is 1,635 mg [1].
- **Chemistry (Norwegian trials):** 10.8% soluble solids, pH 2.7, 1.4 g
  titratable acid and 275 mg anthocyanins per 100 g fresh weight [1].
- **Vitamin C conflict:** 3.0 mg per 100 g in the Norwegian trials, 59 mg
  in the compiled literature [1], and 12 mg in Norway's food table [6].
- **Food table, per 100 g raw** [6]: 51 kcal, water 86 g, protein 1.0 g,
  fat 0.9 g, carbohydrate 7.5 g, fibre 5.0 g, potassium 122 mg, calcium
  29 mg, iron 0.4 mg.
- **Latitude:** berries from southern Finland have less anthocyanin than
  those from central and northern Finland [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** a deciduous dwarf shrub, 10-51 cm tall, spreading
  by rhizomes into large patches. Plants can live 30 years, with roots to
  1 m [4]. Ramets are mostly under 6 years old, sometimes 6-12, and up to
  34 years [1]. Rhizome patches spread 5-10 cm a year [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** wild perennial bearing, one season a year; fruit
  July-September [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** seed production generally begins at age
  three [3]. Planted seedlings first fruited in their third field year
  [12] (snippet).
- **Climate zones:** boreal and montane. Elevation up to 2,350 m in
  Europe [4]; 200-1,150 m in Britain; 1,311-3,965 m in the US Rockies and
  Southwest [3].
  - **Cold:** some strains may be hardy to -57 °C [3]; it depends on
    snow cover (Finding 3) [3].
  - **Chill requirement:** Not sourced. Low chilling may reduce bud
    burst, which can be mistaken for winter injury [1].
- **Composition:** see Finding 5 [1][6].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible. Leaves are described as potentially toxic
  [4].
- **Pollination:** bees are the main pollinators [3]. Self-fertility
  varies by clone. Pollen limitation and inbreeding depression at the
  seed stage favour cross-pollination between clones [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:** see Finding 1 [1][2]. Finnish peatlands give 0-11.1 kg/ha
  [2].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Moisture in July and August is one condition
  for a good crop [1].
- **Propagation:** wild stands spread by rhizome [3]. Seed: 18-20 viable
  seeds a berry, 25 mg per 100 seeds; germination 35-46%, or 41-64% after
  3 weeks at 0 °C [3].
  - **Micropropagation:** microshoots have been grown in vitro, with 72-81%
    rooting [1].
  - **Clonal risk:** Not sourced.
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. Finnish pickers earned €16 million from
  bilberries in 2025 [5]; see Estimates for a rough per-picker figure.
- **Soil:** acid, infertile or intermediate-fertility forest soils with
  raw humus; poor growth on badly aerated soil [3]. Soil pH 4.1 under
  spruce and 4.7 under birch in one study [1]. It needs little potassium,
  can use ammonium as its only nitrogen source [3], and its growth is
  phosphorus-limited [1]. Salinity: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced as temperature and shelf life.
  Most is frozen; Finland's storage aid covered 2.8 million kg of
  bilberry in 2025 [5].
- **Establishment:** none; wild stands. Seedlings are rare in the wild
  and grow very slowly [3].
- **Cultivars:** none. Rare white, yellow, red and spotted-fruit forms
  occur [4].
- **Weather response:** see Finding 3. Spring frost and summer drought
  can greatly decrease yields [3].
- **Water regime:** rain-fed wild stands.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** ripening comes about 50 days after
  flowering [3]. In one Finnish study, flowers opened around day 153 (2
  June) and berries ripened around day 202 (21 July) [3].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** native across its range. No invasive
  listing found in this pass. Mummy berry (*Monilinia vaccinii-corymbosi*),
  a major American blueberry disease, was first confirmed in Europe in
  Austria in 2002 [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. Micropropagation [1] and
  farmland seedlings [12] exist, but no protected-cropping trial was
  found.

## Hazards

- **Norovirus:** five outbreaks in Finland in early 2019 were traced to
  frozen domestic bilberries (norovirus GII.17). People who ate unheated
  bilberries were 3.1 times more likely to fall ill, and the packing lines
  showed contamination. The authors advise heating industrially frozen
  berries [9].
- **Radioactive caesium:** Finnish wild berries carried 10-230 Bq/kg of
  caesium-137 in 2000-2005, mostly from Chernobyl. Eating 2 kg of
  bilberries a year gives a negligible dose (about 1 µSv). Sweden's limit
  for wild berries is 1,500 Bq/kg [12] (snippet).
- **Fox tapeworm:** a Finnish Food Authority project studied whether wild
  berries could carry *Echinococcus multilocularis* eggs, if the parasite
  spread to Finland [12] (snippet). No result was read.
- **Supplement fraud:** see Finding 4 [8].
- **Leaves:** potentially toxic [4].
- **Diseases:** *Valdensia heterodoxa* leaf spot is the most common;
  nitrogen addition and wet summers increase it [1]. Mummy berry, above
  [1].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none sourced for bilberry.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — picker labour conditions (housing, pay, trafficking
  investigations) are a real hazard of this supply chain [12] (snippet)
  but have no home in this project's hazard model yet.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fresh and frozen):** wild berries → hand-picked or combed → sold
  to buyers mostly uncleaned (97% in Finland, 2025, at €2.36/kg; cleaned
  berries fetch more) → cleaned → frozen or sold fresh [5]. Finland
  exported 3.29 million kg frozen (2.5 to Sweden) and 0.22 million kg
  fresh in 2025, and imported 1.6 million kg fresh [5]. Some goes to China
  and Japan [5].
- **Chain (food):** fruit → pies, tarts, cakes, jams, muffins, sauces,
  syrups, juices and sweets [4]; jams and jellies [3].
- **Chain (extract):** fresh fruit → 70% methanol extraction → dry extract
  standardized to 36% anthocyanosides, at 76-153 kg of fruit per kg [7],
  or 100 kg per kg [8] → tablets and capsules [7].
- **Chain (dried fruit):** ripe fruit → dried → comminuted → herbal tea or
  mouth rinse [7].
- **Chain (organic):** certified wild-collection areas covered 6.9 million
  ha in Finland in 2025, mostly in Lapland. Organic bilberry sales were
  0.68 million kg [5].
- **Dye:** the juice stains deep blue and was used to dye food, clothes,
  linen and paper [1][4].
- **Leaves:** tea [3]; folk medicine for skin disorders [4].
- **Wildlife:** an important food for bears in North America; bear-human
  conflicts rise in crop-failure years [3].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Share of Finland's crop sold, about 3.3% (2025).** Sold 6.83 million kg
[5] / national crop 208 million kg [2] = 0.033. The crop figure is a
model average, not a 2025 measurement, and household picking is
excluded. The older 5-6% figure includes all picking [2].

**Swedish export share, about 3% (2015).** Export 10 million kg [2] /
mean crop 336 million kg [2] = 0.030. The crop mean is for 2015-2019, so
this is an order of magnitude.

**Kirov per-hectare yields, about 215-415 kg/ha.** 21.5 and 41.5 g/m² [1]
× 10,000 m²/ha / 1,000 g/kg = 215 and 415 kg/ha.

**Berries per kilogram, about 2,200-3,050.** 1,000 g / 0.457 g = 2,190;
1,000 / 0.328 = 3,050 [1]; the 0.35 g model weight gives 2,860 [2].

**Raw-material cost per kg of extract, about €190-700.** 76-153 kg of fruit
per kg [7] × €2.5-4.6/kg (2011) [8]: 76 × 2.5 = €190; 153 × 4.6 = €704.
At the 2025 Finnish buying price of €2.39/kg [5], 100 kg = €239.

**Bilberry income per foreign picker, very roughly €4,000 (Finland,
2025).** Bilberry picking income €16.3 million [5] × foreign share 75%
[5] / about 3,000 pickers [5] = €4,080. This assumes the foreign share
for all wild berries applies to bilberry, and ignores lingonberry income
earned by the same pickers. Per-species picker shares would replace it.
