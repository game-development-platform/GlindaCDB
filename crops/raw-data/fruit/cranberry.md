## Naming and scope

The American or large-fruited cranberry, *Vaccinium macrocarpon* Aiton
(Ericaceae, sometimes placed in the genus *Oxycoccus*), the cultivated
cranberry crop of North America and Chile [1][3]. It is a low, trailing,
evergreen woody vine: runners spread 0.3-1.8 m along the ground, while
short upright shoots 5-20 cm tall bear the flowers and fruit [3]. Two
related species are excluded from this entry: the small cranberry (a
closely related, mostly wild-gathered species of northern Europe and
Russia), and lingonberry (a distinct Vaccinium-genus crop, not
researched here). This crop shares close family ties — acid-soil
preference, a lack of root hairs, dependence on bee pollination — with
another well-known Vaccinium-genus fruit crop this project has also
researched, and also shares a harvest-method duality (two distinct
harvest methods yielding two different products with different
storage lives) with a tropical fruit tree this project has also
researched.

This is unambiguously a distinct, well-documented commercial crop: it
is grown in diked, sand-capped beds that are deliberately flooded for
winter protection, pest control, and harvest; it is propagated
exclusively from vine cuttings rather than seed; established beds can
remain productive for over a century; and the large majority of the
crop (roughly 92-95%) is destined for processing rather than fresh
sale.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a crop fundamentally run by flooding.** Production beds
are diked, laser-leveled, and topped with 10-20 cm of sand over peat or
mineral soil [1][3]. Water is used in several distinct, purpose-built
ways across the season: a winter flood (roughly 1.5-1.6 acre-feet)
protects dormant vines from desiccating winter winds and cold damage;
a roughly 30-day "late-water" flood in mid-April provides chemical-free
pest control; frost sprinkling (roughly 0.7-1.1 acre-feet) runs from
mid-April through June and again in fall, protecting buds down to
specific documented temperature thresholds; a harvest flood (roughly
1.6 acre-feet) floods the bed 5-6 inches deep so that mechanical water
reels can knock ripe berries loose, after which the floating fruit is
corralled with booms and pumped into trucks; and ordinary irrigation
adds a further 0.5-0.9 acre-feet a year [3]. Measured total water use
across all these purposes runs roughly 5.4-6.0 acre-feet per acre per
year (an unusually large figure for a fruit crop), though much of this
is reused and non-consumptive rather than drawn fresh each time; actual
crop water demand itself averages only about 25 mm a week, rising to
about 50 mm a week during midsummer heat as the berries size [3].

**Finding 2 — the harvest method chosen determines the product.** The
large majority of the US crop (roughly 90-95%) is water-harvested using
the flood-and-reel method described above; this fruit is inevitably
bruised and waterlogged in the process, making it suitable only for
processing, and it is frozen in bulk at receiving stations shortly
after harvest [1][3]. Fruit left in the flood water for more than an
hour after detachment, or immersed eight hours or more, breaks down
measurably more [2]. A smaller share of the crop (roughly 5-10%) is
instead dry-harvested using walk-behind picking machines, supplying the
fresh market; this fresh fruit keeps 2-4 months at 2-5°C and
90-95% relative humidity, though it is notably chilling-sensitive —
after about four weeks held near 0°C it turns rubbery, dull, and more
prone to decay [2].

**Finding 3 — propagated entirely from cuttings, with beds remaining
productive for a century or more.** New production beds are established
using unrooted vine cuttings mowed from existing beds (applied at
roughly 1-2 tons per acre) or using pre-rooted plugs [3]. The first
harvestable crop comes in year 2-3, with full production reached in
years 4-6 [6]. Remarkably, commercial beds over 100 years old remain
productive, and a clear majority of one major growing region's
acreage still consists of such century-old beds planted to older,
unimproved varieties [6]. Established beds are periodically "sanded"
(a thin layer of sand spread over the bed surface, often directly onto
winter ice) every 2-5 years; this practice buries old runners,
suppresses pests, and improves drainage [3].

**Finding 4 — a genuine winter chilling requirement, and detailed,
stage-specific frost-tolerance tables.** The crop requires roughly
1,000 hours below about 7°C under short winter daylengths to properly
break dormancy, or alternatively about 2,500 hours of constant cold in
complete darkness; flower buds for the following year's crop are
actually initiated in early July of the preceding year [3]. Spring frost
tolerance of flower buds rises steadily as the bud develops — from
roughly -7.8°C at full winter dormancy, through intermediate bud-swell
and bud-elongation stages, to only about -1.1°C by full bloom — and
this tolerance curve differs measurably between cultivar groups [3].
Fall frost tolerance of the ripening fruit itself likewise rises with
increasing red coloration, from a documented threshold at an early
"deep blush" stage down to a more frost-tender threshold once fruit
reaches full "maroon" ripeness [3]. Berries mature roughly 80 days
after full bloom [3].

**Finding 5 — yields are unusually high for a fruit crop, and
exceptionally well documented through official statistics.** A national
yield statistic for a recent year stood at roughly 227 barrels per acre
(one barrel equals 100 lb), translating to roughly 25.5 tonnes per
hectare; a leading growing state's figure for the same year was
notably higher at roughly 262 barrels per acre, while another major
growing state came in lower at roughly 176 barrels per acre [4].
Documented typical yields vary considerably by cultivar — older native
selections typically yield around 150 barrels per acre, while newer
large-fruited hybrid cultivars typically yield 200-225 barrels per
acre, with exceptional plantings recorded exceeding 300 barrels per
acre [3]. Of the enormous number of flowers produced per acre (tens of
millions), only roughly 25-45% actually set fruit [3]. In one major
growing state, processed fruit accounted for roughly 94% of the total
crop [4].

**Finding 6 — a genuinely disputed drug interaction with a common
blood-thinning medication.** An early-2000s regulatory warning in one
country rested on what later analysis characterized as poorly
documented case reports [7][9]. A controlled clinical trial using a
substantial daily juice dose found no measurable change in the
medication's effect in patients already taking it [8]. However, a
separate healthy-volunteer trial using a concentrated cranberry
preparation did find a roughly 30% increase in a standard blood-clotting
measurement, suggesting a real but dose- and concentration-dependent
pharmacodynamic effect that ordinary juice consumption may not trigger
[10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A low, trailing, evergreen woody vine forming
  a continuous mat; a single annual harvest, typically mid-September to
  early November.
- **Climate:** Cool temperate to temperate, requiring genuine winter
  chilling; documented commercial production in the US Northeast and
  Upper Midwest, the Pacific Northwest, Canada, and Chile.
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible but very tart, and mostly
  consumed cooked or processed. Roughly 460 kcal/kg and 5 g protein/kg
  raw, with notable vitamin C and potassium content.
- **Pollination:** Required — self-fertile but poorly wind-pollinated;
  insect (bee) pollination is needed for adequate fruit set, and managed
  honey bee hives are standard practice at roughly 1-2 colonies per
  acre, with cross-pollination also documented to produce larger fruit
  with more seeds than self-pollination alone.
- **Yield:** A world-average figure (roughly 25,500 kg/ha) and good,
  exceptional, and poor tiers are derived directly from documented
  official barrel-per-acre yield statistics for recent harvest years
  across several growing regions, converted to a metric per-hectare
  basis (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-coefficient curve for this species
  was found in international references; water-use parameters here are
  derived instead from a documented regional water-budget study giving
  measured seasonal water demand directly. Drought tolerance is judged
  low, given the crop's notably shallow, hairless root system; dormant-
  season waterlogging tolerance is high (vines are deliberately flooded
  for months), but summer waterlogging tolerance of the ripening fruit
  itself is low, since prolonged immersion causes fruit breakdown.
- **Propagation:** Entirely vegetative, by unrooted vine cuttings or
  rooted plugs; seed propagation is used only in formal breeding
  programs, never for commercial planting.
- **Soil:** Strongly acid soil preference (roughly pH 4-5), grown on a
  sand cap over peat or mineral soil with a documented moderate organic
  matter content.
- **Labor:** No comprehensive labor-cost budget was found; harvest,
  bed-maintenance, and post-harvest labor figures here are rough
  estimates, since harvest itself is highly mechanized (water reels,
  booms, and pumps) and most ongoing labor goes into frost-watch,
  flooding, and periodic sanding rather than conventional field work.
- **Storage:** Fresh, dry-harvested fruit keeps 2-4 months at 2-5°C and
  90-95% relative humidity; it is chilling-sensitive, turning rubbery
  and dull after roughly four weeks held near 0°C. Water-harvested fruit
  is bruised and must be frozen or processed promptly rather than
  stored fresh.
- **Stand life:** First harvestable crop in year 2-3, full production
  reached in years 4-6; beds over 100 years old remain productive, with
  periodic renovation (replanting with improved hybrid cultivars) as
  the main alternative to indefinite continued production of old beds.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A leafhopper-spread phytoplasma disease
  affecting the vines, a documented virus, and several distinct fruit
  rot diseases (affecting fruit quality and storage life rather than
  the vine itself).
- **Cultivars:** Spans older native selections (generally smaller fruit,
  earlier harvest, somewhat lower yield but often better spring frost
  hardiness) and newer large-fruited hybrid cultivars (higher yield,
  larger fruit, but in some cases reduced frost tolerance); several
  specific hybrid cultivars bred for disease resistance or higher yield
  are also documented.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not viable — a flood-managed
  field crop requiring genuine winter chilling, fundamentally
  incompatible with controlled-environment production.

## Hazards

Documented diseases include a leafhopper-spread phytoplasma disease
affecting the vines, a virus, and several distinct fungal fruit rots
affecting harvested and stored fruit.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — a genuinely disputed interaction between
cranberry juice or concentrate and a common blood-thinning medication:
an early regulatory warning rested on poorly documented case reports,
a controlled trial using ordinary juice found no effect, but a separate
trial using a concentrated preparation did find a measurable
pharmacodynamic effect. Deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** water-harvested fruit → cleaned and washed → bulk frozen
  → pressed for juice (yielding a press-cake byproduct), cooked into
  sauce, or sliced, sugar-infused, and dried into sweetened dried
  cranberries; dry-harvested fruit → cleaned → graded to a fresh-market
  quality standard → stored cold for 2-4 months; bog trash and culled
  fruit → organic matter returned to the bed.
- Not covered in detail by this research: bulk freezing and canned sauce
  production in detail; juice-drink blending; bog construction in
  detail (beyond the basic sand-cap and diking description above).

## Open research questions

- Standard storage-temperature and humidity recommendations conflict
  somewhat between sources (a 2-5°C range versus a single alternative
  7°C recommendation; a 90-95% relative humidity figure versus lower
  values proposed elsewhere to reduce decay) — not reconciled here.
- Whether controlled-atmosphere storage offers a meaningful benefit for
  this crop is unresolved; research results conflict.
- Recommended honey bee hive density per unit area varies considerably
  across regional sources (roughly 1-2 hives per acre in one source,
  versus a higher figure in the Pacific Northwest, versus a university
  extension recommendation of no more than about 5 hives per hectare) —
  not reconciled here.
- No comprehensive labor-cost budget covering all field operations was
  found; the labor figures in this entry are rough estimates only.
- No standard international crop-coefficient curve for this species was
  found in the references consulted; water-use parameters here rely on
  a regional water-budget study rather than a formal crop-coefficient
  study.
- Salinity tolerance was not researched.
- Soil nitrogen/phosphorus/potassium uptake figures are derived from a
  single regional fertilizer-removal study for one cultivar at one yield
  level and may not generalize well across cultivars or regions.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers are derived directly from documented official barrel-per-
acre statistics for several growing regions and recent harvest years,
converted to a metric per-hectare basis using the standard definition
that one barrel equals 100 lb of fruit.

Days to first harvest (roughly 1,050 days, a bit under 3 years) and the
growing-days range are derived from the documented planting timing (May)
combined with the documented 2-3 year span to first crop and the
documented harvest-timing window (mid-September through October) in
subsequent years.

Water-use parameters (seasonal crop-coefficient-equivalent demand,
drought-buffer days, yield-penalty-per-dry-day) are derived from a
detailed regional water-budget study giving documented seasonal water
demand directly (since no standard international crop-coefficient curve
for this species was found), combined with an estimated reference
evapotranspiration figure for the growing region; drought-buffer days
and yield-penalty figures are further derived from the crop's documented
shallow, hairless root system and documented drought-injury threshold
(a water table dropping below about 18 inches), by analogy to another
shallow-rooted, hairless-rooted Vaccinium-genus crop this project has
also researched.

Growth-stage curve parameters are a rough estimate reflecting low
photosynthetic activity during winter dormancy under flood, rising
through bud break and shoot growth to a peak during bloom and fruit
sizing, then declining through harvest and back into dormancy.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest, bed maintenance, and post-harvest
handling are rough estimates, since no comprehensive regional enterprise
budget was found; harvest is heavily mechanized (water reels, booms, and
pumps for water-harvested fruit, walk-behind machines for dry-harvested
fruit), and bed maintenance (frost-night sprinkling, flood management,
periodic sanding, pest and weed control) is treated as the dominant
labor component.

Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium uptake per tonne of yield are
derived from a documented regional nutrient-removal study for one
cultivar at a specific yield level, converting the per-acre removal
figures for fruit (excluding old leaves and stems, which are removed
separately as bog trash) to a per-tonne basis.

Soil pH tolerance range is a rough estimate, widened somewhat from the
documented "prefers acidic soils, pH 4-5" guidance to match the range
used for another closely related, similarly acid-loving Vaccinium-genus
crop this project has also researched.

Peak harvest rate (yielding roughly 1,210 kg/ha/day) is derived from the
world-average yield figure divided by the documented typical bed-harvest
window of about 21 days; standing-crop duration and quality-decay rate
on the vine are rough estimates reflecting the documented loss of frost
tolerance and storage quality in overripe fruit left too long before
harvest.
