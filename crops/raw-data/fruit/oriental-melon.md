# Oriental Melon (*Cucumis melo*, Makuwa group)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as the Makuwa group of *Cucumis melo* — known as chamoe in
Korea, makuwa uri in Japan, and xiāng guā in China [1]. Korean chamoe
and Japanese makuwa are treated as the same cultivar group and share
one entry. A separate group of small pickling melons in the same
species, and the familiar Western dessert melons (cantaloupe,
honeydew), are excluded from this entry's scope.

Oriental melon differs sharply from the Western dessert melons this
project has also researched: it is a small (roughly 450 g), oblong,
yellow-striped fruit whose thin rind and seeds are eaten along with
the crisp white flesh, is noticeably less sweet than Western melons at
around 90% water by weight, and is grown almost entirely as a grafted
crop on squash rootstock under plastic-house protection in its main
production region, with a cropping calendar running from winter
forcing crops through to summer open-field production. Reported 2023
national statistics put this crop at roughly 4,580 ha and 40.1 t/ha
in its main producing country, with a single county responsible for
70-80% of that country's production [1][3].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a grafted, house-grown melon eaten whole, rind and seeds
included.** Grafting onto squash rootstock is considered essential
against a serious fungal wilt disease and soil replant problems, and
also improves low-temperature growth, nutrient uptake and earliness;
one trial found grafting raised yield by roughly 23-56% [2][6]. An
interspecific squash type is the recommended rootstock; vigorous
hybrid squash rootstocks grow strongly but can cause excess nitrogen
uptake and internal fruit rot, rootstocks from one squash species are
comparatively short-lived, and rootstocks from a third squash species
vary in graft compatibility [2].

**Finding 2 — five distinct, calendar-staggered cropping systems
supply a near year-round market.** Documented systems range from a
"forcing" system (sown in late October to mid-November, transplanted
in December, harvested from early April) through semi-forcing and
tunnel-protected early systems, to a conventional open-field system
(sown in late April to early May, harvested from early August) [2].
Protected (tunnel and semi-forcing) growing area has been expanding
while open-field area has been shrinking, reportedly due to summer
rain and disease pressure; market shipments run nearly year-round,
from before the Lunar New Year through October [3].

**Finding 3 — detailed, numeric management guidance exists for nearly
every stage.** Recommended growing temperatures run 26-30°C in the day
and 17-20°C at night, with soil temperature at least 18°C (ideally
20-25°C); preferred soil is an organic, sandy loam at pH 6.0-6.8, with
sandier soil favoured for early crops and heavier clay loam favoured
for later, sweeter crops. Typical planting density is roughly
7,000-11,000 plants/ha on raised beds, with fertilizer applied at
roughly 25 kg N, 7.7 kg P and 16 kg K per 1,000 m². Pollination is done
by hand in the morning (around 7-10 am, roughly 20°C) since fruit set
fails above about 32°C. Irrigation is heaviest during fruit swelling,
reduced starting around 25 days after flowering, and stopped entirely
about 10 days before harvest. Fruit is harvested roughly 35-38 days
after pollination in cool weather, or as few as 27-30 days in hot
weather, and does not ripen further once picked, so harvest is timed
carefully and done in the cool part of the day [2].

**Finding 4 — storage is short and chilling-sensitive.** The fruit
keeps about 10 days at room temperature, consistent with its thin
rind and climacteric ripening behaviour; recommended cold storage is
7-10°C at 90-95% relative humidity, since storage at 4°C causes a
distinctive browning of the fruit's suture tissue (a chilling-injury
symptom) [5]. Documented production disorders include "fermented
fruit" (an alcoholic, watery-fleshed defect worsened by excess
nitrogen, cold temperatures during fruit swelling, wet soil, certain
squash rootstocks, and fruit-set hormone use), fruit cracking
(triggered by large day-night temperature swings or sudden watering
near maturity), misshapen "gourd-shaped" or navel fruit, and an acute
wilt disorder. The most serious disease in protected cultivation is a
stem-rot disease, with powdery and downy mildew, aphids and mites also
occurring [2]. Separately, fruit grafted onto one particular squash
rootstock species can develop a measurable bitter-tasting compound
(cucurbitacin) under summer heat or drought stress [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual vine, grown as a fruit
  crop; protected forcing crops are started from winter plantings [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Indeterminate/continuous — repeated picks from
  successive fruit sets on later-order ("granddaughter") vines, rather
  than a single harvest [2].
- **Climate:** Temperate climates under protected cultivation
  (plastic houses, tunnels), extending into warm-temperate and
  subtropical open-field production in summer [1][2].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, including the thin rind and small seeds
  along with the flesh [1].
- **Pollination:** Required for fruit set. Most flowers are
  hermaphrodite with some separate male flowers; hermaphrodite pollen
  is relatively weak, so growers hand-pollinate using pollen from
  vigorous male flowers, or substitute fruit-set hormone sprays.
  Pollination window is tightly time-of-day- and temperature-
  dependent [2].
- **Yield:** A national average of roughly 40 t/ha is documented for a
  recent production year; well-managed protected (grafted, house-grown)
  crops are estimated to reach 50-60 t/ha, while open-field or stressed
  crops are estimated to fall to around 20 t/ha.
- **Water use:** Shallow-rooted, with roots spreading only about
  15-25 cm below the surface; water needs peak during fruit swelling,
  are reduced from about 25 days after flowering, and are cut off
  entirely roughly 10 days before harvest. Water shortage during fruit
  swelling causes small or misshapen fruit, while sudden watering near
  maturity causes cracking; both drought and waterlogging tolerance are
  low [2].
- **Establishment:** Grafted seedlings (scion grafted onto squash
  rootstock by a tongue or insertion graft), raised in the nursery for
  roughly 40-50 days before transplanting; planted shallow into raised,
  plastic-mulched beds, since deep planting increases risk of a serious
  stem-rot disease; minimum soil temperature for transplanting is about
  15°C [2].
- **Labor:** No directly sourced labor-hour figures were found; the
  crop is understood to be labor-intensive given its grafting,
  trellising and vine-training, daily hand-pollination or hormone
  application, and protected-structure venting and covering
  requirements.
- **Soil:** Organic, sandy loam preferred, pH 6.0-6.8; sandier soils
  are favoured for early crops and heavier clay loam for later, sweeter
  crops; documented fertilizer application rates are roughly 25 kg N,
  7.7 kg P and 16 kg K per 1,000 m² [2].
- **Stand life:** Annual.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A serious fungal stem-rot/wilt disease
  (the main reason grafting is considered essential), powdery and
  downy mildew, aphids, and mites [2].
- **Cultivars:** A handful of named Korean cultivars are documented,
  distinguished mainly by susceptibility to "fermented fruit" disorder;
  the Japanese form of this cultivar group has declined sharply since
  the 1920s as Western melons spread, though unripe fruit is still
  traditionally pickled there for a summer Buddhist festival; a related
  Chinese cultivar is documented as prone to bitterness when grafted
  onto one particular squash rootstock species in summer; and at least
  one newer premium Korean cultivar has been reported in limited
  commercial trial.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** scion and squash rootstock seed are raised as seedlings,
  grafted together, and planted out; mature fruit is graded, packed,
  and sold fresh or cool-stored at 7-10°C; fruit is also traditionally
  made into a Korean pickle, or, in Japan, pickled while still unripe
  as a seasonal preserve.
- Not covered by this research: fruit-set hormone application in
  detail (no corresponding growth-regulator process modeled);
  protected-structure heating and covering systems; frozen or
  fresh-cut melon processing.

## Open research questions

- Reported production-area and yield statistics conflict meaningfully
  between two sources (one implying a per-hectare yield roughly a
  tenth of the other for a similar production year); the lower, more
  internally consistent figure is treated as probably a decimal error
  in the original source and is not relied upon here.
- Whether commercial cultivars of this crop are F1 hybrids, and their
  seed-production methods, were not researched for this entry
  specifically (though a related melon crop this project has also
  researched may offer useful analogy).
- The specific fruit-set hormone compounds in routine use, and the
  broader question of whether this project should model a dedicated
  growth-regulator-spray activity, remain open.
- The exact number of successive fruit-set flushes per crop under the
  indeterminate harvest pattern was not sourced.
- Whether rootstock choice should be modeled as directly affecting
  finished-fruit quality (via the fermented-fruit and bitterness
  disorders both linked to specific rootstock choices) is a design
  question not resolved here.
- No religious or ceremonial use was found for this crop in the
  sources consulted; artistic depictions of melon-shaped pottery
  appear to be a cultural rather than ritual association.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 95-160 days from sowing to first
harvest) is derived from documented nursery duration (40-50 days)
combined with the sowing-to-harvest span across the fastest (warm,
open-field) and slowest (forcing, winter-started) documented cropping
systems.

Energy and protein content (roughly 300 kcal/kg and 8 g protein/kg) is
estimated by analogy to a related Western melon crop this project has
also researched, adjusted downward somewhat to reflect this crop's
documented lower sweetness and higher water content.

Yield tiers are estimated around the documented national average,
with well-managed protected systems placed above it and open-field or
stressed crops placed below.

Water-use crop-coefficient and stage-length parameters are carried
over by analogy from a related Western melon crop, since no
crop-specific water-use study was found for this entry; drought and
waterlogging tolerance are rated low based on the crop's documented
shallow rooting and strict water-timing requirements.

Peak daily yield (roughly 450 kg/ha/day) is derived by dividing the
documented national-average yield by an estimated harvest-window
length reflecting several successive fruit-set flushes; a short
on-vine standing period is used since the fruit does not ripen further
once picked and overripens quickly if left too long.
