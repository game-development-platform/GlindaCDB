## Naming and scope

The crisp East Asian pears, also called nashi, sand pear, or (somewhat
misleadingly) "apple pear" [1][4]. Three species groups are covered
here and treated as cultivar groups: Japanese and Korean cultivars from
one species, Chinese white pears from a second (hybrid-origin) species,
and a third, more cold-hardy species type [1][7]. A further distinct
species (the Korla fragrant pear) is noted only in passing, having
since been judged distinct enough to warrant its own crop entry. The
European pear is treated as an entirely separate crop.

This crop is genuinely distinct from European pear in several ways that
matter for cultivation: it is eaten crisp straight from the tree with
no off-tree ripening step (unlike the soft, melting texture of common
European pear cultivars); it is harvested ripe across several
color-based picking passes; its main producing countries rely heavily
on hand pollination and fruit bagging, plus unusually heavy hand
thinning; it uses room cooling rather than fast precooling and has
distinct storage disorders; and it has its own distinct market identity
and dominates total world pear production by volume. Pome-fruit water-
use analogies in this entry are drawn from a closely related fruit crop
this project has also researched, and from a standard international
crop-water-use reference's general apple/cherry/pear data.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — picked fully ripe and crisp, then handled almost like
eggs.** Unlike many Western pear cultivars, Asian pears remain firm and
are crisp and juicy when eaten fully ripe. They are picked ripe,
assessed by color, sugar content, and firmness, typically across at
least three separate picking passes per cultivar as the fruit matures
unevenly [1][2][3]. The skin abrades and bruises very easily: fruit is
twisted off the fruiting spur directly into padded trays and packed in
the field, since standard packing-line handling bruises it too much
[1][3]. Picking too late brings on specific storage disorders: internal
browning in the Chinese-type cultivars if picked too long after bloom,
and a separate flesh-spot decay disorder in large, overmature fruit of
the Japanese-type cultivars [1]. The harvest window therefore
represents a real quality trade-off — picking later adds sugar content
and size but increases the risk of serious storage disorders.

**Finding 2 — fruit size is actively engineered through thinning and
pollination management.** Since the market strongly favors large fruit,
trees are hand-thinned to a single fruit per fruiting spur, spaced
several centimeters apart, usually across two separate thinning rounds
— a labor-intensive process that chemical thinning cannot safely or
effectively replace [2][3]. Cultivars are partly self-fruitful, but
cross-pollinated fruit with more seeds tends to be larger and rounder;
genuine cross-incompatibilities exist between some cultivar pairs, and
at least one major cultivar is pollen-sterile [2][3]. In the two major
East Asian producing countries, flowers are commonly hand-pollinated
using collected pollen applied with feather sticks or dusters, a
process with documented but quite variable productivity per worker per
day; a sprayed-pollen alternative uses meaningfully less pollen per
tree [9]. Pollination and thinning labor are therefore a direct trade-
off against fruit size and sale price, with historically documented
price premiums of two to three times for large fruit over small fruit
of the same cultivar [2].

**Finding 3 — at least one major East Asian production system uses an
overhead trellis, fruit bagging, and very high labor input.** In that
system, branches are trained onto a flat overhead trellis at roughly
waist-to-shoulder height above a standing worker, with total labor
input documented in the range of roughly 2,000-3,000 hours per
hectare per year [9]. At least one major cultivar is susceptible to a
specific fungal disease (a region-specific pathotype of a common fungal
pathogen); growers protect against it by bagging each individual fruit
and spraying repeatedly, with bagging also improving fruit appearance
[10].

**Finding 4 — post-harvest storage behavior depends heavily on
cultivar.** Standard storage is near freezing at very high humidity, in
perforated plastic liners; water loss beyond a modest threshold
visibly shrivels the fruit [1]. Distinctively, fruit is room-cooled
rather than force-cooled — rapid cooling within a day of harvest was
found in at least one trial to actually increase a specific storage
disorder [1]. Storage life for good keepers runs several months,
followed by one to two weeks of additional shelf life once removed from
storage; documented cultivar-level storage life ranges from roughly a
month for one short-keeping cultivar to several months for a notably
long-keeping Chinese-type cultivar [1][2][3]. Some cultivars are non-
climacteric with very low ethylene production, while others are
genuinely climacteric — a real physiological split within what is
treated here as a single crop [1]. Fruit stored near apples develops
storage scald [1].

**Finding 5 — one country produces the large majority of the world's
pears, almost entirely of this crop's species.** Recent world pear
production figures show one country producing roughly three-quarters of
total world output, overwhelmingly of species covered by this entry
rather than European pear; two further East Asian countries contribute
meaningfully smaller but still substantial shares [2][7]. In world
agricultural statistics terms, "pear" production figures are therefore
mostly this crop, even though standard international databases do not
separate the species out individually.

**Finding 6 — a ritual and gift fruit in Korean culture.** Pears are
placed on the ancestral-rite offering table at two major Korean
holidays, with a specific color-based placement rule (white fruit,
including pear, placed on one particular side of the table) [11]. More
broadly across East Asia, pears are commonly given as a gift fruit [4].

**Finding 7 — dormancy is temperature-driven, not photoperiod-driven;
frost on this crop is purely a hazard with no quality-event upside; and
elevation acts as a direct chill substitute in low-latitude growing
regions, not just a general climate proxy.** As with apple and pear
generally, Asian pear's endodormancy progression is driven by low
temperature, not day length — no documented photoperiod effect was found
in flowering or any other process, across several reviews of the
underlying molecular dormancy pathway [27]. Frost is confirmed as purely
a hazard here: unlike the ice-cider tradition documented for high-chill
apple, at least one extension specialist explicitly states pears do not
benefit from frost exposure the way some apples or persimmons reportedly
do, and that frost instead accelerates breakdown and reduces storage
quality — sugar increases in this crop come from postharvest cold storage
and ethylene-triggered ripening, not from on-tree frost [28]. Elevation
does more than loosely correlate with climate: in Taiwan, the same
cultivar shows measurably different flower-bud development and necrosis
at roughly 2,000 m versus 500 m, and a specific high-chill cultivar is
grown only at high elevation there, while lower-chill cultivars stay in
the lowlands; in Thailand, commercial production is confined to northern
highland areas specifically because that elevation is the only way to get
mild-enough winters with a short-enough chilling period at that latitude
[29].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A deciduous perennial tree; training style
  varies by growing region (open vase-shaped training in at least one
  major Western growing region, versus a flat overhead trellis in at
  least one major East Asian growing region) [3][9].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one crop per year picked
  across at least three color-based passes per cultivar [2][3].
- **Climate:** Temperate to warm-temperate climates generally. Elevation
  substitutes directly for chill at low latitude rather than being a
  loose climate proxy: Thai production is confined to northern highlands
  specifically because that elevation is the only way to get a short-
  enough chilling period that near the equator, and in Taiwan the same
  cultivar shows measurably different flower-bud development at roughly
  2,000 m versus 500 m, with a specific high-chill cultivar restricted to
  high elevation there while lower-chill cultivars stay in the lowlands
  (Finding 7) [4][29].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; moderate-calorie, modest protein,
  meaningful fiber content [4].
- **Pollination:** Required; insect- (bee-) pollinated under normal
  commercial conditions, but widely supplemented or replaced by hand
  pollination in at least two major East Asian producing countries,
  particularly where natural pollinators are scarce or where cross-
  incompatibility and pollen-sterility issues require careful pollinizer
  management (Finding 2).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not photoperiod-driven. Endodormancy
  progression is governed by low temperature, not day length, consistent
  with pear and apple generally — no photoperiod effect was found in
  flowering or elsewhere across several reviews of the underlying
  molecular dormancy pathway (Finding 7) [27]. Not applicable to
  tuberization/bulbing, since this crop has no such organ.
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield (derived from one major
  producing country's output and area) sits in the low tens of tonnes
  per hectare, with well-managed mature orchards reaching considerably
  higher and a specific documented maximum-density Japanese fruit-load
  recommendation implying an even higher potential ceiling (see
  Estimates below). Yields generally run below those of common European
  pear because of the unusually heavy hand-thinning this crop requires.
- **Propagation:** Exclusively vegetative (grafted); rootstock choice
  strongly affects cold hardiness and dwarfing — at least one rootstock
  species dwarfs some Japanese-type cultivars by roughly half, while
  Chinese-type cultivars grow well on it. Grafted propagation carries a
  real clonal/viral degeneration risk: Pear vein yellows (caused by Apple
  stem pitting virus) is a documented graft-transmissible pear disease
  found in both orchards and nurseries in at least one country, and
  Apple stem grooving virus and Apple chlorotic leaf spot virus have also
  been detected in pear import surveys. Clean-stock control exists via
  the US National Clean Plant Network's lead pome/stone-fruit center,
  which maintains virus-tested foundation stock for distribution to
  certified nurseries, with further state-level certification programs
  in at least two major pear-growing US states [30].
- **Water use:** Root depth commonly 1.0-2.0 m by analogy to related
  pome-fruit water-use data; frequent, even irrigation is specifically
  recommended for good fruit size, and uneven watering or heavy rain
  shortly before harvest is linked to a specific storage disorder
  (Finding 4).
- **Labor:** Extremely labor-intensive relative to many other tree
  fruits — hand thinning (and, in at least one major producing system,
  fruit bagging) represents one of the largest labor demands, with hand
  pollination and multi-pass harvest adding substantially more; total
  labor input in at least one intensive East Asian system runs into the
  low thousands of hours per hectare per year (Finding 3).
- **Soil:** A near-neutral soil pH is assumed by analogy to related
  pome fruits (not independently sourced for this crop specifically);
  planting too deep is linked to a root-rot disorder, so shallow
  planting is recommended. Salinity tolerance, distinct from pH, is
  rootstock-dependent and genuinely variable within this crop's own
  species group: one seedling trial found a related wild pear rootstock
  species showed no visible injury even at a high salt concentration via
  restricted sodium/chloride transport to the shoot, while this crop's
  main cultivated species was itself rated salt-sensitive, with leaf
  injury, defoliation, and death at elevated salt levels in the same
  trial — these are controlled seedling/solution trials, not field soil-
  salinity studies [31].
- **Stand life:** First crop around the third growing season after
  planting [3]; fruit is borne on spurs of several years' wood, with
  the youngest one-to-three-year-old spurs considered most productive.
  Overall productive lifespan was not well researched, though old
  trees are known to need spur removal and rejuvenation pruning.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A bacterial disease (fire blight) affects
  nearly all cultivars to some degree, with resistance varying
  meaningfully by cultivar; a specific fungal disease is a major concern
  in at least one major producing country; a region-specific fungal
  scab and several insect pests (a fruit-boring moth, a psyllid, and
  mites) are also documented.
- **Cultivars:** A very large number of named cultivars exist across
  the three species groups, differing substantially in fruit size,
  color, flavor, bloom and harvest timing, storage life, chill
  requirement, disease resistance, and ripening physiology (climacteric
  versus non-climacteric).

## Hazards

Fire blight affects nearly all cultivars to some degree (one named
cultivar is notably more resistant); a region-specific fungal disease is
a major concern in at least one producing country; a separate regional
fungal scab, a fruit-boring moth requiring several insecticide sprays
per season, a psyllid pest, and mites are also documented. No specific
fruit-toxicity hazards were found in the sources consulted.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Harvest (hand-twisted into padded field trays across
  several color-based passes) → room-cooled → cold storage in
  perforated liners → fresh market. Fruit or culls are also dried into
  slices. Juice production was not researched for this crop
  specifically.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned and frozen processing
  (this fruit's low acidity requires added acidification for safe home
  canning), pollen collection and hand-pollination technique in detail,
  fruit bagging technique, overhead-trellis construction, fresh-cut
  slicing (which has a very short shelf life even with standard anti-
  browning treatments), and wine, vinegar, or folk-remedy uses.

Pears, including this crop specifically, are placed on the ancestral-
rite offering table at two major Korean holidays, following a specific
color-based placement convention among the offered fruits. More broadly
across East Asia, this fruit is a common gift item, and in at least one
culture there is a folk taboo against sharing (splitting) a pear
between people, based on a wordplay association with separation.

## Open research questions

- Chill-hour requirements conflict between cultivar-specific scientific
  studies and nursery marketing claims for at least one major cultivar
  — not reconciled.
- Production figures for at least one major East Asian producing
  country conflict notably between an older trade-literature estimate
  and a more recent official statistic.
- Fire-blight resistance for at least one major cultivar is described
  inconsistently (susceptible in one source, somewhat resistant in
  another).
- A specific fruit-per-acre figure from one source for a major East
  Asian production system could not be fully reconciled with yield
  figures derived from fruit size and tree density.
- Fruit mass and several derived yield-tier figures rely on an
  estimated typical fruit weight rather than a directly sourced average
  across cultivars.
- Labor-hour figures outside the one documented intensive East Asian
  system are largely estimated rather than independently sourced.
- Soil pH preference is assumed by analogy to related pome fruits
  rather than independently researched for this crop.
- Frost-kill thresholds by bloom stage, soil nutrient uptake, and juice
  yield were not researched for this crop.
- The photoperiod, viral-propagation, salinity, and elevation sources
  added 2026-11-08 were accessed at search-snippet depth only; none was
  independently opened and verified in full.
- A "Pear blister canker viroid" appeared only in passing in an older
  regional list of graft-transmissible pear diseases and could not be
  confirmed as a currently recognized agent in this pass — treated as
  unconfirmed rather than included as a sourced finding.

## Estimates and derivations

Growth-cycle length to first harvest (roughly 730-1,100 days from
planting) is derived from a documented "first crop in the third
growing season" figure; the ramp to full production (roughly 2,700
days) is derived from documented yield figures rising from a lower
level in years five through seven to a plateau around years ten through
fourteen.

Yield tiers are derived from several distinct figures: a world-average
figure from one major producing country's output divided by its growing
area; a good-yield figure estimated from a documented typical fruit
count per tree and per-area tree density, multiplied by an estimated
typical fruit weight; an exceptional-yield figure from a documented
maximum recommended fruit-per-area figure for intensive Japanese
production, similarly converted; and a poor-yield figure from a
documented lower-bound packed-fruit yield in a young orchard (years
five through seven), similarly converted.

A seasonal water-use-days index and regional seasonal water totals are
derived by analogy to a standard international crop-coefficient table
for the general apple/cherry/pear tree-fruit group, since no
crop-specific figure was found; a drought-buffer estimate (roughly 10
days before yield penalty begins) is derived from standard soil-water-
holding assumptions, adjusted downward given this crop's documented
sensitivity to uneven watering.

Tree density per hectare is derived by unit-converting documented
per-acre planting-density figures for both vigorous and dwarfing
rootstock systems.

Labor-hour figures are derived from documented per-tree thinning-time
figures (multiplied by tree density and number of thinning rounds),
an assumed hand-picking rate reflecting careful multi-pass harvest, and
documented hand-pollination productivity per worker per day; the
resulting total broadly matches an independently documented total labor
figure for one intensive East Asian production system.

A representative storage-life figure (roughly 120 days) and a peak-
yield-per-day figure (derived from dividing a good-yield figure by an
assumed harvest-window length per cultivar) are similarly derived
rather than directly sourced as single figures.
