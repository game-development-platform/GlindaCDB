# Bush Cherries — Nanking Cherry (*Prunus tomentosa*) and Sand Cherry (*P. pumila*, incl. var. *besseyi*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers two unrelated small "cherry" shrubs grouped together
because they are farmed the same way:

- **Nanking cherry** (*Prunus tomentosa*) — also called Chinese bush,
  downy, or Manchu cherry; Chinese 毛樱桃 (mao ying tao, "hairy cherry")
  [1][8].
- **Sand cherry** (*Prunus pumila*) — four varieties exist, chiefly the
  western sand cherry, var. *besseyi*, native to the northern Great
  Plains [2][5].

Not covered here: sand-cherry × plum hybrids (named cultivars such as
'Sapa' and 'Opata', and the ornamental purple-leaf sand cherry, *P.* ×
*cistena*) are treated as separate hybrid plants rather than part of
either species group [2][8]; and the dwarf sour cherries bred for
Canadian prairie conditions, which this project treats as a separate
crop entry entirely.

Both are "cherries" in name only — taxonomic placement is disputed, with
one general reference source placing Nanking cherry closer to the plums
(subgenus *Prunus*) and a 2025 genetics paper placing it instead in
subgenus *Cerasus* [1][6]; this conflict is not resolved here, and is not
relevant to how the crop is grown.

Both species are notably hardier and more drought-tolerant once
established than the sweet, sour, and dwarf sour cherries this project
has also researched, and unlike those crops they are simply grown from
seed rather than propagated as named cultivars (Nanking cherry has no
named cultivars in general US cultivation) [3]. Their fruit is also
markedly smaller (5-18 mm, 0.4-2.9 g, with a large stone relative to
fruit size) and extremely soft, tearing at the stem on picking (a
"weeping scar") and giving a very short shelf life [1][2][3][8]. Both
species are also used as edible hedges and windbreaks, and as dwarfing
rootstock for plums, apricots, and cherries [1][3][5]. Both grow wild as
well as cultivated — Nanking cherry fruit has been gathered from wild and
semi-wild plants in China for over 2,000 years, and sand cherry is native
to sandy shores, dunes, and prairie in its range [2][6].

The two species are treated as one combined entry (with separate
species-group notes) rather than two separate crop entries, since both
are seed-grown hardy shrubs, hand-picked for jam, juice, and fresh
eating, with broadly similar storage and site requirements.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — among the hardiest, toughest small cherry-like fruits
grown.** Nanking cherry tolerates swings as extreme as roughly -40°F to
90°F within a single growing season in one documented US trial region,
is rated hardy to a very cold USDA zone, and is reported to endure
roughly -40°C in its native Chinese range [1][3][6]. Sand cherry survives
roughly -50°C while dormant [5]. Both species share a weak spot, however:
neither performs well in hot, humid climates, with Nanking cherry
specifically noted to struggle in the southern United States [1]. Pest
and disease pressure includes spider mites, a cherry fruit fly, a black
cherry aphid, a fungal shoot-blight disease, borers, and brown rot under
stress [1][3].

**Finding 2 — Nanking cherry cultivation specifics.** The plant is a
multi-stemmed shrub reaching roughly 2.4-3.7 m (up to 4.6 m). It is
grown from seed after about 100 days of cold-moist stratification near
freezing; fall sowing stratifies the seed naturally outdoors. Two or more
plants are needed for pollination. Spacing runs 1.8-3 m in hedge
plantings, or 2.4-4.6 m for single shrubs. Bearing begins in year 3-4, at
roughly 7-23 kg per bush depending on size. Bloom coincides with apricot
bloom (and so carries similar frost risk), with fruit ripening in June in
one documented US trial region. The fruit itself is tiny (roughly 1.3 cm)
and sweet to tart, but so soft that it is difficult to pick without
damage — a trait specifically identified as the reason the species fell
out of commercial favor. It holds on the bush for 2-3 weeks after
ripening, and freezing is the usual preservation method. Pruning
involves removing 3-5 old canes each late winter. The species is "very
drought tolerant after initial establishment." A survey of 44 Chinese
accessions found fruit size ranging 0.4-2.85 g, with small fruit size
identified as the main obstacle to further commercial development. The
fruit is notably high in total phenolic compounds (flavonols, tannins)
and antioxidant activity [3][7][8].

**Finding 3 — sand cherry: a prairie and dune shrub.** The plant is
smaller, at roughly 0.6-1.8 m, and forms clonal colonies by root
sprouts. Fruit is glossy purple-black, 13-18 mm, sweetish but astringent
— described in one source as making "a rather astringent but tasty
jelly" — and can be dried for storage; typical uses are pies, jellies,
and jams. It is hardy to a cold USDA zone range (roughly -50°C dormant)
and drought-resistant once established. As rootstock, it is compatible
with most plums; the resulting trees are dwarf, poorly anchored, and
sucker severely, but are extremely cold-hardy and resistant to crown
gall. Named selections include 'Black Beauty' and a selection from an
early-20th-century American plant breeder, who also used this species as
the parent of a line of cold-hardy cherry-plum hybrids [2][5][8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Deciduous shrubs — Nanking cherry
  multi-stemmed, 2.4-3.7 m; sand cherry smaller and suckering, 0.6-1.8 m.
  Nanking cherry blooms very early (with apricots) and ripens in June in
  one documented trial region; sand cherry blooms in May, with ripening
  timing not independently sourced.
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate to temperate, continental, and semi-arid
  climates across a wide cold-hardiness range; both species perform
  poorly in hot, humid climates (Finding 1).
- **Food status / composition:** Raw-edible. Caloric and protein content
  are carried over by analogy from a related cherry crop this project
  has also researched, since no direct composition figure was found for
  either species specifically.
- **Pollination:** Nanking cherry's pollination requirement is disputed
  between sources — one source states two or more plants are required,
  while others describe it as partly self-fertile; both are
  insect-pollinated. Sand cherry is insect-pollinated, with
  self-fertility not stated in the sources reviewed.
- **Yield:** No per-hectare yield figure was found directly for either
  species; a yield estimate is derived below from per-bush figures and an
  assumed hedge-planting density. No yield figure at all was found for
  sand cherry.
- **Water use:** Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from a
  related cherry crop this project has also researched, adjusted
  downward to reflect both species' documented high drought tolerance
  once established — both need regular irrigation through their first
  summer, but become notably drought-tolerant afterward, and both
  dislike waterlogged soil, preferring well-drained, often sandy ground.
- **Propagation:** Both are typically grown from seed (seedlings);
  cuttings and layering are also possible, and sand cherry also spreads
  by root sprouts. Nanking cherry seed needs about 100 days of
  cold-moist stratification; sand cherry needs 2-3 months, with
  germination sometimes taking up to 18 months. Neither has a hybrid
  seed system — the sand-cherry × plum hybrids are separate clonal
  cultivars, not an F1 seed trade.
- **Soil:** Well-drained soil is strongly preferred, sandy for both
  species; Nanking cherry tolerates alkaline soil to around pH 8 or
  higher, though another source gives a narrower range. Nutrient-uptake
  figures were not found for either species.
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour study exists for either species; hand
  harvest of the soft, easily damaged fruit is explicitly identified as
  the main obstacle to commercial production, and is assumed to dominate
  total labor hours.
- **Storage:** Both species have a very short shelf life — the fruit is
  soft and tears at the stem — with freezing the usual preservation
  method; no formal cold-storage regime was sourced for either.
- **Stand life:** Not directly sourced for either species; old canes are
  renewed yearly in Nanking cherry, and a related ornamental sand-cherry
  hybrid is reported to live up to 20 years.
- **Cultivars:** Nanking cherry is mostly seed-grown with few named
  forms (a white-fruited form, and several further named forms whose
  traits were not researched in detail); the sand cherry group includes
  two named selections and the western sand cherry species group itself.
- **Controlled-environment viability:** Not researched in depth; container
  and bonsai use is noted for Nanking cherry in at least one general
  reference source.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include spider mites, a cherry fruit fly,
a black cherry aphid, a fungal shoot-blight disease, and — under stress
— borers and brown rot.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other stone-fruit species in the genus
*Prunus*, the leaves and seeds of both species contain cyanogenic
glycosides (a bitter taste signaling the toxicity). Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** hand-picked fruit → same-day sorting → eaten fresh, or made
  into juice, jam, jelly, preserves, pies, or wine; sand cherries can
  also be dried for storage; stones → stratified → grown on as seedlings
  for hedges or rootstock; prunings → cuttings.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-fruit handling in
  detail; pickled unripe Nanking cherry fruit (noted in sources but not
  independently researched); the sand-cherry × plum hybrid cultivars;
  dye uses of the leaves and fruit.

## Open research questions

- Whether these two unrelated species are better modeled as one combined
  crop entry or two separate ones is a genuine judgement call; they are
  treated as one entry here because they are farmed identically, but a
  future revision could reasonably split them, since distinct harvested
  products for each species already exist in principle.
- Nanking cherry's pollination requirement is disputed between sources
  (cross-pollination required versus partly self-fertile) — not
  resolved here.
- No per-hectare yield figure was found for either species directly; the
  figure used here is derived from per-bush yield and an assumed planting
  density (see Estimates below), and sand cherry has no yield figure at
  all.
- Whether unripe Nanking cherry fruit (reported as a pickling ingredient
  in at least one source) represents a genuine second harvest stage was
  not researched.
- Taxonomic placement of Nanking cherry (closer to plums versus a
  distinct cherry subgenus) is disputed between sources, though this
  does not appear to affect cultivation practice.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein content are carried over by analogy from a related
cherry crop this project has also researched, given the broad
morphological similarity of a small, tart, stone-bearing fruit; a direct
composition analysis for either species would improve on this.

Yield tiers (roughly 1,500-15,000 kg/ha across poor-to-exceptional
production) are derived from Nanking cherry's documented per-bush yield
range (roughly 7-23 kg per mature bush) combined with an assumed hedge
planting density of about 650 bushes/ha, itself derived from the
documented in-hedge spacing range and an assumed row spacing; sand cherry
yield is not estimated at all, for lack of any sourced figure.

Water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from a related cherry
crop this project has also researched, adjusted to reflect both species'
documented high drought tolerance once established — the standard
analogy figures likely overstate actual water need for these two species.

Labor-hour estimates assume hand-picking of small, soft fruit at a
judgement-call picking rate, with no mechanized harvest alternative
known for either species; this produces a much higher estimated
harvest-labor share than typical tree-fruit crops, consistent with
picking difficulty being the stated limiting factor on commercial
production.

Storage duration (about 2 days) and a rapid post-harvest quality-decay
rate are estimated from the documented "very short shelf life" and
weeping-stem-scar trait, and from one source's note that ripe fruit
holds acceptably on the bush for 2-3 weeks even though it deteriorates
quickly once picked.

Peak-harvest yield rate is derived by assuming a roughly two-week
synchronous ripening window, consistent with sourced notes that Nanking
cherry fruit ripens all at once and resists rain-induced cracking.
