## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus lakoocha* Roxb. (Moraceae), the lakoocha, lakuch or monkey
jack [1][2]. Current botanical databases accept the name *A. lacucha*
Buch.-Ham., with *A. lakoocha* as a synonym [10]. This entry keeps the
project row name `lakoocha`, since that is the name every source used
here prints.
- **Local names:**
  - **Nepal:** badahar or arhar [1][3].
  - **India:** lakuch, dhau, dephal, badhal [1]; *chama* or *dewaphol*
    in Tripura, and *barhal* in Bengal [2].
  - **Thailand:** *ma-haad* or *lokhat* [1][7].
  - **Elsewhere:** *myankdok* in Burma and *tampang* in Malay [1].
- **Products covered:** the fruit and male flower spikes; lopped leaf
  fodder; and the heartwood extract (*puag-haad*). Timber, latex, tannin
  and dye are noted under Uses.

**Gate check (2026-11-19): promoted.** Proposed in `jackfruit.md`. It
passes because:
- **Taxonomy:** a distinct, deciduous *Artocarpus* species with small
  fruit (200-350 g), not a jackfruit form [1][2].
- **Range:** native from the sub-Himalaya through South and Southeast
  Asia: Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, India, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar,
  Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Vietnam [1]. It grows at 150-1,600 m
  with a dry season of up to 3 months [1]. Jackfruit, by contrast, is a
  lowland evergreen.
- **Farming system:** it has three systems of its own.
  - **Nepal:** a planted and lopped fodder tree of the foothills and
    midhills, ranked by farmers among the best for milk [1][3].
  - **India and Bangladesh:** a homestead fruit tree, grown singly in
    backyards and on waste ground [2][5].
  - **Thailand:** its heartwood is sold in herbal markets for a
    traditional anthelmintic and a cosmetic skin-whitening extract
    [6][7].
- **Harvest and uses:** fruit, leaf fodder, heartwood extract and
  timber come off the same tree [1][6]. Jackfruit has none of the
  fodder-lopping or heartwood-extract chains.

**Separate entries:**
- jackfruit (`jackfruit`), `jackfruit.md`, which first proposed this
  row;
- breadfruit (`breadfruit`), `breadfruit.md`, and champedak
  (`champedak`), `champedak.md`, other *Artocarpus* fruits.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a fodder tree first in Nepal.**
- **Value:** highly valued as a fodder tree in the lower Himalayan
  foothills. It is fed to lactating animals and is "considered one of
  the most important milk producing forages" [1]. Farmers rank badahar
  and *Bauhinia purpurea* (tanki) at the top for feeding value [3].
- **Yield per tree:** 60-200 kg of fresh fodder a year [1], or 36-270
  kg with an average of 128 kg for a mature tree [3].
- **Lopping:** begins when the tree is about four years old [3]. The
  main fodder season is November to February. Old trees can be lopped
  twice, in February-March and again in April-May from the new flush
  [3].
- **Community forests:** at five years old, community-forest plantings
  in the western midhills averaged 4.5 cm diameter, 3.5 m height and
  5.14 kg of green twigs per tree. That was about twice as much as
  *Bauhinia purpurea* [3].
- **Fodder protein:** about 16% [1]. A dry-season study near Pokhara
  found 100-138 g/kg of dry matter (10.0-13.8%) and an unusually high
  ash content of 154-184 g/kg [4] (Open research questions).

**Finding 2 — a scattered homestead fruit with no cultivars.**
- **Tripura survey, 2015-2017:** six seedling types were found in
  homestead gardens or growing singly on undulating waste ground [2].
  They yielded:
  - 58.5-98 kg of fruit per tree (mean 75.1 kg);
  - 250-390 fruits per tree;
  - fruits of 150-350 g [2].
- **Cultivars:** India has no established cultivars [2].
- **Bangladesh:** wide genetic variation is found in coastal
  homesteads, where the tree survives without management [5].
- **Decline:** populations are falling from heavy cutting for fuel and
  timber and from poor seed viability [1][2].

**Finding 3 — the heartwood is a Thai drug and cosmetic raw material.**
- **Traditional use:** a boiled water extract of the heartwood,
  *puag-haad*, is a yellow solid. It is used against tapeworm and
  roundworm [7].
- **Active compound:** oxyresveratrol, which inhibits tyrosinase about
  20 times more strongly than resveratrol [7].
- **Cosmetic use:** a heartwood extract in propylene glycol lightened
  skin within 4 weeks in a 12-week trial on women volunteers. That was
  faster than 3% kojic acid, at 6 weeks [6].
- **Content:** oxyresveratrol made up 6.80% w/w of a water extract and
  7.66% of an ethanol extract [7]. Snippet-level reports give 70-80% in
  purified *puag-haad* and 0.31% in a crude ethanol Soxhlet extract
  [10].
- **Trade:** the raw heartwood is bought as dried powder from
  traditional drugstores in Bangkok [6][7]. No production volume or
  plantation data was found.

**Finding 4 — hard to propagate true to type.**
- **Seed:** recalcitrant. Seeds lose viability within a week inside
  the fruit. They must be kept above 40% moisture and stored at 5 °C
  [1].
- **Grafting:** cleft grafts on seedling rootstock reached at most 40%
  success, and below 20% for three of five scions (Bangladesh,
  2020-2021) [5].
- **No method yet:** "No suitable vegetative propagation technology has
  yet been developed." Seed gives mixed offspring, and the tree is
  difficult to root [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Medium to large deciduous tree with a
  spreading crown, dropping its leaves briefly at the start of the dry
  season [1]. Trees in Tripura were 5.2-6.9 m tall with a 4.2-6.3 m
  canopy [2]; 6-9 m in another description [9].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial; one fruit crop a year [1][2], plus one
  or two fodder loppings [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Seedlings bear in about five years
  [10]. Lopping for fodder starts at about four years [3].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Elevation:** 150-1,600 m [1]; from the Terai to about 1,600 m in
    Nepal [3]; up to 1,200 m in the humid sub-Himalaya [9].
  - **Temperature and rain:** mean annual temperature 17-24 °C; rainfall
    700-2,000 mm [1].
  - **Cold:** young seedlings are badly damaged by frost; do not plant in
    frosty areas [1].
  - **Chill:** not applicable.
- **Composition:**
  - **Fruit (Tripura):** moisture 79.8-87.1%; TSS 12.9-20.2 °Brix;
    acidity 0.87-2.66%; ascorbic acid 168-182 mg/100 g of pulp [2].
  - **Fruit (Indian food table, through [2]):** moisture 89.6%; protein
    1.6%; fat 1.2%; minerals 1.1%; fibre 2.8%; carbohydrate 13.9%;
    calcium 0.067%; phosphorus 0.025% [2].
  - **Fruit (Bangladesh, Jahan 2011, through [8]):** ascorbic acid 171.07
    mg/100 g; β-carotene 3,718 µg/100 g; sodium 46 mg; potassium 785 mg;
    iron 15.09 mg; copper 1.31 mg; manganese 3.76 mg; phosphorus 66.06
    mg per 100 g [8].
  - **A low outlier:** a 2026 review gives about 75.5% moisture and 13.4
    mg/100 g vitamin C [10].
  - **Fruit structure:** pulp makes up 102-216 g of a 150-350 g fruit;
    5-14 seeds per fruit [2]; 10-30 seeds elsewhere [1].
  - **Leaf fodder:** see Finding 1 [1][4].
- **Edibility:** Raw-edible when ripe; the fruit and male flowers are
  also boiled, steamed or roasted [1]. Raw fruit and male spikes are
  acid and astringent when young [2].
- **Pollination:** Monoecious, with separate male and female heads on
  the same tree [1][2]. Fragrant flowers suggest insect pollination [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Fruit per tree:** 58.5-98 kg [2]; about 80 kg [1].
  - **Fodder per tree:** 36-270 kg fresh, averaging 128 kg [3]; 60-200
    kg [1].
  - **Fodder plantation:** 400 kg/ha from 18-month-old plants at 1 × 1 m
    [1] (see Estimates).
  - **Seed:** about 50 g per kg of fruit [1].
- **Water use:** Not sourced. Withstands a dry season of up to 3 months
  and needs a good supply of soil moisture [1].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** the usual method; recalcitrant (Finding 4) [1][5].
  - **Grafting:** cleft grafting up to 40% success [5].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** none reported. Nearly all trees are
    seedlings, so the main limit is variability, not clonal disease
    [2][5].
- **Labor:** Not sourced. Fodder plantations need thorough weeding and
  fertiliser [1].
- **Soil:** Deep, permeable soils with good moisture; does not thrive on
  poor sites [1]. Often found along stream banks in lowlands [1].
  Salinity tolerance not sourced, though it grows in coastal Bangladesh
  homesteads [5].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for fruit. Ripe fruit is
  quickly eaten by monkeys and birds, so it must be picked from the tree
  [1].
- **Establishment:** Seedlings tolerate moderate shade, but older trees
  need full light [1]. Fodder plantations at 1 × 1 m [1]. Community
  forest plantings were made on open land in 1998-1999 [3].
- **Cultivars:** None established [2]. Selections include:
  - **India:** six Tripura types; Type-3 was the best for fresh eating
    [2].
  - **Bangladesh:** five conserved mother trees (Dumki-Al-S1 to S4 and
    Kalapara-Al-S5) [5].
- **Weather response:** Frost kills young seedlings [1]. No other
  sourced data.
- **Water regime:** Rainfed [1][2][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Nepal:** flowers in April; ripe fruit from late June to early
    August [1]. Fodder mainly November-February [3].
  - **Tripura:** flowers December-February; fruits May-July [2].
  - **Seed collection:** only from yellow fruit; seeds from green fruit
    have low viability [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced. The tree's leaves can be used as mulch
  [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Not sourced. Seeds are dispersed by
  birds and monkeys [1]. The leaf fungus *Pseudocercospora artocarpi*
  attacks it in the native range [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Latex:** a sticky white latex is present in all parts of the tree,
  including the seeds [1].
- **Wildlife:** monkeys and birds take the ripe fruit [1].
- **Heartwood extract:** used medicinally as an anthelmintic, so dose
  matters; a 1989 Thai clinical study set the dose for tapeworm
  treatment [7]. Oxyresveratrol is also reported to damage DNA in one
  laboratory study, seen as a title only [10].
- **Conservation:** wild and homestead populations are declining [1][2].
- **Fodder tannins:** leaf tannin activity varied widely within and
  between trees [4]; its effect on livestock was not measured there.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the safety of the heartwood extract as a drug and
cosmetic, and any allergen data for the fruit, are deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh fruit):** ripe fruit eaten raw [1][2].
- **Chain 2 (cooked and preserved fruit):** unripe fruit made into
  curries, pickles and sauces [8]; pickle, ready-to-serve drinks,
  nectar, squash and jelly [2]; chutney, and pickled male spikes [9].
- **Chain 3 (fodder):** leaves lopped and fed to milking cattle and
  buffalo [1][3].
- **Chain 4 (heartwood extract):** heartwood → dried powder sold in herbal
  markets → boiled water extract (*puag-haad*) for anthelmintic use, or
  glycol or ethanol extracts for cosmetics [6][7].
- **Chain 5 (wood and bark):**
  - **Timber:** hard, termite-resistant wood of about 640 kg/m³, used for
    heavy construction, poles, beams, furniture, boats and plywood [1].
  - **Firewood:** an important source of fuel [1].
  - **Bark:** contains 8-9% tannin and is chewed like betel nut [1];
    8.5% tannin in another source [9].
  - **Dye:** wood and roots give a dye [1].
  - **Seed fat:** a light yellow, viscous liquid [1].
- **Folk medicine:** the root is used as an astringent and purgative,
  and the bark for headache [1].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced.

## Open research questions

- **Fodder protein:** about 16% [1] against 10.0-13.8% of dry matter in
  the dry season [4].
- **Fruit vitamin C and moisture:** 168-182 mg/100 g [2] and 171 mg [8]
  against 13.4 mg [10]; moisture 79.8-89.6% [2] against 75.5% [10].
- **Oxyresveratrol content:** 6.8-7.7% [7] against 70-80% [10] and
  0.31% [10]. These figures may be for different extract types.
- **Fodder plantation yield:** 400 kg/ha is very low per plant (see
  Estimates). The source does not say whether it is per cut or per year.
- **Not sourced:** water use, labour, fruit storage, salinity, soil
  biology, invasiveness listings, Thai heartwood production volumes,
  market prices and controlled-environment use.

## Estimates and derivations

**Tripura yield check** [2]:
- Mean: 321.66 fruits × 241.66 g = 77.7 kg, close to the reported mean
  of 75.06 kg.
- Type-3: 280 × 0.350 kg = 98.0 kg, which matches.

**Edible share** [2]: mean pulp 151.07 g ÷ mean fruit 241.66 g = 62.5%.

**Seed per tree** [1]: 80 kg × 50 g/kg = 4 kg of seed, which at
1,600-5,000 seeds/kg is 6,400-20,000 seeds.

**Fodder dry matter** (an estimate). Average mature yield 128 kg fresh
[3]. The 52.09% moisture measured on five-year-old twigs [3] gives 128 ×
(1 − 0.5209) = 61.3 kg of dry matter per tree per year. At 10-14% crude
protein [4], that is about 6.1-8.6 kg of protein.

**Fodder plantation** [1]: 1 × 1 m = 10,000 plants/ha, so 400 kg ÷
10,000 = 0.04 kg per plant. That is far below the 5.14 kg per five-year
tree in [3], which suggests a single early cut.

**Flower to ripe fruit:**
- Nepal: April to late June-early August, about 2.5-4 months [1].
- Tripura: December-February to May-July, about 3-7 months [2].
