## Naming and scope

The Australian finger lime, *Citrus australasica* (formerly classified as
*Microcitrus australasica*), covering its many named cultivars including
the red-fleshed var. *sanguinea* and the various colour forms [1][2].

Not included in this entry: hybrids between finger lime and ordinary
citrus (several named hybrids exist, some of which lack the fruit's
signature caviar-like juice vesicles), and the other Australian limes
(round lime, desert lime, Russell River lime, Mount White lime), which
are distinct species [2].

Finger lime is a clear case for its own crop entry, distinct from other
limes and lemons (closely related citrus this project has also
researched), on several fronts that matter for cultivation modeling: the
fruit is harvested and sold specifically for its free, spherical juice
vesicles ("citrus caviar"), spooned out whole as a garnish, rather than
pressed or juiced like ordinary citrus [1][2]; the plant itself is a very
thorny rainforest understorey shrub, with thorn damage downgrading close
to half the crop in some operations [2][4]; it shows useful tolerance to
citrus greening disease (huanglongbing), now endemic in at least one
major citrus-growing region, making it valuable as a breeding donor
[1][4][6]; its fruit does not ripen off the tree and has its own distinct
storage requirements [2]; and it commands its own premium specialty
market entirely separate from mainstream citrus [4].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a citrus crop sold for its "caviar".** The fruit's
translucent internal vesicles are spherical and nearly free-flowing
rather than fused into juice sacs; when the fruit is cut lengthwise, the
vesicles can be spooned out whole [2]. This "caviar" is used fresh as a
garnish for seafood and oysters, in sparkling drinks, and in desserts; a
freeze-dried powder is a secondary product [2][5]. The outer peel's oil
is kept away from the vesicles during processing, since it would spoil
their delicate flavour [2]. Both peel and pulp show an unusually wide
colour range for a single citrus species, from green-black to yellow and
crimson peel, and clear, green, pink, or crimson pulp [1][2].

**Finding 2 — thorns, wind, and sun decide the grade.** The plant bears
straight thorns roughly 1.3 cm long at its nodes; seedling trees carry
far more thorns than mature selected cultivars, which is why commercial
plantings are grown from grafted or top-worked selections rather than
seedlings [2]. Fruit rubbing against branches and thorns in the wind
marks the skin, and the fruit also sunburns readily, making windbreaks
important; thorn injury alone can push close to half of a harvest into
second-grade or processing categories [2][4]. Trees are trained to a
small number of open leaders kept under about 3 m, sometimes with a
wedge cut out of the canopy for picker access, lower branches skirted
off the ground, and pruning done after harvest [2][5].

**Finding 3 — a lightly fed, carefully watered citrus.** Fertiliser
requirements are notably lower than mainstream citrus, at roughly a
quarter to a third of standard commercial citrus nitrogen-phosphorus-
potassium rates; growers commonly withhold nitrogen entirely from
flowering until young fruit reach a small size, since high nitrogen
specifically suppresses flowering, and zinc and boron deficiencies are
the most commonly corrected micronutrients [2][4][5]. Water requirements
are comparatively modest in absolute terms but management is exacting:
the soil should never be allowed to dry out for more than about a week,
since trees that show visible water stress are described as very
difficult to recover, while on the other end saturated soil promotes
root rot [5]. The species' native habitat receives substantial annual
rainfall on acidic soils [2][5].

**Finding 4 — fast to bear when grafted.** Grafted trees, budded onto
rootstocks that include one which specifically protects against
Phytophthora, fruit within roughly one to three years depending on
region, reaching stable production by the third year; rooted cuttings
strike at a high rate with hormone treatment and bear in four to five
years, while seedlings can take up to fifteen years to flower, which is
why commercial production relies on clonal propagation [2][3]. Mature
trees at five to six years commonly yield in the range of 18-23 kg per
tree under good management [2][4]. Fruit matures roughly five months
after flowering, with repeated hand-picking passes roughly every ten to
fourteen days over a harvest season that can run six to eight weeks or
considerably longer depending on climate; in at least one tropical
location the species flowers and fruits essentially year-round [2][5].

**Finding 5 — disease tolerance makes it a breeding donor.** Research has
documented finger lime tolerating the bacterium responsible for
huanglongbing (citrus greening) without the severe decline typical of
mainstream citrus varieties, associated with redox-control and defence
gene activity and better phloem regeneration than a reference sweet
orange cultivar [6]. Separate preliminary trial work found no detectable
bacterium in one breeding line under active evaluation [3], and other
research has reported a finger-lime-derived peptide capable of killing
the bacterium and priming plant immune response [8]. This tolerance makes
the species an active candidate donor in breeding programs aimed at
developing huanglongbing-tolerant citrus more broadly [5][6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A very thorny evergreen shrub or small tree,
  naturally reaching 6-10 m in the wild but trained under roughly 3 m in
  cultivation; perennial, non-deciduous, with no chilling requirement
  documented [1][2][4].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, with repeated hand-picking
  passes roughly every ten to fourteen days over an extended season;
  fruit is picked at full size and colour development, non-climacteric
  (it does not ripen further once picked) [2].
- **Climate:** Subtropical in its native range, with successful
  plantings also in tropical highland and lowland conditions and mild
  temperate coastal areas; frost-sensitive, particularly as young trees
  [2][5].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; the vesicles are the fresh-eaten portion.
- **Pollination:** Not specifically researched for this species; flowers
  are white to pink with numerous stamens, broadly similar in form to
  other citrus, and the species is sexually compatible with other citrus
  (several commercial hybrids exist) [1][2].
- **Yield:** Documented per-tree yields at five to six years commonly
  fall in the 18-23 kg range under good management, though one Florida
  breeding-trial line under disease pressure yielded far less; industry-
  level per-hectare figures are difficult to source cleanly given mixed
  tree ages and still-developing plantings in most growing regions
  (see Estimates below) [2][3][4].
- **Water use:** Modest absolute water needs (very roughly on the order
  of 20 litres per tree per week), but management is unforgiving of both
  extended dry periods and waterlogging; water is most critical around
  flowering, fruit set, and fruit growth [5].
- **Establishment:** Primarily grafted trees on citrus rootstocks chosen
  partly for Phytophthora resistance, planted in north-south hedgerows
  in full sun behind windbreaks (established before planting), with
  mounding recommended on heavy soils prone to waterlogging; rooted
  cuttings are a secondary propagation route [2][3][5].
- **Labor:** Entirely hand-harvested with no mechanised option
  identified, using gloves and eye protection given the thorny canopy;
  described in at least one growing region as a high manual-labour
  operation overall [2][5].
- **Soil:** Well-drained soils strongly preferred, moderately acidic pH;
  feeder roots concentrate in the upper 30-60 cm, and heavy or poorly
  drained soils are specifically managed by mounding [2][5].
- **Stand life:** Not well documented for this species specifically,
  though wild plants are long-lived understorey shrubs; productive
  commercial orchard lifespan was not found in available sources [1].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Notable tolerance to huanglongbing (citrus
  greening) is a defining trait; by contrast Phytophthora root rot is a
  major disease in at least one growing region even though wild
  selections show high resistance to a related Phytophthora species — a
  tension in the sources that may reflect differences between wild
  selections and commercial rootstock/scion combinations (see Hazards
  below).
- **Cultivars:** Over thirty named varieties are grown commercially in
  the species' home range, spanning fruit and pulp colour, seediness, and
  plant habit, alongside newer disease-tolerant breeding releases from
  at least one university program [2][3][5].
- **Harvest:** Single harvest stage per fruit (non-climacteric, picked
  full-sized and easily detached), but repeated picking passes across an
  extended season from the same planting (Finding 1, 4).
- **Storage:** Fruit does not ripen after picking and has specific
  cool-but-not-cold storage requirements, detailed further below.

## Hazards

Documented disorders include skin marking from thorn and wind rub,
sunburn, twig and branch dieback of uncertain primary cause (frost, hot
dry or cold wind, and dry soil have all been proposed, with worse
outcomes during long wet spells), a fungal disease causing leaf and
fruit lesions that is considered serious in at least one major growing
region, and Phytophthora root rot, which is a major disease in at least
one growing region even though wild selections of the species show high
resistance to a related Phytophthora species — this apparent
contradiction is not resolved in the sources and may reflect differences
between wild and cultivated/grafted material.

No human health hazard, toxicity, or allergenicity information was found
for this crop in the sources consulted. The main occupational hazard
noted is mechanical: pickers require gloves and eye protection against
the plant's thorns [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Harvested fruit is graded, with thorn-marked seconds
  removed, then shade-cool-stored. First-grade fruit and seconds alike
  have their pearl-like vesicles spooned out for fresh use as a garnish,
  with the seeds recovered separately for breeding use. Seconds are also
  processed into freeze-dried powder, marmalade, pickles, or juice.
  Rootstock seed is grown into stocks that are later budded with
  selected cultivar material; prunings are also rooted as cuttings to
  produce additional plants.
- Not covered by this research: frozen pearls and whole frozen fruit in
  detail; cosmetic and pharmaceutical extracts; the specifics of export
  irradiation and cold-disinfestation treatment; the technical details of
  disease-tolerance breeding programs beyond what is noted in Finding 5.

No religious or ritual food use was identified in the sources consulted,
beyond a documented traditional Aboriginal use of the fruit as a food
and in wound treatment, which is cultural rather than ritual in nature
[1][2].

## Open research questions

- A key regional grower-guide reference for this crop could not be
  accessed directly (the source server refused the request); its figures
  appear here only secondhand through other sources, and opening it
  directly would likely firm up spacing, harvest timing, frost
  tolerance, and rootstock recommendations.
- Storage temperature guidance conflicts across sources: some recommend
  never going below a specific moderate-cool temperature, while others
  cite successful chilling at a notably colder temperature, and still
  others recommend an intermediate temperature depending on cultivar and
  export requirements — this is probably cultivar-dependent and not
  fully reconciled here.
- Frost tolerance is described inconsistently: one source says the
  species is not tolerant of frost or low temperatures at all, while
  another says light frosts are tolerated once trees are established;
  young trees in any case need frost protection.
- Phytophthora susceptibility appears contradictory: wild selections are
  described as highly resistant to one Phytophthora species, while a
  related Phytophthora disease is described as a major problem in at
  least one commercial growing region using grafted trees — likely a
  wild-versus-cultivated-material distinction, but not resolved here.
- No published pollination biology specific to this species was found;
  current understanding is inferred by analogy to other citrus rather
  than directly researched.
- Productive commercial orchard lifespan for this species was not found
  in any source consulted.
- Several additional, more distantly related Australian citrus species
  (a frost- and salt-tolerant semi-arid species among them) are
  documented as wild-harvested bushfoods with their own distinct climate
  niches; they are not covered by this entry and would be reasonable
  candidates for their own separate research in the future.
- A commercial hybrid between finger lime and ordinary lime is
  reportedly sometimes sold under the finger lime name despite lacking
  its signature caviar-pearl texture, which is a product-identity
  concern rather than an agronomic one.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein content (roughly 420 kcal/kg and 16 g protein/kg) are
derived directly from a published composition analysis of the edible
pearls, converting a reported energy range per 100 g into a per-kilogram
estimate; the plausible range given measurement variation is roughly
340-500 kcal/kg.

Yield tiers are derived from several partial industry figures rather
than a single clean yield study: a world-average figure of roughly
5,100 kg/ha/yr is estimated by dividing one region's reported total
industry production by its reported number of bearing trees, then
applying a representative planting density; a good-yield figure of
roughly 14,000 kg/ha/yr and an exceptional figure of roughly 18,400
kg/ha/yr are derived similarly from documented strong per-tree yields at
representative planting densities; a poor-yield figure of roughly 1,400
kg/ha/yr is derived from one disease-pressured breeding-trial line's
yield at the same representative density. A dedicated per-hectare yield
survey across mature commercial plantings would substantially improve on
these figures.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement, and salinity sensitivity) are estimated by analogy to
other, better-documented citrus crops this project has researched,
adjusted for this species' smaller mature tree size and shallower
feeder-root zone. Drought and waterlogging tolerance are both rated low,
reflecting direct source statements that water stress is difficult to
remedy once it appears and that saturated soil causes root rot.

Labor-hour estimates for harvest are built from an assumed (unsourced)
picking rate applied to the estimated yield figures above, since hand
harvest of small, thorn-protected fruit has no documented productivity
benchmark; establishment-phase labor is estimated by analogy to other
citrus crops, adjusted upward for windbreak installation and soil
mounding.

A first-harvest timing of roughly 540 days (about eighteen months) after
planting grafted trees is adopted as a middle estimate between sources
giving one to three years; peak yield-per-day and fruit standing-time
figures are likewise estimated from the documented harvest-window length
and general perishability, rather than directly measured.
