## Naming and scope

The yellow-skinned, white-fleshed, spiny-fruited climbing cactus native
to northern South America, also classified under an alternative genus
name, with the taxonomy still somewhat unsettled between sources. This
species is explicitly not the same as a yellow-skinned cultivar group of
a closely related climbing-cactus fruit this project has also
researched (the common dragon fruit), which is a separate species
grown mainly in Mexico and is documented only as a cultivar note within
that other entry. It is also not the same as an unrelated arid-land
cactus genus that is sometimes also called "pitaya" in parts of the
Americas. Ecuador grows two distinct named ecotypes of this species, one
notably smaller-fruited than the other.

This entry passes this project's distinctiveness check against its
closest researched sibling (the common dragon fruit) on four counts:
fruit development here takes roughly twice as long; it is stored at a
notably colder temperature than its sibling can tolerate without
chilling injury; it is grown as a cool-highland crop rather than a
lowland tropical one; and the fruit carries spines that must be brushed
off before sale, unlike its sibling. That sibling crop's data is reused
here only where explicitly labeled as an analogy.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a slow fruit on a fast-growing cactus.** Flowering to
harvest takes roughly 125 days, against a notably shorter documented
interval for red-fleshed pitahaya types — equivalent to a bit over three
months in summer and around five months in winter. Plants begin bearing
roughly fourteen months after transplanting; in a specific major
Colombian growing region, commercial production instead starts in year
three of a documented ten-year replanting cycle. The plant is self-
fertile, unlike several cultivars of its closest researched sibling.

**Finding 2 — a cool-highland, moderate-temperature crop.** A specific
major Colombian growing region's production zone sits at a moderate
elevation band, with a moderate mean-temperature range, moderate annual
rainfall, and a mildly acidic soil pH. Documented minimum growth
temperature sits around 7 °C, with winters below roughly 8 °C best
avoided; this species is grown across a notably wide elevation range
overall. A sharp, deliberate thirty-day cut in water brings on flowering.
It is a long-day plant that flowers above a specific minimum temperature
threshold, consistent with pitahaya-type cacti generally.

**Finding 3 — a real, detailed cost-of-production budget exists for this
crop.** A specific government agricultural-finance agency's detailed
budget for a major Colombian growing region documents planting density on
posts and wire, a yield trajectory that rises from a modest figure in
year three to a peak figure sustained across years five through eight,
then declines in years nine and ten, totaling a specific cumulative yield
figure across the full ten-year cycle. The crop is graded into four
quality classes at specific documented proportions. Labor in the peak
years is costed in detail, converting to roughly 1,330 hours per hectare
per year at a specific documented daily wage; weeding, pest control, and
harvest together account for 70% of total labor. This is a genuinely
well-sourced labor figure, unusual among related fruit crops this project
has researched, most of which rely on rougher estimates.

**Finding 4 — stored cold, and still ethylene-responsive despite being
classed non-climacteric.** Recommended storage is a notably cold
temperature (around 6 °C) and high humidity — specifically a temperature
at which both white- and red-fleshed forms of this crop's closest
researched sibling instead develop chilling injury. Despite being
classed as non-climacteric, ripening is measurably sped up by ethylene
exposure and delayed by a specific ethylene-action-blocking treatment.
Export fruit from one country is picked at a notably lower percentage of
yellow skin coloration than fruit destined for the domestic Colombian
market, which is picked considerably riper.

**Finding 5 — a modest but clearly growing crop.** A specific major
Colombian growing region documented a substantial multi-fold increase in
growing area over roughly a decade and a half, with four departments
holding the large majority of national output. A separate Ecuadorian
growing region also documents a meaningful planted area. This species
makes up the large majority of commercial pitahaya-type cactus
production in Colombia specifically, and is also being newly planted in
at least one European country.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial epiphytic or climbing cactus with
  thin, ribbed stems, trained on posts and wire trellising.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing with flowering occurring in
  waves, so several fruit-development stages are present on one plant at
  once.
- **Climate:** Tropical highlands (a wide elevation range), and
  subtropical/Mediterranean climates under cover in at least one European
  country.
- **Composition:** Composition figures are estimated by analogy to this
  crop's closest researched sibling, since no dedicated food-composition
  source was found specifically for this species; this fruit is noted
  elsewhere as sweeter than that sibling, so actual calorie content may
  run somewhat higher than the analogy suggests.
- **Pollination:** Not required — self-fertile, though cross-pollination
  may raise yield somewhat; flowers are among the largest of any cactus,
  opening for a single night and visited by night-flying moths and bats.
- **Yield:** A range from a national Colombian average up through a
  specific detailed regional budget's mature-year figure and a somewhat
  higher estimated exceptional-management figure (see Estimates below for
  adopted tier values).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-coefficient curve exists for this
  species in the relevant international reference; water-use parameters
  are estimated by analogy to this crop's closest researched sibling,
  given the shared climbing-cactus growth habit and CAM photosynthesis
  pathway. A deliberate, sharp water-withholding period specifically
  triggers flowering (Finding 2); the species is otherwise drought-
  tolerant but sensitive to waterlogging, with clay soils best avoided.
- **Propagation:** By vegetative cuttings (cladode sections) or
  plantlets; clonal planting material can carry a specific cactus virus.
  Interspecific hybrids with this crop's closest researched sibling
  produce pink-fleshed fruit, though this was not researched in detail.
- **Labor:** Unusually well documented via a specific detailed regional
  government cost-of-production budget (Finding 3); the overall total is
  directly sourced, though the breakdown between harvest and other
  maintenance activities is an estimate, since the source budget's
  detailed per-activity chart could not be read in full.
- **Soil:** A narrow, mildly acidic pH preference; well-drained loam
  soils high in organic matter are preferred commercially, though the
  species also grows on poor, stony soils in the wild. Soil fertilizer
  cost represents fully half of total input cost in the detailed regional
  budget referenced in Finding 3; nutrient needs shift across the growing
  cycle, with high nitrogen demand early, phosphorus and magnesium demand
  during reproduction, and potassium demand at fruit set.
- **Stand life:** A documented ten-year productive cycle in a specific
  major Colombian growing region, after which plantings are replaced;
  first harvest around year three of that cycle, full mature yield
  sustained from roughly year five through year eight, then declining
  yield in the final two years.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Bacterial stem (cladode) rot in cold, humid
  winters; a specific fungal "fish-eye" lesion disease; aphids; and a
  specific cactus virus carried in planting material.
- **Cultivars:** Two named Ecuadorian ecotypes (one notably smaller-
  fruited than the other, the larger one treated as this entry's
  baseline), a Colombian commercial type picked considerably riper than
  export fruit and sold into a four-grade market, and interspecific
  hybrids with this crop's closest researched sibling that produce pink
  flesh. Relatively few named cultivars exist in the literature
  consulted.
- **Harvest:** Picked at a specific degree of yellow skin coloration that
  varies considerably by destination market (lower for export, higher for
  domestic Colombian sale); fruit can remain on the plant for an unusually
  long period after ripening (up to roughly fifty days), continuing to
  gain weight during that time.
- **Storage:** A notably cold recommended storage temperature (around
  6 °C) and high humidity; spines must be brushed off before sale or
  storage.

## Hazards

  ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this hazard, noted in this

  data.
- Fruit carries spines that must be removed before handling or sale; this
  is a handling consideration rather than a food-safety hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit picked at a specific degree of ripeness → spines
  brushed off → graded into four quality classes → held in cold storage.
  Culls → pulp (with peel representing up to roughly half the fruit's
  weight by itself) → juice, jam, or jelly. Stem prunings → further
  cuttings for propagation.
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-pulp processing; detailed
  trellis-system engineering; peel mucilage and extract products; wine
  and yogurt products; preharvest elicitor-spray treatments.

## Open research questions

- No dedicated food-composition data were found for this species; figures
  here are carried over by analogy from this crop's closest researched
  sibling and likely understate this crop's actual sweetness and calorie
  content.
- Flowering-to-harvest timing is reported quite differently across
  sources — a detailed peer-reviewed source's roughly 125-day figure
  versus a notably shorter figure in a lower-confidence search-snippet
  source, which likely confuses this species with a faster-fruiting red-
  fleshed pitahaya type — not resolved.
- First-harvest timing is reported differently between a roughly
  fourteen-month figure in one source and a "year three of the
  production cycle" figure in the detailed regional budget — not fully
  reconciled, though the two are roughly consistent if transplanting and
  early establishment time is accounted for.
- Colombian national average yield is reported somewhat lower in an older
  statistical source than the mature-year figure in the detailed regional
  budget — not resolved, though the gap may simply reflect improved
  technified production since the earlier figure was collected.
- No dedicated cold-storage-duration trial was found; the storage-
  duration figure adopted here is an estimate.
- Detailed labor-activity breakdown (beyond the sourced total) is an
  estimate, since the underlying budget's detailed chart could not be
  read in full.

## Estimates and derivations

Composition figures (calorie and protein content) are carried over by
analogy from this crop's closest researched sibling, since no dedicated
food-composition source was found for this species specifically; this
fruit's documented higher sweetness (reflected in a notably high soluble-
solids content) suggests the true calorie figure may run somewhat higher
than this analogy indicates.

The "exceptional" yield tier is estimated at roughly 25% above the
detailed regional budget's mature-year figure, reflecting a well-
managed, irrigated, optimally pruned planting, consistent with a
documented pruning-density study that found maximum yield at a specific
cladode density per unit of trellis length.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy seasonal water slots,
rooting depth) are carried over by analogy from this crop's closest
researched sibling, given the shared CAM climbing-cactus growth habit;
actual water use likely differs somewhat in this crop's cooler highland
climate, where lower reference evapotranspiration reduces absolute water
demand even though the underlying crop coefficient is assumed unchanged.

Labor-hour figures are derived directly from the detailed regional
budget's documented total labor cost for mature-bearing years, converted
from cost to hours using that same budget's documented daily wage rate;
the split between harvest labor and other maintenance labor is an
estimate, since the source budget's detailed per-activity chart could
not be fully read, roughly informed by the documented mature yield and a
plausible per-hour harvest-and-grading rate.

Storage-duration figures are estimated from this crop's closest
researched sibling's documented storage life at that sibling's own
optimum (warmer) storage temperature, adjusted upward somewhat to
reflect this crop's notably thicker peel and colder optimal storage
temperature; no dedicated cold-storage trial was found for this species
specifically.

The yield-profile's first-harvest and ramp-to-full-bearing timing is
derived directly from the detailed regional budget's documented year-by-
year yield trajectory; peak daily harvest rate is derived by dividing the
adopted "good" yield tier across an estimated multi-month harvest-wave
season (itself estimated by analogy to this crop's closest researched
sibling's documented flowering season); on-tree standing-days and
quality-decay-rate figures reflect the documented finding that fruit can
remain on the plant gaining weight for up to roughly fifty days after
ripening.
