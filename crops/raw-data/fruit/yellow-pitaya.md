## Naming and scope

The yellow-skinned, white-fleshed, spiny-fruited climbing cactus native
to northern South America, also classified under the alternative genus
name *Hylocereus megalanthus*, with the taxonomy still somewhat unsettled
between sources [1][3]. This species is explicitly not the same as the
yellow-skinned cultivar group of the common dragon fruit (*Selenicereus
undatus*), this entry's closest researched sibling, which is a separate
species grown mainly in Mexico and is documented only as a cultivar note
within that other entry [2][4]. It is also not the same as the unrelated
arid-land cactus genus (*Stenocereus*) that is sometimes also called
"pitaya" in parts of the Americas [4]. Ecuador grows two distinct named
ecotypes of this species, one notably smaller-fruited than the other [3].

This entry passes this project's distinctiveness check against its closest
researched sibling (the common dragon fruit) on four counts: fruit
development here takes roughly twice as long [3]; it is stored at a notably
colder temperature than its sibling can tolerate without chilling injury
[4]; it is grown as a cool-highland crop rather than a lowland tropical
one [3][5]; and the fruit carries spines that must be brushed off before
sale, unlike its sibling [3]. That sibling crop's data is reused here only
where explicitly labeled as an analogy.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a slow fruit on a fast-growing cactus.** Flowering to
harvest takes roughly 125 days, against a notably shorter documented
interval for red-fleshed pitahaya types — equivalent to a bit over three
months in summer and around five months in winter [3]. Plants begin
bearing roughly fourteen months after transplanting [6]; in a major
Colombian growing region, commercial production instead starts in year
three of a documented ten-year replanting cycle [5]. The plant is
self-fertile, unlike several cultivars of its closest researched sibling
[1][3].

**Finding 2 — a cool-highland, moderate-temperature crop.** A major
Colombian growing region's production zone sits at a moderate elevation
band, with a moderate mean-temperature range, moderate annual rainfall,
and a mildly acidic soil pH [5]. Documented minimum growth temperature
sits around 7 °C [4], with winters below roughly 8 °C best avoided [1];
this species is grown across a notably wide elevation range overall [3][6].
A sharp, deliberate thirty-day cut in water brings on flowering [3]. It is
a long-day plant that flowers above a specific minimum temperature
threshold, consistent with pitahaya-type cacti generally [3].

**Finding 3 — a real, detailed cost-of-production budget exists for this
crop.** A government agricultural-finance agency's detailed budget for a
major Colombian growing region documents planting density on posts and
wire, a yield trajectory that rises from a modest figure in year three to
a peak figure sustained across years five through eight, then declines in
years nine and ten, totaling a specific cumulative yield figure across the
full ten-year cycle [5]. The crop is graded into four quality classes at
specific documented proportions [5]. Labor in the peak years is costed in
detail, converting to roughly 1,330 hours per hectare per year at a
specific documented daily wage; weeding, pest control, and harvest
together account for 70% of total labor [5]. This is a genuinely
well-sourced labor figure, unusual among related fruit crops this project
has researched, most of which rely on rougher estimates.

**Finding 4 — stored cold, and still ethylene-responsive despite being
classed non-climacteric.** Recommended storage is a notably cold
temperature (around 6 °C) and high humidity — specifically a temperature
at which both white- and red-fleshed forms of this crop's closest
researched sibling instead develop chilling injury [3][4]. Despite being
classed as non-climacteric, ripening is measurably sped up by ethylene
exposure and delayed by a specific ethylene-action-blocking treatment [3].
Export fruit from one country is picked at a notably lower percentage of
yellow skin coloration than fruit destined for the domestic Colombian
market, which is picked considerably riper [3].

**Finding 5 — a modest but clearly growing crop.** A major Colombian
growing region documented a substantial multi-fold increase in growing
area over roughly a decade and a half, with four departments holding the
large majority of national output [7]. A separate Ecuadorian growing
region also documents a meaningful planted area [6]. This species makes up
the large majority of commercial pitahaya-type cactus production in
Colombia specifically [3], and is also being newly planted in at least one
European country (where EU-market feasibility has been studied) [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial epiphytic or climbing cactus with
  thin, ribbed stems, trained on posts and wire trellising [1][5].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing with flowering occurring in
  waves, so several fruit-development stages are present on one plant at
  once [3].
- **Days / years to first bearing:** First bearing roughly fourteen months
  after transplanting [6]; in the Colombian ten-year commercial cycle,
  harvest starts in year three (Finding 1; Stand life) [5].
- **Climate zones:** Tropical highlands (a wide elevation range) [3][6],
  and subtropical/Mediterranean climates under cover in at least one
  European country [7]. No chill/vernalization requirement (a frost-tender
  CAM cactus); the limit is cold, with a ~7 °C minimum growth temperature
  and winters below ~8 °C best avoided (Finding 2) [1][4].
- **Composition:** Composition figures are estimated by analogy to this
  crop's closest researched sibling, since no dedicated food-composition
  source was found specifically for this species; this fruit is noted
  elsewhere as sweeter than that sibling (a notably high soluble-solids
  content), so actual calorie content may run somewhat higher than the
  analogy suggests [3].
- **Edibility:** Eaten fresh as a sweet dessert fruit once the spines are
  brushed off; the flesh is white with edible seeds, and culls go to juice,
  jam or jelly (see Uses). The seeds carry an anecdotal mild laxative
  effect (Hazards) [1][8].
- **Pollination:** Not required — self-fertile, though cross-pollination
  may raise yield somewhat [1][3][6]; flowers are among the largest of any
  cactus, opening for a single night and visited by night-flying moths and
  bats [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** A long-day plant — flowering occurs under
  long days above a specific minimum temperature threshold, consistent with
  pitahaya-type cacti generally (Finding 2), and a deliberate 30-day
  water-withholding is additionally used to force flowering [3]. Not
  applicable to tuberization/bulbing (a cactus with no storage organ).
- **Yield:** A range from a national Colombian average [7] up through a
  detailed regional budget's mature-year figure [5] and a somewhat higher
  estimated exceptional-management figure (see Estimates below for adopted
  tier values).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-coefficient curve exists for this
  species in the relevant international reference; water-use parameters
  are estimated by analogy to this crop's closest researched sibling,
  given the shared climbing-cactus growth habit and CAM photosynthesis
  pathway. A deliberate, sharp water-withholding period specifically
  triggers flowering (Finding 2); the species is otherwise drought-
  tolerant but sensitive to waterlogging, with clay soils best avoided [3].
- **Propagation:** By vegetative cuttings (cladode sections) or
  plantlets [1]; clonal planting material can carry a specific cactus virus
  (Soil biology) [1]. Interspecific hybrids with this crop's closest
  researched sibling produce pink-fleshed fruit, though this was not
  researched in detail [1].
- **Establishment:** Set out as rooted cuttings/plantlets on post-and-wire
  trellising at the documented commercial spacing/density; the species
  tolerates some shade, and an optimal shading level is documented
  (Finding 3) [3][5].
- **Labor:** Unusually well documented via a detailed regional government
  cost-of-production budget (Finding 3) [5]; the overall total is directly
  sourced, though the breakdown between harvest and other maintenance
  activities is an estimate, since the source budget's detailed
  per-activity chart could not be read in full.
- **Soil:** A narrow, mildly acidic pH preference; well-drained loam
  soils high in organic matter are preferred commercially, though the
  species also grows on poor, stony soils in the wild [3][5]. Soil
  fertilizer cost represents fully half of total input cost in the detailed
  regional budget referenced in Finding 3; nutrient needs shift across the
  growing cycle, with high nitrogen demand early, phosphorus and magnesium
  demand during reproduction, and potassium demand at fruit set [5].
  *Salinity:* no cactus-fruit crop (including this species) has a listed
  salinity-tolerance entry in the standard drainage-water reference [9];
  the pitahaya review rates the species as salinity-tolerant, but without a
  numeric ECe threshold [3].
- **Stand life:** A documented ten-year productive cycle in a major
  Colombian growing region, after which plantings are replaced; first
  harvest around year three of that cycle, full mature yield sustained from
  roughly year five through year eight, then declining yield in the final
  two years [5].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Bacterial stem (cladode) rot in cold, humid
  winters; a specific fungal "fish-eye" lesion disease; aphids; and a
  specific cactus virus carried in planting material [3][1]; a root-knot
  nematode (*Meloidogyne enterolobii*) was first reported on the crop in
  China in 2024, and a 2026 study surveyed fungal pathogens in Boyacá,
  Colombia [10].
- **Cultivars:** Two named Ecuadorian ecotypes (one notably smaller-
  fruited than the other, the larger one treated as this entry's
  baseline) [3], a Colombian commercial type picked considerably riper than
  export fruit and sold into a four-grade market [3][5], and interspecific
  hybrids with this crop's closest researched sibling that produce pink
  flesh [1]. Relatively few named cultivars exist in the literature
  consulted.
- **Harvest:** Picked at a specific degree of yellow skin coloration that
  varies considerably by destination market (lower for export, higher for
  domestic Colombian sale); fruit can remain on the plant for an unusually
  long period after ripening (up to roughly fifty days), continuing to
  gain weight during that time [3].
- **Storage:** A notably cold recommended storage temperature (around
  6 °C) and high humidity — the same temperature that causes chilling
  injury in the sibling dragon fruit (Finding 4); spines must be brushed
  off before sale or storage [3][4].
- **Weather response:** Frost/cold is the governing limit, as damage only
  — growth stops near 7 °C and winters below ~8 °C are avoided, with no
  quality-improving frost effect; the crop's cool-highland niche is set by
  heat sensitivity relative to lowland pitahaya [3][4]. Drought: tolerant
  (a CAM cactus), and water stress is used deliberately to force flowering;
  waterlogging: poorly tolerated (Water use) [3]. Documented
  frost/heat/salinity/drought tolerances come from the pitahaya review [3].
- **Water regime:** Rain-fed or irrigated upland culture on trellises; no
  flooded/paddy requirement, and good drainage is essential (waterlogging
  drives cladode rot). A deliberate ~30-day irrigation cut-off is used to
  synchronise flowering (Finding 2) [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One ripe stage per fruit, but picked
  repeatedly across the season because flowering comes in waves, so several
  development stages coexist on a plant (asynchronous ripening); ripe fruit
  also holds on the plant up to ~50 days (Harvest) [3].
- **Soil biology:** The practically important stock-borne issue is the
  clonal/viral degeneration risk of vegetative propagation — a specific
  cactus virus (cactus virus X type) is carried in cutting material, so
  clean planting stock matters [1]; bacterial cladode rot and a fungal
  "fish-eye" lesion are the main soil/humidity-linked diseases, and a
  root-knot nematode is a newer concern [3][10]. Mycorrhizal/allelopathy
  findings: not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Much milder than its lowland sibling. A
  species-specific weed-risk assessment exists (Hawaii/PlantPono) and a
  global weed compendium lists several naturalised *Selenicereus* species
  but none as a serious weed — in clear contrast to the sibling dragon
  fruit *S. undatus*, which is rated high-risk/invasive in Hawaii and
  elsewhere [10]. The biosecurity weight is phytosanitary: clean
  virus-tested cutting stock, plus the newly reported root-knot nematode
  and fruit-fly/pathogen concerns that accompany *Mangifera*-style tropical
  fruit movement [10]. *Legal restrictions:* none crop-specific found.
- **Controlled environment:** Grown under cover (greenhouse/protected
  subtropical-Mediterranean culture) in at least one European country,
  where EU-market feasibility has been studied; not otherwise a major
  protected crop [7].

## Hazards

  ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this hazard, noted in this

- Fruit carries spines that must be removed before handling or sale; this
  is a handling consideration rather than a food-safety hazard [3].

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** fruit picked at a specific degree of ripeness → spines
  brushed off → graded into four quality classes → held in cold storage
  [3][5]. Culls → pulp (with peel representing up to roughly half the
  fruit's weight by itself) → juice, jam, or jelly [3]. Stem prunings →
  further cuttings for propagation [1].
- Not covered in detail by this research: frozen-pulp processing; detailed
  trellis-system engineering; peel mucilage and extract products; wine
  and yogurt products; preharvest elicitor-spray treatments.

## Open research questions

- No dedicated food-composition data were found for this species; figures
  here are carried over by analogy from this crop's closest researched
  sibling and likely understate this crop's actual sweetness and calorie
  content.
- Flowering-to-harvest timing is reported quite differently across
  sources — a detailed peer-reviewed source's roughly 125-day figure
  versus a notably shorter figure in a lower-confidence search-snippet
  source, which likely confuses this species with a faster-fruiting red-
  fleshed pitahaya type — not resolved.
- First-harvest timing is reported differently between a roughly
  fourteen-month figure in one source and a "year three of the
  production cycle" figure in the detailed regional budget — not fully
  reconciled, though the two are roughly consistent if transplanting and
  early establishment time is accounted for.
- Colombian national average yield is reported somewhat lower in an older
  statistical source than the mature-year figure in the detailed regional
  budget — not resolved, though the gap may simply reflect improved
  technified production since the earlier figure was collected.
- No dedicated cold-storage-duration trial was found; the storage-
  duration figure adopted here is an estimate.
- Detailed labor-activity breakdown (beyond the sourced total) is an
  estimate, since the underlying budget's detailed chart could not be
  read in full.
- 2026-11-25 pass: the new-checklist additions are mostly covered by the
  existing pitahaya review [3] (photoperiod, salinity-as-tolerant, weather
  response) and the invasiveness source [10]. Open: a numeric salinity
  threshold (none exists for any cactus fruit [9]); the final PlantPono WRA
  score for this species (the snippet did not show it); and whether the
  carried cactus virus is specifically cactus virus X, which the migrated
  text implies but did not name.

## Estimates and derivations

Composition figures (calorie and protein content) are carried over by
analogy from this crop's closest researched sibling, since no dedicated
food-composition source was found for this species specifically; this
fruit's documented higher sweetness (reflected in a notably high soluble-
solids content) suggests the true calorie figure may run somewhat higher
than this analogy indicates.

The "exceptional" yield tier is estimated at roughly 25% above the
detailed regional budget's mature-year figure, reflecting a well-
managed, irrigated, optimally pruned planting, consistent with a
documented pruning-density study that found maximum yield at a specific
cladode density per unit of trellis length.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy seasonal water slots,
rooting depth) are carried over by analogy from this crop's closest
researched sibling, given the shared CAM climbing-cactus growth habit;
actual water use likely differs somewhat in this crop's cooler highland
climate, where lower reference evapotranspiration reduces absolute water
demand even though the underlying crop coefficient is assumed unchanged.

Labor-hour figures are derived directly from the detailed regional
budget's documented total labor cost for mature-bearing years, converted
from cost to hours using that same budget's documented daily wage rate;
the split between harvest labor and other maintenance labor is an
estimate, since the source budget's detailed per-activity chart could
not be fully read, roughly informed by the documented mature yield and a
plausible per-hour harvest-and-grading rate.

Storage-duration figures are estimated from this crop's closest
researched sibling's documented storage life at that sibling's own
optimum (warmer) storage temperature, adjusted upward somewhat to
reflect this crop's notably thicker peel and colder optimal storage
temperature; no dedicated cold-storage trial was found for this species
specifically.

The yield-profile's first-harvest and ramp-to-full-bearing timing is
derived directly from the detailed regional budget's documented year-by-
year yield trajectory; peak daily harvest rate is derived by dividing the
adopted "good" yield tier across an estimated multi-month harvest-wave
season (itself estimated by analogy to this crop's closest researched
sibling's documented flowering season); on-tree standing-days and
quality-decay-rate figures reflect the documented finding that fruit can
remain on the plant gaining weight for up to roughly fifty days after
ripening.
