## Naming and scope

The essential-oil citrus historically centered on a specific coastal
region of southern Italy [1][3]. Its exact parentage is genuinely
disputed between several competing hybrid-origin hypotheses [1][3].
Three named cultivars are grown commercially. Not covered by this
entry: an unrelated herb sometimes called "bergamot" (a *Monarda*
species, a mint-family herb, not a citrus at all), and a separate
sweet, mild citrus sometimes called "bergamot" in French usage, which
this project treats as its own distinct crop.

This crop is genuinely distinct from every other citrus crop in
several ways: its primary commercial product is the cold-pressed peel
oil rather than the fruit itself, used to flavor a well-known tea blend
and as a major ingredient in perfumery; it is grown almost entirely
within a tiny, formally protected growing area on one specific coastal
strip, where a protected-designation-of-origin scheme caps maximum
yield per hectare; harvest timing is specifically optimized for oil
content and quality rather than fresh-eating quality, with picking
dates staggered by cultivar across several months; and it carries two
distinct, well-documented food-safety hazards related specifically to
its chemistry (a phototoxic peel-oil compound and a separate drug-
interaction compound in the juice). Juice, once treated purely as
industrial waste from oil extraction, has more recently become a
marketed nutraceutical product in its own right.

Agronomic detail for this crop (water use, planting density, labor,
soil pH, bearing age, growth-stage shape) is drawn almost entirely by
analogy from a closely related citrus crop this project has also
researched, since the sources found describe the oil industry in
detail but not general orchard practice for this specific crop.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — fundamentally an oil crop that happens to be a citrus
tree.** The main growing region produces roughly 20,000 tonnes of fruit
and roughly 100 tonnes of essential oil per year from a modest total
growing area, implying an oil yield of roughly half a kilogram of oil
per 100 kg of fruit — consistent with the documented range for the
protected-origin product [2][4]. Extraction uses cold-pressing (no
heat or solvent): the rind is scraped or pressed to rupture its oil
glands, and the resulting oil-water emulsion is then centrifuged to
separate the oil [4]. Oil output from this main growing region has
declined over recent decades [2]. A growers' consortium overseeing the
industry was founded nearly a century ago, following earlier price
crises [4].

**Finding 2 — three named cultivars with distinct fruit character and
staggered harvest timing.** One major cultivar produces spherical,
thin-skinned fruit and is picked earliest (from late autumn), growing
fast but with limited tree longevity; a second produces globose,
medium-skinned fruit, is picked somewhat later, and bears in alternate
years; a third has the largest canopy of the three, is robust and
highly productive, and is picked latest, in early winter [2]. All
commercial fruit across all three cultivars is hand-picked [4].

**Finding 3 — juice has shifted from industrial waste to a marketed
nutraceutical product.** Historically, only the oil was harvested
commercially, with the juice, pulp, and seed treated purely as
processing waste [3]. More recently, the juice has been marketed
specifically for its flavonoid content (several named compounds,
including the same drug-interaction compound found at higher
concentration elsewhere in the fruit) [1][3]. Fruit grown within the
protected-origin area was found in at least one comparative study to
have measurably higher soluble solids, juice content, and acid content
than fruit described as not comparable from other growing regions [3].
The fruit itself is described as less sour than lemon but more bitter
than grapefruit, and is also used in marmalade, confectionery, and at
least one regional candy preparation [1].

**Finding 4 — commercial cultivation exists well beyond the main
Italian growing region, though oil quality there is considered
distinct.** The crop is also grown in several other countries across
southern Europe, West Africa, the eastern Mediterranean, several
Indian Ocean and South American countries, and North Africa, but oil
from these other growing regions is explicitly described as not
comparable in quality to oil from the main protected-origin area [1][3].
At least one source describes this crop's flowering as occurring in
winter, which would be unusual timing relative to most other citrus
crops — this is noted here as reported but not independently confirmed
[1].

**Finding 5 — the main growing region's traditional rootstock carries a
documented virus risk that is actively driving rootstock-replacement
research.** Bergamot in the main Calabrian growing region is
traditionally grafted onto sour orange, a rootstock well documented as
highly susceptible to Citrus tristeza virus (CTV); CTV is present in
the region, though no CTV infection had been reported on this specific
bergamot/sour-orange combination as of the research reviewed — the
susceptibility risk alone has been enough to motivate active
alternative-rootstock trials [5]. A 2017-2019 field trial compared sour
orange against two alternative rootstocks on one named cultivar, with
fruit-quality and nutraceutical parameters found broadly comparable
across rootstocks and one alternative identified as the stronger
candidate; a later trial from the same research group tested several
further rootstocks as additional candidate sour-orange replacements
[5]. No source found documents any CTV sensitivity or tolerance
specific to the bergamot scion itself, as opposed to its traditional
rootstock — the risk, as currently sourced, is a rootstock-choice
question rather than a scion trait.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial citrus tree, harvested
  across a multi-month season from late autumn through early spring,
  with exact timing staggered by cultivar (Finding 2).
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; a single harvest stage per
  season, picked by hand as skin color shifts from green to yellow,
  with oil content and quality varying by exact harvest timing and
  cultivar rather than being modeled as distinct harvest stages.
- **Climate:** Mediterranean, frost-free coastal climate in the main
  growing region; also grown in subtropical and tropical climates
  elsewhere.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible but bitter-sour; the fruit is mainly
  processed rather than eaten fresh.
- **Pollination:** Not independently researched for this crop; assumed
  insect- (bee-) pollinated by analogy to related citrus crops.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not independently sourced for this crop.
  By analogy to citrus generally (not confirmed species-specific):
  citrus flowering is largely day-neutral, with flower induction driven
  mainly by cool temperature and water-deficit stress rather than day
  length. Not applicable to tuberization/bulbing, since this is a tree
  crop with no such storage organ.
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield (derived from the main
  growing region's total output and area) sits in the low tens of
  thousands of kg/ha, with the protected-origin scheme's own documented
  yield cap representing the exceptional tier (see Estimates below).
- **Propagation:** Vegetative (budded cultivars), as is standard for
  citrus generally; rootstock choice for this specific crop was not
  researched.
- **Water use:** Assumed by analogy to a related citrus crop (steady
  moisture through fruit growth, no standing water) rather than
  independently researched for this crop specifically.
- **Labor:** Harvest is entirely hand-picked by documented practice;
  other labor figures are estimated by analogy to a related citrus
  crop rather than independently sourced.
- **Soil:** Assumed near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH range by
  analogy to a related citrus crop; the specific microclimate and
  soils of the main protected-origin growing strip are explicitly
  understood to determine final oil quality, though this project has
  no mechanism to model site-specific product-quality variation.
  Salinity tolerance is not independently sourced for this crop either;
  by genus-level analogy, citrus overall is salinity-sensitive (one
  review cites a soil-solution threshold near roughly 1.4 dS/m before
  adverse effects appear), with tolerance strongly rootstock-dependent
  — no source identifies which rootstock(s) bergamot is typically grown
  on with respect to salinity specifically.
- **Stand life:** At least one major cultivar has documented limited
  tree longevity despite fast early growth; overall productive
  lifespan was not independently researched for this crop.
- **Disease/pest profile:** General pest and disease susceptibility was
  not independently researched for this crop, but a specific rootstock-
  linked virus risk was found (Finding 5): bergamot in the main growing
  region is traditionally grafted onto sour orange, a rootstock well
  documented as highly susceptible to Citrus tristeza virus (CTV),
  which is present in that region, motivating active alternative-
  rootstock trials. Certified virus-free budwood as a control measure
  is documented only at the citrus-genus level; no bergamot-specific
  certification program was found.
- **Cultivars:** Three named commercial cultivars, differing in fruit
  shape, skin thickness, harvest timing, tree size, bearing pattern
  (one is distinctly alternate-bearing), and tree longevity.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the peel oil contains a phototoxic compound
(bergapten) and related furanocoumarin compounds, a well-documented
cause of phototoxic skin reactions on exposure to sunlight after skin
contact. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the juice contains a furanocoumarin compound
(bergamottin) associated with significant drug interactions (the same
general compound family responsible for well-known grapefruit-juice
drug interactions). Deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit → cold-pressed peel oil (a small fraction of fruit
  weight) → tea flavoring and perfumery use. Scraped (post-oil-
  extraction) fruit → juice (now marketed as a nutraceutical product)
  and a solid pressed-fruit byproduct. Fruit is also sold fresh in
  small quantities and processed into marmalade and confectionery.
- Not covered by this research in detail: a specific furanocoumarin-
  reduced ("FCF") oil product, polyphenol dietary-supplement
  production, and candied peel or liqueur production.

## Open research questions

- Orchard agronomy (water use, planting density, labor, soil pH,
  bearing age, rootstock choice) is almost entirely carried over by
  analogy from a related citrus crop, since the sources found describe
  the oil industry in detail but not general orchard practice for this
  crop specifically — a dedicated orchard-management source for this
  crop would be the main gap to fill.
- Rootstock choice for this crop specifically was not researched (a
  commonly used citrus rootstock is traditional for related crops, but
  this was not confirmed for this crop).
- Winter bloom timing reported in one source is unusual for citrus
  generally and was not independently confirmed.
- This project currently has no way to model the documented fact that
  product (oil) quality depends specifically on the microclimate and
  soils of one particular growing region, with oil from elsewhere
  considered not comparable — noted here as an unresolved modeling gap.
- Pollination requirements, general disease/pest susceptibility beyond
  the rootstock CTV risk noted in Finding 5, postharvest fresh-fruit
  storage regime, and overall productive lifespan were not
  independently researched for this crop.
- Photoperiod response and salinity tolerance are both carried over
  from citrus-genus-level (not bergamot-specific) sourcing; no study
  addressing either trait for this species specifically was found.
- Which rootstock(s) bergamot is typically grown on with respect to
  salinity tolerance specifically was not found; citrus salinity
  tolerance is strongly rootstock-dependent, so this is a real gap for
  a crop whose traditional rootstock (sour orange) is already flagged
  for a separate CTV-susceptibility concern (Finding 5).

## Estimates and derivations

Most orchard-agronomy figures (water-use curve, planting density,
labor-hour figures, soil pH range, bearing age, growth-stage shape,
stress multipliers, and general composition) are carried over by direct
analogy from a related citrus crop this project has also researched,
since sources found for this crop describe its oil industry in detail
but not general orchard practice. A dedicated orchard-management
source, or composition table specific to this crop, would replace
these analogies.

Yield tiers and oil-per-hectare figures are derived from the main
growing region's documented total fruit output divided by its total
growing area (world average and oil-per-hectare), and from the
protected-origin scheme's own documented maximum permitted yield
(exceptional tier); good and poor tiers are estimated between and
below these two sourced figures.

A growth-stage curve and yield ramp-up period are estimated by analogy
to general citrus bearing patterns (bearing from roughly the third
year, full production by roughly the seventh).

A representative fresh-fruit storage duration and essential-oil shelf
life are both estimated (citrus-like short fresh-fruit storage; the
essential oil itself is assumed to keep for years if properly sealed),
since no source specifically addressed storage duration for either
product.

A peak-yield-per-day figure and harvest-window length are derived from
the documented overall harvest season length (late autumn through
early spring) dividing total yield across that period.
