## Naming and scope

*Citrus* × *limonia* Osbeck (Rutaceae): **Rangpur lime**; also **mandarin
lime**, **lemandarin**, **Canton lemon** (South China), **hime lemon** (Japan),
**limão-cravo** / **Cravo** (Brazil), **Kona lime** (Hawaii), and **Sylhet
lime** / **surkh nimboo** / **sharbati** (South Asia) [1][2]. Named after
**Rangpur**, in present-day Bangladesh [1]. This doc covers the `rangpur_lime`
row, proposed from `lime.md` and `mandarin.md` [6].

- **Hybrid origin:** genomic work indicates an **F1 hybrid of a female citron
  (*C. medica*) × a male mandarin (*C. reticulata*)**; older horticultural
  literature (Morton) called it a lemon × mandarin cross from India, so the
  parentage is stated inconsistently across sources (a classification matter,
  not a crop trait) [1][4].
- **"Lime" is a misnomer:** despite the name it is **not a true (acid) lime**
  and has little in common with the papeda-line limes; it looks like a
  mandarin but is intensely sour [1][6].

**Distinctiveness (gate check, 2026-11-27): passes.** A validly distinct,
long-cultivated citrus with its own fruit, names and — above all — its own
dual farming system [1][6]:
- **vs key/Persian lime** (`key_lime`/`persian_lime`, the acid limes of the
  proposing `lime.md`): a different lineage entirely (citron × mandarin, not
  the micrantha/papeda limes), with mandarin-like loose orange skin, not a
  green true lime [1][6].
- **vs mandarin** (`mandarin`, a parent): mandarin is a sweet dessert fruit;
  Rangpur is a sour juice/rootstock citrus [1][6].
- **Farming system:** uniquely among the project's citrus so far, it is grown
  **principally as a rootstock** in its main production regions (Finding 2),
  with fresh-fruit/flavouring use secondary — a genuinely different crop role
  from a dessert or acid-lime fruit [1][3].

**Relatives in this project:** `lime.md` (key/Persian lime — the proposing doc,
where Rangpur is noted as a rootstock for both limes), `mandarin.md` (a parent),
`citron.md` (the other parent), and `lemon.md` (the older putative parent)
[6].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a mandarin-looking fruit with lime-substitute acidity, used for
juice, marmalade and a branded gin.** The fruit has **orange to reddish-orange
peel and flesh and a loose, mandarin-like skin**, but its juice is **very sour**
— so it stands in for limes and lemons rather than being eaten sweet [1][2].
Uses are juice, **marmalade**, jams, curds, cocktails and dressings; the juice
is **more acidic, pungent and bitter when slightly unripe** [1][5]. It is
culturally prominent in South Asia (Bengali/Sylheti cooking) and gave its name
to **Tanqueray Rangpur gin** (Diageo, 2006), flavoured with Rangpur plus ginger
and bay [1]. In the United States it is grown mostly as an **ornamental and
container tree** rather than a commercial fruit [1][5].

**Finding 2 — outside the US its dominant role is as a citrus rootstock, and in
Brazil it was *the* rootstock.** "Outside the US it is used principally as a
citrus rootstock" [1]. In Brazil, as **limão-cravo / 'Cravo'**, it long
dominated the citrus industry's rootstock use, valued for **high vigour, early
bearing and high yields**, and especially for **outstanding drought tolerance**
(rated among the best rootstocks in Brazilian multivariate trials) and
**tolerance of citrus tristeza virus (CTV)** — the disease that made the old
sour-orange rootstock unusable [3]. This is the project's schema pattern of a
**crop grown mainly as rootstock for another crop** (Entry 25) [3][6].

**Finding 3 — its rootstock weaknesses are driving a slow replacement.** Rangpur
is **susceptible to *Phytophthora* root rot and gummosis**, which can shorten
tree life, and to citrus blight — the main reasons Brazilian programmes have
sought alternatives [3]. Recent trials rank newer rootstocks (e.g. the Changsha
× English citrandarin IAC 1711, and 'Cleopatra' mandarin hybrids) as drought-
tolerant replacements, with Rangpur often a close second rather than the
default [3]. So its story is a dominant rootstock now partly ceding ground on
disease grounds, not on its drought/vigour merits [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial evergreen citrus tree/shrub; everbearing in
  the manner of acid citrus, with no single tight season sourced [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; fruit picked for juice when coloured,
  or slightly earlier for a sharper, more bitter juice (Finding 1) [1][5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** As a **rootstock it induces early bearing**
  in the scion [3]; the Rangpur tree itself is precocious and heavy-fruiting,
  but an exact first-bearing age was not sourced [3].
- **Climate zones:** Subtropical/tropical citrus; noted for **drought tolerance**
  (Finding 2) [3]. Cold-hardiness figure: Not well sourced (grown in containers
  and moved under cover in cool climates) [5].
- **Composition:** Very **high acidity / low sugar** juice (lime substitute);
  precise Brix/acid or per-100 g figures: Not sourced [1][5].
- **Edibility:** Juice used raw as an acidulant; flesh too sour to eat sweet
  [1][5].
- **Pollination:** Not detailed; citrus is largely self-fertile and Rangpur
  seed is **polyembryonic/nucellar** (comes largely true), which is why it is
  propagated from seed for rootstock use (see Propagation) [2][3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced; not expected to apply.
- **Yield:** As a rootstock it gives **high scion yields** (e.g. Pera orange on
  Rangpur performs very well in Brazil) [3]; a fruit yield per tree figure was
  not sourced [3].
- **Water use:** Low relative to other rootstocks — its **drought tolerance** is
  its signature agronomic merit [3].
- **Propagation:** **By seed** for rootstock production — the seed is
  polyembryonic and nucellar, so seedlings come largely true to type, giving
  uniform rootstock liners; scion cultivars are then budded/grafted onto it.
  The fruiting tree is also grown from seed or cuttings [2][3]. (This is the
  "comes true from seed" case — see schema Entry 19 context via `citron`/citrus
  rootstocks.) [3]
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Tolerant of a range of citrus soils; performs well on the lighter,
  drought-prone soils where its vigour and water-thrift matter. pH and
  **salinity tolerance:** Not specifically sourced (not among the classic
  salt-tolerant rootstocks, but not quantified here) [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced; as an acid citrus the fruit keeps
  moderately, but no regime found.
- **Establishment:** As rootstock, raised as seedling liners in the nursery and
  budded; as a dooryard/ornamental, planted as a grafted or seedling tree, and
  readily **container-grown** [3][5].
- **Cultivars:** Several selections exist (e.g. the Brazilian **'Cravo'** lines
  used as rootstock); names overlap with the many vernacular names above
  [1][3].
- **Weather response:** **Drought-tolerant**; frost tolerance moderate and not
  quantified here; CTV-tolerant but *Phytophthora*- and blight-susceptible
  (Findings 2-3) [3].
- **Water regime:** Ordinary irrigation/rainfall; prized precisely for coping
  with **water deficit**; no paddy requirement [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Picked on colour; slightly unripe fruit gives
  sharper juice (Finding 1) [1][5].
- **Soil biology:** Susceptible to ***Phytophthora*** root rot/gummosis — a
  soil-borne disease that is its main rootstock weakness (Finding 3) [3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** A cultivated citrus hybrid; no invasive
  listing found. Its main biosecurity relevance is as a **CTV-tolerant
  rootstock** (a disease-management tool) rather than a weed risk [1][3]. No
  cultivation/movement restriction specific to it found.
- **Controlled environment:** Commonly **container-grown** as an ornamental
  indoors/under glass; no commercial protected fruit production sourced [5].

## Hazards

- No specific human-toxicity hazard was found; it is an ordinary edible acid
  citrus [1]. As with all citrus, the **peel oil is a contact photosensitiser
  (furanocoumarins)** in principle — a minor handling note, not a distinctive
  hazard. **No hazards found in this pass** beyond that generic citrus note.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (fruit):** sour fruit → **juiced/zested** → lime/lemon substitute in
  drinks, **marmalade**, jams, curds, dressings and cocktails [1][5]; → a
  flavouring for **Tanqueray Rangpur gin** [1].
- **Chain (rootstock — the dominant use outside the US):** polyembryonic seed →
  nucellar seedling **rootstock liners** → **other citrus scions budded onto
  them**, conferring vigour, early/high yield, drought tolerance and CTV
  tolerance (Finding 2) [3].
- **Ornamental:** grown as a dooryard and **container** tree, especially in the
  US [1][5].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** essential-oil extraction; precise juice
  composition; the full agronomy of specific 'Cravo' rootstock lines [3].

## Open research questions

- **Parentage is stated inconsistently** (citron × mandarin by genomics vs
  lemon × mandarin in older sources); a single authoritative citation would
  settle the naming (a classification matter) [1][4].
- **Fruit composition** (Brix, titratable acidity, juice yield) and **cold
  hardiness / harvest months** were not well sourced [1][5].
- **Current rootstock share:** how far Rangpur has actually been displaced by
  citrandarins/'Cleopatra' hybrids in Brazil (and elsewhere) is trial-specific
  and not resolved into a single figure here [3].
- **Salinity and soil-pH tolerance** as a rootstock were not quantified this
  pass [3].
