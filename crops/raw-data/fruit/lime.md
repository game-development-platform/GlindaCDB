# Lime (*Citrus × latifolia* / *C. × aurantiifolia*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers the two commercial acid limes together, as two
cultivar groups within one crop entry [1]:

- **Persian lime** (also called Tahiti or Bearss lime; *C. × latifolia*)
  — a triploid hybrid whose flowers carry no viable pollen. Seedless,
  nearly thornless, and the larger-fruited of the two; the most widely
  grown lime worldwide [2][5].
- **Key lime** (also called Mexican or West Indian lime; *C. ×
  aurantiifolia*) — small, seedy, thorny, and more aromatic than Persian
  lime. Raised from true-to-type seed rather than grafted [3][4].

The two differ sharply in propagation method, cold hardiness, harvest
pattern, disease susceptibility, and product streams (see Finding 1),
comparable to how two distinct cultivar groups of the same stone-fruit
species can differ — a pattern this project has also documented
elsewhere. Parentage is genuinely disputed in the sources found: Key
lime is described as a cross either of a wild citrus relative with
citron, or of citron, pomelo, and a third species; Persian lime is
described as a cross either of Key lime with lemon, or of a Mexican
lime with citron [2][3][5][8].

Not covered by this entry: a leaf-and-rind spice citrus relative (a
candidate for a future separate entry, not yet researched in depth); a
small, unusual finger-shaped citrus fruit this project has also
researched as its own crop entry; a mandarin-hybrid lime and a true
sweet lime (the latter is its own separate crop entry in this project);
and an unrelated non-citrus fruit sometimes informally called "Spanish
lime" [1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two limes, two farming systems.** The two cultivar groups
differ in nearly every agronomically relevant way. Persian lime is
propagated by budding or air-layering, since its few seeds are
monoembryonic and seedlings do not come true to type; Key lime is
usually grown directly from seed, since its seeds are highly
polyembryonic and come true to the mother tree [4][5][7]. Persian lime
flowers carry no viable pollen at all, with fruit setting entirely
without pollination; Key lime is self-fertile with perfect flowers
[5][7][8]. Persian lime is harvested in roughly 8-12 picks a year, with
most of the crop coming between late spring and autumn, maturing
roughly 90-120 days from bloom; Key lime has two main harvest seasons in
at least one major historical growing region, with fruit ripening and
falling roughly 5-6 months after flowering, and ripe fallen fruit
gathered from the ground for processing twice a week [4][5][8]. A
serious fungal disease (lime anthracnose, or "withertip") is a major
threat to Key lime but does not affect Persian lime at all [4][5][7].
Cold tolerance also differs: Persian lime's foliage, wood, and whole
tree each have somewhat higher damage thresholds than the corresponding
Key lime thresholds, though lime overall is considered the least
cold-hardy citrus type [7][8][19]. Main product streams differ too:
Persian lime goes mainly to fresh export and juice, while Key lime
additionally yields hand-pressed and distilled essential oils and
pickled product [2][4][5].

**Finding 2 — Key lime is the drought-tolerant citrus, yet
over-watering kills it.** Key lime is described as tolerating drought
better than any other citrus fruit, with mature trees needing little
supplemental watering; too much water, however, can cause tree decline
through root rot [4][7]. Persian lime, by contrast, grows and flowers
essentially year-round and is described as demanding more fertilization
and irrigation than most other citrus species, though air-layered
Persian trees can tolerate a few days of flooded soil [5][8].

**Finding 3 — sold green, so color loss is the practical storage
limit.** Both limes turn yellow as they ripen but are conventionally
sold green [2]. Persian limes store for up to roughly eight weeks under
refrigeration at very high humidity; pitting below a specific threshold
temperature sets the lowest safe storage temperature, and ethylene
exposure hastens the undesired yellowing [4][6]. The characteristic
Persian-lime storage disorder is a blossom-end breakdown in which
internal juice vesicles rupture — oversized fruit picked on hot
mornings and left in the sun is most at risk, with the disorder
sometimes starting within just a couple of hours of picking and losses
reaching as much as 40% in severe cases; prompt precooling greatly
reduces this risk [5][6].

**Finding 4 — Key lime oil is mostly distilled, not pressed.** Essential
oil from Key lime is produced three different ways: hand-pressing in a
traditional spiked copper bowl (giving the finest oil), machine-pressing
spent fruit half-shells, or distilling the oily layer that rises when
crushed fruit is left to settle for a couple of weeks — this last method
gives the highest oil yield and is the main commercial source of lime
oil used as a cola flavoring, with one country responsible for over half
of world supply [4][14]. A general reference source describes lime oil
as "typically" obtained by steam distillation [1]. Juicing yields
roughly half the fruit's weight in juice [4].

**Finding 5 — documented yields for both cultivar groups, with national
statistics often mixed between types.** Key lime yields in one major
Mexican growing region run meaningfully higher under an intensive
disease-management package (targeting a serious citrus greening disease)
than under traditional management, while a separate Mexican state
dominated by Key lime production averaged a yield between those two
figures in one recent year [10][11]. Persian lime yields in Brazil vary
enormously by state, from several times lower than the national average
up to well above it, with one particular growing state averaging
considerably above the national figure in a recent year [12]. A
national Mexican statistic combining all lime and lemon types gives a
yet different average yield [11]. Notably, a formerly significant
Florida lime industry ended in the early 2000s following a major
hurricane and a citrus-disease eradication program [8][9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial trees with no true
  dormant season; both groups need no winter chill at all (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Persian lime is picked repeatedly across most of
  the year (roughly 8-12 picks annually); Key lime has two main harvest
  seasons in at least one historical growing region, plus scattered
  fruit gathered from the ground between the two main seasons (Finding
  1).
- **Climate:** Persian lime tolerates subtropical, tropical, and warm
  Mediterranean climates given its somewhat higher cold-damage
  thresholds; Key lime is restricted to genuinely frost-free tropical
  and subtropical climates given its lower cold tolerance (Finding 1;
  see Estimates below).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (as juice; very sour).
- **Pollination:** Not required for either group — Persian lime sets
  fruit without any pollination at all (its flowers carry no viable
  pollen), while Key lime is self-fertile and needs no cross-pollination
  (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** Persian lime yields documented in Brazil
  range very widely by growing region; Key lime yields in Mexico's
  major growing regions are meaningfully raised by an intensive disease-
  management package (Finding 5; see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No lime-specific crop-coefficient curve was found;
  water-use parameters are modeled on general citrus figures. Persian
  lime is notably more water-demanding than most citrus and tolerates
  brief flooding on certain rootstocks; Key lime is markedly more
  drought-tolerant than any other citrus type documented, but is
  seriously harmed by waterlogged or persistently wet soil, which causes
  root rot (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** Persian lime is propagated almost exclusively by
  budding or air-layering (air-layered trees bear a year sooner but
  yield less at maturity and suffer more wind damage); Key lime is
  commonly raised directly from true-to-type seed, though air-layering,
  cuttings, and budding are all also used. Both share several rootstock
  options suited to calcareous, high-pH soils; sour orange rootstock is
  noted specifically for Key lime as offering somewhat better storm
  resistance.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour study was found for either lime;
  labor cost is nonetheless documented as the dominant production-factor
  cost share in one detailed Mexican cost study. Harvest-labor figures
  here are rough estimates scaled up from a related citrus crop this
  project has also researched, reflecting these limes' smaller fruit
  size and more frequent picking.
- **Soil:** Both grow well on alkaline, calcareous (limestone-derived)
  soils; potassium nutrition is specifically flagged as important for
  yield in both groups.
- **Stand life:** Persian lime bears first fruit roughly 3 years after
  planting (sooner for air-layered trees), with yield rising gradually
  over about a decade to full bearing; Key lime seedlings typically bear
  in 3-6 years, with full production by 8-10 years. A specific
  productive-lifespan figure for either group was not found in the
  sources reached (a formerly significant growing region's industry was
  destroyed by a hurricane and a disease-eradication program before its
  natural productive lifespan could be well documented).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Lime anthracnose (withertip) is a serious
  threat to Key lime but does not affect Persian lime; a serious root
  rot disease threatens over-watered trees of both groups; Key lime
  trees bear numerous sharp thorns requiring gloved harvesting; citrus
  canker is a quarantine concern affecting movement of fruit from
  affected growing areas (Finding 1; see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** Persian lime has relatively few distinct named clones,
  including some disease-free virus-screened selections and at least
  one newer high-yielding Brazilian selection; Key lime has more
  regional clonal variation (notably an Indian clone group with several
  subtypes) plus a few interspecific hybrid selections bred partly for
  improved disease resistance (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Persian lime is picked green across repeated passes
  through most of the year; Key lime fruit for processing is
  characteristically gathered as ripe, fallen fruit from the ground
  twice weekly during peak season (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Persian lime stores up to roughly eight weeks under
  refrigeration at very high humidity, is notably prone to a blossom-end
  juice-vesicle breakdown disorder that precooling greatly reduces, and
  is chilling-sensitive below a specific threshold; Key lime storage
  duration is less well documented, with only a few days' typical shelf
  life described under home-refrigerator conditions (Finding 3).

## Hazards

Peel oil from both limes contains phototoxic compounds
(furanocoumarins) associated with a skin-phototoxicity reaction
(described in source material as itchy, sometimes blistering skin areas
appearing where hand-squeezed lime juice or oil contacted skin that was
then exposed to sunlight). Key lime trees bear numerous sharp thorns
that can cause scratches leading to skin rash in addition to the direct
injury risk, requiring gloved harvesting. Citrus canker is a serious
quarantine concern for fruit movement from affected growing areas, and
some jurisdictions require fumigation treatment against a specific
fruit-fly pest before entry.

## Uses and processing

- **Persian lime chains:** Fruit is precooled and cold-stored, then
  packed. Peel oil is expressed as a by-product of juice extraction,
  before the fruit is juiced (roughly 45-55% of fruit mass becomes
  juice). Whole limes are also sun-dried into a distinctive
  long-storing dried product used in some regional cuisines.
- **Key lime chains:** Fruit is cold-stored at a slightly less cold
  temperature than Persian lime. Peel oil is recovered by hand- or
  machine-pressing before juicing ripe fallen fruit (yielding roughly
  480 kg of juice per tonne of fruit); alternatively, crushed ripe fruit
  is left to settle for about two to four weeks and the resulting oily
  layer is distilled, yielding roughly 4 kg of essential oil per tonne of
  fruit and supplying most of the commercial lime-oil market used in
  cola flavoring. Fruit is also brine-pickled, used in a traditional
  Indian pickle preparation, sun-dried, or made into marmalade.
- Not covered by this research: frozen concentrate production; citric
  acid extraction from juice residue; the specific brine-boiling step
  used in some dried-lime preparations.

## Open research questions

- Parentage of both cultivar groups is genuinely disputed between
  multiple sources, with no clear resolution in the material reached.
- A unit-conversion inconsistency appears in at least one source
  regarding Key lime rainfall requirements and planting density
  (ambiguous inch-vs-millimeter and per-acre-vs-per-hectare figures) —
  the more internally consistent customary-unit figures are used here.
- Lime-oil extraction method is described somewhat inconsistently
  across sources: one general reference describes lime oil as
  "typically" steam-distilled, while more detailed source material
  describes Persian lime oil as cold-pressed during juice extraction and
  Key lime oil as either pressed or distilled — the two cultivar groups
  likely genuinely differ in typical practice, but this is not fully
  resolved here.
- No lime-specific crop-water-use (crop coefficient) data was found for
  either cultivar group; water-use parameters here are modeled on
  general citrus figures from research conducted on a different citrus
  crop.
- A productive lifespan figure for either cultivar group was not found;
  the estimate used here is a rough judgment call.
- Labor-hour figures for both cultivar groups are rough estimates
  scaled from a related citrus crop this project has also researched; no
  direct labor-hour study was found for either lime.
- Whether Key lime should be considered to have genuine wild,
  naturalized populations (as at least one source suggests for parts of
  its range) versus being treated as purely cultivated was not resolved
  here.
- Soil nutrient uptake and pH tolerance figures for both cultivar groups
  are estimated rather than directly sourced.
- Key lime's harvest pattern (two distinct seasonal peaks per year) does
  not map cleanly onto a single-plateau yield-profile model; this is
  noted as a modeling limitation rather than resolved.

## Estimates and derivations

Climate-zone suitability for each cultivar group is derived from their
documented cold-damage thresholds and documented growing regions: Persian
lime's somewhat higher cold tolerance and broader growing-region list
support subtropical, tropical, and warm Mediterranean suitability; Key
lime's lower cold tolerance and narrower growing-region list restrict it
to genuinely frost-free tropical and subtropical climates.

World-average yield for Persian lime (roughly 15,000 kg/ha) is derived
from a national Mexican average for combined lime and lemon types,
cross-checked loosely against a global combined lemon-and-lime yield
figure used elsewhere in this project's research; actual yields vary
enormously by region, from roughly 3,000 kg/ha at the low end up to
roughly 40,000 kg/ha or more in the best-performing growing regions and
newer high-yielding selections. World-average yield for Key lime
(roughly 15,800 kg/ha) is derived from a Mexican state where Key lime
dominates local lime production, sitting between a lower national
average and a higher figure for a specific intensively managed growing
region. A poor-yield tier for Key lime (roughly 6,000 kg/ha) is
estimated from documented unmanaged garden-tree yields converted to a
per-hectare basis, informed by the fact that a serious citrus greening
disease depresses yields in less intensively managed groves.

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days and the resulting water-use "legacy"
figures are derived using the same standard international reference's
general citrus stage-length and crop-coefficient arithmetic used
elsewhere in this project's citrus research, since no lime-specific
crop coefficient was found.

Drought and waterlogging tolerance ratings, and the associated
dry-day-before-penalty and yield-penalty-per-dry-day figures, are
estimated judgementally from the qualitative descriptions in Finding 2:
Key lime is rated notably more drought-tolerant (reflecting its
described superiority to all other citrus in this regard) but still low
on waterlogging tolerance (reflecting its clear intolerance of
persistently wet soil); Persian lime is rated medium on drought
tolerance (reflecting its higher water demand) and low-to-medium on
waterlogging tolerance (reflecting limited flood tolerance on certain
rootstocks).

Peak carbon-uptake rate and growth-stage curve are estimated by analogy
to a closely related citrus crop this project has also researched,
reflecting shared evergreen-canopy behavior.

Labor-hour figures across all operations are rough estimates, scaled
upward from a related citrus crop's own (similarly estimated) labor
tiers to reflect these limes' smaller fruit size, more frequent picking
passes, and — for Key lime — the need for thorn-resistant gloves during
harvest; no direct labor-hour study was found for either lime, though
one detailed Mexican cost study confirms labor as the dominant
production-factor cost share.

Soil nutrient-uptake and pH figures are rough estimates, informed by
both limes' well-documented tolerance for alkaline, limestone-derived
soils and the specific importance of potassium nutrition noted in
source material.

Typical cool-storage duration is derived directly from documented
storage-life figures for Persian lime (roughly six weeks, a point
estimate within a documented six-to-eight-week range) and estimated for
Key lime (roughly two weeks) given only a vaguer "several days to a
week in a home refrigerator" figure was found.

Persian lime's early-years yield-fraction-by-year curve is derived from
a documented sequence of per-tree yields by year in a home-landscape
setting, expressed as fractions of a documented mature per-tree yield;
commercial hedged groves likely reach full cropping sooner than this
home-landscape-derived curve suggests, but no commercial-grove-specific
figure was found.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (days to first harvest, ramp-up
and decline periods, peak daily harvest rate, maximum days fruit can
stand before quality decay, and daily decay rate) are derived or
estimated separately for each cultivar group from their respective
good-yield-tier figures divided across their documented or estimated
effective harvest-season lengths, with Persian lime's peak daily harvest
rate reflecting its documented practice of picking fruit at most 30 days
before risking yellowing and blossom-end breakdown, and Key lime's much
shorter 10-day standing allowance reflecting that its fruit ripens and
falls from the tree naturally rather than being held on it.

Productive lifespan for both cultivar groups (estimated at roughly 20-25
years) is a rough judgment call, since no direct figure was found for
either — the formerly significant Florida lime industry was destroyed
by a hurricane and a disease-eradication program before its natural
stand life could be well documented.
