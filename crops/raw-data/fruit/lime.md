## Naming and scope

This entry covers the two commercial acid limes as two cultivar groups of
one crop entry, with separate status rows (`key_lime`, `persian_lime`):

- **Key lime** (Mexican, West Indian or Egyptian lime; *Citrus ×
  aurantiifolia* Swingle; Indian *kagzi*) [1][7]:
  - small, seedy, thorny and strongly aromatic;
  - raised from true-to-type (polyembryonic) seed [1][3];
  - often simply called "lime" [1].
- **Persian lime** (Tahiti or Bearss lime; *C. × latifolia* Tanaka) [2][8]:
  - larger, seedless, nearly thornless and hardier;
  - its flowers have no viable pollen [2][4];
  - propagated only clonally [2][8];
  - the most widely produced lime worldwide [7].

**Parentage:**
- **Key lime:** a hybrid of the wild papeda *C. micrantha* × citron
  [6][7][16]. Some triploid Key-lime accessions are Key lime backcrossed
  to citron, and 'Giant Key' is a tetraploid doubled Key lime [6].
- **Persian lime:** triploid. Marker work points to a haploid lemon ovule
  fertilised by a diploid Key-lime gamete [6][7][8]. An older account
  suggested Key lime × citron, or less likely lemon [2]. Both views are
  kept visible here; the marker study is the more recent and direct
  evidence.

**Separate entries:**
- **Makrut lime** (`makrut_lime`, *C. hystrix*): a spice-herb entry,
  `makrut-lime.md`.
- **Finger lime** (`finger_lime`): its own entry, `finger-lime.md`.
- **Sweet lime** (*C. limetta*): the `sweet_lemon` entry, `sweet-lemon.md`.
  It is a sour orange × citron type, unrelated to the acid limes'
  papeda line [6].
- **Rangpur lime** (`rangpur_lime`): a mandarin × citron hybrid [6][16] and a
  rootstock for both limes [3][4]. **Now researched (passed its gate check
  2026-11-27; see `rangpur-lime.md`):** a mandarin-looking but very sour citrus
  used as a lime substitute, and — more importantly — a **major citrus
  rootstock** (Brazil's dominant 'Cravo'), prized for drought and tristeza
  tolerance.
- **Lemon** (`lemon`) and **citron** (`citron`): parents in the claims
  above.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two limes, two farming systems.**
- **Propagation:** Key lime seed is mostly polyembryonic and comes true,
  so it is usually grown from seed [1][3]. Persian lime seed is largely
  monoembryonic and variable; only 10 of 114 seedlings were typical [2].
  Persian lime is budded or air-layered; 40% of Florida commercial trees
  were air-layers [2].
- **Pollination:** Persian flowers have no viable pollen and set fruit
  without pollination [2][4]. Key lime is self-fertile [3].
- **Thorns:** Key lime spines reach about 1 cm, and pickers will not
  harvest without gloves [1]. Persian lime is nearly thornless [2].
- **Harvest:**
  - Persian lime is picked 8-12 times a year. About 70% matures from May
    to autumn, with a July-September peak in Florida [2]. Bloom to
    maturity takes 90-120 days [4].
  - Key lime has two main seasons on the Florida Keys, May/June and
    November/December. Fruit ripens and falls 5-6 months after flowering
    [1]; another source gives about 9 months [7]. Processing fruit is
    collected ripe from the ground twice a week [1].
- **Withertip:** Key lime anthracnose (withertip) is a serious Key lime
  disease [1][3]. Persian lime is immune [2].
- **Cold:**
  - Key lime foliage is damaged at -1 to 0 °C and wood below -2 °C [3].
  - Persian lime leaves are damaged below -2 °C, wood below -3 °C, and
    trees die below -4 °C [4].
  - Key lime is more cold-sensitive than lemon [1].
- **Products:** Persian lime goes mainly to fresh sale and juice
  concentrate [2]. Key lime also yields hand-pressed and distilled oil
  and pickles [1].

**Finding 2 — Florida's lime industries were ended by storms and canker.**
- **Key lime:** a Florida Keys industry flourished from 1913 to 1923 and
  was "demolished" by the 1926 hurricane [1]. Growers replanted with the
  hardier Persian lime [7].
- **Persian lime:**
  - there were about 3,250 ha of commercial groves by 1980;
  - Dade County shipped 50 million kg from 2,630 ha in 1985 [2];
  - commercial production ended in the early 2000s because of the
    citrus-canker eradication programme [4].
- **Today:** US production is under 2,000 ha in south Florida and
  southern California [5]. The main producers are Mexico, Brazil, Israel
  and Australia [4].
- **Mexico:** in 2011, 79,456 ha of Key lime on the Pacific coast produced
  over 1.12 million t [9].

**Finding 3 — huanglongbing is now the main yield limit for Key lime in
Mexico.**
- **In one orchard (Colima, 2010-2011):**
  - without psyllid control, 53% of trees showed symptoms within a year
    [9];
  - asymptomatic trees averaged 57 kg of fruit, against 23 kg where over
    75% of the canopy was affected;
  - the next year no tree exceeded 30 kg [9].
- **Colima state average:** fell from 17.8 t/ha in 2010 to 9.3 t/ha in
  2014, then recovered to 13.5 t/ha in 2017 [13].
- **Management packages (2019):**
  - traditional management at 200 trees/ha gave 20.5 t/ha;
  - the INIFAP package gave 26.5 t/ha, with 312 trees/ha, fertigation and
    6-8 psyllid sprays a year [10].
- **Other diseases:** lime witches' broom, a phytoplasma disease, is the
  most destructive lime disease in Oman, the UAE and Iran. Trees collapse
  within 4-5 years of symptoms [15].

**Finding 4 — Key lime tolerates drought but not wet roots.**
- **Drought:** Key lime "tolerates drought better than any other citrus
  fruit" [1]. Mature trees need only infrequent watering, and
  over-watering causes decline [3].
- **Wet climates:** excessive rain brings fungal disease [1], and the
  trees do not tolerate flooding [3].
- **Persian lime:** grows year-round, so it needs more fertiliser and
  irrigation than other citrus [2]. Air-layered trees and trees on
  alemow tolerate a few days of flooded soil [4].
- **Measured water use:** in semi-arid Brazil, drip-irrigated Persian
  lime used 110-130 L per plant per day, with a crop coefficient of
  0.74-0.84 [11]. Citrus needs about 900-1,200 mm a year [11].

**Finding 5 — sold green, so colour and rind injury limit storage.**
- **Green sale:** both limes turn yellow when ripe but are sold green [8].
  US buyers prefer green fruit, which keeps longer than yellow fruit;
  some other countries prefer yellow fruit for its higher juice [5].
- **Persian lime:**
  - store at 10 °C and 95% RH for up to 8 weeks [5], or 6-8 weeks [2];
  - the lowest safe temperature is 7-9 °C, below which the rind pits [5].
- **Key lime:**
  - cold injury occurs at 7 °C; 9 °C at 85-90% RH is recommended [1];
  - another source gives 12.5-15.5 °C and 75-85% RH [7];
  - gibberellic acid plus polyethylene bags kept fruit marketable for 65
    days at ambient temperature in Trinidad [1].
- **Stylar-end breakdown:** this Persian-lime disorder can start within
  2 hours of picking. Oversize fruit picked in the early morning is most
  at risk, and losses have reached 40%. Precooling for 24 hours greatly
  reduces it [2]. Afternoon harvest is advised [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree or shrub [1][2].
  - **Key lime:** 2-4 m tall [1], or up to 5 m [7].
  - **Persian lime:** 4.5-6 m [2], or about 5 m [4].
  - **Persistence:** Persian leaves last up to 3 years [4].
  - **Flowering:** both flower intermittently all year. Persian lime
    blooms mainly in February-April in Florida [4], and Key lime mainly in
    spring [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial, with repeated picking (Finding 1).
  - **Persian lime:** 8-12 picks a year [2].
  - **Key lime:** two main seasons, plus scattered fruit year-round [1].
  - **Productive life:** not sourced.
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Key lime:** seedlings bear in 3-6 years and reach full production
    in 8-10 [1]. Another source gives 4-8 years to bearing and a peak at
    about 10 [7]. Budded trees bear within 3 years, seedlings 1-2 years
    later, and cuttings or air-layers within 1 year [3].
  - **Persian lime:** air-layers bear a year before budded trees but
    yield less later [2].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Cold limits:** see Finding 1 [3][4]. Persian lime is hardier than
    Key lime and less hardy than grapefruit [4].
  - **Elevation:** Key lime grows wild in warm Himalayan valleys and is
    cultivated up to 1,200 m [1].
  - **Rainfall:** Key lime thrives in a warm, moist climate with
    "80 and 150 in (203-381 mm)" of rain a year [1]. The inch figures
    convert to 2,030-3,810 mm, so the millimetre figures are wrong by a
    factor of 10 (see Open research questions).
  - **Chill:** none required; both are evergreen. Not sourced
    specifically.
- **Composition:**
  - **Raw limes, per 100 g:** water 88.3 g; energy 30 kcal (126 kJ);
    protein 0.7 g; fat 0.2 g; carbohydrate 10.5 g; fibre 2.8 g; calcium
    33 mg; potassium 102 mg; vitamin C 29.1 mg [12].
  - **Raw lime juice, per 100 g:** water 90.8 g; energy 25 kcal (104 kJ);
    protein 0.42 g; carbohydrate 8.4 g; potassium 117 mg; vitamin C 30
    mg [12].
  - **Central American Key-lime analyses:** water 88.7-93.5 g; ascorbic
    acid 30.0-48.7 mg [1].
  - **Persian fruit:** about 54 g; 45-55% juice by volume; 5-6% acid;
    8-10% soluble solids [4].
  - **Key lime fruit:** 2.5-5 cm across [1], 3-6 cm [7] or 38-51 mm [3];
    10-15 seeds [3].
  - **US grade:** both limes need 42% juice by volume; Persian lime must
    also be at least 4.76 cm across [5].
- **Edibility:** Juice raw-edible (very sour). Used as juice, garnish and
  flavouring rather than eaten whole [1][2].
- **Pollination:**
  - **Persian lime:** no viable pollen; parthenocarpic. It is seedless in
    solid plantings, but may get a few seeds near other citrus [2][4].
  - **Key lime:** self-fertile [3]. All flowers of 'Everglade' are
    perfect, so fruit is borne in large clusters [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Not applicable to storage
  organs.
- **Yield:**
  - **Key lime, Mexico:**
    - 20.5 t/ha traditional, 26.5 t/ha under the INIFAP package [10];
    - Colima average 9.3-17.8 t/ha in 2010-2017 [13];
    - about 14 t/ha on the Pacific coast in 2011 (see Estimates, below)
      [9].
  - **Key lime, general:** about 6 t/ha at 5-7 years, rising to a steady
    12-18 t/ha; seedling trees eventually out-yield grafted ones [7].
  - **Key lime per tree:** 13-23 kg for vigorous mature home trees [3];
    57 kg for healthy Colima trees [9].
  - **Persian lime, Brazil:**
    - 32.6 t/ha under drip in Alagoas, against a regional average of
      19.65 t/ha in 2019 and 28.15 t/ha in 2020 [11];
    - São Paulo about 33 t/ha in 2021 [14].
  - **Persian lime, Florida:** 600 bushels per acre under advanced
    management, about 20 t/ha (see Estimates, below) [2].
  - **Persian lime per tree:**
    - 41 kg on alemow against 29 kg on rough lemon, for 2.1 m trees [2];
    - home trees rise from 4-5 kg in year 1 to 90-113 kg in year 6, and
      reach 200-250 kg at 12-15 years (see Estimates, below) [4].
  - **Mexico, all lemons and limes, 2024:** about 15.3 t/ha (see
    Estimates, below) [13].
- **Water use:**
  - **Measured (Persian lime, drip, semi-arid Brazil):** 110-130 L per
    plant per day, with crop coefficients of 0.74-0.84 and soil tension
    held at -30 to -50 kPa [11].
  - **Other estimates:** 36-90 L per plant per day, cited from earlier
    work; 900-1,200 mm a year [11].
  - **Irrigation guidance:**
    - Key lime: water young trees in dry spells of 5 days or more; trees
      4 years and older only in prolonged drought [3];
    - Persian lime: water trees 4 years and older in long dry periods,
      especially from bloom to fruit development [4];
    - commercial Florida Persian groves used overhead sprinklers [2].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Key lime:**
    - from seed (true to type); seed must stay moist and is stored 5-6
      months before planting [7];
    - also from root sprouts, cuttings, air-layers, and budding onto
      rough lemon or sour orange [1]. Sour orange gives more resistance
      to hurricanes [1].
    - **Rootstocks for calcareous soils:** alemow, rough lemon, Volkamer
      lemon and Rangpur lime [3].
  - **Persian lime:**
    - budded onto rough lemon, or more recently alemow (*C. macrophylla*)
      [2];
    - **rootstocks for high-pH soils:** rough lemon, alemow, Rangpur
      lime, Volkamer lemon and US-801/812/897; Swingle citrumelo for
      acid soils [4];
    - **topworking:** sweet orange and grapefruit trees have been
      topworked to it [2].
  - **Clonal/viral risk:** Persian lime is clonal and susceptible to
    crinkly leaf, psorosis, tatterleaf, tristeza, exocortis and
    xyloporosis [2]. The virus-free USDA 'No. 1' and 'No. 2' clones were
    released through Florida's budwood registration [2]. 'Idemor' was
    dropped for virus susceptibility [2]. Seed-grown Key lime avoids
    graft-transmitted viruses; that is an inference.
- **Labor:**
  - **Hours:** labour hours per hectare were not sourced.
  - **Cost share (Colima Key lime):** under traditional management, 75% of
    MXN 33,915/ha went to production factors, and 69.2% of that to
    labour, about 52% of total cost (see Estimates, below) [10].
    Harvest is the largest labour item [10].
  - **Harvest pay:** pickers are paid per box, MXN 40-60, depending on
    how abundant the fruit is [10].
  - **Other factors:** Key lime's thorns slow picking [1]. Persian lime
    needs 8-12 harvest passes [2].
- **Soil:**
  - **Key lime:** grows well on Florida Keys oolitic limestone; on sandy
    mainland soils, adding lime to raise pH improves cropping [1]. Stiff
    clays are unsuitable [1].
  - **Persian lime:** oolitic limestone or deep sand; must be well
    drained; raised beds or mounds on wet sites [2][4].
  - **Potash:** important to yield in both [1][2].
  - **Salinity:** Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - **Persian lime:** 10 °C and 95% RH for up to 8 weeks [5]; lowest safe
    temperature 7-9 °C [5]. Ethylene production under 0.1 µL/kg/h and
    respiration under 10 mg CO₂/kg/h [5].
  - **Key lime:** 9 °C at 85-90% RH [1], or 12.5-15.5 °C [7].
  - **Rots:** Key lime is very susceptible to stem-end rot (*Diplodia*)
    and anthracnose [5].
  - See Finding 5.
- **Establishment:**
  - **Key lime spacing:** 7.5 m square, 70 trees per acre (see Estimates,
    below) [1]. Colima uses 200-250 trees/ha traditionally, or 312-416
    under the INIFAP package [10].
  - **Persian lime spacing:** 3-4.5 m in rows 6 m apart, 150-200 trees
    per acre, with mechanical hedging and topping [2]. A Brazilian trial
    used 3 × 1.5 m [11].
  - **Home trees:** pruned to 1.8-2.4 m [3][4].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Key lime:**
    - 'Kagzi' (India, many subtypes);
    - 'Everglade', a seedling with withertip susceptibility;
    - 'Palmetto';
    - spineless 'Yung' and other thornless sports;
    - Trinidad hybrids 'T-1' and 'T-145', bred for withertip resistance;
    - 'Giant Key', a tetraploid [1][3][6].
  - **Persian lime:**
    - 'Bearss', not distinct from Tahiti;
    - 'Idemor', a sport dropped for virus susceptibility;
    - 'Pond';
    - the virus-free USDA 'No. 1' and 'No. 2' [2];
    - 'SPB-7' [8];
    - Brazilian 'CNPMF 02' [11].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Hurricanes:** a 1926 hurricane ended the Keys industry [1], and a
    1979 hurricane crippled production on Niue [1].
  - **Freeze protection:** Persian groves are protected with wind
    machines or overhead sprinkling [2].
  - **Wind:** air-layered Persian trees are more prone to wind damage [4].
  - **Wet weather:** humid weather favours scab [1], and rain favours oil
    spotting at harvest [2].
- **Water regime:** Rainfed in humid tropics; irrigated (sprinkler or
  drip) elsewhere. Flood-intolerant (Key lime [3]); brief flooding
  tolerated by some Persian stocks [4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - **Persian lime:** picked dark to medium-dark green at about 45 mm
    [4], with at least 42% juice [2]. Left too long, it gets stylar-end
    breakdown and yellows [2].
  - **Key lime, fresh market:** picked when colour turns from dark to
    light green; picked too early, it develops rind scald [1].
  - **Key lime, processing:** gathered fully ripe and yellow from the
    ground [1]. A home guide advises harvesting only yellow-peeled fruit
    [3].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced. Foot and collar rot (*Phytophthora*) are
  soil-borne threats [1][3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Invasiveness:** Key lime is not listed as a problem species in
    Florida [3]. It has naturalised in the West Indies, Mexico and the
    Florida Keys [1][7].
  - **Pathogens:** citrus canker and HLB restrict movement [1][5][9].
  - **Fruit fly:** shipments to Hawaii and Arizona were fumigated against
    Caribbean fruit fly [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Potted home trees can be moved indoors in
  cold weather [5]. No commercial protected cropping was sourced.

## Hazards

- **Phytophotodermatitis:** peel oil on skin, followed by sun exposure,
  causes intensely itchy brown or red areas, sometimes with severe
  blistering [2][7]. Tree sap and thorn scratches can cause rash in
  sensitive people [2].
- **Thorns:** Key lime spines up to 1 cm [1].
- **Diseases:**
  - **HLB:** huanglongbing, spread by the Asian citrus psyllid [4][9].
  - **Canker:** Key lime is highly susceptible to citrus canker; Persian
    lime is moderately susceptible [3][4]. A 1982 outbreak in Colima
    affected 20,000 trees [1].
  - **Witches' broom:** lime witches' broom phytoplasma [15].
  - **Others:** tristeza; withertip (Key lime) [1][2][3].
- **Storage rots:** *Penicillium* green and blue mould, and stem-end rots
  [5].
- **Fungal contamination of the product:** no mycotoxin finding.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh fruit):**
  - picked green → precooled, especially Persian lime in summer [2] →
    graded, washed, waxed and packed in 4.5-25 kg cartons [2] → stored at
    9-10 °C [1][5];
  - Florida Persian lime: about 40% of the crop was processed locally into
    juice concentrate [2].
- **Chain 2 (juice):**
  - Key lime: 1 t of fruit gives about 480 kg of juice [1];
  - Dominica juice is settled in vats and exported in casks [1];
  - products include concentrate, limeade, cordials and pies [1][2].
- **Chain 3 (oil):**
  - **Key lime, three methods [1]:**
    1. hand-pressing in a spiked copper bowl, giving the best oil, used
       for hard candy and perfume;
    2. machine pressing of spent half-shells;
    3. distilling the oily layer from crushed fruit settled for 2-4
       weeks, which gives the highest yield and is used for soft drinks
       and confectionery.
  - **Persian lime:** peel oil has been a by-product of juice extraction
    since the late 1960s [2].
  - **Residue:** citric acid can be recovered from the residue [1].
- **Chain 4 (preserved fruit):**
  - Indian pickles of quartered, salted fruit sun-cured for 3-4 days [1];
  - Malay vinegar pickles [1];
  - dried limes, exported from India to Iraq [1]; brine-boiled and dried
    "black lime" [7]; dried Persian lime in Persian cuisine [8];
  - marmalade and jam [1].
- **Chain 5 (by-products):**
  - dehydrated peel fed to cattle [1];
  - dried peel and juice sludge used to clean metal in India [1];
  - lime twigs used as chewsticks in tropical Africa [1].
- **Medicinal and other:** many folk remedies in the source; not treated
  as established uses [1][2].

**Ritual and religious use:** one source notes "many purely superstitious
uses of the lime in Malaya" without detail [1]. No religious use was found
in the sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- **Rainfall:** the Key-lime rainfall figure has a unit error in the
  source [1]. The figures in inches (2,030-3,810 mm) are taken as
  intended.
- **Spacing conversions:** both classic chapters convert tree densities
  incorrectly (see Estimates, below) [1][2].
- **Key-lime ripening:** 5-6 months from flower [1] against about 9
  months [7].
- **Key-lime storage:** 9 °C [1] against 12.5-15.5 °C [7], with chilling
  injury at 7 °C [1] and a 7-9 °C lowest safe range for limes generally
  [5]. A dedicated Key-lime storage study was not found.
- **Brazilian trial density:** the drip trial reports 44 kg per plant
  and 32.6 t/ha, but its stated 3 × 1.5 m spacing implies about 98 t/ha
  (see Estimates, below) [11].
- **Not sourced:** labour hours, salinity tolerance, productive lifespan
  and soil biology. There is also no world area or yield figure that
  separates limes from lemons.

## Estimates and derivations

**Pacific-coast Mexican Key lime, 2011.** 1.12 million t ÷ 79,456 ha [9]
= 14.1 t/ha (a lower bound, since the source says "over 1.12 million").

**Mexico, all lemons and limes, 2024.** 3,275,291 t ÷ 214,467 ha [13] =
15.3 t/ha.

**Florida Persian lime yield.** 600 bushels/acre × 250 limes per bushel
[2] = 150,000 limes/acre. At about 54 g per fruit [4], that is 8.1 t/acre.
Since 1 acre = 0.4047 ha, 8.1 ÷ 0.4047 = about 20 t/ha. The fruit weight
comes from a different source than the bushel count, so this is
approximate.

**Persian per-tree yields** (lb × 0.4536) [4]:
- year 1: 8-10 lb = 3.6-4.5 kg;
- year 6: 200-250 lb = 91-113 kg;
- unpruned trees at 12-15 years: 450-550 lb = 204-249 kg, and up to
  700 lb = 318 kg.

**Key lime per-tree yield.** The 30-50 lb [3] converts to 13.6-22.7 kg,
which matches the source's metric figures.

**Spacing conversions.**
- **Key lime:** 25 ft = 7.6 m square → 1 acre ÷ 7.6² m = 4,047 ÷ 58 =
  about 70 trees/acre, matching the source [1]. That is 70 ÷ 0.4047 =
  about 173 trees/ha. The source's "(28/ha)" is a conversion error.
- **Persian lime:** 150-200 trees/acre [2] = 370-494 trees/ha. The
  source's "(60-80/ha)" is a conversion error.

**Brazilian drip-trial density check.** A 3 × 1.5 m spacing gives 10,000
÷ 4.5 = 2,222 plants/ha. Then 2,222 × 44 kg = 97.8 t/ha, against the
32.6 t/ha reported. Instead, 32,600 ÷ 44 = 741 plants/ha, which fits a
spacing of about 13.5 m² per plant [11]. The per-plant water figures
cannot be converted to millimetres until this is resolved.

**Colima labour share.** 75% × 69.2% = 51.9% of total cost to labour.
Of MXN 33,915/ha, that is about MXN 17,600/ha [10].

**Colima revenue at the 2019 average price** (MXN 5,974/t) [10]:
- traditional: 20.5 t/ha × 5,974 = MXN 122,500/ha;
- INIFAP package: 26.5 × 5,974 = MXN 158,300/ha.

**HLB per-tree loss.** (57 - 23) ÷ 57 = 60% lower yield in trees with over
75% of the canopy affected [9].

**Key lime juice yield.** 480 kg ÷ 1,000 kg [1] = 48% of fruit weight. This
is consistent with the 45-55% juice by volume for Persian lime [4].

**Florida 1985.** 50 million kg ÷ 2,630 ha [2] = 19.0 t/ha. This agrees
with the bushel-based estimate above.
