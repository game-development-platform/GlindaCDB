## Naming and scope

*Artocarpus altilis*, family Moraceae, known by numerous regional names
across the Pacific [1][3]. This entry covers both seedless and seeded
forms. Most seedless Polynesian varieties are triploid clones; seeded
diploid types and hybrids with a related wild species (found in
Micronesia) are adapted to atoll growing conditions. These are treated
as cultivar groups within this one entry rather than separate crops
[3][4].

There is a naming conflict around the term "breadnut": two entirely
different plants carry that name. One is the wild seeded ancestor of
this crop, grown specifically for its seeds — treated as a separate
crop entry in this project's Nut category rather than covered here.
The other is an unrelated tree in a different genus entirely (related
to this crop only at the family level), sometimes called breadnut in
older literature, which this project tracks only as a candidate for
wild/dual-mode foraging content rather than as a farmed crop entry.

No other crop in this project is a close researched sibling; a related
tropical fruit tree this project has also researched is used only as a
water-use analogy in one specific estimate below, not as a general
agronomic comparison.

This crop is genuinely distinct from ordinary tree fruit in several
ways: it functions as a tree-grown starch staple rather than a dessert
fruit, prepared and processed exactly like a root crop (boiled,
roasted, pit-fermented, and dried into flour); it is mostly seedless
and propagated vegetatively from root cuttings rather than seed; and
its fresh fruit perishes extremely fast, within one to three days of
ripening.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — fundamentally a tree that behaves like a root crop.**
The mature green fruit is essentially a starchy vegetable, with a large
majority of its weight being edible pulp [3]. It is prepared in every
form typical of a staple root crop: boiled, steamed, roasted in a fire
or earth oven, or fried; fermented in leaf-lined pits into a sour
preserved paste; roasted and fire-dried; pounded into a starchy paste;
or dried and milled into flour [1][3][6]. Raw, most varieties are
documented as purgative, and traditional preparation sometimes involves
boiling twice and discarding the water, though a few named cultivars
can be eaten raw [1]. Fully ripe, soft fruit, by contrast, is sweet and
eaten as a dessert fruit [1][3].

**Finding 2 — fresh fruit lasts only one to three days; preservation is
the whole point of the crop.** Mature harvested fruit ripens and
softens within one to three days of picking [3]. Cool storage at a
specific moderate temperature and high humidity extends this to roughly
two to three weeks at most, with sources giving somewhat different
upper bounds [1][5]. Chilling injury begins within about a week at
temperatures right around the storage threshold, causing brown surface
scald, failure to soften properly, and decay — the fruit is deliberately
room-cooled rather than hydrocooled, since contact with cold water
browns the skin [5]. Several traditional Pacific preservation methods
exist specifically because of this short shelf life: pit fermentation
(where the fermenting mash's pH falls sharply over about four weeks and
the resulting paste can keep a year or more, reportedly several years in
older traditional practice), dried cooked-paste sheets (documented
keeping for at least three years in one Micronesian atoll tradition),
dried sliced fruit (keeping many months), and milled flour (keeping
roughly six to twelve months) [1][7][8].

**Finding 3 — propagated clonally from root material; bears in two to
six years and produces for 50 years or more.** Seedless varieties can
only be propagated vegetatively [3][4]. Several propagation methods
exist with documented success rates: severed root shoots (a lower
success rate transplanted directly, a much higher rate if first nursed
in containers), root cuttings of a specific length and diameter taken
as the rainy season begins (shoots emerge within three to four weeks,
field-ready plants within four to six months), air-layering (with a
documented failure rate up to half), and grafting onto seeded breadfruit
or breadnut rootstock [3][4][6]. Nursery trees are typically planted
into the field after six to nine months. First fruit arrives in under
three years for some Caribbean cultivars, three to six years for other
clonal material, and six to ten years for true seedlings (used mainly
for rootstocks and breeding); mature trees remain productive for roughly
50 years [1][3][6].

**Finding 4 — yields are large but genuinely irregular.** A typical
mature tree bears a large number of fruit annually, with documented
figures ranging from under a hundred to over seven hundred fruit per
tree depending on variety, site, and management, and at least one
regional average far above the general typical range [3][4]. Per-
hectare figures vary enormously by farming system: a traditional mixed
agroforestry planting yields modestly; a managed but unfertilized
small export orchard yields considerably more; and a theoretical
intensive-planting potential figure runs several times higher still
[1][3]. Most growing regions produce one or two crops a year — a main
crop in the hot rainy season and often a smaller secondary crop several
months later — and a meaningful share of young fruit (up to roughly a
quarter) can drop prematurely early in the fruiting season [3][4][6].
No international agricultural statistics agency tracks this crop
separately, so no standard world-production figure exists.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A large evergreen (or, in regions with a long
  dry season, briefly deciduous) perennial tree, typically 12-15 meters
  tall with buttressed roots and latex throughout all plant parts.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing; typically one or two crops
  per year, a main crop in the hot rainy season and often a smaller
  secondary crop a few months later.
- **Climate:** Tropical, within roughly 17 degrees of the equator in
  maritime and continental tropical climates, performing best at lower
  elevations though tolerated to over 1,000 meters in some regions.
- **Food status:** Mature green fruit is not raw-edible (purgative in
  most varieties); cooked fruit is edible and is the primary food use;
  fully ripe fruit of some varieties is sweet and raw-edible as a
  dessert.
- **Pollination:** Not required for seedless fruit, which forms without
  pollination (parthenocarpic); seeded cultivar groups require cross-
  pollination by wind or insects (bees collect pollen from male flower
  spikes).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** No day-length dependence for flowering
  was found for this species or at the genus level; flowering timing
  is consistently described across sources as driven by temperature
  and rainfall seasonality (a main wet-season crop plus a smaller crop
  several months later, Finding 4), not photoperiod. Not applicable to
  tuberization/bulbing — a tree crop with no storage organ.
- **Yield / composition:** See Finding 4 and Estimates below for the
  range between traditional agroforestry, unfertilized export-orchard,
  and theoretical intensive-planting yield tiers.
- **Propagation:** Entirely vegetative for seedless varieties — root
  cuttings, root shoots, air-layering, or grafting onto seeded
  rootstock; true seed (in seeded varieties only) has no dormancy,
  cannot be dried or stored, and loses viability within weeks.
- **Water use:** No dedicated crop-coefficient study exists for this
  crop; a coefficient is estimated by analogy to another humid-tropical
  evergreen fruit tree this project has also researched, informed by a
  documented regional irrigation guideline rate. Mature trees tolerate
  several months of drought without dying but respond by dropping
  fruit, while young trees need consistently moist soil; the crop
  tolerates waterlogged soil only briefly, with prolonged excess
  moisture killing roots and young trees.
- **Labor:** A documented multi-year household labor budget from one
  Pacific export-orchard model gives a total labor figure per hectare
  per year, though the split between specific operations (harvest,
  pruning, post-harvest handling) is estimated rather than directly
  sourced.
- **Soil:** Neutral to slightly alkaline soil preference; light to
  medium, freely draining soils (sands, sandy loams, loams, sandy clay
  loams) are preferred, though some traditional varieties grow
  successfully on shallow coral sand. The species as a whole tolerates
  saline soils and coralline/atoll soils, and tolerates brief salt-
  spray exposure, though prolonged salt spray causes leaf yellowing
  and leaf drop — a qualitative rating only, no numeric salinity
  threshold given, consistent with Source [9]'s confirmation that no
  entry for this crop exists in the standard FAO salinity-tolerance
  tables [4].
- **Stand life:** First fruit arrives in two to three years for grafted
  trees, under three years for the earliest-bearing Caribbean
  cultivars, three to six years for other clonal material, and six to
  ten years for true seedlings; full bearing is typically reached by
  around the ninth or tenth year; trees remain productive for roughly
  50 years or more, with documented tree decline on some atoll and
  Caribbean sites attributed to storms, drought, age, or salinity
  rather than any single identified pathogen.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Mealybugs, a fruit-spotting bug (a
  significant pest in at least one Pacific growing region), a leaf-
  feeding sharpshooter insect, and fruit flies on ripe fruit are
  documented pests; a root-rot fungus, a water-mold fruit and root rot,
  a separate fungal fruit rot, and a fungal leaf spot are documented
  diseases, along with a historical disease outbreak that killed
  thousands of trees on one Micronesian atoll in the late 1950s.
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named Pacific and Caribbean varieties exist
  (a major germplasm collection holds well over a hundred), differing
  in fruit size, flesh color, tree size, seededness, atoll-salinity
  adaptation, and bearing age; two named Caribbean cultivars are
  particularly well documented for their distinct culinary use (one
  preferred roasted, the other preferred boiled or fried) and yield-
  by-age pattern.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — most varieties' mature green (unripe) fruit is
purgative when eaten raw, with the exact causative mechanism not firmly
established in the sources consulted (specific digestive enzymes and a
plant sterol compound have been proposed); mitigated entirely by
cooking, and in some traditional practice by double-boiling with the
water discarded. A few named cultivars can be eaten raw even unripe.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

The plant's latex is used in folk medicine (including a diluted
preparation for diarrhea and topical use for skin ailments) and has a
documented irritant potential; this is a minor, non-dietary concern
rather than a food-safety hazard. No livestock-safety issue was found;
leaves are readily eaten by cattle, goats, horses, and pigs, and the
fruit is a standard pig feed, though one documented poultry trial found
slower growth on a breadfruit-based diet compared to standard staple-
crop feeds.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit picked green-mature → latex drained → washed and
  graded → cool storage, or ripened briefly for dessert use. Fruit →
  peeled and cored → sliced or shredded, dried, and milled into flour;
  or pit-fermented into a long-keeping preserved paste. Fruit → roasted
  whole → fire-dried into pieces, or pounded into a paste and sun-dried
  into storable sheets. Ripe fruit → mashed into puddings or fruit
  leather. Seeds of seeded varieties → boiled or roasted. Fallen male
  flower spikes → boiled as a vegetable, candied, or dried and burned
  as a mosquito repellent. Latex → caulk, adhesive, and birdlime. Inner
  bark → traditional bark cloth and cordage. Leaves, peel, cores, and
  culls → livestock feed. Old wood → fuel.
- Not covered by this research in detail: commercial canning and
  freezing, fried chips and extruded snack production, vacuum-pouch
  pasteurization, timber and boat-building use, and detailed folk-
  medicine preparation.

## Open research questions

- Maximum storage life conflicts between sources (a roughly three-week
  maximum versus a somewhat shorter range from another source, and a
  shorter still refrigerated figure with skin-blackening noted from a
  third).
- The exact chilling-injury temperature threshold conflicts slightly
  between sources.
- Typical dry-season length conflicts between sources (a shorter versus
  a longer documented range).
- Leaf habit (evergreen versus deciduous) is described differently
  across sources, apparently depending on regional climate.
- Protein and vitamin C content figures conflict somewhat between
  sources.
- Time to first bearing conflicts between sources depending on
  propagation method and region.
- Water requirement guidance conflicts somewhat between sources (one
  recommending irrigation during any dry spell over six weeks, another
  describing mature trees as generally not needing irrigation at all).
- No dedicated crop-water-use (Kc) study was found for this crop; the
  water-use coefficient used here is an estimate derived by analogy to
  another humid-tropical tree crop this project has also researched.
- The detailed per-hectare labor split (harvest versus pruning versus
  post-harvest handling) is an estimate, since the source household
  labor budget gives only a total figure.
- No national or international production statistics exist for this
  crop, so the world-average yield tier relies on a single regional
  orchard model rather than broader statistics.
- Two detailed specialist monographs referenced by other sources
  consulted here were not themselves directly opened during this
  research.
- Photoperiod sensitivity for flowering was not found at either the
  species or genus level; all sources describe flowering timing as
  temperature/rainfall-seasonality-driven instead. One unread lead
  (Borchert et al. 2005, *Nature*, on photoperiodic induction of
  synchronous flowering near the equator, cited in passing in a
  breadfruit seasonality review) was not opened and its relevance to
  this species specifically is unconfirmed.
- The salt-spray and saline-soil tolerance figures now written into
  the Soil bullet are qualitative only; no numeric salinity threshold
  exists in any source consulted for this crop.

## Estimates and derivations

A growth-cycle length to first harvest is derived from documented
bearing-age ranges by propagation method (grafts and early Caribbean
cultivars bearing fastest, other clonal material somewhat slower),
combined with a documented fruit-development period from flowering to
harvestable maturity.

Yield tiers are derived from several documented per-hectare figures
across different farming-system intensities: a conservative,
unfertilized small export-orchard model (world-average tier, rounded
down to better reflect that traditional agroforestry plantings likely
dominate overall world production), a documented peak commercial-
orchard model (good tier), a theoretical high-density mature-orchard
calculation cross-checked against a documented regional "potential"
yield figure (exceptional tier), and a documented traditional
agroforestry yield figure (poor tier).

A water-use coefficient and associated seasonal water-use-days index
are estimated by analogy to another humid-tropical evergreen fruit
crop this project has also researched, informed by a documented
regional dry-season irrigation guideline rate converted to an
equivalent crop coefficient against an assumed reference
evapotranspiration rate.

A soil-moisture-depletion allowance, drought-tolerance rating, and
associated drought-buffer figures are derived from a standard soil-
moisture-holding assumption combined with the estimated rooting depth
and estimated peak water-use rate, informed by documented qualitative
statements that mature trees tolerate drought for months (responding
with fruit drop rather than tree death) while young trees need
consistently moist soil.

A growth-stage curve is estimated reflecting the tree's evergreen,
year-round leaf-flush habit, with a slight estimated dip during
seasonal dry-period slowdown.

A nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium removal-per-tonne figure is
derived from the fruit's documented nutritional composition (protein,
phosphorus, and potassium content converted to elemental uptake),
acknowledging this covers only the edible pulp fraction and likely
understates whole-fruit nutrient removal, since peel and core are not
included.

Total labor hours per hectare are derived from a documented multi-year
household labor budget for a small Pacific export orchard, averaged
over many years including establishment; the split between harvest,
pruning/maintenance, and post-harvest handling operations is then
estimated, since the source gives only a total figure, informed by an
estimated fruit-handling rate per hour and typical operation
proportions from comparable orchard crops.

A yield ramp-up period is derived from a documented age-yield curve
showing yield rising over roughly seven years from early bearing to
near-peak production. A peak-yield-per-day figure is derived by
assuming a majority share of annual yield falls within the main
harvest season's documented length in days. Maximum post-harvest
standing time and quality-decay rate are estimated from the documented
fruit-development timeline from green-mature to full ripeness and the
well-documented one-to-three-day softening window after ripening
begins.

A fruit-to-flour conversion ratio is derived from the documented edible-
pulp fraction of whole fruit, combined with documented pulp and
finished-flour moisture-content figures, with peeling and drying
process losses not accounted for separately.
