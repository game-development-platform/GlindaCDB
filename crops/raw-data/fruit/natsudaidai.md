# Natsudaidai (*Citrus natsudaidai*)

## Naming and scope

*Citrus natsudaidai* Hayata (also written *Citrus* × *natsudaidai*), a
large, late-maturing Japanese citrus of the family Rutaceae [1][8].
Japanese names are natsudaidai (夏橙 / 夏代々, "summer daidai"),
natsumikan (夏みかん, "summer mandarin") and natsukan [1][2]. The older
name natsudaidai was replaced in trade by natsumikan in the Meiji era at
the request of Osaka merchants, because it sounded like a word for a
stroke-type illness rumoured to follow eating the fruit in summer [2].
This entry covers cropID `natsudaidai`, including its low-acid bud sport
**amanatsu** (Kawano natsudaidai), which now makes up most of the crop
and is usually what "natsumikan" means in the market [2][3].

**It is neither a daidai nor a mandarin.** Despite both names, it is
generally treated as a chance hybrid involving pomelo [1][2][3]. A DNA-
marker study of Japanese citrus inferred Kishu mandarin as one parent,
and a later review of that work lists Kishu as the pollen parent with
the seed parent not determined [7]. One germplasm collection files its
accession as a "sour orange hybrid" on the strength of a 1988 opinion,
while its own later notes question whether that rests on anything more
than the "daidai" in the name [6]. Parentage is therefore partly
resolved (Kishu mandarin × an undetermined, presumably pomelo-type
parent) and is not asserted more firmly than that here.

**Gate check (2026-11-17): promoted.** Proposed in `daidai.md`. It has
its own species name, a harvest of roughly 30,000 t a year against
daidai's roughly 600 t, a fresh-eating use that daidai lacks, and a
distinctive hold-on-the-tree-or-in-store deacidification cycle
(Finding 1).

**Why amanatsu is a cultivar here, not a second cropID.** Amanatsu
differs from the original in acid (about 1-2 percentage points lower)
and in earlier loss of acid and juice; fruit shape, rind, tree form and
handling are otherwise described as the same [1][4]. The national
statistics appear to report the two together [9]. That is a
flavour-only difference by this project's standard, so it stays under
Cultivars.

This entry does **not** cover:
- **Daidai** (`daidai`) and **bitter orange** (`bitter_orange`): see
  those entries. The shared trait is fruit that hangs through winter;
  the species are different.
- **Hassaku** (*Citrus hassaku*), a separate Japanese pomelo-type hybrid
  that the prefectural guide used here treats in the same chapter:
  about 400 g, self-incompatible (natsudaidai is interplanted as its
  pollinizer), stored without curing because curing provokes a rind
  disorder, and with a selection chosen for cross-protection against
  severe citrus tristeza virus strains [4]. It has no row in this
  project. Proposed as its own candidate row.
- **Kishu mandarin**, the inferred pollen parent [7], which would fall
  under this project's mandarin entries rather than here. Not proposed.
- **Suruga Elegant**, an amanatsu × pomelo cross [3], and other
  natsudaidai-derived hybrids. Not researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the fruit is coloured months before it is edible, and the
whole production system is built around waiting for the acid to fall.**
Fruit turns yellow in late autumn but is far too sour to eat then [2][9].
Two routes are used to get it to edible acidity. In mild districts it is
left on the tree through winter and picked the following spring: the
original type from mid-April to about June [9], when the tree is already
flowering for the next crop (flowers open around May) [2]. Elsewhere,
and now for amanatsu generally, it is picked in winter and deacidified in
store: one prefectural guide harvests amanatsu in early to mid January,
starting with the exposed outer fruit in late December to dodge cold
injury, and gives the eating period as April-May [4]. Either way roughly
a year passes from flowering to eating. A tree left unpicked can carry
last year's fruit and this year's together, which is the stated reason
for the "daidai" in the old name [9].

**Finding 2 — frost is a quality event with a specific threshold, and it
makes the fruit bitter, not just damaged.** The fruit contains the bitter
flavanone naringin [1]. Exposure to sub-zero temperature causes part of
the pulp to dehydrate and brown (*su-agari*), increases bitterness, and
increases fruit drop [1]. The prefectural guide puts a number on it:
injury follows air temperature of -3 °C or below for four hours or more,
so sites where cold air pools are unsuitable [4]. Because the fruit must
sit through the coldest months before it is edible, this single
constraint decides both where the crop is grown (less cold-hardy than
satsuma, so only the warm districts give good fruit [1]) and which of
the two routes in Finding 1 a district uses.

**Finding 3 — one bud sport replaced the species' own type within a
generation.** Amanatsu was found about 1935 in Tsukumi, Ōita, on a
grower's tree, and registered in 1950 as Kawano natsudaidai [3][4]. Its
acid falls sooner: about 1.5% against 2-3% or more in the original
[1][2][6]. From the late 1960s plantings outside Yamaguchi shifted to
it, and its output passed the original type's after 1976 [1][2]. The
fresh market is now supplied almost entirely by amanatsu, the original
type going mainly to jelly and confectionery [3]. Further sports of
amanatsu followed: shin-amanatsu (found 1962; smoother rind, more sugar
but also more naringin) and beni-amanatsu (registered 1975; red-orange
rind, albedo and flesh) [4].

**Finding 4 — production rose threefold and then fell by about nine-
tenths.** About 100,000 t in 1955, 200,000 t in 1965 and over 300,000 t
in 1969; below 300,000 t after 1985; about 110,000 t in 1995; 36,500 t
from 1,725 ha in 2015 [1]; and about 29,600 t from about 1,280 ha in 2023
[3] (see Estimates, below). The usual explanation is the liberalization
of grapefruit imports, which most sources date to 1971 (others give 1965
or 1975) [10]; one producer's account argues that the wider range of
imported and new fruit, not grapefruit alone, is the better explanation
[10]. One Kumamoto account describes amanatsu output there falling from
around 90,000 t at its late-Shōwa peak to about 7,000 t [10].

**Finding 5 — an unusually complete public cost and labour budget
exists.** A Yamaguchi prefectural guide gives, per 10 a of amanatsu: a
target yield of 4 t or more every year with 90% or more sound fruit and
80% or more in the 2L size class at 11 °Brix or more; 3,600 kg shipped;
130.4 labour hours a year; an average price of 191 yen/kg (2020-2023);
gross receipts of 687,600 yen against costs of 558,405 yen, leaving
129,195 yen before any hired labour [4]. Harvest is the largest single
task at 40.0 hours, and December is the heaviest month at 42.2 hours [4].
Few fruit entries in this project have a labour figure at all.

**Finding 6 — post-war recovery crops: former samurai and displaced
fishermen.** In the Meiji era the crop was promoted in Hagi as a
livelihood for samurai families who had lost their stipends, and old
trees still stand in the town [1][2]. A century later, fishermen around
Minamata who could no longer fish because of mercury poisoning cleared
seaside slopes and planted amanatsu, and tried to grow it without
pesticides [11]. The original seedling tree in Nagato, said to have
grown from a seed washed ashore around 1700, survives as a designated
natural monument (1927), though only its roots are original [1][2][3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen perennial tree, vigorous and
  spreading with a large canopy, 3-5 m [1][2]. Leaves about 10 × 5 cm
  with almost no petiole wing [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, one crop a year, with the two
  harvest routes of Finding 1. Alternate bearing is present; Yamaguchi's
  own late selection is noted for showing little of it [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced directly. The fertilizer
  schedule treats trees as young through year 6 and moves to the mature
  rate from year 8 according to crop load [4].
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate coastal Japan only: Kagoshima,
  Kumamoto and Ehime lead [3], with Wakayama, Shizuoka and Yamaguchi
  (Hagi) also named [1][2][9]. Less cold-hardy than satsuma [1]; injury
  at -3 °C for four hours (Finding 2) [4]; nursery sources give about
  -5 °C as the tree's limit [13]. Chill requirement: none sourced; flower
  buds differentiate over January to March [4]. Elevation: Not sourced.
- **Composition:** National food composition tables, juice sacs, raw
  (item 07093, current edition): 45% refuse; per 100 g edible, 42 kcal,
  water 88.6 g, protein 0.9 g, fat 0.1 g, carbohydrate 10.0 g, fibre
  1.2 g, potassium 190 mg, calcium 16 mg, magnesium 10 mg, vitamin C
  38 mg, folate 25 µg, β-cryptoxanthin 120 µg; organic acids not listed
  [5]. Citric acid 1.67-1.99% and 9.1-9.6 °Brix in two Yamaguchi
  selections [4].
- **Edibility:** Edible raw once deacidified; the thick, bitter segment
  membranes are removed and only the juice sacs eaten [3][9]. Fruit that
  is still too sour mellows after a few days' keeping [3].
- **Pollination:** Not sourced directly. Fruit is seedy (20-30 seeds,
  polyembryonic [1]; about 30 in amanatsu [8]), and natsudaidai is
  planted as a pollinizer for self-incompatible hassaku [4], so its
  pollen is functional. Low-seed and seedless selections exist
  (Cultivars).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. By citrus-genus analogy,
  flowering is cued by cool temperature rather than day length (see
  `daidai.md`, `chinotto.md`). Not applicable to storage organs.
- **Yield:** Target of 4 t per 10 a (40 t/ha) or more every year in
  Yamaguchi, with 3,600 kg per 10 a shipped in the budget [4]. The
  national average implied by 2023 statistics is about 23 t/ha (see
  Estimates, below) [3].
- **Water use:** No crop coefficient found. The guide irrigates after
  about 15 rainless days in summer [4].
- **Propagation:** Grafted; the guide describes spring top-working
  (best in early April) and side/bud grafting until mid-late September
  to replace poor strains [4]. Rootstock: Not sourced for this species.
  Clonal/viral risk is explicit: poor strains and old trees are to be
  replanted with nursery trees carrying a mild (attenuated) virus
  strain, and mother trees for propagation are to be chosen for showing
  little virus effect [4] — that is, cross-protection rather than
  virus-free stock is the working approach in that guide.
- **Labor:** 130.4 hours per 10 a per year (1,304 h/ha) for amanatsu:
  harvest 40.0, garden upkeep 14.5, pruning 15.0, thinning 14.0, sorting
  and shipping 12.2, spraying 8.4, weeding 8.3, fertilizing 6.0, soil
  improvement 6.0, storage 6.0 [4]. About 36 hours per tonne shipped
  (see Estimates, below).
- **Soil:** Not characterized by pH or texture in the sources. Where
  magnesium is short, 100-200 kg of dolomitic lime per 10 a is worked
  in; deep tillage with organic matter and fused phosphate from February
  to March [4]. Mature-orchard fertilizer: N 32, P2O5 22, K2O 26 kg per
  10 a in four dressings (late March, mid June, early September, early
  November), for a 4 t crop [4]. Salinity tolerance: Not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Amanatsu is cured to a 4-5% weight loss
  over 20-25 days, then held at 5-7 °C at 85-90% relative humidity
  (bare fruit) or 70-80% (individually film-wrapped), stacked at
  800-1,000 kg per 3.3 m² [4]. A simpler method lines the crate with
  perforated film after a 3-4% cure of 15-25 days: ambient storage to
  early March, then 5-7 °C to early June [4]. A cooperative notice in
  another prefecture gives a 14-day cure to about 3% loss [14]. At home,
  1-2 weeks cool and dark [3], or 2-4 weeks refrigerated [13]. A stem-
  end drop inhibitor is sprayed 10-20 days before harvest, with a
  pre-harvest spray against storage rots [4].
- **Establishment:** Replanting example of 125 trees per 10 a with
  working lanes of 1.2-1.5 m laid out from the start; a 75-tree planting
  is also referred to [4]. Open-centre trees over 3 m can be cut back to
  about 2 m in one year without loss of yield, to ease work and improve
  quality, provided trees are about 1 m apart at the canopy [4].
- **Cultivars:** Original (ordinary) natsudaidai: 400-500 g [1][3][6]
  (300-400 g or 300-500 g in other encyclopedia entries [1]), rind 7-8 mm
  [1], 11-13 segments [1]. **Amanatsu / Kawano natsudaidai:** 300-400 g
  [3]; slightly less vigorous; fully coloured by mid December [4].
  **Shin-amanatsu** (also sold as New Seven, Sun Fruit, Tanoura or
  Tachibana orange; sources disagree on which trade names belong to
  which sport) [1][3][4]. **Beni-amanatsu** [4]. Yamaguchi selections of
  the ordinary type: No. 167 (late; about 350 g, 9.6 °Brix, 1.99% citric
  acid, low naringin, heavy and regular cropper) and No. 186 (early;
  about 400 g, 9.1 °Brix, 1.67%) [4]. Low-seed and seedless forms
  (Yamaji, Tsuchiya) are named in one encyclopedia entry [1].
- **Weather response:** Frost: Finding 2 (a quality event as well as a
  hazard: bitterness rises). Wind: windbreak trees or nets are standard,
  trimmed to about 70% density, both for rind scarring and because wind
  spreads canker [4]. Young trees are wrapped for winter [4]. Typhoon
  precautions are scheduled for late summer [4]. Heat and waterlogging:
  Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** Ordinary rain-fed orchard with supplementary summer
  irrigation; no flooded requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Winter pick-and-store (late December to
  mid January, eaten April-May) or spring tree-ripened pick (mid April
  to June) [4][9]. Tokyo market shipments of amanatsu run from about
  January to June [3]. Unripe fruit thinned or dropped in summer is a
  product in its own right (Uses). Not successive ripening: one crop,
  picked once.
- **Thinning:** Three passes — 70% of the total by early July, 20% by
  early August, 10% on the tree before harvest — to a final 100-120
  leaves per fruit, taking interior and skirt fruit first [4].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Arose in Japan and is grown almost
  only there. No invasive listing, assessment or account of escape was
  found in this pass. Domestic movement of citrus nursery stock out of
  Japan's citrus-greening regulated islands is subject to inspection
  (see `daidai.md`) [15]. No cultivation outside Japan was documented
  beyond germplasm-collection holdings [6].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Citrus canker:** the main disease target, chiefly on the ordinary
  type: avoid interplanting with susceptible cultivars, prune out
  affected wood, spray before budbreak (March), before bloom (early May)
  and on young fruit (June), and cut off the canker-prone summer and
  autumn flushes in October [4].
- **Citrus tristeza virus:** managed by cross-protection with mild
  strains (Propagation) [4]. No yield-loss figure found.
- **Furanocoumarins / drug interaction:** an immunoassay screen of 25
  citrus fruits found significant furanocoumarin reactivity in
  natsudaidai *peel* but not in its juice [12]. This was a screen, not an
  enzyme-inhibition or clinical study, so a grapefruit-type drug
  interaction is neither shown nor excluded; it is most relevant to
  peel products such as marmalade. TO BE ADDRESSED.
- **Storage rots and rind disorders:** pre-harvest sprays are used
  against storage decay [4]. The rind-spotting disorder that rules out
  curing is described for hassaku, not for this species [4].
- **Fungal contamination of the edible product:** nothing found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fruit → fresh):** winter-picked fruit → cure → cool store →
  shipped January to June, or tree-held fruit picked in spring → peeled,
  membranes removed, juice sacs eaten [3][4][9]. Refuse 45% [5].
- **Chain 2 (fruit → processed food):** juice, squash, jelly, canned or
  bottled segments, fruit wine [1]; juice is blended into satsuma juice
  to sharpen its flavour [1][2]; the original high-acid type goes mainly
  to jelly and confectionery [3].
- **Chain 3 (peel → marmalade, candied peel, whole candied fruit):**
  thick peel → marmalade or sugar-candied peel; hollowed peel is used as
  a serving cup [1][2].
- **Chain 4 (unripe/fallen fruit → citric acid):** early-dropped and
  thinned fruit → citric acid manufacture [1][2]. No yield fraction
  found.
- **Chain 5 (peel → drug and bath):** dried peel → bitter stomachic
  preparations and fragrance; dried peel in bath water [1][2]. Whether
  the unripe fruit is an accepted source of the pharmacopoeial drug
  *kijitsu* was not confirmed (see `daidai.md`).
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** ice cream and other Hagi specialities
  [8]; salad use like grapefruit [2]; the flower scent of Hagi, listed
  among a national set of 100 "fragrant landscapes" in 2001 [2]; a
  mouse study of immature-peel extract in allergic dermatitis [8].

## Open research questions

- **Seed parent** is undetermined [7]; a pomelo-type parent is assumed
  everywhere and demonstrated nowhere in what was read.
- **Origin date:** about 1700 [1][3][8], 1740 in one source [7], and
  "around 1870" in one germplasm record [6], which is at odds with the
  Bunka-era (1804-1818) arrival in Hagi [1] and is probably an error.
- **Statistics year labels:** the 2023 harvest figure is clear, but the
  same page labels area and shipments 2023 in one place and 2024 in
  another [3]. The government tables were not opened. Whether the
  national figure combines ordinary natsudaidai and amanatsu [9] was not
  confirmed.
- **Grapefruit liberalization** is dated 1965, 1971 or 1975 by different
  sources [10], and its causal role is disputed.
- **Trade names of the amanatsu sports** are assigned differently by
  different sources (Cultivars).
- **Furanocoumarin finding** [12] needs follow-up with an inhibition or
  clinical study before any interaction statement can be made.
- **Rootstock, soil pH, salinity, water use, photoperiod, elevation,
  years to bearing, pollination requirement** were not sourced.
- Whether `hassaku` warrants a row (Naming and scope) needs a decision.

## Estimates and derivations

**National average yield, about 23 t/ha.** 29,633 t harvested [3] ÷
1,276 ha [3] = 23.2 t/ha. The area may be a 2024 figure set against a
2023 harvest (Open research questions); the 2015 pair gives 36,500 ÷
1,725 = 21.2 t/ha [1], so the order of magnitude holds. Against the
40 t/ha Yamaguchi target [4] this suggests typical orchards run at a
little over half the target; range roughly 21-40 t/ha. A prefectural
yield survey would replace it.

**Decline from peak, about 90%.** Over 300,000 t in 1969 [1] against
29,633 t in 2023 [3]: 1 − 29,633 ÷ 300,000 = 0.90. Since the peak figure
is "over" 300,000 t, the true fall is slightly more than 90%.

**Labour per tonne, about 36 hours.** 130.4 hours per 10 a [4] ÷ 3.6 t
shipped per 10 a [4] = 36.2 h/t. At the 4 t target yield it would be
32.6 h/t. Excludes hired labour in the cost budget but is a time figure,
so that omission does not affect it.

**Return per labour hour, about 990 yen.** 129,195 yen income per 10 a
[4] ÷ 130.4 hours [4] = 991 yen/hour, before any charge for hired labour
and with depreciation spread over an assumed 2.2 ha citrus holding [4].
