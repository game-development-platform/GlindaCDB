# Wet-Tropics Mangoes (*Mangifera odorata*, *M. foetida*, *M. caesia*, *M. pajang*)

## Naming and scope

This entry covers four related Southeast Asian tree-fruit species native
to the Malesian region's ever-wet lowlands, grouped together as one crop
profile since they share enough underlying biology (a shared genus,
similar chromosome count, large forest-tree habit, seed-based propagation,
and graft-incompatibility with the common mango this project has also
researched) to document as a single entry with species-level cultivar
groups, following the same documentation pattern this project has used
for a prior entry grouping multiple bush-cherry species. These are
species of ever-wet lowland forest where the common dessert mango — a
species with a documented dry-season flowering trigger — fruits poorly:

- **kuini / kuwini** (*M. odorata*; possibly a hybrid between the common
  mango and a second species covered here, never found growing wild) [1];
- **bachang / horse mango** (*M. foetida*) [2];
- **binjai** (*M. caesia*; including specific sweet cultivated forms from
  Bali and East Kalimantan) [3];
- **bambangan / pajang** (*M. pajang*, endemic to Borneo; the largest
  fruit in the entire genus) [4].

All four species share the same chromosome count [1][2][3][4]. This
entry explicitly excludes the common dessert mango (covered by this
project as its own separate entry) and a further related species that
hybridizes with one of the species covered here but was not itself
researched [3].

No items are shared between this entry and this project's common-mango
entry: grafts between this group and the common mango either failed
outright or became incompatible after a short period, and the fruit and
traditional products differ substantially. Each of the four species has
its own fruit product line within this entry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — overall distinctiveness from the common mango.** These four
species are suited specifically to ever-wet lowlands where the common
mango fails to crop well; they carry caustic bark sap and/or acrid fruit
juice that force thick peeling or limewater-steeping before the fruit is
edible; they are used mainly as sour condiments or as pickles and
kernel/seed flour rather than as a primary dessert fruit; they are
propagated by seed rather than grafting, since grafts with the common
mango either fail or become incompatible; and each carries its own
distinct regional market name and identity.

**Finding 2 — kuini: a probable natural hybrid grown as table fruit and
kernel flour.** This species has never been found growing wild and is
possibly a natural hybrid between the common mango and a second species
covered in this entry; it is cultivated across several Indonesian
islands, southern Thailand, and the southern Philippines. The tree
reaches a substantial height, with fruit bearing a notably thick rind;
the seed is often polyembryonic, meaning seedlings are largely true to
type. The flesh is roughly 70% edible by weight, moderate in calories
with meaningful vitamin C content. Fresh fruit is valued specifically
where the common mango struggles to produce well; the rind is peeled
thickly, and acrid skin juice is reduced by steeping the fruit in diluted
limewater. Young fruit is made into chutney and salted pickles, and the
seed kernels are ground into a flour used in two named traditional
confections in Java. This species grows below a specific elevation
ceiling, tolerating a comparatively modest annual rainfall as long as
there are no long dry periods, and produces two crops a year
specifically in regions with two distinct dry seasons. It is mostly
seed-grown, since grafting onto the common mango becomes incompatible
within a couple of years, and air-layering is possible but difficult. At
least one specific growing region is noted as specializing in this crop.

**Finding 3 — bachang: mainly a sour condiment crop with irritant sap.**
This species grows wild in lowland rainforest across parts of the
Malesian region and is also widely cultivated. The tree reaches a very
substantial height; fruit has a notably fibrous, juicy texture and a
single embryo per seed. Documented flowering and fruiting timing varies
somewhat by region, with harvest generally falling in the rainy season.
The fruit's irritant juice inflames the lips and mouth, but at full
maturity this irritant quality is confined to the skin, so ripe fruit can
be eaten safely once thickly peeled; the bark sap is similarly irritant
and whitish, turning black on exposure to air. The flesh is roughly 65%
edible by weight, with somewhat higher protein and vitamin C content than
kuini. This species is used mainly as a sour condiment: unripe fruit is
washed in salted water or soaked in limewater, then sliced for two named
traditional pickle dishes; in at least one region it specifically
replaces tamarind as the souring agent in a traditional chili-paste
condiment. In folk medicine, the leaves are used as an antipyretic and
the seeds applied to skin conditions; at least one indigenous community
uses the bark sap to darken tattoo scars. It is seed-grown, with
seedlings needing ample moisture and light shade; a modified traditional
budding method is used for selected forms, and the species is disputedly
used as a rootstock for the common mango in moist climates, though with
documented incompatibility symptoms. A specific trunk-boring beetle and
mango-weevil larvae are documented pests. This species hybridizes
naturally with the largest-fruited species in this entry where the two
are grown together. Its timber is not durable.

**Finding 4 — binjai: a flood-tolerant riverbank fruit with highly
irritant unripe juice.** This species is native to several parts of the
Malesian region, rare in untouched forest but more frequent in
periodically flooded areas and marshes; it is cultivated in Bali,
peninsular Thailand, and occasionally Java, and was historically carried
to the southern Philippines by seafaring traders. The tree can reach a
very substantial height, with a notably thin skin; fruit ripens roughly
three months after flowering, and old trees are documented bearing
thousands of fruits in a single season. The flesh is roughly 65% edible
by weight, notably low in calories and carbohydrate with high vitamin C
content. Ripe fruit is eaten fresh (wild types are sweet-sour, while
specific cultivated forms are sweet, nearly fiber-free, and command a
high price in two specific regions); it is also used in a chili-and-
fish condiment, and ripe flesh is sometimes salted and jarred for use out
of season. Critically, the immature fruit's juice is highly irritating
both to skin contact and if swallowed, to the point that it has
historically been used as a weapon to injure people. This species grows
at low elevation and specifically tolerates periodic flooding, being
grown on riverbanks subject to seasonal inundation — a notably unusual
trait for a fruit tree. It is seed-grown; grafting onto common mango has
failed outright, though inarching onto its own seedling rootstock is
presumed possible.

**Finding 5 — bambangan: the largest fruit in the genus, with a
distinctive fermented seed condiment.** This species is endemic to the
island of Borneo, found wild in lowland rainforest and on well-drained
alluvial soils, and grown in indigenous orchards across several
political divisions of that island. It is a deciduous tree (unusual among
this group) reaching a very substantial height; its fruit is the largest
in the entire genus, with a notably thick skin, bright deep-yellow
fibrous flesh that is sweet-acid in flavor, and a single embryo per
seed; the bark sap is irritant and can blister skin on contact.
Documented flowering and fruiting timing varies by region. The fruit is
sold in local markets in season; the skin is dried for use in a chili
condiment, and young flushing shoots are themselves sold as a leaf
vegetable in at least one region. A distinctive traditional fermented
condiment from one indigenous community combines half-ripe (or
sometimes fully ripe) flesh with grated dried seed from the same fruit
and salt, sometimes with chili, sealed in a jar and fermented for at
least a week to several weeks; the same grated dried seed is also used to
sour a traditional raw fish salad. This species grows from wet lowlands
to above a specific elevation and specifically avoids swampy sites,
unlike the flood-tolerant species described in Finding 4. At least one
source notes that all horticultural aspects of this species still need
further investigation.

**Finding 6 — yield, bearing age, and storage data are essentially
absent for all four species.** No dedicated per-hectare yield figures,
bearing-age figures, or storage-duration data were found for any of the
four species in this entry; the estimates provided below are built
instead from documented tree spacing and the documented "thousands of
fruits" figure for old specimens of one species.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Large evergreen forest trees for three of the
  four species, reaching very substantial heights; the fourth species
  (bambangan) is deciduous. Perennial bearing, with fruit gathered in
  season from tall seedling-grown trees.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest window for most species
  groups, except kuini, which produces two crops a year specifically in
  regions with two distinct dry seasons; bachang has two distinct harvest
  stages (an unripe stage used for condiments, and a ripe stage eaten
  fresh after thick peeling).
- **Climate:** Tropical, ever-wet lowlands; kuini tolerates a
  comparatively modest annual rainfall provided there are no long dry
  periods; binjai is restricted to low elevation, while bachang and
  bambangan tolerate elevations well above 1,000 meters.
- **Composition:** Composition varies meaningfully by species — kuini is
  the most calorie-dense of the three species with documented figures,
  binjai the least calorie-dense but highest in vitamin C alongside
  bachang, and bachang has the highest documented protein content; a
  blended row-level default is used where species-specific data is
  needed but unavailable.
- **Pollination:** Not specifically researched for any of the four
  species; general insect pollination is assumed by analogy with the
  common mango, and two of the species have documented fragrant flowers
  consistent with insect attraction.
- **Yield:** No dedicated yield data exist for any of the four species;
  figures here are rough estimates built from documented tree spacing and
  a documented "thousands of fruits per old tree" figure for one species
  (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No species-specific standard crop-coefficient curve
  exists; water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to the common
  mango's documented measured water-use figures, adjusted to reflect this
  group's lack of the common mango's dry-season physiological adaptation.
  These are rain-forest-adapted trees that generally need rainfall spread
  evenly through the year rather than concentrated wet-and-dry seasonal
  cycles; binjai specifically tolerates periodic flooding, while
  bambangan specifically avoids swampy sites.
- **Propagation:** Exclusively by seed in practice; kuini's seed is often
  polyembryonic (producing largely true-to-type seedlings), while the
  other three species are single-embryo (producing more variable
  seedlings). Air-layering is possible but difficult for kuini; a
  modified traditional budding method is used on seedling rootstock for
  bachang and bambangan; inarching is presumed possible for binjai.
  Grafting onto the common mango either fails outright or becomes
  incompatible within a couple of years across the species where it has
  been tried.
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor study was found for any of the four
  species; labor figures here are rough estimates reflecting the
  particular difficulty of harvesting fruit from very tall forest trees
  (climbing or gathering windfall) and the additional post-harvest labor
  of pickling, salting, or drying seed and skin for traditional
  preparations.
- **Soil:** Not specifically researched for this group; a mildly to
  moderately acidic pH tolerance is estimated, consistent with typical
  leached wet-tropics lowland soils. Binjai is documented growing on
  periodically flooded alluvium, while bambangan is documented on well-
  drained alluvium.
- **Stand life:** Not documented for any of the four species; bearing age
  is estimated at roughly six to eight years from seed by analogy with
  the common mango, with tall forest-tree habits suggesting a somewhat
  later bearing age than common mango's documented seedling bearing age.
  Old specimens of at least one species are documented bearing thousands
  of fruits in a single season.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Mango-weevil larvae in the flesh, a specific
  fruit-borer pest affecting kuini, and a specific trunk-boring beetle
  affecting both bachang and bambangan.
- **Cultivars:** Each of the four species functions as its own cultivar
  group within this entry, with further named forms documented within
  some species — for kuini, a mild/less-fibrous table-fruit form and a
  distinctly inferior form with a strong turpentine flavor; for bachang,
  a large fine-textured form and a form bearing many fruit with abortive
  seeds; for binjai, a specific high-priced sweet, nearly fiber-free
  cultivated form from two named regions, alongside the sweetish-sour
  wild/riverbank type.
- **Harvest:** Fruit gathered from tall trees by climbing or collecting
  windfall; bachang is harvested at two distinct stages (unripe for
  condiment use, ripe for fresh eating after thick peeling); kuini's
  young fruit is also harvested specifically for pickling before full
  ripeness.
- **Storage:** No dedicated cold-storage data exist; ripe fruit is
  generally soft and juicy with a short ambient shelf life, and much of
  the harvest across all four species is instead converted promptly into
  pickled, salted, or fermented products rather than stored fresh.

## Hazards

- Irritant sap and fruit juice, consistent with the broader cashew-and-
  mango plant family's known irritant-compound class, are documented
  across this group: bark sap in bachang and bambangan, skin juice in
  kuini, and unripe fruit juice in both bachang and binjai — with
  binjai's immature fruit juice specifically documented as irritating
  enough, both on skin contact and if swallowed, that it has historically
  been used to injure people. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of
  this plant-family irritant-compound class, shared with this project's
  other research into related fruit trees, is deferred until this
  project has a dedicated place for this kind of shared health/safety
  data. Mitigation across the group: thick peeling, and washing or
  limewater-steeping of unripe or acrid material before consumption.
- No specific livestock-feed hazards were researched for this group.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** kuini fruit → peeled thickly (or limewater-steeped) →
  eaten fresh; kuini stones → ground into kernel flour for traditional
  confections, or sown as planting material. Unripe bachang (and young
  kuini) → washed in salt water or limewater → sliced → made into
  traditional pickle dishes or a souring condiment. Ripe binjai → eaten
  fresh, juiced, or made into a chili-fish condiment; or salted and
  jarred for later use. Bambangan fruit → scored and peeled → flesh
  eaten fresh or fermented with grated dried seed and salt into the
  traditional condiment described in Finding 5; seed → dried and grated
  for that same condiment or for souring a traditional raw-fish salad;
  skin → dried for a chili condiment; young shoots → eaten as a leaf
  vegetable. Seeds of all four species → grown on as seedlings for
  further village-orchard planting.
- Not covered in detail by this research: timber properties beyond a
  documented density range for bachang; folk-medicine and traditional
  tattoo-ink uses beyond the brief notes above; budding techniques for
  selected forms in commercial detail; wild-tree ecology.

## Open research questions

- No per-hectare yield data were found for any of the four species in
  this entry — the estimates provided here rest on documented tree
  spacing and a single documented "thousands of fruits per old tree"
  figure, and would be considerably improved by a dedicated regional
  production survey.
- Bearing age, total productive lifespan, and detailed storage-duration
  figures were not found for any of the four species and are estimated
  entirely by analogy with the common mango.
- Pollination biology was not specifically researched for any of the four
  species.
- The fermentation stage used for the traditional bambangan-seed
  condiment (half-ripe versus fully ripe flesh) is described somewhat
  differently across sources — not resolved.
- Detailed dry-season flowering-induction behavior (in contrast to the
  common mango's well-documented dry-season flowering trigger) was not
  established for any of the four species; fruiting generally falling in
  the rainy season suggests a different induction mechanism, but this
  was not confirmed.
- Soil chemistry, soil biology, and detailed atmospheric/growth-stress
  data were not researched for this group.
- A further related species that hybridizes with binjai in mixed
  orchards, and the broader set of edible Malesian species in this genus
  beyond the four covered here, were not researched in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season-length figures (flowering to ripe fruit) are derived from
a directly documented three-month figure for binjai, and from documented
flowering-to-fruiting month ranges for bachang and bambangan suggesting a
roughly three-to-five-month interval; kuini's interval is estimated by
analogy within the same range.

Composition figures (calorie and protein content) are derived as a
blended average across the three species with directly documented
composition data, adjusted conservatively downward to reflect binjai's
notably watery, low-calorie flesh.

Water-use parameters (crop-coefficient-days, legacy seasonal water
slots, drought-sensitivity days) are carried over by analogy from the
common mango's documented measured water-use figures, treating these
species as evergreen trees of broadly similar size; the drought-
tolerance rating and soil-moisture-depletion allowance are set more
conservatively than the common mango's, since these species lack that
crop's documented dry-season physiological adaptation and are
specifically documented as needing evenly distributed rainfall.

Yield tiers are built from documented tree-spacing figures (converted to
trees-per-hectare), combined with a documented "thousands of fruits on
old trees" figure for one species and an assumed average fruit weight,
to derive a rough per-hectare yield estimate; the "exceptional" tier
additionally allows for kuini's documented two-crop-per-year pattern in
suitable climates, and the "world average" tier reflects typical mixed-
homegarden conditions where these trees share growing space with other
crops rather than being grown in monoculture.

Soil nutrient-uptake and pH-tolerance figures are estimated by analogy
to general tree-fruit patterns documented for other tropical tree fruits
this project has researched, adjusted to reflect typical leached wet-
tropics lowland soil conditions, since no species-specific source was
found.

Storage-duration and labor-hour figures are rough estimates reflecting
documented fruit texture (soft and juicy, implying short ambient shelf
life) and the practical difficulty of harvesting from very tall forest
trees, since no dedicated source was found for either figure.

Bearing-age and yield-profile figures (first harvest, ramp period, peak
daily harvest rate) are estimated by analogy to the common mango's
documented seedling bearing age, adjusted somewhat later to reflect these
species' very tall forest-tree growth habit; peak daily harvest rate is
derived by dividing the adopted "good" yield tier across an assumed
harvest window.

Recalcitrant (non-storable) seed behavior is assumed by analogy with the
common mango's well-documented recalcitrant seed, since all four species
in this group are propagated by seed and no contrary information was
found.
