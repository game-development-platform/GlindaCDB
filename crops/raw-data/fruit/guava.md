## Naming and scope

The common guava (Myrtaceae), native from southern Mexico into Central
America [1][2]. White-fleshed and red/pink-fleshed types, and seedless
types, are treated here as cultivar-level notes rather than separate
entries [1][4].

Two related plants are explicitly out of scope for this entry: a
different species with small red or yellow fruit, hardier than common
guava and documented as a seriously invasive species in at least one
Pacific island ecosystem (threatening dozens of other native plant
species there), which belongs on a wild/foraged-plant list rather than
as its own cultivated crop entry; and a distinct genus sometimes called
pineapple guava, explicitly "not a true guava," which grows in cooler
subtropical and Mediterranean climates and has its own separate market
identity and flavour profile, making it a reasonable candidate for a
future crop entry of its own [2].

No especially close sibling crop has been researched by this project
yet; a tropical fruit tree with a broadly comparable processing chain
is the nearest comparison available, though nothing in this entry is
drawn directly from research on that other crop.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — three flowering seasons, and growers deliberately choose
one.** This species flowers in three distinct seasons with matching
harvests: a rainy-season flush, a winter flush, and a spring flush [4].
In at least one major growing region, the winter crop is considered
distinctly better quality and escapes a damaging insect pest, while the
rainy-season crop, though heavy, produces fruit widely described as
rough, insipid, and badly pest-damaged [4][5]. Growers deliberately
force a single preferred crop by withholding irrigation water for about
sixty days before the desired flowering window (on heavy soils, root
exposure or pruning is used instead), with three named seasonal
treatment calendars documented, each targeting a different harvest
window through a different water-withholding start date [4][5]. Growth-
regulator and urea sprays are also used to thin out an unwanted flowering
flush and favour the preferred one [4][5]. This makes harvest timing and
fruit quality fundamentally a management choice driven by deliberate
water stress, rather than a fixed seasonal trait of the species.

**Finding 2 — tough, adaptable, and often weedy.** This species grows
across an extremely wide range of soil types, from heavy clay to light
sand and gravel, and tolerates a remarkably wide soil pH range; it is
rated moderately salt-tolerant by at least one standard international
reference, and is documented surviving even where the water table sits
high in the root zone [1][6]. It tolerates real drought, though
protective irrigation improves results, and does best with roughly
1,000 mm of rainfall concentrated in a few wet months [1][7]. It has
been documented growing at elevations above 2,000 m in at least one
South American growing region, and mature trees withstand a few degrees
of frost briefly, though young trees are killed outright by freezing
temperatures [1][2]. Notably, this species is also classified as a
noxious invasive weed in several tropical regions worldwide, and wild,
uncultivated trees are still harvested commercially in at least one
South American country with an estimated wild population numbering in
the millions [1].

**Finding 3 — clonal commercial orchards, true seed mainly for
rootstocks.** Commercial trees are propagated almost entirely by air-
layering (rooting in roughly a month) or by grafting, with reported
grafting success rates in the high 80s to 100 percent depending on
method [1][8]. Air-layered trees lack a true taproot and are prone to
blowing over in wind [1]. Seedlings differ substantially from their
parent plant, and seed viability falls quickly after extraction from the
fruit, with the hard seed coat requiring a hot-water soak or a brief
strong-acid treatment to promote germination [8]. Grafted trees bear
fruit within two to three years, while seedling trees take four to five
years; trees commonly live thirty to forty years, with productivity
declining after roughly the first fifteen [1][9].

**Finding 4 — climacteric, fruit-fly-prone, and chilling-sensitive while
still green.** Fruit is picked at colour break (as skin colour shifts
from green toward yellow), roughly five months after flowering, and
must be picked every few days during the harvest window — as often as
three times a week where fruit flies are a serious problem [1][9]. Mature-
green or part-ripe fruit keeps two to three weeks under cool, humid
storage, while fully ripe fruit keeps only about a week under slightly
warmer refrigeration, or roughly a week at ordinary room temperature
[3]. Critically, green fruit held too cold develops scald, pitting, and
a failure to ripen properly, while genuinely immature fruit ripens with
an undesirable "gummy" texture regardless of temperature [3]. This
species is a preferred host for fruit flies, with insect stinging damage
beginning right at colour break; fruit is commonly bagged on the tree
several weeks after fruit set specifically to limit this damage [3][10].

**Finding 5 — as much a processing crop as a fresh-eating one.** Fruit is
more commonly cooked than eaten fresh in much of its range, made into a
thick fruit paste or "cheese" under several regional names, jelly
(the fruit is naturally rich in pectin), juice, nectar, fruit powder,
canned fruit shells, and frozen pulp products [1][2][11]. Leaves are
brewed into a tea and used in folk remedies for diarrhoea, and both
leaves and bark are notably rich in tannin, used traditionally for
leather tanning and dyeing [1][2]. The fine-grained wood is used for
tool handles, fuel, and smoking meat [1][2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen shrub or small tree, reaching
  roughly 10 m; perennial, with no chilling requirement.
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing, with up to three potential
  seasonal flowering/harvest windows per year, though growers commonly
  regulate the tree deliberately to one preferred window through water
  management (Finding 1); fruit within the chosen window is picked every
  few days over an extended harvest period.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, and irrigated semi-arid climates;
  tolerant of real drought but benefiting from protective irrigation,
  with a preference for a concentrated wet season rather than evenly
  spread rainfall; grows at meaningful elevation in some regions and
  tolerates brief light frost once mature, though young trees are not
  frost-tolerant at all (Finding 2).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible when ripe, or sliced while still mature-
  green.
- **Pollination:** Self-pollinating and not dependent on external
  pollination for fruit set, with initial flower-set rates considerably
  higher than the final rate of flowers that actually mature into
  harvested fruit; seedless cultivar types show reduced fertility as a
  direct consequence of their seedlessness.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented yields vary enormously by region
  and management, with some of the most productive documented plantings
  several times higher-yielding than a typical national average;
  nutritional composition figures for raw fruit also vary substantially
  between different reference sources (see Open research questions).
  World production statistics commonly lump this crop together with
  mango and mangosteen, so a clean global guava-only yield figure is
  difficult to obtain.
- **Water use:** Notably drought-tolerant, to the point that growers
  deliberately withhold water for about two months as a flowering-
  control technique (Finding 1), yet irrigation after fruit set
  meaningfully improves fruit set rate, size, and quality; a specific
  crop coefficient under drip irrigation and a documented net annual
  irrigation requirement (after accounting for effective rainfall at one
  growing site) were found, along with evidence that drip irrigation
  substantially outyields traditional basin irrigation at that site.
- **Establishment:** Planted into prepared pits enriched with organic
  matter and phosphorus fertiliser, with the graft union kept above
  ground level and young trees staked against wind; spacing commonly
  falls in the range of 5-6 m between trees in each direction.
- **Labor:** No directly sourced labor-hour figures were found for this
  crop; estimates here are built from an assumed picking rate applied to
  yield, since harvest requires frequent hand-picking passes over an
  extended season (see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Tolerant of almost any soil type, including shallow, medium-
  black, and even moderately alkaline soils, provided drainage is
  adequate and the root zone is at least half a metre to a metre deep;
  regular fertilisation (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and farmyard
  manure) is applied per tree according to age and timed around the
  chosen flowering-regulation treatment.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees bear from year two to three; trees
  commonly live thirty to forty years, with productivity declining
  after roughly fifteen years (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal wilt disease (worse on alkaline
  soils, with at least one popular cultivar notably susceptible), a
  fungal anthracnose disease capable of causing very heavy crop loss in
  at least one growing region, a Phytophthora fruit rot favoured by wet
  weather, a fungal scab disease, algal leaf spotting, fruit flies, a
  mealybug causing fruit malformation, and a scale-insect-related fruit
  scarring pest are all documented (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** A range of named cultivars exists, primarily developed
  in South Asian breeding programs, varying in fruit size, flesh colour
  (white, pink, or red), seed softness and count, sugar content, pectin
  content (relevant for processing use), and disease susceptibility;
  additional US-selected cultivars are also documented, along with
  seedless types that trade reduced fertility for convenience.
- **Harvest:** Picked by hand at colour break every few days over an
  extended harvest window within the chosen flowering season; fruit
  bagging on the tree is a common practice specifically to reduce fruit-
  fly damage (Finding 1, 4).
- **Storage:** Storage requirements differ sharply by ripeness stage and
  carry a genuine chilling-injury risk for green fruit, detailed further
  in Finding 4 above.

## Hazards

Documented diseases and pests include a fungal wilt disease (worse in
alkaline soils, with at least one popular cultivar notably susceptible),
a fungal anthracnose disease capable of causing very heavy crop loss in
at least one growing region, a Phytophthora fruit rot favoured by wet
weather, a fungal scab disease, algal leaf spotting, fruit flies (this
species is a preferred host and also a regulated quarantine host for
export markets), a mealybug causing fruit malformation, and a scale-
insect pest causing fruit scarring.

No human food-safety or toxicity hazard was found for this crop's fruit
in the sources consulted.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit picked at colour break is graded, packed, and cool-
  stored, or mature-green fruit is sliced and eaten fresh. Culled or
  processing-grade fruit is reduced to pulp (with seeds sieved out) and
  made into a thick fruit paste/cheese or fruit powder; fruit is also
  boiled and strained into juice, which is further made into jelly.
  Leaves are made into tea. Pruned wood is used for fuel and tool
  handles. Seed from fully ripe fruit is used to raise rootstock and
  seedling trees.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned fruit shells and
  nectar, and frozen pulp; fruit bagging as its own distinct process
  step; leaf and bark tannin extraction; seed oil production.

No religious or ritual food use was identified for this crop in the
sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- Nutritional composition figures for raw fruit differ substantially
  across three different reference sources (roughly a near-double
  difference in both calorie and protein content between the highest
  and lowest figures) — not reconciled here.
- Waterlogging tolerance is described inconsistently: one source warns
  against marshy, low-lying soil or a water table within the root zone,
  while another source describes the species surviving even in high-
  water-table conditions — not reconciled here; a middle rating is used
  as a compromise.
- Storage-duration guidance for mature-green or part-ripe fruit differs
  somewhat between two sources (two to three weeks versus a longer
  four-plus-week figure at a similar temperature) — not reconciled here.
- No labor-cost study specific to this crop was found; all non-harvest
  labor-hour figures here are rough, unsourced estimates and should be
  treated with low confidence. A dedicated regional cost-of-cultivation
  study would substantially improve on these figures.
- A clean, quantified relationship between deliberately delaying a
  harvest season (through the water-withholding practice in Finding 1)
  and any resulting yield or quality change was not found; only the
  general qualitative outcome (winter crop better, rainy-season crop
  worse) is documented.
- No world-average yield figure specific to this crop alone was found,
  since international production statistics commonly combine it with
  two other tropical fruit crops.

## Estimates and derivations

A "poor" yield tier (roughly 4,500 kg/ha/yr) is estimated at half of one
major growing region's documented national average, representing an
unregulated, rain-fed planting whose rainy-season-dominant crop is
heavily affected by pests, as described in Finding 1.

Water-use parameters (seasonal water-use index and legacy seasonal-
water-requirement figures) are derived from a crop coefficient figure
documented for drip-irrigated plantings in one growing region, applied
across a full year to reflect this species' evergreen, year-round
canopy; a separately documented net annual irrigation requirement (after
accounting for effective monsoon rainfall at one specific growing site)
illustrates how much of this crop's water need is typically supplied by
seasonal rainfall rather than irrigation. Rooting depth, soil-moisture-
depletion allowance, and drought-day tolerance are estimated from
general soil-depth guidance for this crop combined with standard soil-
water-holding assumptions. Drought tolerance is rated medium-high,
reflecting this species' documented tolerance of real drought and its
use in deliberate water-stress flowering management; waterlogging
tolerance is rated as a middle compromise given the direct conflict
between sources noted in Open research questions.

Labor-hour estimates (particularly for harvest) are built from an
assumed, unsourced picking rate applied to estimated yield, since
harvest requires frequent hand-picking passes by hand over an extended
season and no directly measured labor productivity figure was found for
this crop; pruning labor is estimated as comparatively light, consistent
with qualitative source descriptions of this crop's pruning needs.

Typical cool storage duration (roughly 18 days) is derived as the
midpoint of a documented two-to-three-week storage range for mature-
green or part-ripe fruit under cool, humid conditions.

Yield-profile parameters are derived from documented time-to-bearing
figures (first crop roughly two to three years after planting grafted
trees, full bearing reached by around five years), the chosen single-
season harvest window length, and the fact that ripe fruit is highly
perishable and quickly becomes fruit-fly-infested once it softens,
giving this crop a notably short on-plant standing time and fast post-
maturity quality decay compared to many other fruit crops.
