## Naming and scope

*Prunus persica* (L.) Batsch (Rosaceae), in the cultivars bred to need little
winter chill and grown in subtropical climates [1][2].
- **The cultivar group:** the University of Florida stone fruit breeding
  programme began in 1952 to give Florida early, high-quality fruit. It now
  breeds for about 100-450 chill hours. A joint UF, University of Georgia and
  USDA-ARS programme at Attapulgus, Georgia, breeds for 350-650 [1].
- **Chill classes:**
  - **UF/IFAS cultivar circular:** low-chill is 100-250 chill units;
    moderate-chill (north Florida and south-central Georgia) is 300-525 [1].
  - **Defoliation publication:** low is under 250, medium 300-525 and high
    over 525 [2].
  - **Nutrient guide:** Florida peaches need under 200 chill hours [3].
- **Where else it is grown:**
  - UF cultivars are grown in Australia, Egypt, Morocco and South Africa [1].
  - Subtropical Australia had over 800,000 low-chill stone fruit trees,
    mainly peach and nectarine (1997) [4].
  - Brazil and Taiwan run their own low-chill breeding (snippet only)
    [5][6].
- **Scope:** the low-chill fuzzy-skinned peaches, including the flat
  ("peento") type 'UFO' as a cultivar [1].
- **Gate check against the two sibling rows (re-confirmed 2026-11-24):**
  - **`peach`** stays separate. That row's cultivars need 600-1,000 h (see
    `peach.md`), several times what is needed here. This row has its own
    season (bloom from mid-January, harvest from mid-March to mid-May [2][3]),
    rootstock ('Flordaguard', Finding 6), defoliation and summer-pruning
    system (Findings 2 and 5) and flesh type (mostly non-melting clingstone,
    Finding 1). `peach.md` itself sends Florida peach figures here.
  - **`nectarine`** stays separate, as for high-chill peach. Low-chill
    nectarine cultivars ('Sunbest', 'UFRoyal', 'UFQueen', 'Sunraycer',
    'Sunmist', 'Suncoast') [1] belong in `nectarine`, as `nectarine.md`
    says.

**Relatives in this project:**
- high-chill peach (`peach`, `peach.md`);
- nectarine (`nectarine`, `nectarine.md`);
- low-chill apple (`apple_low_chill`) and low-chill pear (`pear_low_chill`),
  which have the same low-chill subtropical system;
- apricot (`apricot`) and plum (`plum`) as fellow *Prunus* stone fruits.

**Proposed:** none.
- **Flat peach** ('UFO', a non-melting peento) is a cultivar here, not a
  row [1].
- **'Flordaguard'** is a peach grown as rootstock (Finding 6), so it is
  planting material, not a crop row.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a crop bred for an early-season market window.**
- **Window:** cultivars ripen in April and May, ahead of northern states [1].
  Florida growers can usually get higher prices from March through early May
  because they produce the first domestic peaches of the year [7].
  - The market window is "the end of March until the first week of May" [3].
  - To reach it, trees must be in full bloom by the third week of January
    [3]. Chill that accumulates after mid-January is "not useful for
    commercial production" [3] or "do[es] not necessarily benefit" it [2].
- **Short fruit development:** the fruit development period (FDP, fruit set
  to harvest) can be as short as 60 days [1].
  - **FDP is not tied to chill:** 'UFGlo' needs 400-450 chill units with an
    80-85 day FDP, while 'UFBest' needs under 150 with 95 days [1]. (The
    circular gives 'UFGlo' as 450 in its text and 400 in its cultivar list,
    and 'UFBest' as "less than 150" in one place and 100 in another, with
    an 85-day FDP; see Open research questions.)
  - **Size:** a longer FDP tends to give larger fruit, and warm weather
    compresses the stages and gives smaller fruit [1].
- **Non-melting flesh:** the UF programme concentrates on firm, non-melting
  flesh that can be picked tree-ripe and still ship [1].
  - All UF non-melting cultivars are clingstone or semi-clingstone. No
    non-melting freestone exists [1].
  - Melting-flesh cultivars bruise easily, so for distant markets they are
    picked earlier and are less sweet and coloured [1].
- **Industry size:** Florida had about 4,000 acres of stone fruit in the late
  1970s and early 1980s, mostly in the north [8].
  - The 1980-89 freezes, with no frost protection, led to the removal of
    several thousand acres [8].
  - Harvested acres then rose from 1,231 in 2012 to 3,000 in 2014. This was
    partly because citrus growers hit by huanglongbing turned to peaches [7].
  - In 2017 the state produced "over 3,000 tons" from an estimated 2,000
    acres [8]. That is about 3.4 t/ha (see Estimates).
  - Gross returns are put at about three times those of surrounding
    southeastern states [8].

**Finding 2 — chill must be gained in a narrow window, and the leaves have
to come off first.**
- **Chill counting:** the UF model counts 1 hour below 7 °C (45 °F) but
  above 0 °C (32 °F) as one chill unit, usually summed from November to
  January [1].
  - Below 0 °C gives little or no chill. "Prolonged periods above 60°F
    (15°C) can negatively affect chilling accumulation" [2].
  - Chill falls from north to south in Florida [1]. In recent years it has
    fallen significantly below historic averages, most of all in the south
    [2].
- **Windows by region:**
  - **Central Florida:** chill can accumulate from the last week of
    October to early March, but should ideally come between early December
    and mid-January. Full bloom is then expected from the third week of
    January to early February [2].
  - **North-central Florida** (e.g. Marion County): chill comes from late
    November to January, with bloom by early February [2].
  - **South-central Florida** (e.g. De Soto County): chill comes from
    early December to January, with bloom from mid-January [2].
  - Chill that arrives once flowering has started does not count toward
    the requirement [2].
- **Too much chill also harms:** inadequate chill aborts flower buds in
  high-chill cultivars, but in low-chill cultivars abortion follows
  exposure to "excessively long chilling periods" [2]. Low-chill cultivars
  also have shallower dormancy, so they need less warmth to bloom [2].
- **Defoliation:** subtropical trees keep their leaves into late autumn.
  Leaves still on the tree prevent endodormancy and stop the buds taking up
  chill [2].
  - **Too early:** buds lack time to form, so flowers are deformed and
    buds abort even with enough chill. Carbohydrate and nitrogen reserves
    for next season also fall, giving erratic set and poor yields [2].
  - **Too late:** flowers may still form but yields can be significantly
    reduced [2].
  - **Timing:** late November, when nights typically fall below 10 °C
    (50 °F), and ideally by the last week of November [2].
    - The older nutrition page says to defoliate "before November 20th" [9].
    - Natural leaf fall varies by more than 10 days between seasons and
      can take 30 days to complete [2].
  - **Product:** copper sulphate or zinc sulphate both work. Zinc sulphate
    is recommended in Florida because sandy soils are often zinc-deficient
    and part of the zinc moves from the leaves to the buds [2].
    - The rate is 10-20% zinc sulphate [3][9].
    - Zinc sulphate is highly corrosive, so equipment must be rinsed [9].

**Finding 3 — warm bloom nights stop fruit set in some cultivars.**
- 'UFGem' (175 chill units) sets fruit well when minimum night temperatures
  are above 14 °C (57 °F) [1].
- 'UFBeauty' (200 chill units) crops unreliably in south Florida when bloom
  nights are above 14 °C [1][8].
- So the same threshold is a requirement in one cultivar and a failure point
  in another. No mechanism or dose figure was sourced.
- **Frost is the other bloom risk:**
  - Even in central Florida, freezes can occur through February and early
    March in cold spots, so good air drainage is essential [8].
  - 'Flordadawn' has an extended bloom that can help set through early
    spring frosts [1].
  - Defoliation timing can be used to shift bloom away from expected late
    cold [2].

**Finding 4 — a humid-climate crop with a much lower crop coefficient
than the arid references.**
- **Florida Kc for adult trees (over 3 years old), by month [10]:**
  - January 0.38 (dormancy);
  - February 0.47, March 0.55, April 0.61 (leaf development, bloom and
    fruit growth);
  - May 0.66 (harvest);
  - June 0.68, July 0.69, August 0.68, September 0.66, October 0.61 (shoot
    growth);
  - November 0.55 (senescence); December 0.47.
- **Comparison:** FAO-56 gives stone fruit without ground cover a
  mid-season Kc of 0.90, or 1.15 with active cover [11]. The Florida peak is
  0.69 (see Open research questions).
- **Annual totals:** the table's historic reference ET sums to about 1,108
  mm a year, and crop ET to about 683 mm, a ratio of 0.62 (see Estimates)
  [10]. The enterprise budget schedules 16 acre-inches (about 406 mm) of
  irrigation a year [7].
- **Seasonal pattern:** shoot growth coincides with Florida's rainy season,
  so less irrigation is needed then [12]. Spring is the critical time, when
  leaves and fruit develop [12]. In dormancy, depleting more than 50% of
  available soil water is acceptable [12].
- **Dormancy length:** about 50 days in Florida, against over 90 days in
  New Jersey [10].
- **Young trees:** the water use of 1-3-year-old trees scales with
  projected canopy area. For example, 35 ft² of canopy uses 13.18 gallons a
  day per inch of reference ET [10].
- **Roots:** peach roots reach about 3 ft (0.9 m). They go deeper on sandy
  Ridge soils than on Flatwoods soils, where the water table limits them
  [10]. The worked example uses 25% allowable depletion [10].

**Finding 5 — a long post-harvest season means summer pruning, not winter
pruning.**
- **Vigour:** after the May harvest, trees have a growing season of up to
  about 200 days and grow vigorous and large, so they are pruned in summer,
  generally right after harvest [3][13].
  - Commercial peach has no dwarf cultivars or dwarfing rootstocks [13].
  - Summer pruning concentrates fruiting wood lower in the canopy, raises
    flower-bud quality and strengthens scaffolds [13]. It reduces splitting
    under heavy crops and during freeze-protection events [13].
- **Timing:**
  - Trees need 4-5 months after summer pruning to rebuild shoots and flower
    buds before defoliation [2][13].
  - The first summer pruning comes 12-18 months after planting, but not on
    trees with under 18 inches of new growth [13].
- **Topping heights:**
  - manual topping at 40 inches (100 cm) and again at 60 inches (150 cm);
  - mechanical topping above 7 ft (200 cm);
  - main branches thinned to four or five;
  - mature trees cut back to 6-8 ft, and the sides trimmed 1-2 ft to keep
    alleys open [13].
- **Sunburn:** some weak growth is left inside the canopy and on the trunk
  to protect against sunburn and bark cracking [13].
- **Ethylene:** 'UFO' is so sensitive to the ethylene released during
  dormant pruning that it can lose many flower buds, so it is pruned only in
  summer [1].
- **Labour:** hand thinning and manual pruning are the largest production
  cost in Florida, at about 150 h/acre (about 371 h/ha; see Estimates), or
  about $2,000/acre a year [8].

**Finding 6 — an endemic nematode forced a rootstock of its own, and
populations now break it.**
- **The nematode:** *Meloidogyne floridensis*, the peach root-knot nematode,
  was found in 1966 on 'Nemaguard' and 'Okinawa' rootstocks in Gainesville
  and is endemic to Florida [14][15].
  - It overcomes the resistance in 'Okinawa', 'Nemaguard', 'Nemared' and
    'Guardian', the rootstocks of California, Georgia and the Carolinas [15].
  - It occurs from Miami-Dade County north to Alachua County. A survey of 25
    Florida orchards found 36% incidence on peach [15].
  - It also feeds on ornamentals, vegetables, herbs, weeds and fruits [15].
- **'Flordaguard':** a red-leaved UF rootstock released in 1991, combining
  resistance and horticultural traits from 'Okinawa' and Chinese wild peach
  (*P. davidiana*) [14][15].
  - It is the only rootstock UF recommends for commercial peach production
    [14], for low-chill peach, nectarine and plum on non-alkaline soils [14].
  - It needs about 300 chill units, yet grows and fruits as far south as
    Immokalee (about 100 chill units) [14].
  - Its red leaves make rootstock suckers easy to find and remove in the
    first 2-3 years [1][14].
  - Its galls are smaller than on susceptible stocks, and extra lateral
    roots compensate [15].
  - **Weaknesses:**
    - It is susceptible to fungal gummosis (*Botryosphaeria dothidea*),
      which has no effective chemical control, so scions are budded low
      [14].
    - It is not recommended on alkaline soils: above pH 7.0 on calcareous
      soils, iron chlorosis follows [14].
- **Resistance breaking:** *M. floridensis* populations that infect
  'Flordaguard' exist in Florida, and their distribution is unknown [15].
  - A 2022 *Journal of Nematology* paper reports the isolate MFGnv14 galling
    and reproducing on 'Flordaguard', with *M. floridensis* and *M.
    arenaria* established on the rootstock in two commercial orchards
    (snippet only) [16].
- **Alternatives:**
  - **'Sharpe':** a plum hybrid stock, resistant but criticised for small
    fruit. It is not compatible with apricot and is recommended only for
    dooryard plantings [1][14][15].
  - **'MP-29':** a red-leaved plum-peach hybrid, resistant to Armillaria and
    to peach tree short life. It is released for north Florida trial, with
    central Florida trials not yet established [1][14][15].
- **Fumigation:** Telone II (1,3-dichloropropene) and Vapam (metam sodium)
  are the only pre-plant fumigants labelled for peach in the Southeast. They
  are usually applied to 6-8 ft row strips [15].

**Finding 7 — a cold chain built around a chilling-injury "killing zone".**
- **Danger band:** chilling injury (internal breakdown) can start below about
  10 °C (50 °F). It is "most severe" at 2.2-7.8 °C (36-46 °F), the "killing
  zone" [8].
  - Symptoms are pit-cavity and flesh browning and dry, mealy or leathery
    flesh [8].
  - Fruit picked immature are more susceptible [8].
  - Ripe fruit are less sensitive, so a day or two in a home fridge is safe
    [8].
- **Regime:**
  - cool promptly below 7.2 °C by forced air or hydrocooling, then to
    0-0.6 °C [8];
  - store at 0-1.1 °C (32-34 °F) and 90-95% RH, for a shelf life of up to
    6 weeks [8];
  - for short holds, 7.2-10 °C keeps quality for 2-3 days [8];
  - the flesh freezes at about −2.5 to −0.8 °C (27.5-30.5 °F; see
    Estimates) [8].
- **Controlled atmosphere:** 6% O2 plus 17% CO2 reduced internal breakdown
  in 2-3-week trans-Pacific shipments [8].
- **Heat:** fruit held a day or more at 30 °C or above may not soften or
  ripen normally [8]. The fruit surface can run 11 °C above shaded air.
  Prolonged exposure to about 45 °C kills it, as do 10 minutes at about
  52 °C [8].

**Finding 8 — the same cultivars run on very different calendars
elsewhere.**
- **Subtropical Australia (1997):**
  - **Yields:** very low-chill, short-FDP (80-90 day) cultivars such as
    'Flordaprince' "can reach 27 t/ha". Longer-FDP (100-120 day) cultivars
    such as 'Flordagold' "may be as high as 51 t/ha" [4].
  - **Density:** the trend was 1,200-1,800 trees/ha on palmettes, some on
    their own roots from softwood cuttings [4].
  - **Vigour control:** high density needs paclobutrazol or sequential
    summer pruning [4].
- **Hydrogen cyanamide in Australia (snippet only):**
  - In 'Flordaprince', it advanced maturity by about 19 days but cut fruit
    set by over 40% even at 5 mL/L [17].
  - A second trial found a 10-day advance at one site only, with 40.8% yield
    loss at 10 mL/L [17].
  - A third found it worked in late autumn but cut yield in early winter,
    so bud stage, not calendar date, should time it [17].
- **Egypt (North Delta, snippet only):** 'Florida Prince' reached full bloom
  around the end of January and maturity by early April in 2007-2008. It
  needs about 150 chill hours below 7.2 °C [18]. The cultivar circular gives
  'Flordaprince' 150 chill units and calls it a worldwide standard
  low-chill peach [1].
- **Tropics (snippet only):** cycling trees through defoliation, bloom and
  fruiting is reported to give about 1.5-2 crops a year [5]. In the
  Philippines, peaches flowered at several times of year but set no fruit
  [5].
- **Brazil (snippet only):** most Embrapa genotypes need 200-300 chill hours
  at or below 7.2 °C ('BRS Jaspe', 'BRS RubraMoore', for example) [6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** deciduous perennial tree [1].
  - **Calendar (Florida) [3]:**
    - dormancy from early November to mid-January;
    - leaf development from mid-January to mid-March;
    - shoot growth from mid-March to late October;
    - leaf senescence from late October to late December.
  - **Fruit:** full bloom by mid-January, then about 80 days of fruit growth,
    with harvest from mid-March to mid-May [3]. Flowers, set fruit and
    fruitlets can be on one tree at once because bloom is long [3].
  - **Stand life:** the enterprise budget runs a 15-year horizon with 5%
    of trees dying and being replaced each year [7]. No biological lifespan
    was sourced.
- **Harvest pattern:** perennial bearing, with fruit hand-picked into bags,
  baskets or totes [8]. Ripening is uneven within a cultivar, so fruit are
  judged one by one by ground colour and blossom-end firmness [8]. Growers
  should plant two or three cultivars that ripen in succession [8].
- **Days/years to first bearing:**
  - **Enterprise budget [7]:**
    - first fruit in year 2, worth $1,532-3,455/acre in revenue;
    - year 3 yields 3,698-4,836 lb/acre (about 4.1-5.4 t/ha; see
      Estimates);
    - full production from year 4.
  - **'Flordaguard'** itself is precocious, often fruiting in its second
    year [14].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Climate:** humid subtropics. Disease pressure (leaf rust, bacterial
    spot) is high through the season [2].
  - **Florida range:** UF cultivars grow from the Panhandle to Immokalee
    [1]. Cultivars are assigned by zone [1]:
    - **North and north-central Florida:** 250-525 chill units.
    - **Central and south-central Florida:** 100-250 chill units.
  - **Chill:** 100-250 (low) or 300-525 (moderate) chill units (Finding 2)
    [1][2].
  - **Frost sites:** sites with good air drainage are needed (Finding 3)
    [8].
  - **Elevation:** UF/IFAS and the Australian sources describe lowland
    subtropical sites. No elevation range was sourced. Tropical highland
    trials of Taiwanese cultivars in northern Thailand fruited from the
    fourth year (snippet only) [5].
- **Composition:** no low-chill-cultivar analysis was found. The nearest
  is FoodData Central SR Legacy 169928, "Peaches, yellow, raw", per 100 g
  [19]:
  - energy 39 kcal; water 88.87 g; protein 0.91 g; fat 0.25 g;
  - carbohydrate 9.54 g; fibre 1.5 g; total sugars 8.39 g;
  - vitamin C 6.6 mg; potassium 190 mg.
  - This entry is not specific to low-chill cultivars.
- **Edibility:** raw-edible. The fruit is climacteric and keeps ripening after
  harvest, but sugar does not rise off the tree, so tree-ripe fruit is
  sweeter [1]. The kernel is cyanogenic (see Hazards).
- **Pollination:** self-fertile [14]. Outcrossing is generally about 6% in
  peach [14]. No pollinizer or bee requirement was sourced for the scion
  cultivars.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** no day-length effect on flowering was sourced.
  Leaves harden off from October as shorter days and lower temperatures
  arrive [3]. In northern states, short days and cold drive natural leaf
  fall, which in the subtropics comes late (Finding 2) [2].
- **Yield:**
  - **Florida budget, years 4-15 [7]:**
    - 5,600, 6,500 or 7,300 lb/acre of marketable fruit in three scenarios;
    - that is about 6.3, 7.3 and 8.2 t/ha (see Estimates).
  - **Florida state average, 2017:** about 3.4 t/ha (see Estimates) [8].
  - **Subtropical Australia benchmarks:** up to 27 t/ha (short FDP) and 51
    t/ha (long FDP) [4].
  - The budget scenarios and the state average are far below the Australian
    benchmarks (see Open research questions).
  - **Fruit size:** set by crop load and best raised by thinning [1].
  - **Split pits:** light crops give as much as 50% split pits in
    'Flordadawn' [1]. Light crops also raise split pits in 'Flordaking' [1].
- **Water use:** Florida monthly Kc 0.38-0.69 (Finding 4) [10]. FAO-56 stone
  fruit 0.90-1.15 mid-season [11]. Budgeted irrigation is 16 acre-inches
  (about 406 mm) a year [7]. Freeze protection uses extra water (see
  Weather response).
- **Propagation:** scions are budded onto 'Flordaguard' seedlings, because
  every stone fruit in Florida must be grafted against root-knot nematode
  [1][14].
  - **Seed:** 'Flordaguard' fruit ripen in late June, about 130 days after
    bloom. They must be picked before they drop, because heat damages the
    embryo. Seed is stratified (cold-treated) before sowing [14].
  - **Culling:** outcrossed seedlings lack the dark red new leaves, are
    typically susceptible to *M. floridensis*, and are culled at about 6
    inches. A further 10% may be culled as runts [14].
  - **Seed blocks:** grafted 'Flordaguard' seed trees should be isolated
    from other peaches to limit outcrossing [14].
  - **Cuttings:** softwood cuttings (8-10 inches) root better than hardwood
    ones. They must come from grafted 'Flordaguard' trees, not seedlings, to
    be uniformly resistant [14].
  - **Patents:** many scion cultivars are patented ('UFBest', 'Flordabest',
    'Gulfcrimson', 'UFO', 'UFSharp', 'Sunbest'). Others are not patented
    ('TropicBeauty', 'Flordaking', 'Flordadawn', 'Sunraycer') [1].
- **Labor:**
  - **Thinning and pruning:** about 150 h/acre a year (about 371 h/ha; see
    Estimates) [8].
  - **Enterprise budget, as contract costs per acre [7]:**
    - thinning $4.00 a tree, so $624 at 156 trees;
    - hand pruning inside the canopy, 2 passes at $405, so $810;
    - mechanical topping and hedging, 2 passes at $63, so $126;
    - 12 spray or amendment trips; 5 mowings;
    - mounting and removing freeze-protection emitters;
    - harvest and hauling $0.25/lb; packing $0.50/lb; brokerage 6% of
      sales.
  - **Labour hours** for harvest were not sourced.
- **Soil:**
  - **Texture:** Florida's sandy soils, with low nutrient and water holding
    [3].
  - **pH:** 6.0-6.5 ideal. Lime is used where soils are below 6.0 [3][9].
    'Flordaguard' is not suited to alkaline soils above pH 7.0 (Finding 6)
    [14].
  - **Nutrients, 2025 guide [3]:**
    - about 90 lb N/acre a year (about 101 kg/ha; see Estimates);
    - 20-40 lb P/acre a year;
    - up to 140 lb K/acre a year;
    - half before harvest and half after, split four ways;
    - the guide's mature-tree table totals 110 lb N, 36 lb P and 72 lb K
      per acre (see Open research questions);
    - foliar zinc, boron and manganese as needed;
    - avoid calcium nitrate in the rainy season.
  - **Leaf standards (Georgia):** N 2.75-3.50%, K 1.50-2.50%, Zn 15-50 ppm
    and others [9].
  - **Salinity:** not sourced for this cultivar group. The high-chill row
    has the FAO threshold (see `peach.md`).
- **Storage (postharvest):** 0-1.1 °C, 90-95% RH, up to 6 weeks; never
  2.2-7.8 °C (Finding 7) [8].
  - **Ethylene:** climacteric fruit make 10-100 µL/kg·h at the climacteric.
    Scrubbers such as potassium permanganate are used for long storage or
    shipping [8].
  - **Cooling methods:** room cooling (slowest), forced air, or
    hydrocooling, with 7/8-cooling times as the standard [8].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Spacing:** the normal spacing is 15 × 20 ft (145 trees/acre, about
    358/ha). Denser plantings to 10 × 15 ft (290/acre, about 717/ha) work on
    lighter soils [8].
  - **Budget:** 14 × 20 ft, 156 trees/acre (about 385/ha) [7].
  - **Australia:** 1,200-1,800 trees/ha [4].
  - **Training:** open vase with three or four scaffolds [14]. Tree guards
    are installed at planting [7].
- **Cultivars (Florida, chill units and FDP) [1]:**
  - **Central and south-central Florida:**
    - 'UFSun' 100, 80 d;
    - 'UFBest' 100, 85 d (ripens mid-April);
    - 'Flordaprince' 150, 78 d, melting flesh, a world standard;
    - 'TropicBeauty' 150, 89 d, melting flesh, the standard cultivar;
    - 'Flordaglo' 150, melting flesh over a white ground colour, for
      u-pick;
    - 'UFGem' 175, 83 d;
    - 'UFBeauty' 200, 82 d;
    - 'UFGold' 200, about 80 d;
    - 'TropicSnow' 225, 90-97 d, white flesh;
    - 'UFOne' 250, 95 d.
  - **North and north-central Florida:**
    - 'Flordabest' 250, 82 d;
    - 'UFO' 250, 95 d, flat;
    - 'UF2000' 300; 'UFBlaze' 300, 83 d;
    - 'Flordadawn' 300, 60 d, the shortest FDP of any named peach;
    - 'UFSharp' 325, 105 d;
    - 'Gulfking' 350, 77 d; 'Flordacrest' 350, 75 d;
    - 'Gulfsnow' 400, 110 d; 'Gulfcrimson' 400, 95 d; 'UFGlo' 400, 80-85
      d; 'GulfAtlas' 400, 120 d; 'Gulfprince' 400, 110 d;
    - 'Flordaking' 450, 65-70 d;
    - 'Gulfcrest' 525, 62-75 d.
  - **Australia:** 'Flordagold' [4].
  - **Brazil:** Embrapa's 'BRS' series (snippet only) [6].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** freezes in February and early March can hit flowers and young
    fruit. Advective (windy) freezes do the most damage [8].
    - Micro-sprinklers on 5 ft PVC risers protect against frost [7][12].
    - The budget assumes three frost events a year of 12 hours each, 36
      pumping hours in all [7].
    - Overhead sprinklers also protect, but must be removed for pruning
      [8][12].
    - Frost is not a quality improver in any source found.
  - **Warm nights at bloom:** above 14 °C, some cultivars fail to set
    (Finding 3) [1].
  - **Heat on fruit:** sunburn, and failed ripening above 30 °C (Finding 7)
    [8].
  - **Rain:** summer rain drives peach rust, which defoliates trees early
    and hurts next season's flowers and fruit [13].
  - **Waterlogging:** not sourced. On Flatwoods soils the water table limits
    roots (Finding 4) [10].
- **Water regime:** ordinary micro-sprinkler irrigation and fertigation [7][12].
  No flooded requirement.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one fresh-fruit stage, judged by the ground
  colour changing from green to yellow, or by blossom-end firmness where red
  blush hides it [1][8].
  - **Tree-ripe non-melting:** picked fully ripe [1].
  - **Melting flesh:** picked earlier for distant markets [1].
  - Early-morning picking should be avoided because wet fruit invites decay
    [8].
- **Soil biology:** root-knot (*Meloidogyne* spp.), lesion (*Pratylenchus*
  spp.) and ring (*Mesocriconema xenoplax*) nematodes; root-knot matters
  most [15]. Cropping history is the best predictor, since soil samples miss
  patchy deep populations [15].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - No invasive listing was found for this cultivar group. The high-chill
    row records peach as a naturalised weed in Australia (see `peach.md`).
  - Wild plum thickets carry brown rot, so orchards should be at least ¼
    mile (about 400 m) away (see Estimates) [8].
- **Controlled environment:** not sourced for low-chill cultivars. One
  reference in the defoliation publication's reading list is on
  out-of-season peach production under glass [2], but it was not opened.

## Hazards

- **Kernel cyanogens:** peach seed held 6.8 mg/g amygdalin in a UK survey,
  with earlier reports from 0.02 to 43.3 mg/g [20]. It was not measured for
  low-chill cultivars.
- **Brown rot (*Monilinia*):** brown rot can spread through a ripe fruit
  within 48 hours [8].
  - It is controlled by thinning before pit hardening (so dropped fruit rot
    before they mummify), siting away from wild plums, sanitised containers
    and prompt cooling [8].
  - Rhizopus and Gilbertella rots are controlled by prompt, continuous
    refrigeration [8].
- **Peach rust (*Tranzschelia discolor*):** all Florida cultivars are
  susceptible. It causes early defoliation that harms next season's crop
  [13].
- **Bacterial spot and bacterial canker:** leaf loss from canker leads to
  sunburned fruit [8]. Resistance to bacterial spot varies by cultivar:
  'Flordaglo' is resistant and 'Flordaking' moderately so [1].
- **Root-knot nematode and fungal gummosis:** see Finding 6 [14][15].
- **Inking:** a skin blackening caused by heavy metals entering abraded peel
  [8]. It is avoided by gentle handling and by stopping foliar nutrient
  sprays 15 days before harvest [8].
- **Worker and chemical hazards:** zinc sulphate defoliant is highly
  corrosive [9]. Pre-plant soil fumigants are used (Finding 6) [15].
  Hydrogen cyanamide was used in trials (Finding 8) [17].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported in this pass.

**TO BE ADDRESSED:** the safety data (exposure limits, re-entry intervals)
for kernel cyanide, livestock poisoning from wilted *Prunus* foliage,
hydrogen cyanamide and the fumigants has not been sourced. It is deferred
until this project has a place for health-and-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (commercial fresh):**
  - hand-picked into bags and poured gently into bins on trailers [8];
  - precooled in bins by hydrocooler or forced air on arrival, or packed
    within 24 hours and forced-air cooled in the package [8];
  - sorted and graded (split pits, bruises and decay count as defects) [8];
  - stored at 0-1.1 °C [8];
  - shipped by broker. The budget charges packing at $0.50/lb and brokerage
    at 6% [7].
- **Chain 2 (direct sale):** u-pick, roadside stands and farmers' markets.
  These cut marketing costs and need less cooling, because fruit sells
  within a day [8]. Melting-flesh cultivars such as 'Flordaglo' are kept for
  these [1].
- **Chain 3 (rootstock seed):** 'Flordaguard' fruit → seed cleaned and
  stratified → seedlings culled for leaf colour and size → budded with
  scion cultivars [14].
- **Not detailed here:** canning, freezing, drying and cull uses for
  low-chill fruit; ornamental use.

## Open research questions

- **Kc gap:** Florida's adult-tree Kc peaks at 0.69 [10], against FAO-56's
  0.90-1.15 for stone fruit [11]. It is not clear whether this reflects
  pruned canopy size, humidity, or method.
- **Yield gap:** the Florida budget gives 6.3-8.2 t/ha and the 2017 state
  average about 3.4 t/ha [8][7], against Australian benchmarks of 27-51 t/ha
  at much higher density [4]. Density, cultivar and survey basis differ, so
  there is no single representative yield.
- **Circular inconsistencies:** 'UFGlo' is 450 or 400 chill units, and
  'UFBest' "less than 150" or 100 chill units with an FDP of 95 or 85 days,
  in different parts of the same circular [1].
- **Nitrogen rate:** the 2025 guide's text suggests about 90 lb N/acre a
  year, but its mature-tree table totals 110 [3]. The older nutrition page
  gives 99 [9].
- **Defoliation date:** "before November 20th" [9] against "late
  November ... by the last week" [2].
- **Chill classes:** 100-250 chill units [1], under 250 [2] and under 200
  chill hours [3] all describe "low-chill".
- **Resistance breaking:** the extent of the 'Flordaguard'-breaking
  populations and their effect on yield [15][16]. 'MP-29' is not yet tested
  in central Florida [14].
- **Hydrogen cyanamide:** the full trial rates, timing and yield results;
  only abstracts were seen [17].
- **Outside the US:** yield and water data for Egypt, Brazil and tropical
  cycling systems are only at snippet level [18][5][6]. Mexican low-chill
  production was not re-found.
- **Harvest labour:** hours per acre for harvest were not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Florida 2017 average yield, about 3.4 t/ha.** "Over 3,000 tons" from an
estimated 2,000 acres [8] gives 1.5 short tons/acre. 1.5 × 907.2 kg ÷
0.4047 ha = 3,363 kg/ha, about 3.4 t/ha. It is a floor, since output was
"over" 3,000 tons, and the acreage may include non-bearing trees. USDA NASS
state data would replace it.

**Budget yields, 6.3 / 7.3 / 8.2 t/ha.** 5,600, 6,500 and 7,300 lb/acre [7]
× 1.1209 (kg/ha per lb/acre) = 6,277, 7,286 and 8,182 kg/ha. Year 3:
3,698-4,836 lb/acre gives 4,145-5,421 kg/ha.

**Annual reference and crop ET, about 1,108 and 683 mm.** Monthly ETo
[10]: 1.87 + 2.46 + 3.54 + 4.44 + 5.24 + 5.05 + 5.10 + 4.82 + 4.06 + 3.20 +
2.15 + 1.68 = 43.61 in (× 25.4 = 1,108 mm). Monthly ETc [10]: 0.70 + 1.16 +
1.95 + 2.72 + 3.44 + 3.46 + 3.54 + 3.30 + 2.67 + 1.96 + 1.19 + 0.79 = 26.88
in (683 mm). 26.88 ÷ 43.61 = 0.62. These are historic means for the source's
station, not a measured orchard balance. Rain covers much of the summer
demand [12].

**Budgeted irrigation, about 406 mm.** 16 acre-inches per acre [7] = 16 in ×
25.4 = 406 mm.

**Thinning and pruning labour, about 371 h/ha.** 150 h/acre [8] ÷ 0.4047 =
371 h/ha. The source says "around" and "depending on tree size and fruit
set".

**Tree densities.** 145, 156 and 290 trees/acre [8][7] ÷ 0.4047 = 358, 385
and 717 trees/ha.

**Nitrogen, about 101 kg/ha.** 90 lb N/acre [3] × 1.1209 = 101 kg/ha. The
110 lb table total gives 123 kg/ha.

**Wild plum setback, about 400 m.** ¼ mile [8] = 402 m.

**Temperature conversions.** 57 °F = 13.9 °C (the source rounds to 14 °C)
[1]. 50 °F = 10 °C [2][8]. 60 °F = 15.6 °C, which the source gives as 15 °C
[2]. 36-46 °F = 2.2-7.8 °C. 32-34 °F = 0-1.1 °C. 27.5-30.5 °F = −2.5 to −0.8
°C [8].
