## Naming and scope

*Vasconcellea pubescens* A.DC. (Caricaceae) [1][3][7]. Synonyms include
*Carica pubescens* Lenné & K.Koch, *C. candamarcensis* Hook.f. and *V.
cundinamarcensis* V.M.Badillo [1][3][7].
- **Names:** mountain papaya, mountain pawpaw, papayuelo and chamburo
  [1]. In Chile it is simply "papaya" or *papayo* (the plant) [1][5][6].
- **Biology:** a diploid (2n = 18) [3].
- **Native range:** the Andes from Colombia (Panama in [7]) to Bolivia
  and central Chile, at 1,500-3,000 m [1][7]. It comes particularly from
  dry low mountain forest in Colombia and Ecuador at 2,000-3,000 m [3].
- **Chile:**
  - grown near sea level on the coast at about 30-36° S [3];
  - in Chile probably since pre-Columbian times [1][3]; Chilean product
    records go back to the late 1890s [5].
- **Scope:** one species. The sterile babaco hybrid has its own doc.
  *V. chilensis*, a separate dioecious Chilean species, is noted in [2]
  but not treated as a crop here.
- **Gate check (2026-10-03, re-confirmed 2026-11-23): passes** against
  common papaya (`papaya.md`):
  - **Climate:** a cool subtropical crop that cannot take heat above 30 °C
    [2].
  - **Sex system:** different (Finding 3) [4].
  - **Disease:** resistant to papaya ringspot virus (PRSV-P) [1].
  - **Market:** mostly processed, with 80-90% industrialised [2].
  - **Replanting:** a documented replant barrier (Finding 1) [10].

**Relatives in this project:**
- common papaya (`papaya`, `papaya.md`): the tropical relative [1][13];
- babaco (`babaco`, `babaco.md`): a hybrid with this species as one
  parent [1].

**Proposed:** none. The encyclopedia notes this species crosses with
other highland papayas [1], but none was sourced as a separate crop in
this pass.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a short-lived, seed-grown orchard that cannot be replanted
straight away.**
- **First fruit:** plants reach reproductive age in 10-12 months [3]. At
  one year a tree is about 1 m tall and bearing; by three years it is
  about 2 m and in full production [3]. Another source gives fruit in 2
  years from seed [7].
- **Commercial life:** orchards are kept commercially for about 5 years.
  After that fruit becomes fewer and poorer, though trees over 20 years
  old exist in Chilean orchards [3].
- **Variable orchards:**
  - seedling orchards vary in productivity because of the plant's
    several sexes [10];
  - standout trees with high yield and drought tolerance are lost when
    the orchard ends [10].
- **Replant barrier:** at the end of an orchard's life, growers "cannot
  replant immediately" and have to leave the crop for some years [10].
  The cause is not stated.
- **Clonal propagation:** vegetative propagation has worked in trials but
  "is not technically sustainable" in practice [6]. A FIA project
  (PYT-2014-0033, completed) aimed at virus-free in-vitro clones of
  elite selections [10].
- **Yield:** Not sourced as a per-hectare figure (see Open research
  questions). A tree produces 50-60 fruits a year [1][7], and a mean
  fruit weighs about 200 g [3] (see Estimates).

**Finding 2 — a small and shrinking Chilean industry, centred on La
Serena.**
- **Area:** the figures disagree because they come from different years
  and areas covered:
  - 180 ha of commercial orchards (ODEPA-CIREN 2019), with 13.6 ha (9%)
    in Arica and Parinacota at about 18.5° S [3];
  - 225 ha, 94% of it at 30-33° S [2];
  - 103.39 ha in the Coquimbo Region in 2024 (CIREN). This is mostly La
    Serena (58.53 ha), Canela (25.70 ha) and Coquimbo commune (16.5 ha)
    [5]. The article's text calls about 103 ha "the national planted
    area", while its table covers Coquimbo only [5].
- **Orchards:** most are 10-40 ha, with terracing, fertigation and
  standard management [2]. They are monocultures today, though trees
  were formerly grown along edges or among other fruit trees for shade
  [6].
- **Maule coast:** after the 2010 earthquake and tsunami, the Maule
  area "almost disappears", because seawater salinised the growing
  areas [1][3].
- **Recent stress:** in the summers of 2023 and 2024, plants were hit by
  heat and solar radiation under scarce irrigation water [6]. Frosts are
  also named as a risk to plantings [6].
- **Climate:** plantings favour coastal sites with maritime influence,
  such as the La Serena and Huentelauquén coastal plains, and avoid
  areas with wide temperature swings [6].

**Finding 3 — three plant sexes, with growers thinning out the males.**
- **Sex system:** described as trioecious (male, female and
  hermaphrodite plants) where it originates [4][2]. In La Serena
  orchards it is dioecious and monoecious [4]:
  - monoecious ("ambisexual") plants carry female and male flowers plus
    usually-deformed bisexual flowers [4];
  - female plants stay female whatever the climate [4];
  - flower sex on ambisexual plants depends on the season [4].
- **Pollination:** male flowers on ambisexual plants occur all the time,
  which "ensures pollination without the need for male plants" [4][6].
  Male-flower pollen germinated 75% in summer and 56% in spring [4].
- **Nursery practice:**
  - nurseries supply 3-4 seedlings of unknown sex per unit (bag) [4][6];
  - farmers remove male plants 8-10 months after planting [4];
  - plants do not show their sex all at once, so thinning drags on and
    raises costs [4].
- **Sex ratio and fruit:** the female : ambisexual ratio in two nearby
  orchards was 6:1 and 1:1 [4]. Female plants are preferred for their
  better fruit [4]. Fruit shape shows the sex: elliptical and deeply
  ribbed from hermaphrodite plants, globose with shallower ribs from
  females [5].
- **Historical advice:** an older Chilean recommendation, quoted in [6],
  was to plant in groups of three and keep 6-10% of the males.

**Finding 4 — a climacteric fruit picked at first yellow.**
- **Harvest:** fruit is picked green, with yellow just starting at the
  base or on one side [5]. Ripe fruit is yellow, waxy and shiny [5].
  Fruit should be firm, at 6-7 kgf (penetrometer), for sale [5].
- **Ripening sequence:** quick degreening, then the climacteric ethylene
  rise and softening [2][8]:
  - 3-5 days at 20 °C take fruit from 10% to 90% yellow [2];
  - ethylene reached 44 µL/kg/h after 11 days at 20 °C [2];
  - the 2004 study gives peaks of 1.8-3.2 µmol/kg/h [8].
- **1-MCP:** blocked the ethylene rise but only partly delayed softening
  and colour. pH, acidity and soluble solids did not depend on ethylene
  [8].
- **Losses:** rapid softening and short shelf life cause "significant
  postharvest losses" [9]. No storage temperature or storage life was
  sourced this pass.

**Finding 5 — eaten fresh or processed: the sources conflict.**
- **Fresh:** "mainly consumed as fresh fruit, for dessert or salad" in
  Chile [2].
- **Cooked:** "seldom eaten raw", and usually stewed and sweetened [7];
  "usually cooked as a vegetable", and also eaten raw [1]. The NRC
  chapter (snippet) says usually cooked with sugar [12].
- **Industrial:** papaya reaches Chile's highest rate of
  industrialisation, 80-90% [2]. Of the volume processed, 50-60% is done
  artisanally with basic equipment [2].
- **Reading:** likely both are true. The fruit is eaten raw, but most of
  Chile's crop is processed. The old doc's "never eaten raw" is not
  supported and has been dropped.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** evergreen pachycaul shrub or small tree, about
  5 m and up to 10 m [1][7]; 3-6 m in [2]. It is fast-growing in [1][7]
  but slow-growing in [3], a conflict. It has a short fall-winter
  dormancy (June-August in Chile) and grows and fruits from September to
  May [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** continuous. Fruit at different stages hangs on the
  same tree [3], over the September-May growing season [2]. INIA's
  2023-2025 fruit data are from the summer harvest [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 10-12 months [3]; 2 years in [7].
  The commercial life is about 5 years [3].
- **Climate zones:**
  - **Temperature range:** 10-25 °C in Chile, with no frost and nothing
    over 30 °C [2]. In general 10-28 °C is tolerated, with a preferred
    mean minimum of 17 °C and maximum of 22 °C [7].
  - **Frost:** brief frosts to about −3 °C when dormant, but only −1 °C
    when in growth [7]. Damage below 2 °C in [1]. This is a conflict.
  - **Rainfall:** 1,000-1,700 mm/yr best, 500-2,800 mm/yr tolerated [7].
  - **Elevation:** 1,500-3,000 m where native [1][7], but near sea level
    on the Chilean coast [3].
  - **Climate type:** Chilean sites span 4 Köppen-Geiger types [3].
- **Composition (per 100 g fresh pulp, Chilean food table quoted in
  [2]):**
  - energy 18 kcal; protein 1.0 g; fat 0.3 g; fibre 1.4 g;
  - moisture 93.2 g; ash 0.8 g;
  - calcium 36 mg; iron 1.3 mg; phosphorus 28 mg.
  - **Another study [2]:** carbohydrate 4.9 g, pH 4.1, acidity 0.1%.
  - **Sugars:** "approximately 5% sugar" and "7 and 9% of total sugars"
    both appear in [2].
  - **INIA 2023-2025 means [5]:** pulp 58.4% of fruit weight; juice
    yield 48.1%; pulp acidity 1.1%; pH 4.6.
  - **Edible share:** 46% in [2] against 58.4% in [5].
  - **USDA:** no entry [11].
- **Edibility:** raw-edible, though often cooked (Finding 5)
  [1][2][7].
- **Pollination:**
  - required for fruit [4][7];
  - ambisexual plants supply the pollen, so no male plants are needed
    [4][6];
  - self-fertility is "No" in [7];
  - the pollinator species was Not sourced.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - Not sourced per hectare;
  - 50-60 fruits per tree a year [1][7];
  - about 10-12 kg per tree (see Estimates);
  - a 60 t/ha figure attributed to FAO (1992) is snippet-level only
    [12].
- **Water use:** about 6,000 m³/ha a year with drip irrigation, down from
  about 15,000 m³/ha recommended in 1983 [6] (see Estimates). Established
  plants tolerate long droughts but young plants do not [1][7].
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** the commercial method [3][6]. Sow in containers out of
    direct light; it germinates best at 24-30 °C, in 2-6 weeks [7].
  - **Nursery stock:** 3-4 seedlings per bag [4][6].
  - **Clonal:** in-vitro clones are research-stage only [10];
    vegetative propagation is not sustainable in practice [6].
- **Labor:** Not sourced as hours. Removing male plants is a recurring
  cost [4].
- **Soil:** fertile, well-drained, sunny but sheltered [7]. pH 6-7 is
  preferred, and 4.5-8.4 tolerated [7]. Salt is toxic to it (the Maule
  tsunami) [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced as temperature or duration. It
  ripens fast at 20 °C [2]; 1-MCP partly slows it [8].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Orchard size:** 10-40 ha, terraced and fertigated [2].
  - **Spacing:** varies [6]. Historical figures are 4 m in all
    directions (an older text quoted in [6]) and 1.5 m between plants
    (a grower's account in [6]). No current density was sourced.
- **Cultivars:** none named. Chilean orchards are unselected seedlings
  with "not high" genetic variability [10]. INIA has selected elite
  accessions [10].
- **Weather response:**
  - **Frost:** see Climate zones [7]; named as a risk [6].
  - **Heat and radiation:** stress in the dry summers of 2023-2024 [6].
  - **Drought:** established plants tolerate it, young plants do not [7].
  - **Salinity:** the tsunami salt killed plantings [3].
  - **Storms:** one grower recalls losing 80% of a harvest in winter
    storms (an anecdote) [6].
- **Water regime:** irrigated (drip) in Chile [6]; rain-fed where native
  [7].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** one fresh stage, at first yellow [5].
  Immature fruit is richest in latex [3]. Grades by fruit count per kg:
  Extra 5, Primera 6-7, Segunda 8-9, Tercera 10-11 [5].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced as data. The FIA project title includes
  mycorrhizal inoculation [10], with no results seen.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - naturalised in New Zealand (North Island) and called "moderately
    invasive" in some tropical areas [1] (no citation in the entry);
  - resistant to PRSV-P [1];
  - the FIA project's virus-free aim implies viruses in orchards [10],
    but none was named.
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Proteolytic latex:** freeze-dried latex shows "5 to 8 times more
  proteolytic activity" than common papaya latex [3]. Latex is most
  abundant in immature fruit [3]. No handling or allergy data was sourced
  for this species; Useful Tropical Plants lists "None known" [7].
- **Ergot / mycotoxin:** none reported.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — latex handling and possible allergy are deferred

## Uses and processing

- **Chain 1 (fresh):** picked at first yellow → graded by fruit count per
  kg → eaten fresh as dessert or salad [2][5].
- **Chain 2 (preserves):** pulp (mesocarp) → canned fruit, juice and
  nectar, "honey" (syrup), jam and candied fruit [2]. This is 80-90% of
  the crop [2], and 50-60% of it is artisanal [2].
- **Chain 3 (latex):** papain-type enzymes → meat tenderiser, beer
  clarifier, pharmaceutical and cosmetic uses [2][3].
- **Other products:** a tea blend [6]; a Chilean origin seal is being
  sought [6][10].
- **Medicinal (traditional):** the juice as a vermifuge and digestive,
  per older Chilean texts quoted in [6]. It is "recommended for
  dyspeptics" in [7].
- **Not detailed here:** seed oil and encapsulation (research stage) [2].
- **Ritual and religious:** Not sourced. There is a Chilean origin legend
  (Chalalupanqui's tears) [1], and the fruit gives La Serena's football
  club its "papayeros" nickname [6].

## Estimates and derivations

**Fruit per tree by weight** [1][3][7]: 50-60 fruits × 0.2 kg = 10-12 kg
per tree a year. The two inputs come from different sources. INIA's 2023-2025 mean of
184 g [5] gives 9.2-11.0 kg.

**Grade fruit weights** [5]: 1,000 g ÷ 5 = 200 g (Extra); 1,000 ÷ 7 to
1,000 ÷ 6 = 143-167 g (Primera); 1,000 ÷ 9 to 1,000 ÷ 8 = 111-125 g
(Segunda); 1,000 ÷ 11 to 1,000 ÷ 10 = 91-100 g (Tercera). The 184 g
mean [5] sits between Extra and Primera.

**Water as depth** [6]: 6,000 m³/ha ÷ 10,000 m² = 0.6 m = 600 mm a year
(drip). 15,000 m³/ha = 1,500 mm (1983). The cut is
(15,000 − 6,000) ÷ 15,000 = 60%.

**Implied national area from the Arica share** [3]: 13.6 ha ÷ 0.09 = 151
ha, against the stated 180 ha (13.6 ÷ 180 = 7.6%). The two figures in [3]
do not fully agree.

**Coquimbo commune shares, 2024** [5]: La Serena 58.53 ÷ 103.39 = 57%;
Canela 25.70 ÷ 103.39 = 25%; La Serena + Canela = 84.23 ha. The text's
"100 ha" for La Serena and Canela probably includes Coquimbo commune
(84.23 + 16.5 = 100.73 ha).

**Frost in growth** [7]: −1 °C. The 2 °C figure [1] is taken as a
damage-onset value, not a killing temperature.
