## Naming and scope

*Citrus tachibana* (Makino) Tanaka (also written *C.* × *tachibana*; Rutaceae):
**tachibana** (橘), **Japan's native wild mandarin** [1][2]. This doc covers the
`tachibana` row, proposed from `hyuganatsu.md` (tachibana is hyuganatsu's
inferred pollen parent) and `mandarin.md` [4].

- **Taxonomy (a near-wild ancestral Japanese citrus):** isozyme and genomic work
  finds tachibana **genetically isolated from mainland Asian citrus and native to
  Japan**; a 2021 genome study describes it as a constellation of natural **F1
  hybrids of *C. ryukyuensis* (a Ryukyu wild mandarin) with a single mainland
  *C. reticulata* lineage**, and it is often read as a **wild ancestor of the
  cultivated mandarin** [1][2]. It is distinct from, but related to,
  *C. ryukyuensis* and `shiikuwasha` [2].

**Distinctiveness (gate check, 2026-11-27): passes** — but on a different basis
from the food-citrus rows. Checked against the rejected wild-citrus precedent
`ichang_papeda` (rejected for having *no* farming system, being rare in
cultivation, and bearing near-inedible fruit) and the researched mandarins:
- **vs `ichang_papeda` (the key contrast):** tachibana is **genuinely and
  enduringly cultivated** — a 1990 survey found most tachibana trees are
  *cultivated* (not wild), and it is planted at shrines, palaces and gardens, in
  the ornamental/nursery trade, and in conservation plots (Findings 2-3). That
  is a real cultivation system, where `ichang_papeda` had none [1][2][3].
- **Distinct uses:** a **heritage/ornamental/ceremonial crop** with a unique
  cultural identity (imperial emblem, the ¥500 coin, Hinamatsuri) and minor
  historic food/incense use, plus a **breeding-parent** role — a profile unlike
  any food-citrus row (Finding 3) [1][3].
- **Taxonomy/range:** a separately named, genetically isolated near-wild Japanese
  species with its own indigenous range [1][2].
It qualifies as its own row as a **cultural/ornamental heritage citrus**, not as
a fruit crop.

**Relatives in this project:** `hyuganatsu.md` (offspring; proposing doc),
`mandarin.md` (the mandarin backbone it may ancestrally underlie), and the
Japanese sour-citrus cluster `shiikuwasha.md`, `sudachi.md`, `kabosu.md`,
`kunenbo.md` [4].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a native Japanese citrus whose fruit is barely a food.** Tachibana
fruit is small, fragrant but **bitter and somewhat dry/stringy — "tolerable" at
best, and not prized for eating** [1]. The tree is notably **cold-hardy** but
"has yet to make its mark in horticulture" as a fruit [1]. Its historic food use
is slight: the *Nihon Shoki* notes tachibana **juice used as a sweetener**, and
there are modern **revival efforts** ("resurrecting tachibana") to find culinary
and commercial uses, but it is not a commercial eating or juicing citrus [1][3].

**Finding 2 — native, part-wild, and a conservation subject.** Tachibana is
**indigenous to Japan**, mainly on the **Pacific side of the south-west main
islands** (from the Izu Peninsula, Shizuoka, south to Kushima, Miyazaki); a 1990
survey found many trees there, **most cultivated and some wild** [1][2]. A wild
**Heda (Shizuoka) population** of 64 trees has been studied to assess genetic
diversity and **promote its conservation and use**, with the note that
propagation by **polyembryonic (nucellar) seed** can maintain the population
**asexually/clonally** [2]. Genomically it traces to multiple *C. ryukyuensis*
individuals plus a single mainland mandarin lineage, and is genetically distinct
from both *C. ryukyuensis* and shiikuwasha [2].

**Finding 3 — the product is really the tree and its meaning: an ornamental and
deeply cultural/ceremonial crop.** Tachibana is an **evergreen symbol of
longevity and eternity**, planted at **shrines** as an auspicious tree since at
least the **Heian period**, and traditionally set before the **Shishinden** of
the Imperial Palace — the famous **"Ukon no tachibana."** That palace tree
inspired the **badge of the Order of Cultural Merit (Order of Culture)**; the
flowers appear on **family crests**; **miniature tachibana trees** are a fixed
part of the **Hinamatsuri (Girls' Day) doll display**; and the fruit and flowers
appear on the **¥500 coin** [1][3]. It also has a legend of origin (Tajimamori
bringing the fruit from *Tokoyo-no-kuni* in the *Nihon Shoki*) and historic
**incense** use (burned in the *Kokin Wakashū* era; Toba craftspeople still make
**tea and incense** from it) [1][3]. The living ornamental/ceremonial plant — and
its symbolism — is the product, far more than the fruit: this doc records that
both as **the living plant being the product** (schema Entry 50) and as a
**religious/cultural use** (schema Entry 65) [3][4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial **evergreen** citrus tree; **notably
  cold-hardy** for a mandarin (Finding 1) [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing of small fragrant fruit; largely
  **unharvested as food** — the tree is grown ornamentally (Findings 1, 3)
  [1][3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate to subtropical Pacific south-west Japan;
  cold-hardier than most mandarins [1][2].
- **Composition:** Fruit **bitter, low-quality** eating flesh; fragrant (aroma/
  essential oil the valued fraction); quantitative composition **not sourced**
  [1].
- **Edibility:** Fruit **barely edible fresh** (bitter, dry, stringy); historic
  **juice-as-sweetener** only (Finding 1) [1].
- **Pollination:** A **breeding parent** (inferred **pollen parent of
  hyuganatsu**); **polyembryonic/nucellar seed** allows clonal maintenance
  (Finding 2) [2][4].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced; not expected to apply.
- **Yield:** Not sourced (not grown for fruit yield).
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Seed (**polyembryonic — nucellar seedlings reproduce the tree
  clonally**) and standard citrus grafting; conservation plantings maintain wild
  genotypes (Finding 2) [2].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not specifically sourced; grows in indigenous Pacific-coast sites and
  cultivation [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced (little fresh-fruit trade).
- **Establishment:** Planted as an **ornamental/shrine/garden tree** and in
  conservation plots; nursery stock exists (Finding 3) [1][3].
- **Cultivars:** No commercial fruit cultivars; it is a **near-wild species with
  regional wild/cultivated populations** (e.g. the Heda population) rather than a
  cultivar set (Finding 2) [2].
- **Weather response:** **Cold-hardy**; other tolerances not quantified [1].
- **Water regime:** Ordinary rainfall; no special regime sourced [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not a staged fruit harvest; the "crop" is the
  **standing ornamental/ceremonial tree** (Finding 3) [1][3].
- **Soil biology:** Not sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** A **native, part-wild Japanese species** of
  **conservation** rather than invasive concern; wild populations are studied for
  genetic-diversity preservation (Finding 2) [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced; grown outdoors (and as potted/
  miniature ornamentals, Finding 3) [1][3].

## Hazards

- As with all citrus, **peel-oil furanocoumarins** are a minor contact-
  photosensitiser note, not a distinctive hazard; the fruit is little eaten
  anyway [1]. **No distinctive hazard found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (ornamental / living plant — the main "product"):** the tree is grown
  and sold as a **shrine, garden and palace ornamental**, and as **miniature
  trees** in Hinamatsuri doll sets — the living plant, not the fruit, is the
  product (schema Entry 50) [1][3].
- **Chain (religious / cultural):** longevity/eternity **symbol**; shrine and
  imperial (Shishinden "Ukon no tachibana") plantings; the **Order of Culture
  medal** design; **family crests**; the **¥500 coin**; legend and poetry (schema
  Entry 65) [1][3].
- **Chain (fragrance):** fruit/peel → historic and craft **incense**, and Toba
  **tea and incense** products [3].
- **Chain (minor food, historic/revival):** fruit → **juice as a sweetener**
  (*Nihon Shoki*); modern **revival** attempts at culinary use [1][3].
- **Chain (breeding):** **pollen parent** of hyuganatsu and an ancestral mandarin
  used in citrus genetics/breeding [2][4].
