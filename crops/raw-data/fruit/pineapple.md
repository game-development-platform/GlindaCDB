# Pineapple (*Ananas comosus*)

## Naming and scope

Modeled as the cultivated pineapple and its major cultivar groups
(including a dominant worldwide group, plus several regionally
important groups). Unlike every other fruit crop this project has
researched so far, this is not a tree crop at all: it is a herbaceous
perennial of the bromeliad family, a rosette roughly 0.75-1.5 meters
tall that fruits once from its central stem and then again from
suckers as subsequent "ratoon" crops. None of the other fruit crops
this project has researched is a close botanical analogue; citrus
research was drawn on only for general storage and handling practice as
a point of comparison.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a CAM plant, with a water-use physiology genuinely unlike
any other fruit crop this project has researched.** This species uses
Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) photosynthesis: it takes in carbon
dioxide at night and stores it as an intermediate acid rather than
fixing it during the day as most crops do. A standard international
crop-water-use reference notes that because the plant keeps its stomata
closed during the day, the majority of its actual water use comes from
soil evaporation rather than plant transpiration. As a striking
consequence, its documented midseason crop coefficient is actually
*lower* than its initial-stage coefficient on bare soil, because full
ground cover from the growing plant reduces soil evaporation even as
the plant itself transpires very little. The crop tolerates a very wide
annual rainfall range, with a documented ideal figure well below what
most fruit crops need, but "cannot stand waterlogging." This is also
the first crop in this project's research to need a CAM-type
photosynthesis classification, a schema value not used in any prior
entry [1][4].

**Finding 2 — flowering is routinely forced on a schedule using
ethylene-releasing treatments, a practice with real historical
origin.** In the 19th century, smoke was discovered by accident to
bring plants into bloom within about six weeks; the active agent was
later identified as ethylene gas. Since that discovery, growers have
forced flowering using various ethylene sources (including acetylene or
calcium carbide dropped directly into the plant's central growing
point, or several synthetic plant-growth-regulator compounds), typically
applied around six months after planting, at a specific leaf-count
growth stage, roughly three months ahead of when the plant would
naturally flower on its own. Separately, a ripening-inducing spray
applied at the first sign of natural ripening can bring an entire field
to ripeness simultaneously, substantially cutting the number of harvest
passes needed [2].

**Finding 3 — a genuine plant-then-ratoon production cycle, with
pollination deliberately prevented rather than encouraged.** The crop
is propagated vegetatively from several distinct plant-part types
(crown pieces, "slips," suckers, and basal "butts"); true seed is used
only in breeding programs, since seeds are actively unwanted in
commercial fruit and lower eating quality. Notably, hummingbirds — the
species' main natural pollinators — were formally banned from
introduction to at least one major growing region specifically to
prevent unwanted seed formation in commercial fruit. After the initial
"plant crop" is harvested, growers trim back all but two sucker shoots
per plant, which then fruit again as a first "ratoon" crop some 15-18
months later; a second or even third ratoon crop can follow before a
field is finally cleared and replanted. A documented trial in a
Caribbean growing region found a dense planting yielding roughly 86
t/ha in the main crop and roughly 45 t/ha in the following ratoon crop
— meaning the ratoon crop yielded only a little over half of the main
crop's own yield [1][2].

**Finding 4 — fruit genuinely does not ripen or sweeten further once
picked, and its chilling-injury symptoms show up only after the fruit
warms back up.** Standard commercial storage runs at roughly 7-12°C
and high relative humidity for two to three weeks, with an outer limit
of about four weeks even at the lower end of that temperature range.
Chilling injury — manifesting as internal flesh browning and a specific
dark-flesh disorder — develops only once the fruit is returned to
warmer room temperature, typically two to three days after removal from
cold storage, rather than being visible while the fruit is still cold.
A documented one-day heat treatment at a specific elevated temperature
range is used commercially to control that dark-flesh disorder [2][3].

**Finding 5 — essentially the entire plant is used commercially, and
several of its parts carry genuine hazards.** Leaves yield a fine
traditional textile fiber, woven into cloth in the Philippines since
before the late 16th century, with at least two named cultivar groups
particularly well suited to fiber extraction. Stems trimmed at field
clearing are a commercial source of a protein-digesting plant enzyme
used in food processing and other applications. Factory processing
waste is dried into a livestock feed product. Unripe fruit is
genuinely poisonous, acting as a drastic purgative, and eating too much
of the fibrous fruit core has been documented to form a type of
digestive-tract fiber mass (a bezoar). Separately, in one documented
1982 incident in a major growing region, an insecticide sprayed on
plants was found to carry over into dairy cows' milk after plant waste
was fed to cattle as green fodder [2][8][9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Herbaceous perennial (bromeliad family),
  replanted after one to three ratoon harvest cycles; not a tree crop
  [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing via a plant crop followed by
  ratoon crops; each individual plant produces a single harvest window
  per crop cycle [2].
- **Climate:** Tropical and frost-free subtropical climates, extending
  into tropical highland elevations up to roughly 2,300 meters in at
  least one South American growing region; frost is not tolerated at
  all [2].
- **Food status:** Raw-edible only when ripe; unripe fruit is
  poisonous [2].
- **Pollination:** Not required — the fruit forms parthenocarpically
  (without pollination) in commercial practice, and actual pollination
  (by hummingbirds, and by bats in some wild relatives) is treated as a
  quality cost rather than a benefit, since it introduces unwanted
  seeds that lower fruit quality [1][2].
- **Yield:** A world-average figure sits around 27 t/ha, derived from a
  recent-year international production-and-area statistic; a
  documented close-spacing trial reached roughly 69 t/ha, and a
  documented Puerto Rican selection reached roughly 86 t/ha in its main
  crop at very high planting density; a "poor" tier is estimated well
  below a documented Venezuelan benchmark for what counts locally as
  "very good."
- **Water use:** Crop coefficient values are unusual for a fruit crop,
  reflecting the CAM photosynthesis pathway described in Finding 1:
  values are given for both bare soil and grass-covered ground cover,
  assuming partial plastic mulch and sprinkler irrigation (with a
  downward adjustment for drip irrigation under mulch). The crop
  tolerates a very wide annual rainfall range but cannot tolerate
  waterlogging, developing a fungal root-and-crown rot disease in
  prolonged wet conditions unless grown on raised, well-drained beds.
  Standard practice is to irrigate only during dry seasons, at a
  modest, capped rate [2][4].
- **Establishment:** Cured crown pieces, slips, or suckers are planted
  by hand hoe or machine on raised beds, typically mulched with
  sugarcane bagasse or black plastic. Documented planting densities
  range from roughly 33,800-42,700 plants per hectare in staggered
  double-row systems, up to as high as roughly 51,265 plants per
  hectare in trial plantings; closer spacing gives the highest total
  yield weight but smaller, more elongated individual fruit and fewer
  usable slips for the next planting cycle, while very wide spacing
  instead produces multiple crowns per plant [2].
- **Labor:** No directly sourced labor-hour figures were found; hand
  planting of tens of thousands of propagule pieces per hectare is
  documented as the dominant labor cost, and hand weeding is
  specifically described as "difficult and expensive." Harvest uses
  dedicated cutter-and-carrier field crews, with mechanized harvesting
  and planting available only in the most capital-intensive systems.
- **Soil:** Well-drained sandy loam with high organic matter, friable
  to a meaningful depth; optimal pH around 5.5 (range roughly 4.5-6.5),
  with soils that are not naturally acidic enough sometimes treated
  with elemental sulfur. A documented magnesium application
  meaningfully raised yield on acid clay soil in one Caribbean trial,
  and zinc deficiency is a documented cause of a specific stem-
  deformity disorder in plants roughly a year old [2].
- **Stand life:** A plant crop is followed by one to three ratoon
  crops, each roughly 15-18 months after the previous harvest, before
  the field is cleared and replanted; at least one documented cultivar
  group has been kept in continuous production in one US growing
  region for 25-26 years through repeated ratooning. Field clearing
  involves cutting and drying plants for several months (sometimes
  accelerated with a drying spray) before burning, since the stumps
  themselves take several months to rot down naturally [2].
- **Disease/pest profile:** A mealybug-vectored wilt disease (the
  mealybugs themselves spread by ants), plant-parasitic nematodes, and
  the fungal root-and-crown rot disease noted above under water use.
  Postharvest, a fungal black rot disease can enter through the cut
  stem within half a day of harvest, with at least one cultivar group
  notably more resistant to it than the dominant worldwide group [2][3].
- **Cultivars:** The dominant worldwide cultivar group is valued for
  its large, cylindrical, low-fiber, juicy fruit and near-spineless
  leaves, used above all for canning as well as fresh eating, though it
  is disease-prone and ships poorly; a more compact variant of this
  group was selected in Hawaii in 1960. A second major group is smaller,
  more fragrant, more cold-resistant and more disease-resistant than
  the dominant group, with better storage life but poor canning
  suitability and a tendency to sucker heavily. A regionally important
  Caribbean and Philippine group is more resistant to the black rot
  disease and has long been grown specifically for traditional fiber
  extraction; a related spineless, fiber-optimized group is grown in
  northern South America specifically for its long, rigid leaves. Two
  further regional groups are grown respectively in Brazil (notably
  prone to sunburn even when the fruit stands upright) and more broadly
  in various regions for a notably sweet, low-acid eating quality. A
  natural tetraploid selection and a specific high-yielding Puerto
  Rican selection round out the documented cultivar landscape.

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include a mealybug-vectored wilt disease,
plant-parasitic nematodes, a fungal root-and-crown rot disease under
poor drainage, and a fungal black rot disease entering through the cut
stem after harvest. Most cultivars (except the near-spineless dominant
worldwide group) have sharp, spine-edged leaves with needle-like tips.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — unripe fruit is genuinely poisonous, causing
throat irritation and acting as a drastic purgative, and has a folk
history of use as an abortifacient; eating excessive amounts of the
fibrous fruit core has been documented to form digestive-tract fiber
masses (bezoars). The plant's protein-digesting enzyme, present in all
plant parts, has its own handling and exposure considerations. A
documented 1982 incident saw an insecticide sprayed on plants carry
over into dairy cattle's milk after the plants' waste leaves were fed
to cattle as green fodder. All deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** harvested fruit has its cut stem treated and is graded,
  then cold-stored; trimmings and culls are pressed into juice within
  about a day of cutting, with remaining waste dried into livestock
  feed; leaves are processed into traditional fiber through
  decortication, retting, drying and combing, yielding a documented
  weight of fiber per unit weight of leaf processed.
- Not covered by this research in detail: canned pineapple processing
  specifically; traditional fiber-cloth weaving as a textile craft; the
  plant-enzyme extraction process in detail; a traditional skin-juice
  vinegar and alcoholic beverage, and a related fermented coconut-water-
  style product; and silage use of plant waste.

No religious or ceremonial use specific to this crop was found in this
research.

## Open research questions

- Days from forced flowering to harvest were not found directly; the
  overall plant-crop cycle length used here (roughly 330-600 days from
  planting to first harvest) is estimated from several partial figures
  (typical maturation time, forced-flowering timing, and a documented
  multi-stage growing-season length from a Hawaiian reference) rather
  than a single direct source.
- A "poor" yield tier is estimated from a documented Venezuelan
  benchmark for what counts locally as "very good," converted to a
  per-hectare figure under assumptions about typical fruit weight,
  rather than from a direct source for poor-performing plantings.
- Detailed labor-hour figures across all operations rest on rough
  estimates derived from qualitative operational descriptions, since no
  dedicated regional labor-cost study was found.
- CAM-specific atmosphere physiology values (peak daily carbon-dioxide
  uptake rate and night-time carbon-dioxide compensation point) are
  placeholder estimates based on general CAM-plant physiology rather
  than a measurement specific to this species.
- The standard multi-stage growing-season length used as a basis for
  water-use calculations spans a single very long, multi-year-scale
  cycle (including the extended ratoon period) rather than a simple
  single season, which sits awkwardly against this project's usual
  single-season modeling conventions — a structural mismatch flagged
  here rather than resolved.
- Soil phosphorus and potassium uptake figures were not directly
  researched; at least one documented regional fertilizer trial found
  no yield benefit from additional phosphorus or potassium.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-season length (roughly 330-600 days from planting to first
harvest) is derived from a combination of partial figures: a general
one-year maturation estimate, documented forced-flowering timing at
around six months (roughly three months ahead of natural flowering),
and a Hawaiian reference source's documented multi-stage growing-season
length; the true range likely depends heavily on whether flowering is
artificially forced and on local climate.

Yield tiers are derived from a combination of sources: the world
average is calculated from a recent-year international total
production figure divided by total harvested area; "good" and
"exceptional" are taken directly from documented close-spacing and
high-density trial yield figures respectively; "poor" is estimated from
a documented Venezuelan qualitative benchmark ("very good crop"),
converted to a per-hectare figure under an assumed typical fruit
weight, since no direct source for poor-performing plantings was found.
A ratoon-crop yield fraction (ratoon yield as a share of main-crop
yield) is derived directly from the documented Caribbean trial figures
discussed in Finding 3.

A water-use index is derived directly from the standard international
crop-water-use reference's documented bare-soil and grass-covered crop
coefficients and multi-stage growing-season length for a Hawaiian
reference system, adjusted for the CAM-driven soil-evaporation-
dominated water-use pattern described in Finding 1; the derived
seasonal water-use figures span the crop's full multi-stage cycle
length rather than a single calendar year, a structural note carried
over into the open-questions section above. Drought-tolerance and
waterlogging-tolerance ratings and the associated daily yield-penalty
estimate are derived qualitatively from the crop's documented tolerance
of low rainfall and its documented complete intolerance of waterlogged
soil.

Atmosphere physiology values (photosynthesis pathway classification,
peak carbon-dioxide uptake, and compensation point) are estimated from
general CAM-plant physiology principles rather than a species-specific
physiological measurement, since no such study was found; the growth-
stage curve reflects the plant's documented slow early leaf
accumulation toward its flowering-trigger growth stage at around six
months.

Labor-hour estimates by operation are derived from qualitative
operational descriptions (hand-hoe planting density, described
difficulty and expense of weeding, and harvest crew structure) rather
than a direct regional labor-cost study, with mechanized operations
reducing the estimate at higher technology tiers.

Soil nitrogen uptake is derived from a documented Kenyan trial's
beneficial nitrogen application rate divided by that trial's achieved
yield; phosphorus and potassium uptake figures are rougher estimates,
reflecting a documented regional trial finding no yield benefit from
additional phosphorus or potassium.

A yield-ramp profile (time to first harvest, ramp to full bearing, peak
daily yield) uses the midpoint of the estimated growing-season-length
range for days to first harvest; the harvest window itself is modeled
as compressed, reflecting the documented use of a ripening-inducing
spray to bring an entire field to ripeness at once; peak daily yield is
calculated by dividing the "good" yield tier by this compressed harvest
window.
