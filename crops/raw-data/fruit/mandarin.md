## Naming and scope

The whole mandarin group — by production volume, one of the largest
citrus crops this project covers [2]. "Tangerine" is an older name for
deep orange-red mandarins; the two names are used interchangeably [3].
Taxonomists disagree on how to split the group: some treat all
mandarins as a single species, while others give several distinct types
(satsuma, willowleaf, king, and others) full species rank [1][3]. This
entry covers the common mandarins and tangerines broadly, with other
major types noted as cultivars within it:

- a particularly cold-hardy, parthenocarpic canning type, treated here
  as a cultivar group but proposed as a candidate for its own separate
  crop entry given how distinctly it behaves (see Open research
  questions);
- a seedless hybrid of willowleaf mandarin and sweet orange, treated
  here as a cultivar;
- tangors (mandarin × orange hybrids) and tangelos (mandarin × grapefruit
  hybrids), both noted as cultivars.

General citrus background shared across this project's citrus research
is covered in more depth in a closely related citrus crop this project
has also researched, and is repeated here only where mandarin
specifically differs.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — tough trees, tender fruit.** Mandarin trees are
considerably more cold-hardy than sweet orange and more drought-
tolerant, yet the fruit itself is tender and readily damaged by cold
[4]. The hardiest commercially grown type survives temperatures well
below freezing for extended periods and even colder for a few hours —
among edible citrus, only kumquat is hardier [4][6]. At least one named
cultivar has survived an exceptionally severe freeze in a US growing
trial [4]. The same thin, loose peel that makes mandarins notably easy
to peel by hand also makes them ship and store considerably worse than
thicker-skinned citrus [3][4].

**Finding 2 — pollination decides seediness more than fruit set.** Most
mandarin types are either self-fertile or, like the hardy canning type
noted above, parthenocarpic (setting seedless fruit without any
pollination at all) [1]. The widely grown seedless hybrid noted above,
however, is self-incompatible: it stays seedless only so long as no
compatible pollen reaches it, and specifically turns seedy if cross-
pollinated — a well-documented 2006 dispute saw California growers
threaten legal action against beekeepers specifically to keep bees away
from their seedless plantings [5]. At least one other named hybrid
cultivar is the opposite case, being self-infertile and requiring
cross-pollination just to set any crop at all [4]. For mandarins as a
group, then, a beehive can be either a quality cost or a yield benefit
depending on the specific cultivar.

**Finding 3 — short storage life.** Mandarins generally store under
refrigeration at a moderate cool temperature and very high humidity for
up to about four weeks, with chilling injury setting in below a
specific threshold [3]. Documented trials show rapid decay at room
temperature within a couple of weeks, and total spoilage within about a
month [4]. A documented Indian study found unwaxed fruit shriveling
within about ten days at room temperature, with waxing, perforated
storage bags, and proper refrigeration each extending shelf life
progressively further [4]. Specific storage-related disorders include a
peel-rupture condition linked to wet soil before harvest, a peel-
separating-from-flesh condition, stem-end rind breakdown, and
granulation [3].

**Finding 4 — the peel itself is a product.** Sun-dried, aged mandarin
peel is a prized traditional Chinese seasoning and medicinal ingredient,
with peel from one specific growing region considered most prized; price
rises steeply with age, with peel aged a decade commanding several times
the price of one-year peel [7]. Mandarin peel oil flavors candy, ice
cream, and soft drinks, and is used in liqueurs; a related essential oil
(distilled from leaves, twigs, and unripe fruit rather than ripe peel)
is produced mainly in a few specific Mediterranean growing regions [4].
Small-fruited types and the hardy canning type noted above are
specifically canned in syrup as a major commercial product [4].

**Finding 5 — alternate bearing, and water research centered on one
specific hybrid.** Mandarins as a group tend to alternate heavy and
light crop years; at least one major US cultivar's heavy crops are
chemically thinned specifically to prevent this swing [4][8]. A
standard international crop-water-use reference's citrus chapter
includes water research specific to the popular seedless hybrid noted
above: flowering and fruit set are its most water-sensitive stage;
summer water stress costs no yield so long as a specific internal
plant-water-status indicator stays above a defined threshold; and this
hybrid's yield responds more steeply to water deficit than a standard
orange type does [8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen perennial tree; one harvest
  season per cultivar, generally running from autumn through spring, as
  fruit holds poorly once mature on the tree (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single annual harvest window per cultivar, with
  fruit needing to be picked promptly once ripe rather than held on the
  tree.
- **Climate:** Subtropical, Mediterranean, and humid subtropical
  climates, with the hardiest cultivar type tolerating distinctly cooler
  margins than other mandarins; also grown in tropical highland regions.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Variable by cultivar — most mandarin types set fruit
  without cross-pollination (self-fertile or parthenocarpic), but at
  least one widely grown cultivar turns seedy if cross-pollinated
  (making bees a quality risk rather than a benefit for that type), and
  at least one other named cultivar is self-infertile and genuinely
  needs cross-pollination to set any crop (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** World-average yield sits in the low-to-mid
  teens of tonnes per hectare; well-managed commercial growing regions
  reach considerably higher, while regions affected by a serious citrus
  disease can fall far lower (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Citrus crop-coefficient curves vary with canopy cover;
  the one mandarin-specific figure available comes from research on a
  single popular seedless hybrid cultivar, which shows notably lower
  water demand than the general citrus curve but a water-yield response
  that is more sensitive to deficit than standard orange. Mandarin trees
  overall are described as more drought-tolerant than sweet orange, but
  waterlogging tolerance is poor, and wet soil shortly before harvest
  predisposes fruit to a serious peel-rupture disorder.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by budding a selected cultivar onto a
  rootstock seedling; one specific willowleaf-type cultivar is unusual
  in coming true from seed. Rootstock choice varies by cultivar and
  growing region, including trifoliate orange, sweet orange, sour
  orange, rough lemon, a specific citrange hybrid, and even another
  mandarin type used specifically as a rootstock in some regions.
- **Labor:** No dedicated mandarin labor-hour study was found; figures
  here are rough estimates based on analogy to other citrus crops this
  project has researched, adjusted for mandarin fruit's smaller size and
  the need for gentler hand-clipping to avoid peel-oil spotting damage
  (see Estimates below).
- **Soil:** No mandarin-specific soil-nutrient or pH data was found;
  figures here are estimated by analogy to a related citrus crop this
  project has also researched. The crop is rated salt-sensitive in a
  standard international reference, without a specific numeric
  threshold given.
- **Stand life:** No mandarin-specific figures for years to first
  harvest or total productive life were found; estimates here are drawn
  by analogy to other citrus crops this project has researched, except
  for the hardy canning-type cultivar, whose seedling-grown trees take
  notably longer (around eight years) to first bear fruit.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Citrus canker is a quarantine concern in at
  least one major growing region; the hardy canning-type cultivar is
  notably more resistant than sweet orange to several specific citrus
  diseases (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** Four broad cultivar groups (the hardy canning type, the
  popular seedless hybrid, common tangerines, and various other
  hybrids) each contain numerous named cultivars differing substantially
  in cold-hardiness, seed count, pollination behavior, season, and
  intended use (see Sources).
- **Harvest:** Hand-clipped carefully, given the fruit's tender, thin
  peel and poor tree-holding capacity once ripe.
- **Storage:** A few weeks under refrigeration at moderate cool
  temperature and very high humidity; notably shorter-lived than
  thicker-skinned citrus, and prone to several specific peel-related
  storage disorders (Finding 3).

## Hazards

The popular seedless hybrid cultivar's phytochemicals have been
suggested, in at least one study, to interact with certain drugs in a
manner similar to the well-documented grapefruit-drug interaction,
though a later study questioned this finding. A historical packing
practice used a specific fungistatic chemical pad inside shipping
cartons; documented trials found this chemical's residue could exceed a
specific US regulatory limit within a couple of weeks of storage at room
temperature, though this reflects a historical rather than necessarily
current practice.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the possible drug-interaction hazard associated
with the seedless hybrid cultivar is deferred in fuller detail until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** Fruit is degreened where needed, given a protective
  fungicide coating, packed, and cold-stored under refrigeration. Peel
  oil is expressed before the same fruit is juiced. Peel is also
  sun-dried into a traditional Chinese seasoning and medicinal product,
  then aged for years to increase both flavor and value. A related
  essential oil is distilled from leaves, twigs, and unripe fruit.
  Small-fruited types and the hardy canning-type cultivar are canned in
  syrup as whole segments.
- Not covered by this research: canned-segment processing detail,
  including how segment membranes are removed; oil-paste production and
  deterpenation.

## Open research questions

- A fruit-specific (as opposed to tree-specific) frost-damage threshold
  was not found in any source reached — only tree-hardiness figures are
  sourced.
- The hardiest canning-type cultivar group shows distinctly different
  traits from the rest of the mandarin group (notably greater cold
  hardiness, parthenocarpic seedlessness, an early and short harvest
  season, and its own distinct market names) strongly enough that it is
  a candidate for its own separate crop entry rather than a cultivar
  within this one; it is kept here as a cultivar group for now.
- A single pollination-required field cannot capture the real
  cultivar-level variation documented in Finding 2 (parthenocarpic,
  self-fertile, self-incompatible-but-fruit-setting, and genuinely
  self-infertile types all exist within this one crop group) — this is
  noted as a modeling limitation rather than resolved.
- No dedicated mandarin-specific labor-hour study was found; current
  labor estimates rely entirely on analogy to other citrus crops this
  project has researched.
- No dedicated mandarin-specific soil nutrient-uptake or pH data was
  found; current soil estimates are copied by analogy from a related
  citrus crop.
- Years to first harvest and total productive lifespan are not directly
  sourced for mandarins generally (only for the hardy canning-type
  cultivar from seed); current figures are estimated by analogy to other
  citrus crops this project has researched.
- Wild mandarin populations are documented in specific mountain regions
  of southern China, but since only one source documents this and they
  appear to be wild relatives rather than an actively gathered food
  resource, this crop is not treated here as having a wild-gathered
  counterpart.

## Estimates and derivations

Days from bloom to harvest (roughly 200-330 days) are estimated by
analogy to the documented range for other citrus crops this project has
researched, combined with the documented mandarin harvest calendar
(earliest cultivars picked from autumn, latest types continuing into
spring, following a spring bloom).

Climate-zone suitability is derived from documented tree cold-hardiness
figures across different mandarin cultivars (from the hardest canning
type down to less hardy common types) combined with documented growing
regions spanning Mediterranean, humid subtropical, and tropical
highland climates.

Pollination-required is set to false as a group-level default, since
most mandarin types are self-fertile or parthenocarpic, while
acknowledging that at least one documented cultivar is a genuine
exception requiring cross-pollination to set any crop at all — a
limitation of modeling pollination at the whole-crop rather than
cultivar level.

Yield tiers are derived from recent standard international production
statistics (world average), a multi-season regional average from a
major US growing state (good tier), that same state's single best
recent season (exceptional tier), and a different US growing region's
recent season affected by a serious citrus disease (poor tier), with
unit conversions applied from customary-unit box-and-acre figures to
metric per-hectare figures.

Seasonal crop-coefficient-days and the resulting water-use "legacy"
figures are derived using the same standard international reference's
general citrus stage-length and crop-coefficient arithmetic used
elsewhere in this project's citrus research, cross-checked against a
notably lower documented single crop coefficient specific to one popular
seedless hybrid cultivar.

Drought tolerance is rated medium-to-high (reflecting mandarin trees'
documented greater drought tolerance than sweet orange, and the
seedless hybrid cultivar's documented modest yield loss even under a
substantial irrigation cut) and waterlogging tolerance is rated low
(reflecting both the documented wet-soil-before-harvest peel-rupture
risk and citrus roots' general susceptibility to root-rot disease
documented elsewhere in this project's citrus research).

Peak carbon-uptake rate, growth-stage curve, and within-season stress
multipliers are estimated by analogy to a related citrus crop this
project has also researched, reflecting shared evergreen-canopy
physiology.

Labor-hour figures across all operations are rough estimates: harvest-
tier labor is derived from an assumed hand-picking productivity rate
(set somewhat below a documented productivity rate for a larger-fruited
citrus crop this project has also researched, reflecting mandarins'
smaller fruit size and the gentler handling needed to avoid peel-oil
spotting) divided into the adopted good-yield-tier figure; pruning labor
is set modestly above that same comparison crop's figure to account for
fruit-thinning operations; land-preparation and post-harvest-handling
labor are copied directly by analogy from a related citrus crop.

Soil nutrient-uptake and pH figures are copied by analogy from a
related citrus crop this project has also researched, since no
mandarin-specific figures were found.

Typical cool-storage duration (roughly 21 days) is derived by taking a
representative point within a documented "up to four weeks" storage
range, informed by a separate documented 31-day Indian storage trial,
reflecting that true storage life depends heavily on decay-control
practice and specific cultivar.

Years to first harvest and total productive lifespan are estimated by
analogy to other citrus crops this project has researched, since no
mandarin-specific figures were found beyond a documented roughly
eight-year figure for seedling-grown trees of the hardy canning-type
cultivar specifically.

Within-season yield-profile parameters (peak daily harvest rate, harvest
-plateau length, maximum days fruit can stand before quality decline,
and daily decay rate) are derived from the adopted good-yield-tier
figure divided across a documented multi-month harvest-season length for
one representative cultivar, with the maximum standing-days figure
reflecting documented poor fruit-holding capacity on the tree once
mature.
