## Naming and scope

This entry covers cultivated *Carica papaya* L. (Caricaceae) [1][2]. Names:
- **Australia and parts of the West Indies:** papaw or pawpaw.
- **Spanish:** lechosa, fruta bomba or mamón; **Brazil:** mamão [1].
- **Australian English:** "pawpaw" is used for the yellow-fleshed types
  and "papaya" for the elongated, red-fleshed types [2].

It is not the North American pawpaw, *Asimina triloba* (Annonaceae), which
is a different plant [1][2].

The entry covers both trade types [3]:
- the small Solo or "Hawaiian" types, 0.5-1 kg;
- the large "Mexican" types, such as 'Maradol', up to about 4.5 kg.

Green papaya, cooked as a vegetable or made into salad, is the same crop
picked unripe, and stays in this entry.

The plant is a fast-growing giant herb, not a tree. It grows 1.8-3 m in its
first year and can reach 6-9 m [1]. Its origin is probably southern Mexico
and Central America [1][2]. Wikipedia also treats it as native to southern
Florida, introduced there by about AD 300 [2].

**Relatives in this project:**
- `mountain_papaya` (*Vasconcellea pubescens*): an Andean, cold-tolerant
  relative [1].
- `babaco` (*V.* × *heilbornii*): a seedless Ecuadorian hybrid grown from
  cuttings [1].

**Proposed:** none.
- Philippine hybrids of papaya with *V. quercifolia*, bred for ringspot
  resistance [2], are breeding material, not a crop.
- Naturalized semi-wild papayas with small 2.5-15 cm fruit [1] are not
  treated as a separate product by any source.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — plant sex decides the crop, and growers can only pick the
plants they want after flowering.**

There are three sexes [1][4][2]:
- **Male plants** bear long sprays of male flowers and no useful fruit.
- **Female plants** bear round, thin-walled fruit, but only if pollinated.
- **Hermaphrodite plants** pollinate themselves and bear the pear-shaped
  or cylindrical fruit with thick flesh that the trade prefers.

Almost all commercial orchards are hermaphrodite [2]. Seed from
self-pollinated hermaphrodites gives about 2 hermaphrodites to 1 female
[1][4]. Other crosses give these splits [1]:
- female × hermaphrodite: half and half;
- female × male: half male, half female;
- hermaphrodite × male: one third each of female, hermaphrodite and male.

Growers can't tell the sexes apart until flowering, so in Hawaii they
[4]:
- sow 15-20 seeds per hole;
- thin to 6-10 seedlings after a month;
- thin to 3 at three months, which gives a 97% chance of keeping one
  hermaphrodite;
- keep the single hermaphrodite at flowering, about 6 months (8 months for
  'Kapoho').

Two variations:
- The F1 hybrid 'UH Rainbow' splits 1:1, so five seedlings must be kept per
  hole [4][5].
- In Indian dioecious plantings, one male is kept for every ten females
  [1].

Male plants appearing in a Hawaiian Solo field are a sign of out-crossing
with a non-Solo type [4]. Temperature can also change a plant's sex
[1][4]:
- At 32-35 °C (90-95 °F), hermaphrodites can produce male flowers only
  ("female sterility"), and fruit set fails.
- Below 15.6 °C (60 °F), stamens turn into carpel-like structures
  (carpeloidy), giving deformed "cat-face" fruit.

**Finding 2 — the quickest-bearing fruit crop here, but plantings last only
three or four years.**

Time to first fruit:
- 6-9 months from seed in hot parts of South Africa, and 9-11 months in
  cooler parts [1];
- 10-12 months after planting in Hawaii, or about 14 months for 'Kapoho'
  [4].

Fruit takes 22 weeks to mature in summer and 26 in winter [4]. It is
picked weekly at colour break, and twice weekly in summer [4]. Each plant
ripens 2-4 fruit a week [1].

Hawaii yields are high but fall quickly [1]:
- Kapoho district: 38,000 lb/acre in the first year and 25,000 lb/acre in
  the second, or 42.6 and 28.0 t/ha (see Estimates).
- Plants bear well for about two years.
- Plantings are replaced after 3-4 years, when they are too tall to pick.
- On Maui, picking with hydraulic lifts starts at 11 months and runs for 48
  months, until plants reach 7.5 m [1].

Other yields:
- National averages (FAOSTAT 2010, via FE913): Mexico 43.4 t/ha, Brazil
  54.4 t/ha and Belize 65 t/ha [3].
- Florida: about 29,000 lb/acre, about 32.5 t/ha, with 85% packed out
  [6].
- South Africa: up to 100 t/ha [2].

Replanting papaya on the same land fails, because *Phytophthora palmivora*
and *Pythium* build up in the soil [1][4]. The fixes [4]:
- **Lava soils at Puna, Hawaii:** half a cubic foot of "virgin" soil in
  each planting hole lets roots get established. Otherwise the land needs
  a 3-5-year fallow, because the porous lava cannot be fumigated.
- **Mineral soils:** fumigation under plastic.

**Finding 3 — a virus nearly ended the Hawaiian industry, and the first
transgenic fruit crop on the US market saved it.**

Papaya ringspot virus (PRSV) is aphid-borne. It is called the most severe
papaya disease and often the limiting factor in production [4][3]. It is
not seed-borne, but infected seedlings carry it, so seedlings are not moved
between islands [4].

History:
- PRSV drove the industry from Oahu to Puna in the 1960s [5].
- It reached Puna's orchards from 1992 [5].
- Florida's plantings declined after about 1959, and Puerto Rico's about
  ten years earlier [1]. Morton puts this down to pests and diseases,
  chiefly viruses.

The fix:
- UH and Cornell put the virus's coat-protein gene into papaya. The result
  was the true-breeding red 'UH SunUp' and its yellow F1 hybrid with
  'Kapoho', 'UH Rainbow' [5].
- Federal approval came in September 1997 [5], and the cultivars were
  released in 1998 [3].
- By 2009, GM cultivars covered 82% of Hawaii's papaya area (Rainbow 77%)
  [7]. Wikipedia gives 80% in 2010 [2].
- China approved its own GM cultivar, 'Huanong 1', in 2006 [3].

The cultivars resist Hawaiian virus strains but are highly susceptible to
Asian strains [5]. 'Rainbow' plants are susceptible until about seven
weeks old, and fully resistant only after three months [5]. To protect
the resistance, growers [5]:
- rogue infected plants and abandoned orchards;
- never plant seed saved from 'Rainbow' fruit, which segregates for flesh
  colour and resistance.

Without resistant cultivars, the only management is removing infected
plants, spraying aphids and keeping cucurbits away [1].

**Finding 4 — a crop tied to licences and import rules as well as to the
weather.** The GM-papaya licences that Hawaii's Papaya Administrative
Committee (PAC) negotiated with four patent holders set five conditions
[5]:
- plant only in Hawaii;
- buy seed only from PAC;
- sell fruit only in countries that have accepted GM papaya;
- attend a training session;
- sign a purchase agreement.

Growers must keep GM and non-GM fruit apart [5]. Wikipedia says the seed
was free to farmers [2]. The extension sources describe licensed sale
through PAC [4][5] (Open research questions).

Export fruit needs fruit-fly disinfestation by vapour heat, irradiation or
high-temperature forced air [4]. The older method was a double hot-water
dip of about 40 minutes at 43 °C, then 20 minutes at 48 °C [1]. Hawaiian
fruit for Japan is vapour-heat treated [8].

**Finding 5 — fruit can be stored chilled, but only within limits that
change with ripeness.** HB66 [8]:
- **Harvest:** Hawaii's grade needs at least 11.5% soluble solids, so fruit
  is picked once the skin starts to yellow.
- **Storage:** 7-13 °C at 90-95% RH. At colour break, fruit keeps 14 days
  at 7 °C and then ripens normally. Fully ripe fruit holds for more than a
  week at 1-3 °C.
- **Chilling injury:** after 14 days at 5 °C in mature-green fruit and 21
  days at 60% yellow. The symptoms are scald, hard lumps and
  water-soaking.
- **Ripening:** 10-16 days at 23-28 °C.
- **Weight loss:** a loss of about 8% from colour break makes the fruit
  unmarketable.
- **Disease and pests:** anthracnose and fruit-fly risk both rise once
  fruit is 25% yellow. A 49 °C, 20-minute hot-water dip controls the main
  rots.
- **Controlled atmosphere:** 2-5% O₂ with 5-8% CO₂ is recommended, but no
  commercial use is reported.

Morton says partly ripe fruit stored below 10 °C never ripens fully [1].
That conflicts with HB66's 7 °C result.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A short-lived, giant herbaceous perennial with a
  hollow stem. Leaves live 4-6 months [1]. All parts have latex [1][2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Fruit is picked every week for 2-4 years of bearing
  [1][4]. The plant flowers and fruits all the time, so fruit on one plant
  ripens in succession.
- **Days to first bearing:** About 6-14 months (see Estimates) [1][4].
- **Climate zones:**
  - Range: tropical and near-tropical, 32°N to 32°S [1].
  - Frost: very frost-sensitive. Brief exposure at 0 °C damages plants
    [1], and below −2 °C is "greatly harmful, if not fatal" [2].
  - Temperature limits: carpeloidy below 15.6 °C and female sterility at
    32-35 °C (Finding 1) [4].
  - Rainfall and humidity: ideally at least 100 mm of rain a month and 66%
    average RH, with irrigation in drier areas [4].
  - Sun: needs full sun. Shade gives low yields, low sugar and more disease
    [4].
  - Elevation in Hawaii: below about 150 m (500 ft) on windward sides, and
    higher only on leeward sides such as Kona [4].
  - Elevation limit for 'Cariflora': fails above 800 m [1].
  - Mainland US: South Florida only for year-round growing. California's
    San Joaquin Valley season runs February-November and gives half- to
    two-thirds-size green fruit, and Texas plantings rarely survive the
    freezes for more than a few years [7].
  - Chill requirement: none.
- **Composition:** USDA FoodData Central 169926, "Papayas, raw" (SR Legacy;
  primary database, opened directly), per 100 g [9]:
  - water 88.06 g, energy 43 kcal;
  - protein 0.47 g, fat 0.26 g, carbohydrate 10.82 g, fibre 1.7 g;
  - vitamin C 60.9 mg, folate 37 µg, β-carotene 274 µg, lycopene 1,828 µg;
  - potassium 182 mg, calcium 20 mg, magnesium 21 mg.

  Morton's Central American and Cuban ranges are 23.1-25.8 kcal and
  35.5-71.3 mg vitamin C per 100 g [1].
- **Edibility:**
  - Ripe flesh is eaten raw. The seeds are peppery and edible in small
    amounts [1][2].
  - Morton says green fruit is "never eaten raw" because of its latex, and
    must be boiled first [1]. Wikipedia describes raw green papaya in
    Southeast Asian salads [2] (Open research questions).
  - Young leaves and male flower sprays are cooked, with the water changed,
    to remove bitterness [1].
- **Pollination:** Flowers open at night, are scented, and are pollinated
  by wind or insects [2]. Hermaphrodites pollinate themselves. Female
  plants need pollen, and poorly pollinated plants bear a light, uneven
  crop, so hand pollination is advised where plantings are not all
  hermaphrodite [1][4]. Cold can make unfertilized female flowers drop.
  Growth-regulator sprays reduce flower drop [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - Hawaii: 42.6 t/ha in the first year and 28.0 t/ha in the second
    (Kapoho), and about 33.6 t/ha around Hilo [1] (see Estimates).
  - Florida: 32.5 t/ha [6].
  - National averages (2010): Mexico 43.4, Brazil 54.4 and Belize 65 t/ha
    [3].
  - South Africa: up to 100 t/ha [2]. One field of 1,000 plants on 1 ha
    gave 30 t [1].
  - Per plant: healthy plants average 34 kg a year, with up to 136 kg on a
    single plant [1].
  - World: 14.65 Mt in 2024. India grew 5.29 Mt (36%), the Dominican
    Republic 1.94 Mt, Indonesia 1.21 Mt, Brazil 1.15 Mt and Mexico 1.14 Mt
    [2]. World area and yield are not sourced this pass.
- **Water use:**
  - FAO-56 has no papaya row.
  - Florida's crop coefficients (Kc) are 1.0 at 0-3 months, 1.2 at 4-6
    months and 1.5 from 7 months [6].
  - In two 2-year Florida trials, ET-based and tensiometer-based
    scheduling used 31-36% of the water of a fixed drip schedule. Fruit
    numbers and yield did not change, and trunk growth was better [6].
  - Drought stress drops flowers, leaves and young fruit and gives smaller,
    less sweet fruit [6].
  - Maui slopes with about 250 mm of rain a year are cropped on constant
    drip, given as 12 US gallons (about 45 L) a day [1]. Morton doesn't say
    whether that is per plant.
  - Roots are shallow, and mechanical cultivation damages them [1].
    Rooting depth is not sourced.
- **Propagation:**
  - **Seed:** normal. Germination takes 3-5 weeks, or 2-3 weeks once the
    aril is washed off [1]. Seed can be stored up to about 3 years with
    falling germination [1]. In Hawaii, seed germinates in 10-21 days [4].
  - **Direct seeding vs. transplanting:** direct-seeded plants have deeper
    taproots, flower earlier and yield more [1]. Puna growers direct-seed
    into the lava, while soil growers transplant 6-week-old seedlings
    [4].
  - **Cuttings and air layering:** used for clones that don't breed true.
    'Honey Gold' is grown entirely from cuttings [1].
  - **Clonal decline:** a vegetatively propagated strain is reported to
    deteriorate and be worthless after 3-4 generations [1]. Morton gives
    no cause. Clean-stock schemes are not sourced.
  - **Grafting:** 'Dwarf Solo' grafted onto 'Solo' did better, and the
    reverse did worse [1].
  - **Tissue culture:** 'Huanong 1' is sold only as tissue-cultured
    seedlings [3].
  - **Seed stability:** the Hawaiian inbreds come true from seed, while
    'Rainbow' F1 seed must be bought for every planting [4][5].
- **Labor:**
  - Hawaii: hand picking and field sorting are about 40% of the crop's
    labour cost, put at 1,702 man-hours per acre to pick and pack [1]. That
    is about 4,206 h/ha (see Estimates). The period it covers is not
    stated.
  - Picking rate: one picker with a suction-cup pole gathers 363-454 kg a
    day. A 1970 mechanical aid with one operator and two pickers harvested
    454 kg an hour, the work of eight hand pickers [1].
  - Weeding is a major cost in young fields [4].
- **Soil:**
  - Light, porous, well-drained soil [1][4].
  - pH: 5.5-6.7 [1], or 5.5-6.5 [4]. In Hawaiian soils below pH 5.5,
    aluminium restricts roots, and some need liming to pH 6 against
    manganese toxicity [4].
  - Lime: 2.4-4.8 t/ha on acid soils [1].
  - Very rich organic soil gives lush growth but poor fruit [1].
  - Fertiliser: Hawaii applies 45-56 kg N/ha a month after flowering (see
    Estimates) [4]. India applies 250 g N, 250 g P and 500 g potash per
    plant per year [1].
  - Nutrient disorders: boron deficiency gives "bumpy" fruit, and 0.3% B
    is added every six months [4]. Low calcium gives "soft fruit" [8][4].
    'Rainbow' needs about 1,680 kg/ha of calcium carbonate plus 560 kg/ha
    of dolomite (1,500 and 500 lb/acre) [5].
  - Salinity: FAO-61 rates papaya moderately sensitive, from seedling
    growth and leaf injury, with no threshold or slope [10]. Coastal salt
    spray dries out and kills leaves [4].
- **Storage (postharvest):** 7-13 °C and 90-95% RH. Shelf life depends on
  ripeness. Chilling injury below about 7 °C, with the 10 °C conflict
  noted (Finding 5) [1][8].
- **Establishment:**
  - **Hawaii [4]:** the field is subsoiled to 60-90 cm. Rows are 10-11 ft
    (3.0-3.4 m) apart, with plants 5-6 ft (1.5-1.8 m) apart on lava or
    6-7 ft (1.8-2.1 m) on mineral soil. That gives 566-871 plants per acre,
    or 1,400-2,150 per ha (see Estimates).
  - **Puerto Rico:** plants on 1.8 m centres grew more strongly than plants
    set closer [1].
  - **Windbreaks:** needed before planting, because constant wind crinkles
    the leaves and 64 km/h winds uproot plants on mineral soil [4].
- **Cultivars:**
  - **Hawaii Solo types (0.5-1.4 kg) [4]:** red-fleshed 'Sunrise', 'Sunset'
    and 'UH SunUp'; yellow-fleshed 'Waimanalo', 'Kapoho' and 'UH Rainbow'.
  - **Climate and harvest differences [4]:**
    - 'Waimanalo' suits hot, dry sites.
    - 'Kapoho' needs mild, even weather.
    - 'Sunrise' has the shortest shelf life.
    - 'Sunset' and 'UH SunUp' are picked at a quarter to half ripe, because
      their sugar develops slowly.
  - **Older Hawaiian lines:** 'Solo' came from Barbados in 1911.
    'Dwarf Solo' and 'Higgins' followed [1].
  - **Large types:** 'Maradol' is the main large type and was bred in Cuba
    [7]. Mexico grows 'Maradol' and 'Red', and Brazil grows 'Golden' and
    'Formosa' [3].
  - **South Africa and Australia:** South Africa has 'Hortus Gold' and
    'Honey Gold', the latter from cuttings and 10 months from fruit set to
    maturity. Australia has 'Hybrid No. 5', 'Sunnybank' and 'Guinea Gold'
    [1].
  - **India:** 'Coorg Honey Dew', 'Co. 1' and 'Co. 2' (for papain), and the
    'Pusa' lines, including 'Pusa Dwarf' for high-density planting [1].
  - **Florida:** 'Cariflora', which tolerates ringspot, and the GM line
    X17-2, deregulated by USDA in 2009 [1][7].
- **Weather response:**
  - Frost: kills plants; it is not a quality event (see Climate).
  - Cool weather: causes carpeloidy, low sugar and late maturity [4]. Cold
    winter winds cause "star spot" on young fruit in Queensland [1].
  - Heat: 32-35 °C causes female sterility [4].
  - Waterlogging: very intolerant. Morton puts flooding at 48 hours as
    fatal [1], Wikipedia says 24 hours [2], and CTAHR says a few hours of
    puddling can kill [4].
  - Late rain: heavy rain 2-3 months before harvest raises the share of
    soft fruit [8].
  - Wind: see Establishment.
- **Water regime:** Ordinary irrigation; standing water is lethal [1][4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - Fruit is sold at colour break or a quarter, half or three-quarters ripe
    [8].
  - It is ready to eat at 75% skin colour or more [8], with the best flavour
    at 80% colour [1].
  - Green fruit is harvested separately, for cooking or for papain [1].
  - In South Africa, stripping six newly set fruit delays the crop by 6-8
    weeks [1].
- **Soil biology:** Replant failure from *P. palmivora* and *Pythium*, and
  root-knot and reniform nematodes (Finding 2) [1][4]. Symbionts: not
  sourced.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - **Native range:** the Neotropics [11].
  - **Pacific:** PIER scores it **low risk (score 2)**. It persists at old
    homesites and is "sparingly naturalized" in low dry-to-mesic areas of
    Hawaii. It escapes in Niue, Tonga, Guam and Fiji. Some island
    references list it as invasive, for example Tutuila and Swain's Island
    [11].
  - **Wild populations:** confined to disturbed tropical forest and canopy
    gaps. It is abundant in Everglades hammocks after hurricanes [2].
  - **Movement rules:** PRSV-infected seedlings must not move between
    Hawaiian islands [4]. The GM licences restrict where GM papaya may be
    planted and sold (Finding 4) [5]. Export fruit needs fruit-fly
    quarantine treatment [8][4].
- **Controlled environment:** Not sourced. The crop's height of 6-9 m and
  its sensitivity to wind and waterlogging are relevant, but no
  greenhouse source was read.

## Hazards

- **Latex:** irritates skin, so harvesters wear gloves and protective
  clothing [1][2].
- **Papain allergy:** includes reactions to papain-tenderized meat [1][2].
  Papaya pollen can cause severe respiratory reactions, after which
  sensitized people react to any part of the plant [1].
- **Abortifacients:** unripe fruit, latex, seeds and roots are used in folk
  medicine to induce abortion [1].
- **Fruit from *Phytophthora*-infected plants:** "may be toxic to
  consumers" [2].
- **Nitrate in green fruit for canning:** above 30 ppm it strips the tin
  from cans, so lacquered cans are required [1].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED:**
  - Leaves contain carpaine, pseudocarpaine and dehydrocarpaines, which act
    on the heart like digitalis and are destroyed by heat [1].
  - Seeds contain the cyanogenic compound prunasin [2], and crushed seeds
    release benzyl isothiocyanate [1].

  This project has no home yet for health and safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Fresh fruit chain:**
  - Picked at colour break.
  - Hot-water dipped (49 °C, 20 minutes) [8].
  - Disinfested for export [4].
  - Sleeved or padded, packed in 4.5 or 10 kg cartons of 6-18 fruit [8].
  - Stored at 7-13 °C, then ripened at 23-28 °C [8].
- **Fresh-cut:** halved or sliced fruit at 60-80% yellow keeps up to 3
  weeks at 0-4 °C [8].
- **Processed fruit chain:** puree with added sugar (frozen in Hawaii),
  juice and nectar, jam, marmalade, crystallized half-ripe fruit, and
  frozen papaya-pineapple cubes [1].
- **Green fruit chain:**
  - Boiled as a vegetable, canned in syrup (Puerto Rico), or made into
    salads and pickles (atchara) [1][2].
  - It can be peeled in batches with hot lye: 10% for 6 minutes, 15% for 4
    or 20% for 3 [1].
- **Papain chain:**
  - Green fruit is scored early in the morning, every 4-5 days, until the
    latex stops.
  - The latex is oven-dried at low temperature and ground.
  - 1,500 fruit give about 0.68 kg of papain [1].
  - Tapped fruit can still be ripened, dried as leather or powder, or used
    for pectin [1].
  - Papain is used in meat tenderizers, beer clarification, wool and silk
    treatment, de-hairing hides, toothpaste and medicines [1].
- **Seed:** a pepper adulterant [1]. Seed from unripe fruit yields 16.1%
  oil (white seeds) or 26.8% (black seeds) [1].
- **Leaves and flowers:** cooked greens and candied flowers [1]. Dried
  leaves are sold for tea [1].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** freezing methods, pectin yields, seed
  oil processing and chymopapain.

## Open research questions

- **Storage below 10 °C:** Morton says partly ripe fruit never ripens [1].
  HB66 says colour-break fruit ripens normally after 14 days at 7 °C [8].
- **Waterlogging:** fatal after 48 hours [1], 24 hours [2] or "a few hours"
  of puddling [4].
- **Frost:** damaging at 0 °C [1] vs. "greatly harmful" below −2 °C [2].
- **pH:** 5.5-6.7 [1] vs. 5.5-6.5 [4].
- **GM seed:** "without charge" [2] vs. sold under PAC licence [4][5].
- **GM share:** 82% of area in 2009 [7] vs. 80% of plants in 2010 [2].
- **Raw green papaya:** "never eaten raw" [1] vs. raw Southeast Asian
  salads [2].
- **Solo progeny:** Morton says 'Solo' gives females and hermaphrodites "in
  equal proportions" [1]. That fits crossed seed. For selfed seed, Morton
  and CTAHR give 2:1 [1][4].
- **Morton's yield conversions:** "38,000 lbs/acre (roughly 38,000 kg/ha)"
  and "25,000 lbs (11,339 kg)" are wrong as written. 'Honey Gold' "45 lbs
  (100 kg)" per plant has its units mismatched. See Estimates.
- **Pick-and-pack labour:** 1,702 man-hours per acre [1], with the period
  not stated.
- **Clonal decline** after 3-4 vegetative generations [1]: the cause is
  not given.

## Estimates and derivations

**Kapoho yields, 42.6 and 28.0 t/ha.** 38,000 lb/acre [1] × 0.4536 kg/lb ÷
0.4047 ha/acre = 42,590 kg/ha, so 42.6 t/ha. Year 2: 25,000 × 0.4536 ÷
0.4047 = 28,020 kg/ha, so 28.0 t/ha. Year 2 is 66% of year 1. Morton's
own "roughly 38,000 kg/ha" and "11,339 kg" are unit slips. The second is
25,000 lb converted as a plain weight.

**Hilo yield, about 33.6 t/ha.** 15 tons/acre [1]. As US short tons: 15 ×
907.2 kg ÷ 0.4047 = 33,630 kg/ha. Morton gives 37 t/ha, which matches long
tons (15 × 1,016 ÷ 0.4047 = 37,660). The range is 33.6-37.7 t/ha.

**Florida yield, 32.5 t/ha.** 29,000 lb/acre [6] × 0.4536 ÷ 0.4047 =
32,500 kg/ha.

**Hawaii density, 1,400-2,150 plants/ha.** 566-871 plants/acre [4] ÷
0.4047 = 1,399-2,152 per ha.

**Hawaii nitrogen after flowering, 45-56 kg N/ha a month.** 40-50
lb/acre/month [4] × 0.4536 ÷ 0.4047 = 44.8-56.0. That is about 540-670 kg
N/ha a year if kept up for 12 months. This is applied fertiliser, not crop
removal.

**Pick-and-pack labour, about 4,206 h/ha.** 1,702 h/acre [1] ÷ 0.4047 =
4,206 h/ha. Morton doesn't say whether this is per year or per crop
cycle. Over a 3-4-year planting it would be about 1,050-1,400 h/ha a year.
A Hawaii or Florida enterprise budget would replace it.

**Days to first bearing, about 180-430.** The lowest is 6 months from seed
in hot South Africa [1], about 180 days. The highest is 14 months for
'Kapoho' in Hawaii [4], about 430 days. Most Hawaiian cultivars take 10-12
months [4], about 300-365 days. A cross-check: flowering at about 6 months
plus 22-26 weeks to mature [4] gives about 330-365 days.

**Papain per fruit, about 0.45 g.** 0.68 kg ÷ 1,500 fruit [1] = 0.45 g of
dry papain per fruit tapped.
