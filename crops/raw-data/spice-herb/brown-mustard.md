# Brown Mustard (*Brassica juncea*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-02

## Naming and scope

This covers the condiment seed use of *Brassica juncea* L. (Dijon-style
mustard, South Asian cooking) only. A related oilseed use of the same
or a closely related species, and a separate mustard-greens leaf crop,
are out of scope here and would need their own dedicated research with
fresh sources rather than importing this document's figures wholesale.

No close sibling in this project's own research shares this species'
genus or even family (Brassicaceae) with the umbellifer-family seed
spices (cilantro, cumin, dill) this project has also researched — this
is a genuinely different plant family, and this document does not lean
on any of those crops' own findings as an analogy basis.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — brown mustard's own genome structure is unusually clean
and well-converged, confirmed as a true allotetraploid (amphidiploid)
with both parent species and their own chromosome numbers independently
identified, and a real chromosome-scale genome assembly already
published.** *Brassica juncea* is an allotetraploid (AABB genome,
2n=36), formed by interspecific hybridization between two diploid
progenitor species: *Brassica rapa* (AA genome, 2n=20) and *Brassica
nigra* (BB genome, 2n=16) [1]. This is a genuinely well-resolved
polyploidy story. A 2021 chromosome-scale genome assembly (of the
yellow-seeded variety 'Sichuan Yellow') captured 933.5 Mb of total
sequence, with 867.3 Mb (about 92.9%) anchored into chromosomes [1] — a
real, specific, modern assembly figure, though this is an assembly
length, not a direct flow-cytometry genome-size measurement, and the
source gives no separate A- versus B-subgenome size breakdown. Whether
*B. juncea* itself arose once or multiple times from this same parental
cross is genuinely disputed: earlier morphological work favored a
single origin, later chemotaxonomic and marker-based studies suggested
multiple origins, and a 2021 peer-reviewed study instead supports a
single origin in West Asia followed by at least three independent
domestication events (not multiple hybridization origins) [1] — a
nuanced, actively-researched distinction preserved rather than
collapsed into a single claim.

**Finding 2 — brown mustard is reported largely self-pollinated with a
specific, sourced partial-outcrossing rate, and explicitly confirmed
self-compatible (no self-incompatibility system has been found in the
species).** An Indian study reports Indian mustard is "largely self-
pollinated," with outcrossing historically reported at roughly 11-24%
[2]. A review of self-incompatibility breakdown in the Brassicaceae
separately states that an SI (self-incompatibility) system has not been
found in *B. juncea*, directly supporting self-compatibility as a
species-level trait, not merely an incidental breeding-population
observation [2] — one of the cleanest self-compatibility confirmations
this project has sourced for any spice/herb crop. Because of this
partial outcrossing, seed-certification isolation distances are
specifically sourced: at least 100 m for breeder and foundation seed,
and 50 m for certified seed [2] — a genuinely specific, actionable
isolation-distance figure. A separate Japanese review further notes that
spontaneous hybridization between *B. juncea* and the related crop *B.
napus* is "generally difficult," attributed specifically to *B.
juncea*'s own self-compatibility reducing its reliance on outcrossing
[2] — consistent with, not contradicting, the partial-outcrossing figure
above.

**Finding 3 — yield figures scatter across an exceptionally wide range
(more than 5x) within a single two-year trial at one location, with
sowing-date and seeding-rate both separately confirmed as significant
yield levers.** A 2024 Turkish trial (Ankara, semi-arid, two growing
seasons) found the best seed yield from a named *B. juncea* line in the
better of the two years, reaching 3,080 kg/ha (with a correspondingly
high oil yield of 612 kg/ha in that same year/line), while a different
line in the worse year yielded only 572 kg/ha [3] — a greater than 5x
spread, driven by year-to-year (not merely line-to-line) variation at
the same trial location. Sowing date is separately confirmed as an
actionable lever: an Indian study found yield attributes and seed yield
were significantly higher with an October 10 sowing compared to delayed
sowings [3], though no equivalently specific multi-day date range or
magnitude was sourced for brown mustard this pass. Seeding rate also
matters: a Pacific Northwest study on oriental/brown mustard found the
medium seeding rate yielded significantly higher than the low rate, but
was not significantly different from the highest rate tested [3] — a
genuinely different shape from a simple "more seed = more yield"
relationship, implying a plateau above the medium rate, though the
specific rate figures (kg/ha) for this particular study were not
captured this pass.

**Finding 4 — the crop's own dominant chemical identity, the
glucosinolate sinigrin, is specifically quantified for its pungency-
generating hydrolysis product, and the crop's own best-known culinary
product (Dijon mustard) is explicitly tied to this same compound.**
Brown mustard's most important constituent is reported as the
glucosinolate sinigrin, which on hydrolysis yields up to 1.4% allyl
isothiocyanate — the compound directly responsible for the seed's
pungency [4]. Traditional Dijon mustard, originating in the city of
Dijon, France, is reported made from black or brown mustard seed
specifically [4] — directly tying this crop to one of the most
commercially recognizable named mustard products. On the oilseed side
(outside this document's own scope), the same source separately notes
that a canola-type variety was developed from *B. juncea* specifically
to achieve low erucic acid and glucosinolate content [4] — flagged here
only to clarify that the low-glucosinolate oilseed type is a different
breeding target from the condiment seed covered here, which relies on
glucosinolate content (sinigrin) for its own characteristic pungency;
the two uses are genuinely in tension over this single trait, not
independently optimizable.

**Finding 5 — disease pressure is dominated by four named fungal
diseases with a sourced, actionable weather-dependent prediction model
for two of them, and the mustard aphid is confirmed the dominant insect
pest with a specific, quantified yield-loss range tied directly to
aphid control.** Alternaria blight, white rust, Sclerotinia stem rot,
and downy mildew are reported the major fungal diseases of *B. juncea*,
with most commercially grown varieties described as susceptible or
moderately susceptible to all of them [5] — a genuinely broad, not
single-pathogen-concentrated, disease burden. Weather-driven prediction
models exist for two of these: Alternaria blight is favored by specific
temperature and humidity ranges, and white rust specifically by
temperatures above 15°C [5] — a sourced, model-backed weather dependency
not found with comparable specificity for most other spice/herb crops
this project has researched. The mustard aphid, *Lipaphis erysimi*, is
confirmed the major insect pest, with one 2022 study estimating that
aphid-population control could prevent 10.2-61.1% yield loss [5] — a
wide but directly actionable range, framed as a preventable loss (the
loss avoided by control). A separate finding reports that mustard
genotypes in the related *B. campestris* group are more susceptible to
aphid than *B. juncea* itself [5] — a genuinely favorable relative-
resistance finding for brown mustard specifically. Pollinator attraction
(bees) is only generally claimed by a farm-advisory source, not
independently confirmed by a dedicated pollination study this pass —
consistent with, but weaker evidence for, the self-compatible-with-
partial-outcrossing finding already sourced in Finding 2.

**Finding 6 — an authoritative pet-safety reference's own direct
listing (under the name "White/Black/Yellow Indian Mustard," which
explicitly includes *B. juncea*) names the species toxic to horses only
via isothiocyanates causing gastrointestinal irritation and colic, with
no dog or cat listing found on that same page, and a separate, unrelated
heavy-metal-hyperaccumulation finding specific to contaminated growing
sites.** That reference's own page for "White/Black/Yellow Indian
Mustard" lists toxicity to horses; toxic principles as isothiocyanates;
and clinical signs of gastrointestinal irritation and colic [6] —
directly naming the species covered by this document, and the same
toxic-principle class (isothiocyanates) already independently sourced
for sinigrin's own hydrolysis product in Finding 4, a mechanistically
consistent finding across two independent search threads. No dog or cat
toxicity information appears on this page, in contrast to some other
spice crops this project has researched with fully-scoped (dog/cat/
horse) toxicity findings — dogs and cats are not assumed safe on that
absence, since the same reference's related mustard-family pages (also
covering two related weeds, both also horse-only listings per the same
search) may simply reflect which species have been assessed for which
animals, not a confirmed absence of risk to dogs/cats. Separately, a
horse-health source notes *B. juncea* is specifically known for its
ability to hyperaccumulate heavy metals from soil (selenium, lead,
arsenic, and others), with toxicity risk rising specifically when the
plant is grown on contaminated soils [6] — a genuinely different hazard
category (soil-contamination-dependent bioaccumulation) from the plant-
intrinsic isothiocyanate hazard above.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** True annual, herbaceous — broadly consistent
  with the mustard family's own general cultivation shape, contrasting
  with the umbellifer-family seed-spice crops this project has also
  researched.
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure was found
  despite a dedicated search.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in the usual sense — the seed is used
  as a ground/whole condiment spice (and pressed for the separate
  oilseed use, outside this document's scope); no fresh product was
  sourced or expected.
- **Pollination:** Partially required — largely self-pollinated, self-
  compatible (no self-incompatibility system found), with a sourced
  partial-outcrossing rate of roughly 11-24% (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Extremely wide scatter even within a single
  two-year trial: 572-3,080 kg/ha (a >5x spread) across different lines
  and years at one Turkish location (Finding 3). Seeding rate shows a
  plateauing relationship (medium rate significantly beats low rate, but
  is not significantly different from the highest rate tested) —
  specific rate figures not captured this pass. Sowing date matters:
  October 10 sowing significantly outperformed delayed sowings in one
  Indian study, though the magnitude was not captured this pass.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, the sole propagation route found
  this pass. Named varieties reach flowering in 46-48 days and
  physiological maturity in 89-90 days, per one Indian variety study
  [3].
- **Soil:** Heavy-metal hyperaccumulation (selenium, lead, arsenic, and
  others) specifically noted on contaminated sites, raising a site-
  dependent (not intrinsic) toxicity risk (Finding 6). No specific soil
  pH or nutrient-rate figure sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** True annual, 89-90 days to physiological maturity per
  named varieties [3].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Four named fungal diseases (Alternaria
  blight, white rust, Sclerotinia stem rot, downy mildew), with most
  commercial varieties susceptible or moderately susceptible to all;
  weather-dependent prediction models exist for Alternaria blight
  (temperature/humidity dependent) and white rust (favored above 15°C)
  (Finding 5). Mustard aphid (*Lipaphis erysimi*) is the dominant insect
  pest, with control estimated to prevent 10.2-61.1% yield loss; a
  related species group is reported more aphid-susceptible than *B.
  juncea* itself, a relatively favorable finding.
- **Genome:** A real, modern chromosome-scale assembly exists (933.5 Mb
  total, 867.3 Mb anchored, ~92.9%), though this is an assembly length
  rather than a direct flow-cytometry measurement, and no separate A-/
  B-subgenome breakdown was sourced (Finding 1). Chromosome number and
  parentage well-converged: allotetraploid AABB, 2n=36, from *B. rapa*
  (AA, 2n=20) × *B. nigra* (BB, 2n=16). Single-vs-multiple origin of the
  species itself remains genuinely disputed across different marker-
  based studies, though the most recent (2021) genomic study favors a
  single origin with multiple later domestications.
- **Cultivars:** Two named varieties were the fastest to flower and
  mature among tested varieties in one Indian study (46-48 days to
  flowering, 89-90 days to maturity) [3]. A third named line was the
  highest-yielding line (3,080 kg/ha seed and 612 kg/ha oil) in the
  better of two trial years at one Turkish location (Finding 3). No
  disease-resistance cultivar comparison was sourced for this species
  specifically, beyond the general "most commercial varieties
  susceptible" finding.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one seed harvest per
  planting; no leaf product or multi-stage system within this
  document's own scope.

## Hazards

- **Isothiocyanates (horse toxicity)**: an authoritative pet-safety
  reference's own listing names the species toxic to horses via
  isothiocyanates (gastrointestinal irritation, colic), with no dog or
  cat finding located — not assumed safe for dogs/cats on that absence
  (Finding 6).
- **Heavy-metal hyperaccumulation**: the species is noted for
  hyperaccumulation of heavy metals (selenium, lead, arsenic) on
  contaminated soils, a site-dependent hazard distinct from the plant-
  intrinsic isothiocyanate finding (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → direct-seeded, harvested → dried → used whole or
  ground as a condiment spice (including traditional Dijon mustard, made
  from black or brown mustard seed) (Finding 4).
