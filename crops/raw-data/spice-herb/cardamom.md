# Cardamom (*Elettaria cardamomum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/cardamom.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers true/green cardamom (*Elettaria cardamomum* Maton) only.
Black cardamom (*Amomum subulatum*), a separate crop this project has
already researched, is a genuinely distinct species (different genus,
smoke-dried rather than sun-dried, markedly different flavor and
culinary use, Himalayan rather than South Indian origin).

Cardamom is in the ginger family (Zingiberaceae), the same family as
ginger and turmeric, both separate crops this project has also
researched, but this is not treated as a close sibling relationship the
way some same-family crop pairs are — cardamom's harvested organ (a
fruit capsule, not a rhizome), its shade-obligate cultivation under
forest canopy, and its curing/bleaching/grading chain are all
structurally different from ginger or turmeric. No finding below imports
an assumption from those crops by analogy without saying so explicitly.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — cardamom's genome size and chromosome number show the
same kind of multi-way disagreement this project has found repeatedly
for other rhizomatous spice crops, though the specific shape of the gap
differs.** The genus *Elettaria* has a basic chromosome number of x=12,
with the somatic count most commonly reported as 2n=48, though at least
one source separately gives 2n=52 without the same cytological detail
[3][4]. A dedicated flow-cytometry, cytology, and ISSR study specifically
confirmed 2n=48 for the accessions it examined, and is the more
methodologically detailed source of the two figures [4]. That same
study found a mean 2C nuclear DNA content of 2.87 pg (roughly 2,806 Mbp
for the diploid genome), with a 1.25-fold range (2.57-3.22 pg) across
wild and cultivated genotypes examined, and specifically noted that
genetic-variability parameters did not correlate with genome size [4].
A later draft genome assembly of one cultivar came to only 1.06 Gb —
less than half the flow-cytometry-based estimate — a gap recorded here
as unreconciled rather than assuming the assembly simply under-counted
repetitive sequence, since no source opened this pass explained the
discrepancy directly [3][4].

**Finding 2 — cardamom's pollination biology is genuinely disputed
between a peer-reviewed study and a non-peer-reviewed AI-generated
source.** A 2009 peer-reviewed study found that both wild and cultivated
cardamom populations are self-compatible, with no reproductive barriers
between them [2]. A separate, non-peer-reviewed AI-generated
encyclopedia page instead claims the species is self-incompatible or
self-sterile, requiring cross-pollination between genetically diverse
clones for effective fruit set [2]. The peer-reviewed study is treated
as the more reliable source given the stark difference in source
authority, but the conflict is recorded explicitly rather than silently
dropping the lower-authority claim. Separately, and more reliably
sourced: wild cardamom is pollinated primarily by solitary bees, while
cultivated cardamom is instead visited mainly by social bees — a
pollinator-community shift the source authors attribute to the large
number of flowers available for prolonged periods in plantations
(attracting and sustaining social-bee colonies) rather than to any
co-evolved relationship with the cultivated form specifically [2]. Bee
pollination measurably improves product quality: one study found
bee-pollinated capsules were of more uniform shape and larger size than
capsules without effective pollinator visitation [2].

**Finding 3 — cardamom's climate, elevation, and soil requirements are
reasonably consistent across sources on the broad strokes, but the
specific pH range is not reconciled, and this crop has a sourced soil-
micronutrient-deficiency association.** Cardamom is obligately shade-
grown, typically under thinned forest-tree canopy, with one source
specifically noting that areas exposed to heavy winds are unsuitable
[1]. Elevation is given as 600-1,500m by one source and more narrowly
600-1,200m by another [1]. Temperature tolerance is 10-35°C by one
source [1]. Cultivation is mainly rain-fed, dependent on monsoon
rainfall, with no irrigation figure sourced this pass [1]. Soil pH
guidance genuinely disagrees across three sources: 4.5-5.8 (one
e-book), 5.0-6.5 (an agricultural university extension page), and
5.5-6.5 (an organic-farming guide) [1] — a real, unreconciled spread
rather than a rounding difference. Distinctively, one source specifically
notes that cardamom's preferred acidic soils are often short of zinc,
boron, molybdenum, and manganese [1] — a sourced soil-micronutrient-
deficiency association this project has not found for ginger or
turmeric.

**Finding 4 — cardamom's propagation method carries a genuine, sourced
disease-epidemiology trade-off between vegetative and seed propagation,
not simply a convenience choice.** Vegetative propagation (rhizome
division, pieces with 3 or more shoots) is the standard commercial
route, and plants propagated this way come into bearing roughly one
year earlier than seedling-propagated plants, while also breeding true
to type [5]. However, vegetative propagation has a specific, named
disadvantage: it clonally spreads 'katte' (cardamom mosaic virus), so in
areas where the disease is already widespread, seedling propagation is
recommended instead as the safer route despite its slower time to
bearing [5] (Finding 8). True seed itself is fragile: it should be sown
within about 15 days of extraction, since viability is lost quickly in
storage, and acid scarification (25% nitric acid) has been found to
improve germination percentage [5]. Time-to-first-harvest figures
genuinely disagree across sources: one guide gives 18-24 months to
first flowering with harvestable pods 3-4 months later; another gives
2-3 years to harvestable pods; a university extension source gives up
to 4 years, though that source specifically covers black cardamom, a
different species, and should not be assumed to transfer directly to
*Elettaria* [5]. Productive life is given as roughly 15 years by one
older, general, lower-authority source, with no higher-authority figure
found this pass to corroborate or contradict it [5].

**Finding 5 — four independent agronomic-economics studies give
consistent, well-sourced per-hectare annual labor figures, in sharp
contrast to the near-total absence of this data for most spice crops
this project has researched.** One agricultural journal study reports
729.6 labour days/ha/year during the bearing period, with women
constituting 64.08% of that labor, and harvesting (55.56%) plus
trashing/spent-stem removal (11.01%) together accounting for two-thirds
of the total [6]. A field study from a different growing region reports
a higher 869.8 labour days/ha/year, with women at 87.4% and picking
alone at 57.8% [6]. A third study gives 747.42 labour days/ha/year, with
harvesting and processing together at 63% [6]. A valley-bottom forest-
shade trial reports a markedly higher 1,120 man-days/ha, plausibly
reflecting site- or management-specific differences the sources do not
explain [6]. All four studies independently agree that harvesting/
picking is by far the dominant single labor category (55-64% of the
total), and that the workforce is overwhelmingly female — a sourced
labor-demographics finding. No source converted labour-days to labour-
hours (the sources do not state an assumed working-day length).

**Finding 6 — cardamom's post-harvest processing chain (curing, drying,
bleaching, grading) is well-sourced from government and trade-body
material.** Freshly picked green capsules must enter processing within
24-36 hours or color quality deteriorates [7]. The first step is a
brief alkaline dip — soaking in approximately 2% sodium carbonate
solution for about 10 minutes — which fixes the green color through the
subsequent drying and storage period [7]. Drying follows by sun (spread
on concrete) or in artificial chambers (wood-fired kilns, electric or
gas units), reducing moisture from roughly 85% fresh to about 10-11%
for storage, with sources specifically emphasizing that the drying
process should be as short as possible to avoid mould growth and
preserve the bright green color [7]. No source gave a direct dry-
recovery percentage. A separate, optional bleaching step produces the
"white" trade grade: primarily sulphur-dioxide fumigation, or
alternatively potassium metabisulphite combined with hydrogen peroxide
(described as degrading chlorophyll to a golden-yellow/white color) [7].
An official government grading system names Alleppey Green, Coorg
Green, Bleached or Half-bleached, Bleached White, and Mixed cardamom as
distinct trade classes, with bleached grades defined by shade (pale
cream to white) and capsule condition [7].

**Finding 7 — cardamom essential oil composition is reasonably well-
sourced at the compound level but shows large sample-to-sample
variability, and a genuine storage-relevant stability finding was
sourced this pass.** Oil yield from cured capsules is given as 4.5-9.5%
by an Indian accession study [8]. The two dominant compounds are
consistently alpha-terpinyl acetate and 1,8-cineole across all sources
opened, but their relative proportions vary widely: one 2025 study of
commercial cardamom fruit gave alpha-terpinyl acetate at 35.99-62.28%
and 1,8-cineole at 0.24-30.62%, while the Indian accession study instead
gave alpha-terpinyl acetate at 29.9-61.3% and 1,8-cineole at 15.2-49.4%
— overlapping but not identical ranges, left unreconciled [8]. A
regulatory specification table gives narrower typical ranges (roughly
30-42% terpinyl acetate, 16-35% 1,8-cineole) [8]. A stability study
specifically relevant to storage and handling found that heat exposure
sharply reduced 1,8-cineole content (29.4% in heated oil versus 89.4% in
untreated oil), while sunlight exposure instead promoted formation of
linalyl acetate and borneol — a genuine, sourced heat/light sensitivity
finding for the oil product, though no source gave a quantified shelf-
life figure in days for either the oil or the dried capsules themselves
[8].

**Finding 8 — cardamom's disease complex is well-documented from
Indian government extension sources, and includes a specific, sourced
epidemiological link back to the propagation-method trade-off in
Finding 4.** 'Katte' (cardamom mosaic virus) is aphid-vectored, has no
cure, and infected plants can persist for years as a reservoir —
management is limited to removing and burning infected clumps including
their rhizomes, and removing alternate hosts such as taro growing
nearby; resistant varieties are reportedly in development, though this
specific claim comes from a lower-authority commercial source [9].
Thrips can cause roughly 47% crop loss, with damaged capsules selling
at reduced prices; management includes removing collateral hosts and,
where chemical control is used, targeting early larval stages about
15-20 days after adult emergence, avoiding application ahead of heavy
rain; neem-based products were found comparable in trials to a
conventional organophosphate [9]. Clump/rhizome rot appears mid-monsoon
and worsens in late monsoon under intermittent rain and misty
conditions, with affected tillers easily pulled from a decayed,
discolored collar region; management relies on drainage (raised beds,
drainage channels), avoiding overwatering, and destroying infected
plants including the rhizome — though the specific causal pathogen (one
lower-authority source names *Pythium*) is not independently
corroborated by the higher-authority extension sources opened this
pass, and is recorded with that caveat rather than stated as fact [9].

**Finding 9 — cardamom's cultural and trade history is deep and well-
attested for its broad strokes, with a genuinely interesting production-
geography shift and two unreconciled competing historical narratives
for one specific claim.** Cardamom-flavored coffee (qahwa/gahwa) is
widely described as central to Gulf Arab hospitality, typically prepared
with lightly roasted coffee beans and one or two green cardamom pods
per cup, with multiple sources tying this tradition to Bedouin
hospitality customs (though presented by at least one source as
received tradition rather than independently verified history) [10].
Cardamom is similarly well-attested as a staple of South Asian cooking
(garam masala, chai, biryani, gulab jamun) [10]. Scandinavian baking's
adoption of cardamom is attested by multiple sources but via two
competing historical narratives that this document does not reconcile:
one account credits Viking traders encountering cardamom in
Constantinople during the 10th-11th centuries via Varangian trade
routes, while a competing account instead credits Moorish introduction
via the Iberian Peninsula from the 8th century onward [10]. A genuinely
notable production-geography finding: Guatemala is now described as the
world's leading cardamom supplier (roughly 30-40% of global
production), introduced there by German planters around 1914 — a
production base shifted almost entirely away from the crop's native
South Indian range [10]. No source opened this pass described a formal
religious/ritual production rule for cardamom — its cultural role (Gulf
hospitality coffee, South Asian ritual cooking contexts) is significance
without a stated production rule.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical perennial, shade-obligate; harvested
  annually from capsule-bearing panicles (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Tropical, shade-obligate, monsoon-rainfed — 10-35°C
  tolerance, 600-1,500m elevation (600-1,200m per a second source),
  heavy wind unsuitable (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — no source described fresh/
  unprocessed capsules as a standard culinary form; the dried (or
  bleached) capsule is the marketed product (Finding 6).
- **Pollination:** Bee-pollinated (solitary bees in wild populations,
  social bees in cultivated plantations); self-compatibility disputed
  between a peer-reviewed study (self-compatible, no reproductive
  barriers) and a non-peer-reviewed AI-generated source (self-
  incompatible) (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 200-250 kg/ha (dried capsules, the
  most widely repeated figure across two sources); exceptional yield
  >1,000 kg/ha (location-specific improved varieties, per one source, no
  specific variety named); poor yield 35-45 kg/ha (dried, one source's
  figure assuming a 5:1 green-to-dried ratio — flagged as a possible
  outlier, not reconciled with the other figures). Yield figures
  disagree by more than an order of magnitude across sources with no
  stated explanation for variety, management, or measurement-method
  differences; a separate, independent source (300-1,000 kg/ha "from
  the 4th or 5th year") gives yet a third range (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-style crop-coefficient curve specific to
  *Elettaria cardamomum* was found. Drought tolerance is likely low,
  inferred from the rain-fed, monsoon-dependent cultivation finding, not
  an independently sourced drought-tolerance figure. Waterlogging
  tolerance is likely low, inferred from the clump/rhizome-rot disease
  finding, which is explicitly drainage-dependent and worsens under wet,
  misty monsoon conditions.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative rhizome-piece planting (3+ shoots per
  piece) is standard; true-seed establishment requires sowing within
  ~15 days of extraction and benefits from nitric-acid scarification
  (Finding 4).
- **Soil:** pH not reconciled to a single figure — 4.5-5.8 / 5.0-6.5 /
  5.5-6.5 across three sources, a real unresolved spread [1]. Preferred
  acidic soils are often short of zinc, boron, molybdenum, and manganese
  [1].
- **Stand life:** Time to first flowering 18-24 months by one source;
  time to first harvestable pods 2-3 years by another, up to 4 years by
  a third (though that third source specifically covers black cardamom,
  a different species); productive life ~15 years by one older, lower-
  authority, uncorroborated source (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** 'Katte' (cardamom mosaic virus), aphid-
  vectored, incurable, clonally spread by vegetative propagation
  specifically (Finding 4, Finding 8). Thrips, up to 47% crop loss.
  Clump/rhizome rot, monsoon-associated, causal pathogen not
  independently corroborated.
- **Genome:** Multi-way disagreement (flow cytometry ~2,806 Mbp vs.
  draft assembly 1.06 Gb, more than a 2x gap) and a secondary
  chromosome-count disagreement (2n=48, the better-sourced figure, vs.
  2n=52 from a lower-detail source) (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** No named cultivar with a sourced trait-delta profile
  was found this pass beyond a general mention that "location-specific
  varieties" releasing >1,000 kg/ha exist in various countries, without
  naming any of them.
- **Harvest:** Repeat-cut — capsules mature at different times through
  the bearing season and are hand-picked in repeated rounds within one
  annual bearing season; exact round count not sourced this pass.
  Capsules are picked green/immature, not at two distinguishable
  maturity stages.

## Hazards

No human- or animal-health hazard (toxin, allergen, drug interaction)
was found this pass for cardamom itself. The disease hazards (katte
virus, thrips, clump/rhizome rot) are agronomic concerns, covered above
under Disease/pest profile.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh green capsule → processed within 24-36 hours →
  alkaline dip (~2% sodium carbonate, ~10 minutes, fixes green color) →
  dried (sun or artificial chamber, ~85% to ~10-11% moisture) → dried
  green capsule (Alleppey Green/Coorg Green trade grades), or optionally
  → bleached (sulphur-dioxide fumigation, or potassium metabisulphite +
  hydrogen peroxide) → bleached/white capsule (a distinct, separately
  priced official trade class, Finding 6). Essential oil separately
  distilled from cured capsules (Finding 7).

## Open research questions

- Self-compatibility is disputed between a 2009 peer-reviewed study
  (self-compatible) and a non-peer-reviewed AI-generated encyclopedia
  page (self-incompatible) — the peer-reviewed source is followed here,
  but the conflict should be checked against additional primary
  literature before being treated as settled.
- No irrigation figure was sourced for cardamom's otherwise rain-fed,
  monsoon-dependent cultivation pattern.
- Time-to-first-harvest figures disagree across sources, and one figure
  specifically comes from a source covering black cardamom, a different
  species, which may not transfer to *Elettaria* at all.
- The ~15-year productive-life figure comes from a single older,
  general, lower-authority source with no independent corroboration.
- No source converted the sourced labor-day figures into labor-hours,
  and the exact picking-round count per season was not sourced.
- No quantified shelf-life-day figure was found for either the dried
  capsule product or the essential oil, only a general "non-perishable"
  characterization for the capsule and a heat/light-sensitivity finding
  (without a day-count) for the oil.
- Scandinavian baking's adoption of cardamom has two competing,
  unreconciled historical narratives.
- No named cultivar with a sourced trait-delta profile was found, only
  an unspecific mention that "location-specific varieties" releasing
  higher yields exist in various countries without naming any of them.
- Cardamom's shade-obligate light requirement was not independently
  assessed for controlled-environment/off-world tractability this pass
  — worth a dedicated follow-up given how much stronger this crop's
  shade dependence appears to be relative to ginger's own partial-shade
  tolerance.

## Estimates and derivations

A dry-recovery yield-chain fraction of roughly 0.14-0.20 is derived
using simple mass-balance arithmetic: Finding 6 sourced before/after
moisture-content figures (~85% fresh moisture, ~10-11% final moisture).
Treating 1 kg of fresh-equivalent material as 15% dry matter + 85%
water, the dried product at 10.5% moisture (midpoint) retains dry mass
fraction = 0.15 / (1 - 0.105) ≈ 0.168 of the original total mass,
widened to 0.14-0.20 to cover the 10-11% moisture endpoint spread plus
ordinary capsule-to-capsule variation. A time-to-first-harvest estimate
of roughly 2-3 years (plausible range 18 months to 4 years) is taken
directly from Finding 4's sourced range, since cardamom is a multi-year
perennial stand rather than an annual crop with one clean "planting to
harvest" cycle. A bleaching-step mass-loss estimate of 0.97-1.0 is
reasoned by comparison to other rhizome/capsule finishing steps this
project has sourced (roughly 5-15% estimated loss from physical
abrasion/rubbing): cardamom's bleaching is a surface chemical treatment
without physical material removal, so the mass loss should be smaller
still.
