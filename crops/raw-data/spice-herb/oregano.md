# Oregano (*Origanum vulgare*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/oregano.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This project's own thyme and sage research was read in full as the
closest siblings — all three are cold-hardy Mediterranean Lamiaceae
subshrubs/herbs grown for essential oil and dried leaf, sharing some
disease (mint rust) and propagation (seed-and-cuttings-both-viable)
patterns, though oregano's own chromosome story is notably cleaner than
either.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oregano's own chromosome number is the most tightly
converged of any Lamiaceae herb this project has researched, with a
consistent base number across multiple independent studies and genome-
size figures an order of magnitude smaller than several other crops.**
Multiple independent studies converge on 2n=30 (basic chromosome number
x=15) for *O. vulgare* and its subspecies, including *virens* and
*hirtum* [1] — a Moroccan taxonomic study and a Turkish karyotype study
both report this figure, with the Turkish study noting two other
*Origanum* species as exceptions at 2n=28, while the rest of the genus
studied holds at 2n=30 [1]. A chromosome-count database independently
classifies *O. vulgare* (and named subspecies *virens*, *hirtum*) as
diploid with base count 15 [1]. Genome size (flow cytometry, Moroccan
material) is reported small: 1.43 pg/2C for *O. vulgare* subsp. *virens*
up to 1.53 pg/2C for the related species *O. compactum* [1] — notably
smaller and more tightly converged than thyme's own secondhand estimate
or sage's own disputed 2C-DNA figures, making oregano the best-resolved
genome-size finding in this project's recent Lamiaceae-herb research.
The Turkish authors separately argue these chromosome counts support
*Origanum* speciation via homoploid hybridization (hybridization without
a ploidy change) [1] — a genuinely different hybridization mechanism
from every mint-genus finding in this project's own recent research,
all of which involved ploidy changes (allohexaploidy, autotetraploidy,
multiple coexisting cytotypes).

**Finding 2 — oregano is reported genuinely self-incompatible in at
least one rigorous 2023 study, with insect pollination required for seed
set, but the same study's own reported figures contain an apparent
internal contradiction this document does not resolve, alongside a
separately sourced finding of substantial inter-subspecies
hybridization.** A 2023 study found Greek oregano (subsp. *hirtum*)
"largely self-incompatible" — plants grown indoors without insect access
developed no seeds at all, indicating self-pollination alone is
insufficient for seed set, and insects were necessary for seed
development [2]. The same study is also reported to state that over 70%
of seeds likely resulted from self-pollination [2] — a genuinely
contradictory pair of claims within one search synthesis (zero seed set
without insects, versus most seed being self-pollinated) that this
document flags rather than silently resolves; the full paper would need
to be opened to determine whether this reflects two different
experimental conditions, a self-compatible minority of plants within an
otherwise self-incompatible population, or a synthesis error. The same
source reports substantial cross-subspecies hybridization:
cross-pollination between the two subspecies (*vulgare* and *hirtum*)
can "completely take over" self- or within-subspecies cross-pollination
when both are grown near each other [2] — a sourced seed-isolation risk
between the two named subspecies, relevant to this document's own
flagged candidate split (Finding 4). Oregano is separately described as
an excellent nectar source for honeybees and other pollinating insects
[2], consistent with its own insect-pollination dependence.

**Finding 3 — propagation follows the same seed-and-cuttings-both-
viable pattern already established for sage and thyme in this project's
research, with seed germination specifically flagged as unreliable and
seed-grown plants explicitly not matching the parent's own chemistry/
flavor, while division is described as unusually fast and reliable on
established plants.** Oregano is propagated by seed, stem cuttings, or
root division, all commonly done in spring [3]. Seed germination is
reported inconsistent — one source calls germination rates "notoriously
low and inconsistent," while a commercial seed retailer instead cites a
specific 7-14 day germination window [3], an unreconciled quality-of-
sourcing gap similar to the "both routes genuinely viable, dominance
unresolved" pattern this project already found for sage. Critically,
seed-grown plants are reported to not match the parent plant's own
chemistry — vegetative propagation (cuttings or division) is
specifically needed to keep new plants identical to a named parent [3],
a genuinely actionable cultivar-fidelity finding not as explicitly
stated for either sage's or thyme's own propagation findings. Division
is described as "quick, simple" with a high success rate, but only on
established, mature plants [3] — paralleling thyme's own division
finding, which required division specifically from 3+-year-old plants.
USDA hardiness zone is disputed, with sources ranging zone 4-9 to 5-9 to
5-8, and this document could not independently confirm zone 4 survival
against an authoritative source [3] — the same kind of disputed-
hardiness pattern already seen for every other Lamiaceae herb this
project has researched (rosemary, sage, thyme, the mint genus). Drought
tolerance is consistently and clearly reported across multiple sources
(a horticultural society: "compact and drought tolerant"; a separate
grower guide: "natural rainfall is all that is required... in most
cases") [3] — a notably consistent finding, in contrast to the
hardiness-zone disagreement.

**Finding 4 — Greek oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) is reported as a
chemically and commercially distinct form from common oregano (subsp.
*vulgare*), prized specifically for higher essential-oil content and
flavor, a genuine candidate for a future split that this document flags
but does not resolve this pass.** Multiple sources distinguish Greek
oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) from common oregano (subsp. *vulgare*),
describing the former as "the finest culinary form" with a "robust
peppery flavour" and "high essential oil content" [4]. This document
does not independently verify a quantified essential-oil or carvacrol-
content delta between the two subspecies from a primary source this
pass — the distinction is sourced from general gardening/culinary
guidance, not a dedicated comparative study, a notably weaker basis than
the cultivar-level deltas sourced for several other crops this project
has researched (e.g. black cardamom's own elevation-dependent cultivar
yield delta, or spearmint's own cultivar-level carvone delta). Given
Finding 2's own cross-subspecies-hybridization finding, a future split
would also need to address seed-isolation risk between the two forms if
both were modeled as separate crops. Flagged as a candidate split here,
not resolved this pass.

**Finding 5 — oregano's own essential-oil chemistry is organized around
carvacrol as the dominant compound across the genus, with yield figures
scattered by more than an order of magnitude and a genuinely actionable,
sourced harvest-timing and nitrogen-fertilization combination, plus two
newly released cultivars with a sourced, quantified carvacrol advantage
over existing commercial material.** A Greek comparative study across
cultivated *Origanum* species reports carvacrol as the most abundant
essential-oil constituent across the genus, with a wide oil-content
range of 0.8-44.1 mg/g dry plant material [5] — more than a 50-fold
spread, the widest raw oil-content range sourced for any crop in this
project's recent Lamiaceae-herb research. Harvest timing is a sourced,
actionable lever: essential-oil yield is higher for plants harvested at
full bloom, with carvacrol "presented in great amounts" at that stage,
while autumn harvests both lower total oil output and shift the
chemical profile toward other, unnamed compounds [5] — a genuinely dual
effect (yield and composition) from harvest timing alone, a stronger
combined finding than this project's other harvest-timing results (e.g.
spearmint's own time-of-day tradeoff, which affects yield and
composition independently rather than both shifting together with a
single timing variable). A two-season Greek field trial found oil yield
peaking significantly at a nitrogen rate of 80 kg/ha in both seasons [5]
— though this document flags the trial's own reported per-hectare oil-
yield units as apparently garbled in the search synthesis and does not
reproduce a specific kg/ha figure from it. Two new cultivars, named
'Pierre' and 'Eli', are reported with significantly greater essential-
oil and carvacrol yields than existing commercial alternatives, with
'Pierre' specifically reaching 68.50% carvacrol share of total essential
oil [5] — the most precisely quantified cultivar-level chemistry figure
sourced for any crop in this project's recent Lamiaceae-herb research, a
notably stronger sourcing outcome than the general Greek-vs-common
subspecies distinction in Finding 4. A separate 2024 study specifically
advises that cultivation for the pharmaceutical industry should proceed
from propagation material pre-selected for chemotypes rich in both
carvacrol and thymol [5] — a sourced, two-compound chemotype-selection
practice, structurally similar to basil's own named linalool-vs-methyl-
chavicol chemotype finding but naming two compounds together rather than
presenting them as alternative chemotypes.

**Finding 6 — a major pet-safety organization's own primary listing was
located directly and names a specific subspecies by name, a notably
cleaner sourcing outcome than this project's own earlier mint-genus pet-
toxicity research, and the plant shares a named disease (mint rust) with
the entire mint genus this project has researched.** That organization's
own page, located directly, lists oregano specifically as *Origanum
vulgare* subsp. *hirtum* ("Greek oregano"), toxic to dogs, cats, and
horses, with gastrointestinal irritants as the toxic principle and mild
vomiting/diarrhea as the typical signs [6] — notably, that organization's
own listing names the subspecies specifically (hirtum), not the species
generically the way its mint-genus listing did, a different sourcing
shape from the genus-level mint listing. Several secondary (lower-
quality) sources separately describe concentrated oregano extract or oil
as potentially more hazardous than the fresh plant, naming carvacrol and
thymol as compounds cats specifically process poorly, with possible
liver injury at higher exposures [6] — not independently verified
against a primary source, flagged as weaker than the pet-safety
organization's own plant-level finding (the same oil-vs-plant
distinction pattern already established for several mint-family and
other Lamiaceae crops this project has researched). Disease and pest
findings include root rot in wet, poorly drained soils; fungal diseases
generally; aphids; leaf miners; spider mites; thrips; cutworms; and —
notably — mint rust [7], the same named pathogen (*Puccinia menthae*)
already established as a dominant disease for crops in the mint genus
this project has researched, now confirmed to also affect this
Lamiaceae herb from a different genus entirely, strengthening the
cross-genus-shared-pathogen pattern this project has separately noted
for rosemary and lavender's own shared *Phytophthora* finding.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, herbaceous subshrub — same general
  cold-hardy-Mediterranean-Lamiaceae shape as rosemary/sage/thyme.
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, by analogy to the companion
  Lamiaceae herbs' own framing, not independently re-verified for
  oregano specifically.
- **Climate:** USDA zone 4-9 to 5-9 to 5-8 depending on source, zone 4
  specifically unverified against an authoritative source (Finding 3) —
  the same disputed-hardiness pattern already seen for every other
  Lamiaceae herb this project has researched.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh and dried culinary herb, a widely
  used Mediterranean seasoning.
- **Pollination:** Not required for vegetative propagation (division/
  cuttings); required for seed production — genuinely self-incompatible
  per at least one study, insect pollination required for seed set
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Essential-oil content 0.8-44.1 mg/g dry plant
  material across cultivated Origanum species in one comparative study
  — more than a 50-fold spread, the widest raw oil-content range in this
  project's recent Lamiaceae-herb research (Finding 5). Harvest at full
  bloom maximizes both yield and carvacrol share; autumn harvest lowers
  both. Named cultivars 'Pierre' (68.50% carvacrol share) and 'Eli' show
  a quantified advantage over existing commercial material.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance high, consistently reported across
  multiple sources — a notably consistent finding, unlike the
  hardiness-zone disagreement. Waterlogging tolerance low — root rot
  specifically reported in wet, poorly drained soils.
- **Establishment:** Seed or vegetative (cuttings/division), both
  genuinely viable — the same "both routes viable, dominance unresolved"
  pattern already found for sage (Finding 3). Seed germination reported
  inconsistent/unreliable by one source, a specific 7-14 day window by
  another, not reconciled. Seed-grown plants do not match the parent's
  own chemistry — vegetative propagation specifically required for
  cultivar/chemotype fidelity, a stronger, more explicit finding than
  either sage's or thyme's own propagation-fidelity framing. Division is
  specifically described as fast/reliable but only on established,
  mature plants, paralleling thyme's own 3+-year-old-plant division
  requirement.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Nitrogen fertilization at 80 kg/ha found to peak oil yield
  significantly across two seasons in a Greek field trial (Finding 5) —
  the exact kg/ha oil-yield figure from this trial is flagged as
  apparently garbled in the search synthesis and not reproduced.
- **Stand life:** Perennial subshrub; division viable only once plants
  are established/mature, same pattern as thyme. No specific maximum-
  lifespan or replanting-cycle figure sourced.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Root rot in wet/poorly-drained soils;
  general fungal disease susceptibility; aphids; leaf miners; spider
  mites; thrips; cutworms; and mint rust (*Puccinia menthae*) (Finding
  6) — the same pathogen already established as a dominant disease for
  crops in the mint genus this project has researched, now confirmed
  cross-genus.
- **Genome:** Small and well-converged — 1.43-1.53 pg/2C across *O.
  vulgare* subsp. *virens* and the related species *O. compactum*
  (Finding 1), notably better-resolved than several other Lamiaceae
  herbs' own genome-size gaps this project has found. Chromosome number
  is tightly converged at 2n=30 (x=15) across multiple independent
  studies — the cleanest chromosome finding in this project's recent
  Lamiaceae-herb research.
- **Cultivars:** 'Pierre' (68.50% carvacrol share of total essential
  oil, a quantified advantage over existing commercial material) and
  'Eli' (also significantly greater essential-oil/carvacrol yield,
  specific percentage not captured) (Finding 5) — the most precisely
  quantified cultivar-level chemistry figures in this project's recent
  Lamiaceae-herb research. Greek oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) vs. common
  oregano (subsp. *vulgare*) is a broader, less precisely quantified
  distinction, flagged as a candidate future split (Finding 4), not
  resolved this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — full-bloom harvest maximizes both yield
  and chemistry together, not independently characterized as a formal
  multi-stage system this pass. Single window per planting season, cut-
  and-come-again through the season.

## Hazards

Root rot in wet/poorly-drained soils; general fungal disease
susceptibility; aphids; leaf miners; spider mites; thrips; cutworms; and
mint rust (*Puccinia menthae*) — the same pathogen already established
as a dominant disease for crops in the mint genus this project has
researched, now confirmed cross-genus (Finding 6).

A major pet-safety organization lists the specific subspecies *hirtum*
("Greek oregano") as toxic to dogs/cats/horses via gastrointestinal
irritants; concentrated extract/oil separately described (lower-quality
sourcing) as potentially more hazardous via carvacrol/thymol, cats
specifically flagged as poor metabolizers — not independently verified
against a primary source (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** herb harvested cut-and-come-again, ideally at full bloom
  (maximizes both yield and carvacrol content) → used fresh or dried, or
  distilled into essential oil.

## Open research questions

- A 2023 pollination study's own reported figures contain an apparent
  internal contradiction: zero seed set without insect access (implying
  strict self-incompatibility) versus a separate claim that over 70% of
  seeds likely resulted from self-pollination. The full paper was not
  opened to resolve this.
- No quantified essential-oil or carvacrol-content delta between Greek
  oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) and common oregano (subsp. *vulgare*) was
  independently sourced from a dedicated comparative study — the
  distinction in Finding 4 rests on general gardening/culinary guidance,
  not a primary source.
- A Greek nitrogen-fertilization field trial's own reported oil-yield
  units appeared garbled in the search synthesis — no specific kg/ha
  figure from that trial was reproduced in this document.
- Grow-days figures were not independently sourced this pass.
- kcalPerKg/proteinGPerKg were not independently sourced this pass.
- Water-use figures (crop coefficients) were not independently sourced
  this pass beyond the qualitative drought-tolerance and waterlogging-
  risk findings.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any operation this
  pass.
- Storage-life figures (fresh herb, dried leaf) were not independently
  sourced this pass.
- The concentrated-oil pet-toxicity claim (carvacrol/thymol, cat-
  specific liver-injury risk) is from lower-quality secondary sourcing,
  not independently verified against a primary veterinary-toxicology
  source.
- No maximum productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was
  sourced.
- No seed-yield-per-hectare or isolation-distance figure was sourced for
  oregano's own seed route.
- Religious/ritual use was not independently searched with the same
  depth as other crops this pass — one source notes the Greek name
  derives from words meaning roughly "mountain" and "joy," and that
  ancient Greeks used the plant as a wound poultice, a historical/
  medicinal use note, not a formal religious-ritual production rule.
- Soil pH optimum was not independently sourced this pass.
- Light requirement was not independently sourced this pass.
- Controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not independently
  assessed this pass.
- A candidate future split between Greek oregano (subsp. *hirtum*) and
  common oregano (subsp. *vulgare*) is flagged but not resolved — a
  genuinely distinct, commercially recognized culinary/chemistry
  distinction (Finding 4), but this pass found no quantified
  comparative-study figure to ground a split, and a split would also
  need to address the sourced cross-subspecies hybridization/seed-
  isolation risk (Finding 2).
