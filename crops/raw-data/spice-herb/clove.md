# Clove (*Syzygium aromaticum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/clove.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

A single species, *Syzygium aromaticum*, Myrtaceae, formerly classified
under a different genus name [1][2]. Native to the Maluku Islands
(Moluccas), Indonesia [1][3]. The commercial spice is the dried,
unopened flower bud; essential oils are also distilled from three
distinct plant organs (bud, leaf, stem), each with its own eugenol/
eugenyl-acetate chemistry [1][6]. This project's black pepper research
is the closest sibling already completed (woody perennial spice tree,
dried product plus multiple essential-oil grades from different
organs), and its cassia and Ceylon cinnamon research share the same
multi-organ-oil pattern and the same under-documented-pest-literature
caveat style.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — taxonomy is stable, but cultivation climate figures
disagree across every source opened this pass, none of them high-
authority.** The species name is consistent across a major botanical
database and all cultivation sources [1][3]. Native range is the Maluku
Islands; major commercial production today is in Indonesia, Madagascar,
Tanzania (Zanzibar/Pemba), and Sri Lanka, with introduced plantings in
Brazil, Haiti, India, Kenya, Malaysia, Mauritius, Mexico, and the
Seychelles [1]. Temperature optima conflict across sources: 20-30°C,
22-30°C, 23-32°C, and 25-35°C are all separately cited, with general
agreement the tree cannot tolerate frost or temperatures below 15°C
[1]. Rainfall figures also diverge: 1,500-2,500 mm/year from one source
vs. 1,270-2,540 mm from another [1]. A regional invasiveness assessment
adds a specific agronomic nuance: a marked dry season actually promotes
flowering, even though the tree copes poorly with the dryness itself —
a real trigger/stress tension, not a contradiction [1]. Soil pH guidance
is comparatively consistent at 5.5-6.5, slightly acidic, well-draining,
with one source extending tolerance down to pH 4.5 provided waterlogging
is avoided [1][7]. Most of this cultivation-climate material comes from
a single commercial publisher whose pages repeat each other with
inconsistent numbers — flagged here as a reliability caveat across the
whole finding, not silently smoothed over.

**Finding 2 — yield figures are reasonably consistent per-tree but
widely inconsistent per-hectare, and tree density is rarely stated.**
Indian extension material gives 4-8 kg of dried buds per well-maintained
full-grown tree under favorable conditions, settling to a long-run
average of about 2 kg/tree/year after year 15, with explicitly
irregular/alternate-bearing yield [4]. A standard crop database gives a
wider per-tree range of 1-12 kg/year [4]. Per-hectare figures vary
sharply and rarely state the assumed tree density: 400 kg/ha (one Indian
source), 500 kg/ha at a specific station (based on the ~2 kg/tree
figure), and 200-400 kg/ha depending on age and management from a
farmbook-style source [4]. Trade sites assuming ~150 trees/ha would
imply far higher per-hectare figures than any of the above, so are not
relied on here [4]. An Indonesian breeding study gives the most
specific, named-type comparison found this pass: one type averaged
161.8 kg/tree/year vs. 93.1 kg/tree/year for another, over a multi-year
trial [10] — nearly 10-20x higher than the Indian per-tree figures
above, a discrepancy not reconciled here (possibly reflects selected
high-yielding breeding-trial trees vs. ordinary smallholder trees, but
this is not confirmed by any source opened this pass).

**Finding 3 — harvest is slow, physically hazardous, and a major cost
driver, with buds maturing unevenly across a season.** Harvesting is
described as a difficult, slow, and dangerous process because the trees
are very tall (up to 20 m) and vigorous [8]. Harvest labor represents up
to 40% of total production cost in Indonesia [8]. Buds are picked by
hand, over several passes per season, because they do not mature
uniformly on the tree [4][8]. An Indonesian agroforestry thesis measured
harvest labor in person-days rather than hours per tree: roughly 29
person-days with 6 workers on one farm, 35 person-days with 4 workers
on a second, with no conversion to a per-tree or per-hour rate [8] — a
genuinely different unit from what this project's other spice research
has sourced, and not convertible without more information. No dedicated
clove-specific hand-picking rate (kg/hour/worker) was found; general
hand-harvest rates for other crops (olives, hand-picked citrus) are
recorded as general hand-harvest-rate context only, not as analogies for
clove's own rate, since clove buds are far smaller and more numerous per
unit mass picked.

**Finding 4 — drying and storage figures are the most mutually
consistent part of this document, though grading detail is thin.** Buds
are spread in a drying yard and dried over 4-5 days, judged ready when
the stem turns dark brown and the rest of the bud light brown; dried
weight is roughly one-third of fresh weight [4]. Target moisture is
under 10% (one source) or a 10-12% band (a retail-market source) [4][9].
Whole dried cloves are commonly quoted at an 18-24-month shelf life
(some sources say 12-18 months); ground cloves lose aromatic oil much
faster and are explicitly recommended against for long storage [9].
Grading is described only at a coarse level: Indian material names
"special" (hand-picked), Grade 2, and Grade 3 tiers for whole cloves
plus a separate ground-cloves category [4]; an Indonesian export-
supplier listing additionally names size-and-purity tiers, with the top
tier most sought after for export [9] — this second source is a B2B
marketplace listing, lower authority, and no single official national or
international standard's full grading table was opened this pass.
Dried-cloves count is given as roughly 11,000-15,000 per kilogram [4].

**Finding 5 — propagation is overwhelmingly by fresh seed, with
viability lost within days to at most two weeks, and grafting exists but
is thinly documented.** Seed is the common commercial propagation
method, but is reported as short-lived — plant within 2-3 days of
extraction per one grower guide, within one week per Indian
horticulture material, or at most about two weeks if kept moist [5].
Germination commences in 10-15 days and may run to 40 days, delayed to
about 60 days at higher elevations [5]; a separate study reported 93.3%
germination within 14 days using a plant-hormone seed treatment [5].
Nursery media matters: an Indonesian study found sand the best
germination medium among sand, cocopeat, and zeolite; a separate review
found sand plus farmyard manure (1:1) best for early, uniform, higher
germination [5]. Pre-sowing treatment (soaking overnight, removing the
pericarp) gives quicker and higher germination than sowing the whole
fruit directly [5]. Grafted trees are reported to reach production in
5-7 years vs. 8-10 years for seedling-grown trees [5] — a real, sourced
timing delta — but no source opened this pass described the actual
grafting technique or rootstock choice.

**Finding 6 — the plant yields three chemically distinct essential-oil
grades from three different organs, with eugenyl acetate as the key
marker separating bud oil from the other two.** Bud oil is eugenol-
dominant at roughly 65-87% across sources (one Indonesian study: eugenol
65.29%, trans-caryophyllene 20.06%, α-humulene 3.38%; a review cites
70-87%) [6]. Leaf oil is also eugenol-dominant but with proportionally
more caryophyllene (the same Indonesian study: eugenol 64.47%, trans-
caryophyllene 27.19%, α-humulene 3.62%; a regional study found leaf oil
eugenol as high as 86.525%, the highest of any organ in that study) [6].
Stem oil is the most eugenol-rich of the three: one source gives eugenol
83-92% plus caryophyllene 4-12% and eugenyl acetate 0.5-4%; another gives
80.2% eugenol and 6.6% β-caryophyllene [6]. Eugenyl acetate itself is
the commercially used marker distinguishing genuine bud oil (8-21%
eugenyl acetate, a supplier claim not independently verified) from leaf/
stem oil (under 2%) [6]. Regional variation in bud-oil eugenol is itself
substantial: 55.6% (Java), 70.0% (India), 74.7% (one Indonesian
production region), and 82.6% (Madagascar) by one Indian comparative
study [6] — no single figure is representative. Stem oil is produced at
real industrial scale: roughly 100 tons/year combined from Tanzania,
Madagascar, and Indonesia [6]. No Zanzibar-specific oil-composition
figure was found this pass despite Zanzibar/Pemba's commercial
importance.

**Finding 7 — chromosome count and genome size are well-sourced from a
single 2022 assembly, in contrast to most of this document's agronomic
figures.** Clove is diploid, 2n=22, consistent with the Myrtaceae
family's base chromosome number x=11 [11]. The 2022 chromosome-scale
genome assembly gives 11 chromosome-scale scaffolds totaling
370,257,752 bp (~370 Mb), with 27,528 predicted genes; a k-mer-based
short-read estimate gave a smaller 343 Mb figure [11]. The assembly's
own authors note the species' genome size had not previously been
recorded in a major plant genome-size database [11] — so the gap here
is in the reference database itself, not just in this pass's search. A
2023 comparative study confirms the 11-chromosome organization is
conserved with related species, several of which are autotetraploid
(2n=4x=44) by contrast with clove's diploidy [11].

**Finding 8 — disease pressure in Zanzibar/Pemba has a genuinely
unresolved historical cause, kept visible rather than resolved.** A
devastating "sudden-death disease" killed at least half of Zanzibar
Island's clove trees and caused over 2,000 outbreaks on Pemba by 1950,
with new outbreaks arising at roughly 14%/year by a 1951 estimate [12].
Early symptomology: slight yellowing and foliage thinning, death from
days to many months later, caused by breakdown of the absorbing root
system (water shortage) [12]. The original 1949 study suspected a virus,
carried by a scale insect that is itself tended and transported by a
red tree-ant species [12]. Later sources disagree on the pathogen
entirely: one later study attributes the most widespread Zanzibar die-
back to a specific fungus, and a 1953 paper separately describes a
different fungal species as a sudden-death pathogen [12] — virus,
and two distinct fungal causal claims across these sources, not picked
between here. All these sources are dated (1949-1953); no current
(post-2000) status update for this disease complex was found this pass.
Separately, an Indian-focused general overview names a stem-boring
caterpillar species as the most significant clove pest in that country
[12] — an entirely different pest from the Zanzibar disease complex,
and not confirmed present in East Africa.

**Finding 9 — eugenol/clove-bud-oil carries a real, well-documented
acute human-health hazard at ingested doses, distinct from normal
culinary spice use.** Oil of clove is roughly 60-90% eugenol [13].
Serious toxicity in infants and small children has been reported from
doses as small as 5-10 mL, with documented outcomes including CNS
depression, seizures, hepatotoxicity, metabolic acidosis, coagulation
defects, and hypoglycemia [13]. The mechanism is reasonably well
characterized in animal studies: eugenol depletes hepatocyte
glutathione, producing liver toxicity, and a glutathione-replenishing
treatment is suggested given that mechanism [13]. UK clove-oil product
labeling explicitly contraindicates teething use and advises against use
in children under 2, despite clove oil's long traditional use for
teething and dental pain — a herbal reference notes direct scientific
evidence for teething safety/efficacy specifically is limited [13]. This
is a genuine dose-dependent hazard on the oil product, not the whole
dried spice in normal culinary quantities.

**Finding 10 — named "cultivars" in the literature are really regional
landrace/type groups, not registered breeding-program releases, and
naming is inconsistent across sources.** Indonesian sources name four
main cultivated types, two of which are reported native to West
Sumatra, while a third is believed descended from a small number of
seeds reintroduced from the island of Zanzibar itself [10] — an
interesting historical reversal (a type named for Zanzibar that was
reintroduced into Indonesia from there, rather than the other way
round). Maluku-focused research additionally names three further local
types, and separately a slightly different four-way naming in another
source [10] — spelling and grouping are genuinely inconsistent across
Indonesian regional studies, not normalized here. The island/production-
region names themselves are not cultivar names, and should not be read
as varieties [1][10]. An Indian agricultural source states clove is
"not commonly propagated through named varieties" there, being
multiplied mainly by seed and some grafting [5] — consistent with
Finding 5's thin grafting-procedure sourcing. No single authoritative
primary-source list resolves all of the above into one canonical type
taxonomy.

**Finding 11 — a genuine wild/non-aromatic relative population exists
in the native range, but this project does not treat it as its own
crop.** "Forest clove" is described as the main wild type, found in the
northern and middle Moluccas and the Irian/Papua region [14]. A 2022
study classifies forest clove alongside several other named wild types
on three Maluku islands, and specifically characterizes one island
population as "non-aromatic" [14]. A 1986 survey (single source, not
independently cross-checked) claims the indigenous clove populations of
the northern islands — regarded as the species' true center of origin —
were forcibly eradicated in the 17th century, a historical claim
connected to colonial-era monopoly enforcement but not verified against
a more recent source this pass [14]. Because the wild forest-clove
population is explicitly described as non-aromatic and is not reported
as a currently wild-gathered food or spice product, it is treated here
as a genetic-diversity/breeding-resource note rather than a product this
project catalogs separately.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical, perennial — a large evergreen tree
  up to 20 m. Single harvest per flush, repeated across the harvest
  season as buds are picked in several passes per season as they mature
  unevenly; the tree itself bears for many decades once mature, though
  no sourced upper stand-life figure was found this pass.
- **Climate:** Tropical, with real cross-source range conflicts kept
  visible: a 15°C frost-sensitivity floor is consistent, but preferred-
  temperature figures range 20-35°C and rainfall figures 1,270-2,540mm
  across sources.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — always dried before sale or
  culinary use.
- **Pollination:** Not independently sourced this pass — a genuine gap,
  unlike the detailed pollination findings this project has for black
  pepper and both cinnamons.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield ~400 kg/ha (Indian station figure,
  built on a ~2 kg/tree long-run average) — the most representative
  ordinary-practice figure. The 4-8 kg/tree "well-maintained" figure and
  the 161.8 kg/tree/year breeding-trial figure are both recorded
  separately as upper bounds under very different management/selection
  conditions, not converted to a per-hectare figure since tree density
  was not stated.
- **Water use:** No formal crop-coefficient curve was found. Drought
  tolerance is low — a 2021 island study found trees died outright in a
  2015 drought; estimated tree-phase dry-season water need 640.1 mm/year
  (53.3 mm/month), above normal-condition requirement. Waterlogging
  tolerance is low — multiple sources agree waterlogging/poor drainage
  causes root rot; the fine root system is reported to damage quickly in
  soil that is too wet or too dry.
- **Establishment:** Propagated overwhelmingly from fresh seed (plant
  within days of extraction), with sand or sand+farmyard-manure (1:1)
  nursery media giving the best germination results; pre-sowing soaking
  and pericarp removal improves germination speed and rate over direct
  sowing. Grafting exists (5-7 years to production vs. 8-10 for
  seedlings) but no sourced procedure was found this pass. Young trees
  reportedly need shade from companion plants while establishing, per
  one lower-authority source, not independently cross-checked.
- **Soil:** pH optimal 5.5-6.5, min 4.5 (tolerance limit provided
  waterlogging is avoided), max not independently sourced beyond a
  6.5-7.0 range given by one source. No reliable root-depth figure was
  found; only a passing note that the root system is "relatively
  shallow" and wind-vulnerable.
- **Stand life:** First bearing 5-7 years (grafted) / 8-10 years
  (seedling). No sourced figure for peak production age, economic
  productive lifespan, or senescence was found this pass — a genuine
  gap relative to most other woody-perennial crops this project has
  researched.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Sudden-death disease complex, historically
  devastating to Zanzibar/Pemba, cause genuinely disputed across three
  source accounts. A stem-boring caterpillar, the most significant named
  pest in India specifically. Root rot from waterlogging/poor drainage.
- **Genome:** 2n=22 (diploid, Myrtaceae base number x=11); chromosome-
  scale assembly ~370 Mb across 11 scaffolds, 27,528 predicted genes;
  the species' own genome size was not previously recorded in a major
  reference database per the assembly paper's own authors.
- **Cultivars:** Named regional landrace/type groups rather than
  registered breeding-program releases (Finding 10). One type is the
  highest-yielding named type in the one breeding-trial comparison found
  (161.8 kg/tree/year); another is lower-yielding in the same trial
  (93.1 kg/tree/year). Two further types are named alongside these as
  main cultivated types with no yield figure sourced. Several further
  local types are named in Maluku-region research, not cross-referenced
  against the main grouping. No eating-quality/flavor cultivar deltas
  were sourced this pass — the sourced differences are entirely about
  yield and regional origin, and the type-naming itself is genuinely
  inconsistent across the Indonesian regional literature.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for the bud spice itself (buds are always
  picked unopened, before flowering) — there is no multi-maturity-stage
  choice. However, the same plant does feed three organ-level products
  (bud, leaf, stem) that are harvested separately and processed into
  chemically distinct oils (Finding 6) — modeled here as three separate
  harvest/process chains rather than a maturity-stage choice, since
  organ choice, not maturity-stage choice, is what selects the product.
  This is the same modeling approach already used for this project's
  Ceylon cinnamon bark-vs-leaf-oil split.
- **Storage:** Whole dried cloves keep 18-24 months (cool, dry,
  airtight; a minority of sources cite 12-18 months), moisture content
  target 10-12% (sources disagree on the exact cutoff). Ground cloves
  lose aromatic oil much faster and are not suited to long storage.

## Hazards

Sudden-death disease complex, historically devastating to Zanzibar/
Pemba, cause genuinely disputed across three source accounts (virus/
scale-insect vector, and two distinct fungal species) — no current
source confirms this disease's present-day status (Finding 8). A stem-
boring caterpillar species, the most significant named pest in India
specifically. Root rot from waterlogging/poor drainage. Eugenol (clove
bud oil) hepatotoxicity and CNS/metabolic effects at ingested doses as
low as 5-10 mL in small children; contraindicated for infant teething
use despite long traditional use for that purpose (Finding 9).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (bud):** unopened flower buds hand-picked over several passes
  per season → spread in drying yard, dried 4-5 days → graded (coarse
  tiers) → sold as whole dried cloves, or ground, or distilled into bud
  oil. **Chain (leaf):** fresh leaves → distilled into leaf oil.
  **Chain (stem):** stems separated from bud harvest → distilled into
  stem oil (real industrial-scale product, ~100 t/yr combined across
  three producing countries).

## Open research questions

- Cultivation-climate figures (temperature, rainfall) disagree across
  every source opened, and most come from one low-authority commercial
  publisher.
- Per-tree yield figures disagree by roughly 10-20x between ordinary-
  practice Indian extension figures (2-8 kg/tree) and an Indonesian
  breeding-trial figure (161.8 kg/tree/year).
- No sourced per-tree-hour or per-worker kg/hour hand-picking rate for
  clove specifically — the only labor figure found is in person-days,
  not convertible to more standard units.
- No official national or international grading-standard table was
  opened for clove's size/purity or special/Grade-2/Grade-3 tiers —
  only B2B marketplace listings and general extension material.
- Grafting procedure and rootstock choice were not sourced, only the
  resulting production-timing delta.
- No Zanzibar-specific essential-oil composition figure was found,
  despite Zanzibar/Pemba's commercial importance for clove generally.
- Sudden-death disease complex's current (post-2000) status in
  Zanzibar/Pemba is unknown — all sources opened this pass are dated
  1949-1953, and the causal agent itself is disputed across three
  accounts.
- Named "cultivar"/type taxonomy for Indonesian and Maluku clove is
  genuinely inconsistent across sources — multiple only-partially-
  overlapping namings from different regional studies, not reconciled
  here.
- Pollination biology (self-fertility, outcrossing, pollinator type) is
  entirely unsourced this pass — a genuine gap relative to this
  project's other woody spice/herb research.
- No sourced peak-production age or economic stand-life/senescence
  figure, only first-bearing age.
- Seed-crop vs. spice-crop tree allocation trade-off not quantified: a
  tree grown for seed must be allowed to flower through to fruit rather
  than having its buds harvested as spice that season.
- No boron/chloride/micronutrient/heavy-metal soil-chemistry figures
  were sourced, only the pH range.
- Kretek (clove cigarette) manufacturing and the clove-pomander
  decorative tradition are both real, significant uses of this crop's
  primary product, out of scope for this agronomic research.

## Estimates and derivations

A yield figure of 400 kg/ha was chosen as the primary representative
figure over the 200-500 kg/ha range of per-hectare sources, kept
explicitly separate from the much higher per-tree breeding-trial
figures, since no single source gives both a per-hectare figure and the
tree density it assumes; this figure was chosen as internally the most
self-consistent pairing available (built on the ~2 kg/tree long-run-
average figure from the same broad literature), rather than averaging
across sources with unstated density assumptions.
