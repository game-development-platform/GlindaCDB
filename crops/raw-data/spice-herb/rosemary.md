# Rosemary (*Salvia rosmarinus*, syn. *Rosmarinus officinalis*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/rosemary.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers true rosemary, now accepted as *Salvia rosmarinus* Spenn.
(the long-used name *Rosmarinus officinalis* L. is treated as a synonym
by current taxonomic sources) [1]. This document does not cover named
cultivars as separate crop entries.

This project's own lavender research was read as the most recent
genuinely structural sibling — both are cold-hardy Mediterranean woody
Lamiaceae subshrubs, and that research proposed a shared cold-hardy-
Mediterranean-woody-subshrub archetype as a future candidate explicitly
covering rosemary/sage/thyme. This document confirms rosemary is a
genuine fit for that shape, as a second data point.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — rosemary's taxonomy has been formally revised (genus
merged into *Salvia*), and its chromosome-number literature disagrees
between two counts in the same handbook, unresolved to a single value.**
A standard Southeast Asian plant-resources handbook's own chromosome-
number entry for *Rosmarinus officinalis* gives both 2n=20 and 2n=24,
without reconciling which applies to which population or cytotype [1];
a separate source gives a single 2n=24 figure with no mention of 2n=20,
and also notes the now-current placement of the species as *Salvia
rosmarinus* rather than its own genus *Rosmarinus* [1]. A 2024
chromosome-level genome assembly (Nanopore + Hi-C) resolved 1.17 Gb of
sequence into 12 pseudo-chromosomes with 46,121 annotated protein-coding
genes, and dates the rosemary lineage's divergence from its closest
relatives to roughly 33.7 million years ago, with one whole-genome
duplication event around 28.3 million years ago [2] — the 12-chromosome
assembly is consistent with the 2n=24 figure (12 homologous pairs) but
does not itself resolve the handbook's 2n=20 alternative count, which
this document did not trace to a primary cytology source. This is a
genuinely less settled taxonomic/cytogenetic picture than this project's
own lavender/lavandin ploidy findings, despite rosemary's much longer
cultivation history.

**Finding 2 — rosemary's fresh and dried herbage yield figures are
reasonably convergent across several extension and field-trial sources,
in genuine contrast with the essential-oil yield figures (Finding 3),
which scatter by roughly an order of magnitude.** One agricultural
university's own extension guidance gives green leaf yield of 12-13
t/ha and roughly 2.5 t/ha of dried leaf per year [3]; a national
aromatic-crop research program cites 100-150 quintals/ha/year of dried
leaf (10-15 t/ha) [3], a notably higher dried-yield figure than the
first source's own 2.5 t/ha from what reads as a similar green-yield
base — the two sources were not reconciled this pass. An organic-farming
field trial reported fresh biomass of 13.1-13.6 t/ha and dry biomass of
4.3-4.4 t/ha in its best treatments, roughly 60% above an untreated
control [3] — both the fresh figure (close to the first source's 12-13
t/ha) and the dry figure (between the other two sources) sit inside the
spread of the other two sources, giving three at-least-partially-
converging data points for fresh yield specifically. A review citation
gives per-plant biomass of 0.4-0.5 kg/plant [3], and standard planting
density is given as 50,000-60,000 plants/ha or roughly 45×45 cm spacing
[3] — multiplying the per-plant figure by that density gives an implied
per-hectare range of roughly 20,000-30,000 kg/ha (see Estimates and
derivations), well above every one of the three directly-stated hectare
figures above, a genuine unreconciled discrepancy this document does not
resolve.

**Finding 3 — rosemary's essential-oil yield figures disagree by roughly
an order of magnitude across geographically distinct field trials, a
now-familiar pattern in this project's spice/herb oil-yield research
(parallel to lavender/lavandin's own yield spread).** An Ethiopian
variety trial reported essential-oil yields of 210.57, 196.89, and
152.96 kg/ha across three candidate varieties, with oil content of
roughly 0.7-0.9% w/w [4]; a Crimean botanical garden reported a lower
60-65 kg/ha across a season, from 3- to 5-year-old plants of one
cultivar [4]; a Turkish Ankara-ecological-conditions trial of lines from
six southern locations reported oil content ranging 0.35-2.08% (highest
line mean 0.94%) but gave no per-hectare figure in the material opened
this pass [4]; and a northern Indian subtropical seasonal-crop trial
reported oil yield in litres per hectare (83.34 L/ha at 210 days after
transplanting, 88.03 L/ha at 225 days) [4] — a volume figure this
document did not convert to mass for lack of a sourced rosemary-oil
density figure, so it is not directly comparable to the other sources'
kg/ha figures. A 2025 Moroccan drought/phosphorus trial found moderate
drought stress combined with optimal phosphorus fertilization produced
up to 113% higher essential-oil yield than well-watered controls [4] —
a relative finding, not an absolute yield, but notable as a rare sourced
case where a documented drought-stress response increases rather than
decreases a target yield metric. None of these five sources' field
conditions (plant age, density, harvest number, extraction method) were
fully reconciled against each other this pass.

**Finding 4 — rosemary's propagation-reliability picture extends a
rosemary-thyme-sage reliability gradient this project has found, with a
lower, more precisely-bracketed seed-germination figure than other
Lamiaceae herbs this project has researched, while independently
confirming cuttings as the standard commercial route.** Seed germination
rates are reported inconsistently across sources as 30-50%, 40-60%, or a
wider 30-80% depending on species, with seedlings additionally described
as slow-growing, taking months to reach usable transplant size [5]; one
grower-oriented commercial source gives a seeding rate of 10g of seed for
4,000-5,000 plants and a 10-12-week sowing-to-transplant window [5] — a
specific, actionable seed-crop figure this document did not find for
lavender's own secondary seed route. Cuttings are uniformly described as
the standard commercial method, producing true-to-type plants more
reliably and more cheaply than seed, typically taken in spring or early
summer during active growth, sometimes with rooting hormone [5]. No
peer-reviewed cutting-rooting-success-percentage figure was found this
pass; the germination and seeding-rate figures above are themselves from
gardening/commercial-content sources rather than peer-reviewed agronomy,
a genuine sourcing-quality caveat this document flags.

**Finding 5 — rosemary's pollination biology is unusually well-studied
at the mechanism level, with a sourced geitonogamy/inbreeding-depression
finding that goes beyond any other Lamiaceae herb this project has
researched, including lavender's own pollinator-community finding.**
Rosemary flowers are reported as both self-compatible and capable of
cross-pollination, with nectar at the flower base and a lower "lip"
providing a landing platform for honey bees and bumblebees [6]. A cited
peer-reviewed study found that under natural conditions most seed comes
from cross-pollination rather than self-pollination, attributed to a
high proportion of male-sterile flowers and abortion of most self-
pollinated seed [6] — a stronger, more specific self-incompatibility-
adjacent finding than this document found for lavender's own simple
"pollination required" framing. A separate Spanish study on the shrub
describes the flowers as protandrous (male parts mature before female)
and self-compatible, but reports inbreeding depression specifically from
geitonogamous pollination (pollen moving between flowers on the same
plant, not necessarily the same flower) [6] — a more granular within-
plant-vs-within-flower self-pollination distinction than any other crop
this project has researched. A French study additionally found that
artificially stressed plants produced slightly more fruit, interpreted
as evidence that small wild bees pollinate rosemary more efficiently
than honeybees under certain conditions [6] — not reconciled against the
geitonogamy-depression finding, but recorded as a further, not-yet-
integrated pollination-efficiency data point.

**Finding 6 — rosemary's disease complex is dominated by water-
management-sensitive Phytophthora root rot, closely paralleling
lavender's own dominant-disease-category finding, with one specific
pathogen now genome-sequenced for this host that lavender's disease
sources did not name.** A 2025 Tuscany, Italy study found *Phytophthora
pseudocryptogea* made up 94% of isolates recovered from rosemary stems,
roots, and rhizosphere, with *P. megasperma* the remaining 6%, and the
authors specifically note that poorly-drained soils and climate
constraints favor this oomycete's spread [7] — a close parallel to
lavender's own *Phytophthora nicotianae* finding, though a different
named species, not a repeat citation. A separate Spanish study
specifically ties *Phytophthora nicotianae* to collar and root rot in
containerized rosemary and lavender nursery stock together [7] —
directly connecting the two crops' disease risk in a shared commercial-
propagation context (both are cutting-propagated nursery crops), a
genuinely new cross-crop finding this document's search surfaced that
lavender's own research pass did not record. Other named fungal threats
are *Rhizoctonia solani*, *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*, and *Fusarium*
spp. [7] — overlapping closely with the Fusarium/Rhizoctonia names in
lavender's own four-pathogen root-rot list, though without the same
source stating all of them for rosemary specifically in one place this
pass. No spittlebug or other named insect-pest source was found this
pass despite a dedicated search — a genuine information gap, not a
"pest-free" finding.

**Finding 7 — rosemary itself (the fresh culinary herb/plant) is
reported non-toxic to common pets by a major pet-safety organization,
but its essential oil is a genuinely distinct, separately-hazardous
product, carrying its own species-specific metabolic risk — a finding
this document did not find paralleled by any other crop this project
has researched.** That organization's own listing rates rosemary (the
plant) as non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, with mild gastrointestinal
upset (vomiting, diarrhea) the main reported side effect of eating it
[8]. The concentrated essential oil is repeatedly described as the real
hazard, via its camphor and related terpene content, with reported risk
of liver or kidney damage and neurologic effects after large ingestions;
cats are specifically described across multiple sources as poor
metabolizers of these compounds, a cat-specific vulnerability this
document did not find paralleled for any other essential-oil-bearing
crop this project has researched [8]. Undiluted oil applied topically to
a dog's skin is separately reported to cause muscle weakness and
behavioral changes [8]. No source opened this pass specifically
addressed human pregnancy-related rosemary-oil risk, despite a dedicated
search — this document does not assert a pregnancy-specific hazard claim
without a source. Most sources for this finding are pet-care blogs
rather than veterinary-toxicology literature, a genuine sourcing-quality
caveat; the pet-safety organization's own citation itself is the
strongest single source.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial subshrub, Mediterranean-
  climate-adapted — directly comparable to lavender's own woody-subshrub
  growth habit.
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again — this crop's sources describe
  continuous year-round cutting of an evergreen plant rather than a
  single annual flush, so this pattern is a better-supported fit here
  than it was for lavender — not independently re-verified against a
  dedicated multi-cut-per-season source this pass.
- **Climate:** Mediterranean, warm temperate, subtropical. Hardiness-
  zone sources this pass disagree moderately: USDA Zones 6-10 in one
  profile, 7-11 in another, 6-9 in a third, with named cultivars narrower
  still (one named cultivar Zones 8-10, another Zones 7-10 with
  protection advised at the cold edge, a third noted for improved cold
  tolerance over the species) [9] — genuinely less cold-hardy than
  lavender's own USDA zones 5-8 rating.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — used as a seasoning, not eaten in bulk.
- **Pollination:** Not required for food/leaf yield (harvested as
  foliage); required for seed production specifically — self-compatible
  but substantially outcrossing in practice, geitonogamy causes
  inbreeding depression (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh herbage 12-15 t/ha reasonably convergent
  across three sources (Finding 2). Dried leaf 2.5-15 t/ha, not
  reconciled across sources — a per-plant-biomass-times-density back-
  calculation implies a notably higher 20,000-30,000 kg/ha figure not
  reconciled with any of the three direct hectare figures (see Estimates
  and derivations). Essential-oil yield 60-210 kg/ha across four kg/ha-
  basis field trials, not reconciled; a fifth source gives 83-88 L/ha,
  not unit-converted this pass (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance very high, consistent with every
  source opened this pass describing rosemary as drought-tolerant once
  established [9]. Waterlogging tolerance low — wet/poorly-drained soil
  in winter is repeatedly described as usually fatal, and is the crop's
  dominant disease driver (Finding 6) [7][9]. A 2025 Moroccan trial found
  moderate drought stress combined with optimal phosphorus fertilization
  increased essential-oil yield by up to 113% over well-watered controls
  — a genuinely atypical drought-response finding this document flags
  rather than generalizes (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Vegetative cuttings (primary; spring/early-summer
  softwood cuttings, sometimes with rooting hormone) (Finding 4). Seed is
  a real secondary route: 30-80% germination (sources disagree on the
  exact range), slow seedling growth (months to usable size), 10-12
  week sowing-to-transplant window; commercial seeding rate 10g seed per
  4,000-5,000 plants — a more specific seed-crop figure than this project
  found for lavender's own secondary seed route. Self-compatible but
  substantially outcrossing in practice (bee-pollinated, geitonogamy
  causes inbreeding depression) (Finding 5).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Woody perennial subshrub; no specific maximum
  productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was sourced this pass —
  the Crimean oil-yield trial's own biomass source spanned 3- to 5-year-
  old plants without stating an upper productive-age limit (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Dominated by water-management-sensitive
  Phytophthora/Fusarium/Rhizoctonia/Sclerotinia root-and-crown pathogens,
  directly paralleling lavender's own disease-profile finding, with a
  genuinely new cross-crop nursery-propagation-context connection
  (shared *P. nicotianae* risk in containerized rosemary and lavender
  stock together) (Finding 6). No named insect pest was sourced this pass
  despite a dedicated search.
- **Genome:** 1.17 Gb (chromosome-level assembly, 12 pseudo-chromosomes,
  46,121 annotated genes) [2] — a directly species-specific figure, in
  contrast with lavender's own genus-wide-only genome-size range. This
  crop's own chromosome-number picture is nonetheless less settled
  (Finding 1, 2n=20 vs. 2n=24 disagreement not resolved to the 12-
  chromosome assembly's own implied 2n=24).
- **Cultivars:** No single peer-reviewed source compared culinary flavor
  directly across named cultivars this pass. A 2024 peer-reviewed study
  of six Mediterranean cultivars (including two named cultivars) found
  broadly similar phenolic profiles with quantitative differences: one
  named cultivar had the highest phenolic content, while terpene-content
  rankings disagreed between two citations of the apparently same study
  — not reconciled [11]. Lower-quality encyclopedic/blog sources
  describe one named cultivar as especially cold-hardy with a mild,
  lemon-scented character; another as similarly cold-hardy with pale
  blue flowers and woody stems sometimes used as kabob skewers; a third
  as having improved cold tolerance over the species baseline [9][11] —
  none of these specific claims were independently peer-reviewed-sourced
  this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — foliage cut as needed/at usable size
  through the growing season; no distinguishable immature-vs-ripe
  harvest-stage choice sourced. Single window — cut-and-come-again
  continuous cutting through the growing season on an evergreen plant,
  not a discrete multi-flush/separate-cohort pattern (contrast with
  lavender's own single annual flowering window).
- **Storage:** Dried rosemary commonly reported to retain peak flavor
  roughly 6-12 months, with usable life extending to 1-3 years in an
  airtight container away from light and heat [10] — notably less
  precisely sourced than this project's typical storage figures, since
  nearly all sources found for this specific question were a single low-
  quality content-farm domain. Fresh-to-dried ratio commonly cited as
  roughly 3:1 by volume (1 Tbsp fresh = 1 tsp dried), though sources
  disagree, with alternative 2:1 and 4:1 figures also given [10] — the
  same low-source-quality caveat applies.

## Hazards

Phytophthora root rot (*P. pseudocryptogea* dominant in one Italian
study, *P. nicotianae* in a Spanish nursery-stock study), plus
*Rhizoctonia solani*, *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*, and *Fusarium* spp.,
all water-management-sensitive (Finding 6) — the dominant disease-hazard
category. No named insect pest was sourced this pass despite a dedicated
search.

The plant itself (fresh herb) is non-toxic to dogs/cats/horses per a
major pet-safety organization; the concentrated essential oil is a
separate, genuinely distinct hazard (liver/kidney/neurologic risk on
large ingestion, cats specifically poor metabolizers, topical undiluted-
oil reactions in dogs) (Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** foliage cut cut-and-come-again through the growing season →
  used fresh or dried, or distilled into essential oil (concentrated
  camphor/terpene content, a hazard distinct from the plant itself).

- No named insect pest was sourced for rosemary this pass, despite a
  dedicated search including the term "spittlebug" (a pest named in some
  general Mediterranean-herb pest lists this document did not

- No source opened this pass specifically addressed human pregnancy-

- Dried-rosemary storage-life and fresh-to-dried-ratio figures came
  almost entirely from a single low-quality content-farm domain.
- No specific maximum productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was
  sourced for this perennial crop.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any rosemary
  operation this pass.
- No seed-yield-per-hectare or isolation-distance figure was sourced for
  rosemary's own (secondary) seed-propagation route, though a specific

- No peer-reviewed source directly compared named-cultivar culinary
  flavor or cold-hardiness this pass.
- Rosemary's light requirement was not independently sourced this pass —
  a full-sun requirement was assumed by analogy to every other

- Rosemary's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass — this crop's own acute waterlogging/

  paralleling the same flag raised for lavender.

## Estimates and derivations

A per-plant-biomass-times-density implied fresh yield of roughly
20,000-30,000 kg/ha is derived but not adopted as this document's own
headline yield figure: one review-cited per-plant biomass figure of
0.4-0.5 kg/plant [3], multiplied by a sourced planting density of
50,000-60,000 plants/ha [3], gives 0.4×50,000=20,000 kg/ha up to
0.5×60,000=30,000 kg/ha. This is substantially above every one of the
three directly-stated per-hectare fresh-yield figures. The discrepancy
likely reflects either a different plant age/maturity assumption behind
the 0.4-0.5 kg/plant figure, a different harvest frequency (cumulative
season total vs. single cut), or simply population-level yield losses
(mortality, uneven stands) not captured by a clean per-plant
multiplication — this document records the calculation but does not
adopt it as the headline yield figure, deferring instead to the three
directly-stated hectare figures. A dedicated field trial reporting both
per-plant and per-hectare figures together under the same conditions
would resolve this.
