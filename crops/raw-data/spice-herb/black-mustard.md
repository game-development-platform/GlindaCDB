# Black Mustard (*Brassica nigra*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/black-mustard.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as black mustard, condiment seed (*Brassica nigra* L.).
Brown mustard and yellow mustard are the closest comparison crops this
project has also researched — *Brassica nigra* is one of the two
diploid parent species (the BB genome donor) of brown mustard's own
allotetraploid genome (AABB, formed from *B. rapa* AA × *B. nigra* BB),
a genuinely closer genomic relationship than a typical same-family
sibling, not merely a shared-genus convenience grouping. Yellow mustard
(*Sinapis alba*, a different genus entirely) is used for secondary
comparison only.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — black mustard's own genome is comparatively messier and
less converged than its own allotetraploid offspring's genome, with
sequencing-based size estimates spanning a wide range and assembled
lengths falling notably short of the k-mer-based size estimate itself.**
Multiple sources confirm *B. nigra*'s chromosome number as 2n=16 (BB
genome) [1] — this is clean and well-converged, directly corroborating
brown mustard's own cited parentage. However, genome size estimates
diverge substantially: k=17 k-mer-based estimates run roughly 570-608
Mb, while actual assembled lengths across several independent efforts
fall notably short of that — 397 Mb (a short-read assembly), 506 Mb (a
long-read Nanopore assembly), and 537 Mb (another long-read assembly)
[1] — meaning even the best assembly captures only about 88-94% of the
k-mer-estimated genome size, a real, sourced assembly-completeness gap.
A separate chromosome-scale assembly of a doubled-haploid line is
reported with "revised pseudochromosome nomenclature," implying the
field itself is still reconciling how to name and compare these parallel
assembly efforts — paradoxically, the simpler diploid parent species has
a less settled genome-assembly story than its own allotetraploid
offspring (brown mustard's own 2021 chromosome-scale assembly captured
92.9% of its total length with no comparable multi-assembly size
disagreement).

**Finding 2 — black mustard's own pollination biology is sourced only
from a single, dated secondary citation describing the species as
"largely self-sterile," a genuinely weaker and older sourcing outcome
than brown mustard's own clean, recent, directly-confirmed self-
compatibility finding for the same broader breeding-system question.**
An older pollination handbook, citing earlier work, describes *B.
nigra* as largely self-sterile, with insect (bee) pollination therefore
mattering for seed set, while separately noting that if any degree of
self-fertility exists, some selfing can still occur in principle [2] —
this is treated as thin evidence: a secondary citation of older primary
work, not a recent, dedicated self-compatibility study of the kind this
project has sourced for brown mustard (which found no self-
incompatibility system in that species via a dedicated review). If
black mustard is indeed self-sterile while its own allotetraploid
offspring (brown mustard) is self-compatible, this would be a genuine,
interesting breeding-system divergence between parent and derived-hybrid
species — but that divergence is not asserted with confidence given how
thin and dated the black-mustard-specific evidence is. No seed-
certification isolation-distance figure (comparable to brown mustard's
own sourced 50-100 m) was found for black mustard specifically [2] — a
direct, named sourcing gap relative to this document's own closest
sibling.

**Finding 3 — black mustard's own seed yield is reported consistently
lower than brown mustard's own best-case figures across multiple
independent trials, including within the same Turkish study that
produced brown mustard's own high-end figure, and the species is
reported to have been commercially displaced by brown mustard
specifically because of a harvest-mechanization-relevant trait (pod
shattering), though that specific displacement claim is weakly
sourced.** A Greek field study found black mustard's highest yields
(1,352-1,456 kg/ha) under inorganic fertilization [3]; an Iranian study
found a best sowing date (January 30) yielding 1,474.2 kg/ha, with yield
decreasing at high planting density due to reduced branching and
siliques per plant [3] — a genuinely different, negative density-yield
relationship from brown mustard's own plateauing (not declining)
seeding-rate response. A Finnish trial found black mustard yielding
less than 700 kg/ha [3], and the same 2024 Turkish trial that produced
brown mustard's own 3,080 kg/ha high-end figure reported a black mustard
line's own worst-year yield at only 572 kg/ha [3] — this direct within-
study comparison is treated as the strongest available evidence that
black mustard's yield ceiling sits meaningfully below brown mustard's
own, though a black-mustard best-year figure from that same study was
not captured. Separately, an unverified secondary (blog-level) source
claims black mustard was commercially displaced by brown mustard
varieties after 1950 specifically because mechanical harvesting requires
non-shattering seed pods, which black mustard's own pods reportedly lack
[3] — this claim is flagged as plausible (it is consistent with the
sourced harvest-timing advice below) but not independently confirmed by
a more reliable source. Harvest timing itself is sourced as a real
operational concern: premature harvest leaves shriveled grain, while
delayed harvest loses oil content and increases shattering losses, with
morning-hours harvest specifically recommended to reduce shattering [3].

**Finding 4 — black mustard's own disease profile shows a genuinely
favorable within-genus resistance finding for one major disease (white
rust) shared with brown mustard, while simultaneously showing a
genuinely worse susceptibility finding for a different disease
(Alternaria) in at least one regional study.** A production guide
reports that B-genome species, including *B. nigra* specifically, are
more resistant to white rust (*Albugo candida*) than other Brassica
species, with overall white-rust risk rated low for the crop generally
and rising mainly when plants are wounded [4] — directly corroborating
and extending brown mustard's own white-rust weather-dependency finding
with a genuinely favorable relative-resistance angle specific to the
B-genome lineage black mustard belongs to. However, a Baltic-region
study found black mustard was the most infected plant (by Alternaria) of
all species studied across both growing seasons tested [4], while a
separate screening study found *B. nigra* showed only moderate yellowing
after Alternaria brassicae inoculation — less severe than brown mustard
or a related species in that same screening [4] — these two Alternaria
findings are flagged as apparently in tension (most-infected in one
field study vs. moderately-resistant in one inoculation screening) and
not resolved here, noting the two studies likely measured different
things (field infection incidence vs. controlled-inoculation symptom
severity). No sinigrin- or allyl-isothiocyanate-specific finding was
located for black mustard in this pass's own disease/pest-focused
search. Damping-off and wirestem root rot are separately reported as
high-risk diseases for mustard generally, not specifically isolated to
black mustard in the sources found [4].

**Finding 5 — black mustard is independently confirmed as a widespread,
non-native invasive species in California with a specific, sourced
historical-introduction pathway, and is simultaneously a deliberately
cultivated condiment crop and a well-documented ecological invader.** A
study of plant remains preserved in historic adobe bricks found that
only three invasive weed species predate 1769 California colonization,
while fifteen (including black mustard) first appear in Spanish mission-
era bricks [5] — a specific, sourced historical-introduction dating,
stronger than the often-repeated but contested legend that missionaries
deliberately sowed mustard seed to mark the road between missions, which
is treated as unverified here: one source calls it fiction, another
cites a local historian's claimed evidence for deliberate introduction,
and the sources explicitly disagree [5]. The species' ecological impact
in California is specifically sourced: it grows up to 6 feet tall,
outcompetes native plants for sunlight, its thick deep root system
limits water availability to neighboring species, dried late-season
stands raise fire risk, and its seed can persist in the soil seed bank
for up to 50 years [5] — black mustard is a strong candidate for
treatment as both a genuinely cultivated condiment crop and a well-
documented, currently wild-growing/foraged naturalized population,
since wild-gathered seed of common wild-growing black mustard is
reported used directly in cooking, as is done in India [5]. On toxicity:
one authoritative pet-safety reference's own listing covers "White/
Black/Yellow Indian Mustard" as one combined page explicitly naming
*Brassica* species collectively, toxic to horses via isothiocyanates
(gastrointestinal irritation, colic) [6] — this is the same page already
cited for brown mustard, not a separate black-mustard-specific listing,
and this finding is treated as directly shared rather than re-derived as
newly independent, consistent with the fact that the page covers all
three species by name together. No dog or cat toxicity information was
located for black mustard specifically, mirroring the same gap for brown
mustard. No information on the mustard-seed biblical parable or
"smallest seed" claim was located in either search pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** True annual, herbaceous — same general
  mustard-family cultivation shape as brown and yellow mustard.
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure was
  independently sourced this pass, though the species' own confirmed
  California-naturalized status (Finding 5) implies at least
  Mediterranean/warm-temperate tolerance.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in the usual sense — the seed is used
  as a ground/whole condiment spice; wild-gathered seed is also reported
  used directly in cooking (Finding 5).
- **Pollination:** Likely required — one older, thinly-sourced handbook
  describes the species as "largely self-sterile" (insect/bee
  pollination mattering for seed set), a genuinely weaker and more dated
  finding than brown mustard's own clean, recent self-compatibility
  confirmation for the same broader question (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** 572-1,474 kg/ha across multiple independent
  trials (Greek, Iranian, Finnish, Turkish), consistently lower than
  brown mustard's own best-case 3,080 kg/ha figure, including within the
  same Turkish study (Finding 3). Yield decreases at high planting
  density (reduced branching/siliques per plant) — a genuinely different,
  negative density-yield relationship from brown mustard's own
  plateauing response.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, the sole propagation route found or
  expected for this true annual. Likely largely self-sterile per one
  dated secondary source; no seed-certification isolation-distance
  figure sourced, unlike brown mustard's own 50-100 m. Seed can persist
  in the soil seed bank up to 50 years in naturalized California
  populations (Finding 5) — this is not assumed to apply to cultivated-
  field seed-bank dynamics without further sourcing.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  fertilizer-response yield figures in Finding 3.
- **Stand life:** True annual; no specific days-to-maturity figure
  sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** White rust (*Albugo candida*) risk reported
  low, with B-genome species (including *B. nigra* specifically) more
  resistant than other Brassica species — a genuinely favorable relative-
  resistance finding (Finding 4). Alternaria findings are in tension
  across sources: most-infected species in one Baltic field study, but
  only moderately symptomatic (less than brown mustard) in one
  inoculation screening — not resolved. Damping-off and wirestem root
  rot reported high-risk for mustard generally, not specifically
  isolated to black mustard.
- **Genome:** Chromosome number 2n=16 (BB genome) is clean and well-
  converged, directly corroborating brown mustard's own cited parentage.
  K-mer-based genome-size estimates ~570-608 Mb; best assembled length
  only 506-537 Mb (88-94% of the k-mer estimate), a real, sourced
  assembly-completeness gap, genuinely messier than brown mustard's own
  well-converged offspring-genome assembly despite black mustard being
  the simpler diploid parent (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** No named black-mustard cultivar was sourced this pass
  beyond a single unnamed line tested in the 2024 Turkish trial alongside
  brown mustard's own named variety — a notably thinner cultivar-
  sourcing outcome than brown mustard's own three named varieties.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one seed harvest per
  planting. Harvest timing is operationally significant: premature
  harvest causes shriveled grain; delayed harvest loses oil content and
  increases shattering losses; morning-hours harvest specifically
  recommended to reduce shattering (Finding 3).

## Hazards

- **Isothiocyanates (horse toxicity)**: an authoritative pet-safety
  reference's own listing covers "White/Black/Yellow Indian Mustard" as
  one combined page, toxic to horses via isothiocyanates
  (gastrointestinal irritation, colic) (Finding 5). No dog or cat
  toxicity information was located for black mustard specifically.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → harvested (morning-hours recommended to reduce
  shattering losses) → dried → used whole or ground as a condiment
  spice. Wild-gathered seed from naturalized populations is also used
  directly in cooking in some traditions (Finding 5).

## Estimates and derivations

A dried-seed shelf life of roughly 1460 days (plausible range
1095-1825 days) is carried over by direct analogy to brown mustard's and
yellow mustard's own dried-seed figures (both sourced at 1460 days in
this project's own research), on the reasoning that black mustard's own
dried, intact condiment seed should behave similarly in storage to its
two closely related mustard-family condiment-seed siblings. This is a
stronger-than-usual analogy since the comparison crops are genuinely
close (one is this species' own direct allotetraploid offspring), but it
is still not an independently sourced figure for black mustard itself.
