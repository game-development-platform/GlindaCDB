# Cornmint (*Mentha arvensis*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-02

## Naming and scope

This covers *Mentha arvensis* (cornmint, corn mint, field mint, Japanese
mint; frequently treated in the commercial/industrial literature under a
different accepted synonym, see Finding 1). It does not cover peppermint,
spearmint, or the remaining ungrouped genus (watermint, apple mint,
horse mint, pennyroyal, and all other species), which this project
treats separately or leaves out of scope.

This project's closest grounding for cornmint is its own research into
other crops in the same genus, which share the general cultivation shape
(rhizomatous herbaceous perennial, oil-focused) but a genuinely different
economic role: cornmint is the dominant global feedstock for crystallized
menthol, not a fresh-herb/confectionery crop the way its siblings
primarily are.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — cornmint's own economic identity is as the world's
dominant industrial menthol-crystallization feedstock, a genuinely
different market position from fresh-herb mints, with India the clearly
dominant producer and a documented semi-perennial-to-annual cultivation
shift made specifically for price competitiveness.** Cornmint's
essential oil is the principal industrial source of crystalline
(-)-menthol, recovered by freeze-crystallization, with the residual
"dementholized oil" sold separately to pharmaceutical and cosmetic
industries [1] — the same general crystallization/byproduct shape this
project has found for some other mint-family oils, but here as the
crop's primary commercial purpose rather than a secondary, unresolved
downstream option. India is reported as the world's dominant producer of
crude *M. arvensis* oil and the leading exporter of processed natural
menthol, having shifted cultivation from a semi-perennial to an annual
crop specifically to improve price competitiveness, with commercial
Indian cultivation traced to a 1964 introduction in one source [1].
China is a smaller producer (one estimate: under 1,500 mt/year,
concentrated in one province) [1]. Japan has the oldest documented
commercial history: menthol was first isolated by freeze-crystallization
from a Japanese prefecture's own cornmint oil in the 1870s, with large-
scale commercial production dated to Shanghai in 1923 [1]. The United
States currently has no commercial cornmint production, though a
Mississippi trial found it could be grown successfully there, with
menthol content rising from ~50% (2007) to 67-78% (2008) across trial
years [1]. Some sources treat a different scientific name as the correct
one and *M. arvensis* as a synonym, or vice versa; this document does
not resolve which name is taxonomically primary and uses *M. arvensis*
as the more commonly encountered name in the agronomic literature
reviewed.

**Finding 2 — cornmint's own chromosome/ploidy picture is the most
scattered this project has found for any mint-genus species, spanning
nearly an order of magnitude across different sources, with at least one
sourced triploid-breeding programme demonstrating the practical breeding
consequences of that scatter.** Reported somatic chromosome counts vary
dramatically — a Japanese cytogenetics study on a commercial cultivar
grown in Hokkaido since 1937, and a separate Chinese commercial variety,
both report 2n=96 [2]; a separate 1959 study instead reports 2n=90 for
*M. arvensis* [2]; a Dutch study reports a much lower 2n=24 [2]; one
source separately states that 12 is the basic chromosome number of the
species' own polyploid series [2] — none of these figures reconciled to
each other in this pass. A 1959 Japanese breeding study demonstrates the
practical stakes of this ploidy complexity: 35 triploid clones (3n=144)
were successfully raised by crossing induced tetraploid clones (4n=192)
with diploid clones (2n=96), but the reverse cross failed outright
because the resulting seeds did not germinate [2] — a sourced,
directionally-asymmetric ploidy-breeding constraint.

**Finding 3 — propagation is reported as no commercial seed route at
all, the same constraint common across this mint genus, with an
unusually fast and easy vegetative-spread property specifically noted
for this species.** A university propagation protocol states *M.
arvensis* is a perennial that does not produce seeds commercially,
reproducing instead through cuttings and division, adding that "almost
any part of the root system can become a new plant rather quickly" [3].
Clonal propagation via nodal explants (tissue culture) is also
separately documented [3], a propagation route this project has not
independently cross-checked against other mint species to see if it
also applies there or is cornmint-specific.

**Finding 4 — mint rust is reported caused by the same pathogen species
that attacks other mint-genus crops, not a cornmint-specific organism,
with sourced, substantial, quantified yield and oil-quality damage
across multiple independent studies.** Mint rust is caused by *Puccinia
menthae*, reported to attack many Lamiaceae members including
peppermint [4]. Left uncontrolled, mint rust is reported to cut oil
yield by 50% or more and lower both oil quality and quantity [4]. A
Brazilian field study specifically found cornmint genotypes, despite
being traditionally grown for their high menthol yield, showing low
biomass and low essential-oil/menthol productivity because of heavy
mint-rust pressure [4]. A separate pot study on peppermint (not
cornmint directly) found rust infection significantly reduced oil
content, stolon number, leaf area, and plant weight, and increased
defoliation by 67% [4] — a peppermint-specific severity figure, not
independently confirmed for cornmint. A French extension fact sheet
notes rust reduces menthol and menthofuran production while increasing
total terpene content overall [4] — a genuinely actionable, sourced
quality-degradation mechanism, not just a yield loss. Two separate plant
patents describe breeding responses: one peppermint variety patent
reports greater rust resistance than its own parent while remaining
susceptible to a separate wilt disease; a separate *M. arvensis* hybrid
patent describes higher menthol-rich oil yield together with rust
tolerance [4] — a sourced, simultaneous yield-and-resistance breeding
target specific to cornmint's own hybrid development.

**Finding 5 — field yield figures scatter across a roughly 2-3x range
depending on spacing, fertilization, and variety, with nitrogen/sulphur
co-fertilization and a named high-yielding cultivar both sourced as
actionable levers.** A Uttar Pradesh spacing/NPK trial (2015-16) found
oil yield ranging roughly 109-150 kg/ha across treatments, with the
highest oil yield (~150 kg/ha) coming from the lightest fertilizer dose
at tight 30x30 cm spacing, while the best fresh/dry herbage instead came
from a heavier dose at wider 60x60 cm spacing — a genuinely sourced
spacing-vs-fertilizer tradeoff where the treatment maximizing herbage
does not also maximize oil yield [5]. A separate nutrient/zinc trial on
one named variety found its best treatment (a specific NPK ratio plus
foliar micronutrients) reaching fresh herb yield of 232.7 q/ha (23,270
kg/ha), oil content 1.05%, and oil yield 244.34 kg/ha — its lowest
treatment instead gave only ~45 kg/ha oil [5], a more than 5x spread
within that single trial's own treatment range. A Lucknow late-
transplanting trial found high planting density (2.5x10^5 plants/ha)
combined with 160 kg N/ha gave significantly higher yields under delayed
(following a preceding winter crop) planting, with the authors
estimating attainable oil yield around 164 kg/ha for north Indian plains
conditions [5]. A nitrogen/sulphur trial found the combination of 50 kg
S/ha with 200 kg N/ha best for herb, dry-matter, and oil yield together
[5] — a sourced co-fertilization finding (sulphur alongside nitrogen). A
named cultivar is reported with a yield potential of 185-190 q/ha
herbage (18,500-19,000 kg/ha) [5] — the highest single herbage figure
found in this pass, though not independently cross-validated against the
trial-level figures above.

**Finding 6 — no cornmint-specific pet-toxicity source was found this
pass, but the crop is already covered by the same genus-level pet-
toxicity listing that applies to other mint-family crops.** A dedicated
search for pet-toxicity sources specific to *M. arvensis* returned
nothing [6]. However, an authoritative pet-safety reference's own
genus-level "Garden Mint" (*Mentha sp.*) listing (toxic to dogs/cats/
horses, essential oils as the toxic principle) applies to the whole
genus, not a named species [6]. No menthol-crystallization-specific
yield percentage (what fraction of cornmint oil's own menthol is
recovered by crystallization) was found this pass — a genuine sourcing
gap, despite cornmint's own crystallization-focused economic identity
(Finding 1) making this figure more directly relevant here than for
other mint-family crops.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, herbaceous, rhizomatous — but
  commercially grown as an annual in India specifically to improve
  price competitiveness, a genuinely different cultivation-cycle choice
  from an open-ended perennial framing (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure found this pass.
- **Food status:** Raw edible by genus-wide analogy, though not
  independently confirmed as a distinct culinary-use crop this pass —
  cornmint's own economic identity is overwhelmingly industrial/
  menthol-focused (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Not required — vegetatively propagated, no
  commercial seed route (Finding 3).
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh/dry herbage 9,400-23,270 kg/ha
  depending on trial and treatment, including a cultivar-potential
  figure up to 18,500-19,000 kg/ha (Finding 5). Essential oil yield
  45-244 kg/ha across trials and treatments, with the same trial showing
  a >5x spread between its own best and worst treatments. Oil content
  as low as 1.05% of fresh herb weight in one cited trial — not
  independently reconciled against a dry-matter-basis figure.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative only — cuttings and division; "almost
  any part of the root system can become a new plant rather quickly," a
  notably fast/easy vegetative-spread property specifically sourced for
  this species (Finding 3). Tissue-culture (nodal explant) propagation
  is also documented.
- **Soil:** Nitrogen/sulphur co-fertilization (200 kg N + 50 kg S per
  ha) is a sourced combined yield driver for herb, dry-matter, and oil
  yield together (Finding 5) — a genuinely sulphur-inclusive
  fertilization finding. No pH figure independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Perennial by growth habit, but commercially managed as
  an annual crop in India specifically for price-competitiveness
  reasons.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Mint rust (*Puccinia menthae*) is the
  dominant named disease — the same pathogen species that attacks other
  mint-family crops. Left uncontrolled, reported to cut oil yield 50%+
  and degrade oil quality (shifting the menthol/menthofuran-to-total-
  terpene balance); a Brazilian field study found this pressure
  specifically severe enough to undermine cornmint's own high-menthol
  breeding advantage.
- **Genome:** No reference-genome assembly or size figure was found for
  cornmint this pass. Chromosome number is the most scattered this
  project has found for any mint-genus species (2n=24/90/96 reported
  across different sources, with 12 cited as the series' own basic
  number), not reconciled.
- **Cultivars:** One named Japanese commercial cultivar (grown in
  Hokkaido since 1937, 2n=96). A separate named variety with a sourced
  best-treatment yield figure from a nutrient/zinc trial. A third named
  cultivar with a yield potential of 185-190 q/ha herbage. No cross-
  cultivar trait-delta comparison (chemistry, disease resistance) was
  independently sourced this pass beyond their individual trial
  contexts.
- **Harvest:** Repeat-cut, single stage, not independently characterized
  as a formal multi-stage harvest system this pass. Single window per
  planting season; the annual-cultivation framing implies a single-
  season cycle, though this was not independently re-verified as a
  harvest-window-level distinction this pass.

## Hazards

Mint rust (*Puccinia menthae*) is the dominant named disease — the same
pathogen species that attacks other mint-family crops, not a distinct
organism. Left uncontrolled, reported to cut oil yield 50%+ and degrade
oil quality (Finding 4). Pet toxicity is covered by the same genus-level
authoritative pet-safety listing ("Garden Mint") that applies to other
mint-family crops this project has researched.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — genus-wide mint essential-oil pet toxicity is

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** herbage cut (repeat-cut) → distilled into essential oil →
  oil freeze-crystallized to recover crystalline (-)-menthol → residual
  "dementholized oil" sold separately to pharmaceutical/cosmetic
  industries. The crystallization process itself and its recovery-yield
  percentage are not modeled in detail here pending clearer sourcing.

## Estimates and derivations

Herbage yield of 23,270 kg/ha is derived from a nutrient/zinc trial's
own reported 232.7 q/ha figure by the standard unit conversion 1
quintal (q) = 100 kg. Similarly, a named cultivar's own reported
185-190 q/ha converts to 18,500-19,000 kg/ha by the same conversion.
Both are straightforward unit conversions of the sources' own reported
figures, not independent productivity estimates.
