# Star Anise (*Illicium verum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/star-anise.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

"Star anise" is confused with at least one severely toxic look-alike
species within the same genus, not merely a different genus as with bay
leaf's own look-alikes. This document covers only culinary/Chinese star
anise (*Illicium verum*). It does not cover Japanese star anise
(*Illicium anisatum*), which contains the neurotoxin anisatin, is not
edible (its dried, powdered leaves are instead burned as incense in
Japan), and is documented as the cause of real-world poisoning outbreaks
specifically through adulteration of the culinary species (see Finding
5) — a within-genus, species-level confusion risk, genuinely more
dangerous in practice than bay leaf's own cross-genus naming confusion,
since the two *Illicium* species' dried fruits are visually similar
enough to have caused documented contamination incidents in the real
spice trade.

No close existing sibling in this project's own research shares the
genus or family (Schisandraceae/Illiciaceae) — the closest structural
comparison is this project's own prior research into other crops' pet-
safety-article sourcing patterns and look-alike-toxicity framing, both
used here only as analogous documentation shapes, not botanical
analogies.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — chromosome number is cleanly and consistently sourced as
diploid (2n=28, x=14) across two independent sources, but the genus
itself is separately hypothesized to be ancient paleotetraploid in
origin, and genome-size figures scatter across a dramatic range with one
recent, unpublished figure describing an "ultra-large angiosperm
genome."** A 2018 peer-reviewed study and a standard plant-resources
handbook both independently report a base chromosome number of x=14
with diploidy (2n=28) for *I. verum*, and the peer-reviewed study
separately notes the same base number (x=14) applies across several
other tested *Illicium* species [1] — a genuinely convergent, cross-
species-consistent chromosome-number finding, in sharp contrast to bay
leaf's own severely disputed, incompatibly-framed chromosome claims. The
same study separately hypothesizes that this consistent x=14 base number
across the genus suggests *Illicium* may be ancient paleotetraploids —
explicitly flagged by the source itself as a hypothesis, not a settled
result [1]. Genome size (2C DNA content) was measured across 29 taxa in
the same study, ranging from 24.5 pg to 27.9 pg — with the same study
separately noting *Illicium* genome sizes are considerably higher than
other early-diverging angiosperm lineages, pointing to genome expansion
specifically within this genus [1]. A separate, not-yet-peer-reviewed
preprint estimates the *I. verum* genome at approximately 14.98 Gb, with
roughly 89% repetitive sequence, explicitly described by its own
authors as an "ultra-large angiosperm genome" [1] — this document flags
this figure with its own un-peer-reviewed status explicitly, and does
not attempt to reconcile it against the 24.5-27.9 pg flow-cytometry
range from the other study, since converting picograms to gigabases
requires an assumption this document does not make without a directly
stated conversion factor from either source.

**Finding 2 — time to first bearing is reported across a notably wide
range (4-7 years from planting, or a minimum of 6 years from seed
specifically) with a separately sourced, specific viable-seed-
production delay (9-10 years) distinct from first flowering (5-6
years).** One source reports trees typically take 4-7 years from
planting before they begin to bear fruit, with productivity continuing
for several decades [2]. A second source instead gives a minimum of 6
years from seed specifically [2] — broadly consistent with, though not
identical to, the first source's own range. A horticultural reference
gives the most granular finding: flowering begins at 5-6 years from
seed, but viable seed production does not begin until around 9-10 years
old [2] — a genuinely useful distinction this document preserves rather
than collapsing into a single "years to maturity" figure, since
flowering and viable-seed-bearing are explicitly reported as two
different milestones several years apart. Productive life is separately
reported around 80 years by the same horticultural reference [2] — a
notably long productive lifespan, though this document does not merge
this with nutmeg's own 35-40-year peak-productivity figure, since
"productive life" and "years to peak yield" are different claims from
different sources about different crops.

**Finding 3 — yield figures are sourced with a specific per-tree fresh-
to-dry conversion ratio, and propagation is reported via three parallel
routes (seed, cuttings, and grafting).** One agroforestry database
source reports a mature tree may yield 8-12 kg fresh fruit per season,
with a specific stated conversion ratio: 4-5 kg fresh fruit yields
approximately 1 kg dry fruit [3] — a genuinely useful, specific fresh-
to-dry conversion figure not sourced with comparable precision for any
other crop in this project's recent research. The same source reports
*I. verum* is "usually propagated by seeds, cuttings and grafting" [3] —
three parallel routes, more than either bay leaf (seed + cuttings only)
or nutmeg (grafting as the only commercially viable route due to
dioecy); this document does not find or expect a dioecy-driven
propagation constraint for star anise, since no source in this pass
described the species as dioecious — this document does not claim star
anise is confirmed monoecious/hermaphroditic either, only that no dioecy
claim was found, a genuine absence rather than a confirmed negative
finding.

**Finding 4 — the species' own primary cultivation climate requirement
is specifically sourced as cooler tropics/subtropics at altitude (up to
2000 m) with a narrow average-annual-temperature band (12-18°C), and
frost sensitivity is separately confirmed to make cultivation in
temperate zones reported impossible.** The main cultivation areas are
reported in cooler tropics and subtropics specifically at altitudes up
to 2000 m, with average annual temperatures of 12-18°C [2] — a specific
combined altitude-and-temperature cultivation niche, a notably more
detailed climate-requirement finding than cumin's own general "arid/
dry/cool/low-humidity" qualitative finding. A separate cultivation
review states the tree is sensitive to frost, making cultivation
impossible in temperate zones [2] — this document treats "impossible"
as a stronger claim than a simple cold-intolerance threshold. One
gardening source separately gives USDA zones 8-11 [2] — this document
notes this figure appears, on its face, inconsistent with the
"impossible in temperate zones" claim from the cultivation review, since
USDA zone 8 reaches into temperate territory; this document does not
resolve this apparent tension, flagging it as a genuine open item rather
than assuming one source is simply wrong.

**Finding 5 — real-world poisoning outbreaks have been directly,
specifically documented from Japanese star anise adulterating culinary
star anise shipments or products, with a named incident, a named toxin
and mechanism, and a specific symptom profile.** In 2001, a large
outbreak of toxicity occurred in the Netherlands, attributed to
accidental contamination of a tea blend with excess Japanese star anise
[4] — a specific, dated, named real-world incident. More broadly, case
reports tie poisonings to Japanese star anise found mixed with the
culinary Chinese variety in commercial spice supplies [4] — an
adulteration-driven, not merely accidental-foraging-driven, hazard
mechanism, genuinely different in kind from most other crops' own
toxicity findings this project has researched. The main toxin in
Japanese star anise is anisatin, a neurotoxin that can cause seizures
[4] — Japanese star anise is separately reported so toxic that its
fruit is not edible at all; instead its dried, powdered leaves are
burned as incense in Japan [4] — this document records this as the
traditional, non-food use of the toxic species, not a culinary one,
directly reinforcing why its presence in a food product is specifically
an adulteration/contamination event rather than an intentional
ingredient.

**Finding 6 — the culinary species' own shikimic acid content is the
direct raw-material basis for a named, globally significant
pharmaceutical product (oseltamivir/Tamiflu), with a documented,
specific supply-chain bottleneck event tied directly to this crop's own
low extraction yield and narrow seasonal harvest window.** A patent
background source identifies shikimic acid from Chinese star anise as a
base material for Tamiflu (oseltamivir) production, with a major
pharmaceutical company's own 2005 supply shortage attributed to a
bottleneck in shikimic-acid availability, since at the time it was only
practically isolated from this specific spice [5] — a genuinely
significant, named pharmaceutical-supply-chain event directly tied to
this crop. The same patent source reports star anise yields roughly
3-7% shikimic acid by extraction, with harvests occurring in a short
seasonal window [5] — this document treats the 3-7% figure and the
"short seasonal window" framing as consistent with, though not directly
reconciled against, Finding 3's own 8-12 kg fresh fruit/tree/season
yield figure, since the two figures describe different measurement
dimensions (extraction percentage vs. absolute fruit mass). Fermentation
using engineered *E. coli* is reported as a more consistent alternative
route to shikimic acid, with a named biotechnology company having
supplied the pharmaceutical company with fermentation-derived shikimic
acid alongside star anise during the shortage [5] — this document notes
this as evidence that star anise's own historical near-monopoly on
shikimic acid supply has since been at least partially displaced by
biotechnology, though this document does not independently verify star
anise's own current (2026) market share of global shikimic acid supply,
since no current-year source was located this pass. Several commercial
spice-retailer sources make unsourced, unverified claims (e.g. "90% of
the world's shikimic acid," a "1200% price spike") that conflict with
or are not corroborated by the patent and academic sources above — this
document explicitly does not adopt these unverified commercial claims.

**Finding 7 — anthracnose is confirmed the dominant disease by a
specific, severe, recently-surveyed incidence figure from a real
production region, with two (not one) distinct causal fungal species
identified in peer-reviewed 2023 research, alongside a broader review
naming additional diseases and a notably large count of insect-pest
species.** A 2021 survey at a nature reserve in Guangxi, China found
anthracnose incidence greater than 80% across a 2,500-hectare planting
area [6] — an extremely severe, specific, recently-measured incidence
figure at real production scale, exceeding cumin's own already-severe
35-80% Fusarium-wilt loss range at its own upper bound and matching it
as a floor here. A 2023 peer-reviewed study identifies two distinct
causal fungi on star anise in China, with one specifically noted as the
first report of that species infecting star anise in China [6] — a
genuinely dual-pathogen finding (not a single named causal organism),
with one of the two explicitly a new host record. A separate report
from a different location in the same region (September 2020) found
85% incidence and identifies a third causal species at a different
location [6] — this document treats the two surveys as geographically
distinct, reporting broadly similar severity with partially overlapping
but not identical causal-species identifications, not assuming they
describe the same outbreak. A 2024 Chinese-language review separately
reports 13 common diseases overall (anthracnose, brown spot, and "soot
disease" named as the main three) and more than 20 kinds of insect
pests, with leaf beetles, aphids, and scale insects named as the most
frequent [6] — a notably large total pest-and-disease count, though
this document does not independently verify the review's own
completeness or methodology beyond the search-snippet level. No root-
rot-specific finding was located despite a dedicated search, and no
pollination-mechanism or floral-structure finding was located despite a
dedicated search — a genuine gap relative to nutmeg's own (disputed)
and bay leaf's own (bee-pollinated, dioecious) pollination findings.

**Finding 8 — no listing specifically for star anise could be located
from a major pet-safety organization despite a dedicated, direct site-
restricted search, and the only pet-toxicity claims found are explicitly
judged weak and mutually contradictory by this document's own search
process.** A direct, site-restricted search for a star-anise or
*Illicium* listing from that organization returned no results [7] —
this document treats this as a genuine absence, not merely an unopened
search hit, since the search was explicitly site-restricted and
returned zero matching pages. One secondary pet-safety source attributes
a "toxic to cats" claim to that organization's own poison-control
program, but provides no direct link to an actual listing, and this
document treats that attribution as unverified [7] — this document does
not adopt this unverified attribution as a sourced finding, directly
paralleling the discipline already applied to this project's own
mustard-crop research. A separate vet-oriented source states that in
small, controlled amounts, star anise is "not considered toxic" to dogs
[7] — directly contradicting the first secondary source's own cats-
toxic claim in overall framing (though the two sources discuss
different animals), and a third source notes there simply isn't much
research available on star anise's effects on cats specifically [7] —
this document treats the overall pet-toxicity picture for the culinary
species specifically (as distinct from the already-well-documented
human-poisoning risk from Japanese star anise adulteration in Finding 5)
as genuinely unresolved, not merely under-sourced, and explicitly
declines to assert either a toxic or non-toxic finding for pets.
Separately, a European food-safety regulator has formally evaluated
star anise oil as a livestock feed additive, with species-specific
maximum dose limits: 1.6 mg/kg for sheep/goats, horses, and cats; 1.9
mg/kg for dogs [7] — a genuinely different, regulatory (not toxic-plant-
database) source, giving specific numeric dose limits, though this
document notes these are feed-additive safety-approval limits (i.e.,
doses the regulator has evaluated as acceptable for inclusion in
commercial feed), not toxicity thresholds in the sense a pet-safety
organization's own toxic-plant entries use elsewhere in this project's
research — this document does not conflate an approved-safe feed-
additive dose with a toxicity threshold.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree, long-lived — productive life
  ~80 years per one source (Finding 2).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per fruit crop, within a short
  seasonal harvest window (Finding 7) — not independently structured
  into a formal multi-window pattern this pass.
- **Climate:** Cooler tropics/subtropics at altitudes up to 2000m,
  average annual temperature 12-18°C; frost-sensitive, reported to make
  cultivation impossible in temperate zones by one source, though a
  separate source's own USDA zones 8-11 figure appears, on its face,
  inconsistent with that claim (Finding 4).
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — dried fruit pods are used whole or
  ground as a spice; not eaten raw/fresh.
- **Pollination:** Not independently sourced this pass — no dioecy claim
  or pollination-mechanism finding was located despite a dedicated
  search.
- **Yield / composition:** 8-12 kg fresh fruit per tree per season, with
  a specific fresh-to-dry conversion ratio (4-5 kg fresh yields ~1 kg
  dry fruit) (Finding 3). Separately, shikimic-acid extraction yield is
  reported 3-7% (Finding 6) — a genuinely different measurement
  dimension, not merged with the fresh-fruit yield figure.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  cooler-tropics/subtropics climate requirement.
- **Establishment:** Three parallel routes reported routine — seed,
  cuttings, and grafting (Finding 3) — more diverse than either bay
  leaf's (seed+cuttings) or nutmeg's (grafting-only, dioecy-mandated)
  own propagation findings. No dioecy constraint found or expected.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Long-lived evergreen tree, productive life ~80 years
  per one source; first fruiting 4-7 years, viable seed production
  delayed to 9-10 years (Finding 2).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Anthracnose confirmed the dominant disease,
  with incidence >80% in one 2021 survey across a 2,500 ha planting
  area, and 85% in a separate 2020 survey — the most severely quantified
  single-disease incidence figure this project has researched to date
  (Finding 7). Three distinct causal fungal species identified across
  two surveys, one a new host record for China. A broader review
  separately names brown spot and "soot disease" as additional major
  diseases, and reports more than 20 insect-pest species, with leaf
  beetles, aphids, and scale insects most frequent. No root-rot finding
  located despite a dedicated search.
- **Genome:** Genuinely extreme and only partially reconciled — flow-
  cytometry 2C-DNA values 24.5-27.9 pg across 29 taxa in the genus
  (Finding 1); a separate, not peer-reviewed preprint estimates *I.
  verum* specifically at ~14.98 Gb, ~89% repetitive sequence, described
  by its own authors as an "ultra-large angiosperm genome" — the two
  figures are not directly converted/reconciled this pass. Chromosome
  number cleanly and consistently sourced: diploid, 2n=28 (x=14),
  convergent across multiple tested *Illicium* species, with a genus-
  wide ancient paleotetraploid origin hypothesized (not settled) by the
  same source.
- **Cultivars:** No specific named commercial cultivar with sourced
  trait deltas was found this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — one fruit harvest per season within a
  short seasonal window; no leaf or dual-organ product split sourced for
  this crop. Single window per year, not independently characterized
  with specific calendar dates this pass.

## Hazards

Anthracnose confirmed the dominant disease, with incidence >80% in one
2021 survey across a 2,500 ha planting area, and 85% in a separate 2020
survey — the most severely quantified single-disease incidence figure
this project has researched to date (Finding 7). Three distinct
Colletotrichum species identified as causal agents across two surveys —
one a new host record for China. A broader review separately names
brown spot and "soot disease" as additional major diseases, and reports
more than 20 insect-pest species, with leaf beetles, aphids, and scale
insects most frequent. No root-rot finding located despite a dedicated
search.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — severe, specific, documented real-world human-
poisoning risk from Japanese star anise (*Illicium anisatum*)
adulteration: a named 2001 Netherlands outbreak, anisatin neurotoxin
causing seizures, Japanese star anise itself inedible (used only as
incense) (Finding 5) — the most severe documented real-world safety
incident for any crop this project has researched. This is a
human-health adulteration hazard, deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

Pet toxicity for the culinary species itself is genuinely unresolved —
no listing was located from a major pet-safety organization; secondary
sources are weak and mutually contradictory; a European food-safety
regulator has approved specific feed-additive dose limits for star
anise oil (1.6-1.9 mg/kg depending on species), but this is a feed-
additive safety approval, not a toxicity threshold (Finding 8).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed/cutting/graft establishment → fruit harvested once per
  season within a short window → dried (4-5 kg fresh yields ~1 kg dry
  fruit) → used whole or ground as a culinary spice. Separately,
  shikimic acid is extracted from the dried fruit (3-7% yield) as a
  pharmaceutical raw material (Finding 6).
