# Marjoram (*Origanum majorana*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-03

## Naming and scope

This covers *Origanum majorana* L., a separate species from oregano
(*Origanum vulgare*, covered separately elsewhere in this project's own
research), sharing the genus but not treated as the same crop. This
project's own oregano research was read in full as the closest sibling
— same genus, same general Mediterranean-Lamiaceae herb shape, but
marjoram's own hardiness and essential-oil chemistry turn out to be
genuinely different from oregano's, not merely a milder version of the
same plant.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — marjoram is reported genuinely more cold-sensitive than
every other Lamiaceae herb this project has researched, including
oregano itself, with sources disagreeing sharply on exactly how tender
it is.** Most sources place marjoram's reliable perennial range at USDA
zones 9-10, explicitly describing it as "somewhat tender, not frost
hardy," and note it is commonly grown as an annual outside those zones
[1] — a genuinely different cultivation shape from oregano's own
disputed hardiness range, none of which treated the plant as needing
annual replanting. Within zones 7-8, sources report a mixed, unreliable
outcome, with explicit winter-protection advice [1]. The disagreement is
sharper than a simple zone-number dispute: one source gives a specific
frost-hardiness figure of 23°F (-5°C), a UK source says it will stand "a
few degrees of frost for a couple of days," while a third source's own
range (zones 5-8) is hard to reconcile with the 9-10 figures elsewhere
[1] — this document does not attempt to resolve the disagreement,
flagging it as a genuinely wider spread (zones 5 through 10 depending on
source) than any other Lamiaceae herb's own hardiness dispute this
project has found. The common practical recommendation — container
growing with winter indoor overwintering outside the warmest zones — is
itself a specific cultivation-path finding not needed for rosemary,
sage, thyme, or oregano in this project's research.

**Finding 2 — marjoram's essential-oil chemistry is dominated by
terpinen-4-ol and trans-sabinene hydrate, a genuinely different
chemotype from oregano's own carvacrol-dominant chemistry despite the
shared genus, with a specific composition figure that itself fails an
international purity standard.** Two Polish field studies on a named
cultivar both name terpinen-4-ol and trans-sabinene hydrate as the
dominant essential-oil components [2] — a chemically distinct profile
from oregano's own carvacrol-centered chemistry, despite both species
belonging to *Origanum*. A 2022 glasshouse trial found terpinen-4-ol
ranging 22.63-36.72% of the oil, and explicitly states this range falls
outside the ISO standard's own limits of acceptability [2] — a sourced,
specific regulatory-compliance finding not paralleled by any other
spice/herb crop's own chemistry sourcing in this project's research.
Essential-oil content itself is reported at 1.7-2.2% in the named-
cultivar study, higher in full-bloom-harvested material than bud-stage
material, and higher at denser plant spacing — while fresh/dried herb
yield itself was highest at the widest spacing tested (40x40 cm) [2], a
genuine yield-vs-oil-content tradeoff across planting density, a more
specific agronomic tension than oregano's own single-direction harvest-
timing finding. A separate 2022 pot-trial study reports promising
productivity under high air temperature (16.7-36.6°C), 200 kg/ha
nitrogen, and low irrigation on sandy loam soil, though this document
flags the trial's own pot-based methodology as a weaker basis than a
field trial for direct yield transfer [2].

**Finding 3 — marjoram's own pollination biology was not independently
and specifically sourced this pass; the best available evidence is a
general Lamiaceae review that names marjoram only in passing alongside
oregano's own, more specifically studied, self-incompatibility
finding.** A 2000 review of medicinal Lamiaceae species reports
marjoram's flowers as protandrous, making true self-pollination within a
single flower unlikely, with geitonogamy (between flowers on the same
plant) and xenogamy (between plants) as the main fertilization routes;
the same review notes self-fertilization has nonetheless succeeded in
several Lamiaceae species, and that cross-species/cross-cultivar crosses
often yield less seed [3] — a genuinely weaker, more general sourcing
outcome than oregano's own dedicated self-incompatibility study, which
this document does not assume transfers directly to marjoram despite the
shared genus. The same review separately mentions marjoram alongside
thyme and oregano as having occasional pistillate (female-only) flowers
[3] — a floral-polymorphism finding shared across at least three
Lamiaceae herbs this project has researched, not independently
investigated further for marjoram specifically. Marjoram is separately
described by horticultural sources as a valuable nectar source for bees,
consistent with insect-pollination dependence, though none of those
sources address its actual breeding system [3].

**Finding 4 — marjoram's disease and pest profile is reported notably
milder than oregano's own, with multiple sources explicitly
characterizing the crop as "generally disease-free," though named
fungal pathogens still partially overlap with the broader Lamiaceae
pattern this project has already established.** A horticultural society
describes sweet marjoram as susceptible to aphids and red spider mite
but otherwise "generally disease-free" [4], and a gardening source
similarly calls fungal problems rare, naming powdery mildew, rust, and
botrytis (gray mold) only as occasional exceptions [4] — contrasted with
oregano's own longer, more actively-reported pest/disease list including
mint rust, which no source in this pass's own search set named for
marjoram specifically. Root rot in waterlogged or poorly-drained soil is
consistently reported across multiple sources as the main cultivation
hazard, the same general soil-drainage-driven hazard already established
for oregano and the broader Lamiaceae herb group this project has
researched [4]. A Slovak horticultural resource separately names
*Fusarium solani* and *Fusarium oxysporum* as marjoram diseases [4], and
a Canadian provincial extension page lists Phomopsis leaf/stem blight as
observed on the crop in that region specifically, alongside Pythium root
rot, leaf spots, powdery mildew, and rust as more general potential
concerns [4] — this document notes that extension page's own disease
list largely matches a companion *Origanum* source used for oregano's
own research and does not assume every named pathogen is independently
confirmed for marjoram without re-reading that source in full. A
dedicated Egyptian pest study on sweet marjoram was located but yielded
no disease results in this pass's own search depth.

**Finding 5 — a major pet-safety organization's own direct marjoram
listing differs from oregano's own in a specific, species-level way:
marjoram is listed toxic to dogs and cats but with no horse entry
found, unlike oregano's three-species listing.** That organization's own
page for *Origanum majorana* rates it toxic to dogs and cats, with
gastrointestinal irritants as the toxic principle and vomiting/diarrhea
as the clinical signs [5] — this document's own search did not locate a
horse entry for marjoram specifically, a genuine point of difference
from oregano's own listing (dogs, cats, and horses), though this
document does not assert marjoram is confirmed safe for horses on the
basis of an absent listing — absence of a finding is not evidence of
non-toxicity. A secondary pet-care source separately rates marjoram
"caution" for dogs while calling it toxic for cats, a claim that
conflicts with the authoritative organization's own dog rating and is
treated as unreliable against that organization's own page [5], the same
sourcing-hierarchy choice already applied to caraway's and cumin's own
conflicting secondary sources. One source separately notes oregano and
marjoram are commonly confused in pet-care content generally, a naming-
confusion risk directly paralleling this project's own caraway/cumin
naming mix-up [5].

**Finding 6 — marjoram's ancient Mediterranean history is well attested
but its claimed uses are genuinely hard to separate from oregano's own,
since both species have historically been called by the same or
overlapping names.** Several sources describe marjoram as a common
condiment and medicine in ancient Egypt, with one claim that Egyptians
used it in embalming and decorated mummies with marjoram wreaths [6] —
though that source covers oregano and marjoram together, so the finding
may not be specific to *O. majorana*. In ancient Greece, marjoram is
associated with Aphrodite, and fragrant garlands of the plant were
reportedly given to newlyweds and planted on graves [6], findings this
document treats as culturally attested but not independently cross-
verified against a primary classical source. One source itself flags the
core naming problem: oregano (*O. vulgare*) is also called "wild
marjoram," and ancient texts using the Latin/Greek root "origanum" may
refer to either plant [6] — meaning this document's own historical
findings above should be read as attested for the plant group broadly,
not confidently disambiguated to *O. majorana* specifically. No wild-
origin region more specific than "Mediterranean basin and southwest
Asia" was sourced with confidence this pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tender perennial, commonly grown as an annual
  outside USDA zones 9-10 — a genuinely different cultivation shape from
  oregano's own cold-hardy-perennial framing (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, sourced by a Polish cultivar
  trial's own two-cut (mid-July/end-August) schedule (Finding 2).
- **Climate:** USDA zone 9-10 for reliable perennial survival per most
  sources, with a genuinely wider and less-reconciled spread (zones
  5-10 depending on source) than any other Lamiaceae herb's own
  hardiness dispute this project has found (Finding 1). Commonly
  container-grown and overwintered indoors outside zone 9-10.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh and dried culinary herb.
- **Pollination:** Not required for vegetative propagation (cuttings/
  division); likely required for seed production by analogy to the
  general Lamiaceae floral-biology review, though not independently
  confirmed as a self-incompatibility finding specific to marjoram the
  way oregano's own study was (Finding 3).
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh herb yield 3,050-4,460 kg/ha depending
  on harvest date (Polish cultivar trial: mid-July vs. end-August cuts)
  (Finding 2). Essential-oil content 1.7-2.2% of dried herb, higher at
  full bloom and at denser spacing, while fresh/dried herb yield was
  highest at the widest spacing tested (40x40 cm) — a genuine yield-vs-
  oil-content tradeoff across planting density. Terpinen-4-ol content
  22.63-36.72% of the oil in one glasshouse trial, explicitly outside
  ISO acceptability limits.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance and waterlogging tolerance not
  independently quantified this pass beyond the consistently reported
  root-rot risk in waterlogged/poorly-drained soil (Finding 4). No
  crop-coefficient figure sourced.
- **Establishment:** Seed or vegetative (stem cuttings/division), both
  viable — seed typically started indoors about 6 weeks before the last
  frost; germination reported about a week slower than oregano's own.
  Stem cuttings taken in spring (before flowering) or autumn (then
  overwintered indoors in cold climates) (Finding 1 basis).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** A 2022 pot trial reports promising productivity under 200
  kg/ha nitrogen, high air temperature, and low irrigation on sandy loam
  soil, though this document flags the pot-based methodology as a
  weaker basis than a field trial.
- **Stand life:** Tender perennial, commonly grown as an annual outside
  USDA zones 9-10 — a genuinely shorter practical stand life than every
  other Lamiaceae herb this project has researched (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Aphids and red spider mite are the named
  pests per a horticultural society; powdery mildew, rust, and botrytis
  named as occasional, rare fungal exceptions by one source, with
  *Fusarium solani*/*F. oxysporum* and Phomopsis leaf/stem blight named
  by others, the latter specifically observed (not merely potential) in
  one Canadian province (Finding 4) — genuinely milder than oregano's
  own disease/pest profile, which additionally includes mint rust, not
  found for marjoram this pass. Root rot in waterlogged/poorly-drained
  soil is the consistently reported main cultivation hazard.
- **Cultivars:** One named cultivar (styled two ways across two
  companion Polish studies) is the only named cultivar sourced this
  pass, used in both the harvest-timing and plant-density field trials
  (Finding 2). No cultivar-level chemistry delta (comparable to
  oregano's own named-cultivar carvacrol figures) was sourced for
  marjoram.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — cut-and-come-again foliage harvest, no
  separate seed-harvest stage independently sourced this pass. Single
  window per planting season; a Polish cultivar trial's own two-cut
  (mid-July/end-August) schedule is a within-season cut-and-come-again
  pattern, not separate flowering cohorts.
- **Storage:** Dried-leaf shelf life conflicts across sources (6 months
  to 1-3 years; one supplier gives "a year or longer") — not reconciled,
  a looser consumer/retail framing similar to caraway's own contested
  storage claims.

## Hazards

Root rot in waterlogged/poorly-drained soil is the consistently reported
main cultivation hazard (Finding 4). Aphids and red spider mite are the
named pests; powdery mildew, rust, and botrytis named as occasional,
rare fungal exceptions by one source, with *Fusarium solani*/*F.
oxysporum* and Phomopsis leaf/stem blight named by others — genuinely
milder than oregano's own disease/pest profile, which additionally
includes mint rust, not found for marjoram this pass.

Toxic to dogs and cats (gastrointestinal irritants; vomiting/diarrhea)
per a major pet-safety organization's own listing; no horse entry found,
a point of difference from oregano's three-species listing, though
absence of a finding is not evidence of safety (Finding 5).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh or dried herb harvested cut-and-come-again (two cuts
  per season: mid-July and end-August in one study) → used fresh or
  dried, or distilled into essential oil (terpinen-4-ol/trans-sabinene-
  hydrate dominant, a different chemotype from oregano's carvacrol-
  dominant oil).

## Open research questions

- Marjoram's own USDA hardiness range is disputed across a genuinely
  wider spread (zones 5-10 depending on source) than any other
  Lamiaceae herb's own hardiness dispute this project has found — not
  reconciled.
- A 2022 pot-trial nitrogen/temperature/irrigation productivity finding
  was not independently verified against a field trial — pot-based
  methodology is flagged as a weaker basis for direct yield transfer.
- No dedicated self-incompatibility or insect-pollination-dependence
  study specific to *O. majorana* was found — the sourced pollination
  findings are from a general Lamiaceae review, not species-specific
  like oregano's own study.
- Mint rust, oregano's own dominant disease in this project's research,
  was not found named for marjoram in this pass's own search set — not
  assumed absent, simply not sourced either way.
- A Canadian provincial extension page's own marjoram disease list
  largely overlaps with a companion *Origanum* source already used for
  oregano's own research — not independently re-read in full to confirm
  every named pathogen is specific to marjoram rather than carried over
  from the genus generally.
- A dedicated Egyptian pest study on sweet marjoram yielded no disease
  results in this pass's own search depth.
- No horse-toxicity entry for marjoram was found from the major pet-
  safety organization consulted — this is not treated as evidence of
  safety for horses, only as an absent finding, a distinction from
  oregano's own confirmed three-species listing.
- The ancient-Egyptian embalming/mummy-wreath claim is sourced from
  material covering oregano and marjoram together — may not be specific
  to *O. majorana*.
- No wild-origin region more specific than "Mediterranean basin and
  southwest Asia" was sourced with confidence.
- Grow-days figures were not independently sourced this pass.
- kcalPerKg/proteinGPerKg were not independently sourced this pass.
- No crop-coefficient or quantified drought/waterlogging-tolerance
  rating was sourced this pass.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any operation this
  pass.
- Dried-leaf shelf-life claims conflict across sources (6 months to 1-3
  years) — not reconciled.
- No sourced finding this pass on whether seed-grown marjoram plants
  match the parent's own chemistry — not assumed to carry over from
  oregano's own explicit finding without a dedicated source.
- No isolation-distance or seed-yield-per-hectare figure was sourced for
  marjoram's own seed route.
- No cultivar-level chemistry delta (comparable to oregano's own named-
  cultivar carvacrol figures) was sourced for marjoram.
- Soil pH optimum was not independently sourced this pass.
- Light requirement was not independently sourced this pass.
- Controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not independently
  assessed this pass — the crop's own tender-perennial, commonly-
  container-grown cultivation shape is flagged as a plausible advantage
  over the cold-hardy Lamiaceae herbs, not independently confirmed.
- No chromosome number or genome-size figure specific to *O. majorana*
  was found — the closest available figures are for *O. vulgare* and
  relatives and are not assumed to transfer across species.
