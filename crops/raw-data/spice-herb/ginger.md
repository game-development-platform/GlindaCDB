# Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

This covers *Zingiber officinale*. This project's own turmeric research
is the closest sibling — both are Zingiberaceae rhizome geophytes,
cultivated as annuals, with a broadly similar boil/dry curing-adjacent
processing chain and the same general "vegetatively propagated,
fertility genuinely disputed rather than cleanly resolved" pattern —
but, as Finding 1 below establishes, the specific cytogenetic story
differs meaningfully between the two crops, and this document is careful
not to assume turmeric's triploid-sterility framing simply transfers to
ginger's diploid one.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — ginger is a genuinely fertile diploid (not a sterile
triploid like turmeric), yet still fails to set seed in practice due to
specific, well-characterized meiotic irregularities — a meaningfully
different cytogenetic story from its closest sibling crop, not simply a
repeat of it.** The sequenced cultivar used in one key genome study is
2n = 2x = 22, and 2n=22 is the most commonly reported chromosome count
across modern studies, though older literature also reports 2n=24 and
2n=66, and some named variety types (giant ginger, small ginger) show
2n=30, with red ginger at 2n=22 [5]. This is not the triploid (2n=63)
picture this project found for turmeric — ginger's reproductive failure
has a different cause. Ginger does flower under some growing
conditions, but seed set is the specific, well-documented problem: one
study found no seed set under either selfing or cross-pollination, with
pollen sterility measured at 60.80%; a separate Kerala study similarly
found 60-84% pollen sterility, linking it to meiotic irregularities and
explicitly concluding that the absence of fruit and seed set "does not
seem to be controlled by a single factor" [5]. The specific cytogenetic
mechanism proposed is non-homology among bivalents during meiosis, with
irregular segregation of genomic complements, and one study separately
describes the genome as "highly unstable," producing chromosome bridges
and laggards that form micronuclei during meiosis [5]. Colchicine-
induced tetraploids (2n = 4x = 44) have been successfully produced from
diploid ginger in breeding work, with chromosome counts confirming
successful polyploidization in two of the resulting lines —
demonstrating that ginger's reproductive barrier is a specific meiotic-
irregularity problem at its natural ploidy level, not an inherent
inability to tolerate altered chromosome numbers at all [5]. No source
opened this pass described ginger as a "sterile triploid," and this
document explicitly avoids importing that framing from the turmeric
research by analogy.

**Finding 2 — genome-size estimates for ginger are comparatively well-
converged across independent studies, a clear contrast with both
turmeric's and saffron's own flagged multi-way genome-size disagreements
this project has found elsewhere.** Multiple independent estimates
cluster tightly around 1.6 Gb per haploid genome — one genome assembly
gives approximately 1.59 Gb (3.6% heterozygosity), another gives
approximately 1.6 Gb (described as "similar to the previously estimated
size" of ~1.57 Gb), and a flow-cytometry-based table gives a 1Cx value
of 1.800 pg (1,760.40 Mbp) [5]. While these three figures are not
identical, their spread (roughly 1.57-1.80 Gb) is far narrower, both in
absolute terms and as a fraction of the total value, than turmeric's own
three-way 1.02-1.33 Gbp disagreement or saffron's multi-Gb-scale spread
— ginger's genome is, by the sourcing available this pass, one of the
more cleanly-characterized polyploid-adjacent spice-crop genomes this
project has researched.

**Finding 3 — ginger's climate and soil requirements are reasonably
well-agreed across Indian government extension and peer-reviewed
sources, with one genuinely useful trait distinguishing it from
turmeric: a documented tolerance for partial shade.** Optimal
temperature is given as 19-28°C by an agricultural-research-institute
guide, broadly consistent with a review's 19-28°C "favourable range"
bounded by a 13°C base and a 32°C day/27°C night upper limit [1]. Frost
is an absolute limit — ginger cannot tolerate frost or freezing
temperatures at all [1]. Rainfall figures are reasonably consistent:
1,500-3,000mm/year (well-distributed across 8-10 months) by one review,
versus a narrower 1,300-1,500mm "during its crop cycle" figure from the
institute's own guide — these may describe different measurement windows
(full year vs. crop-cycle-specific) rather than genuinely conflicting
[1]. Altitude range is cultivated up to 1,500m, with an optimum
specifically narrower at 300-900m [1]. Relative humidity in ginger-
growing regions runs 70-90% [1]. Soil guidance favors well-drained sandy
loam, clay loam, red loam, or lateritic loam, with one source giving a
preferred pH of 6.0-6.5 [1]. Waterlogging is explicitly flagged as
harmful at any time during cultivation, not just during specific growth
stages, with direct rotting risk [1] — the same "drainage matters
acutely" pattern this project has found for both turmeric and saffron.
One source specifically notes ginger is an "exhausting crop," making
continuous same-plot cultivation undesirable — a soil-fertility-
depletion finding this project has not similarly found for turmeric
[1]. Distinctively, ginger is described as partially shade-tolerant,
grown successfully in semi-shaded conditions [1] — turmeric's own
research found no comparable shade-tolerance finding, making this a
genuine point of difference between the two sibling crops rather than a
shared trait.

**Finding 4 — planting density and seed-rhizome-size effects on yield
are reasonably well-sourced from multiple independent regional trials,
with density effects that are large and directionally consistent across
very different growing regions.** A Nigerian thesis found rhizome yield
at 500,000 plants/ha was 63% higher than at 166,666 plants/ha — a
substantial density effect [2]. A Peruvian ("kion") study recommended
0.40m × 0.50m spacing for highest yield (~20,216 kg/ha), while a tighter
0.30m × 0.50m spacing was instead recommended specifically for taller
plants with good fresh/dry rhizome weight — two different optimization
targets from the same study [2]. General fresh-yield figures cluster in
a plausible 15-25 t/ha range per a Nigerian production guide, with an
Indian trial of an improved named cultivar under integrated nutrient
management reporting 22,754 kg/ha fresh — within that general range, not
an outlier [2]. A Karnataka planting-technique study found whole seed
rhizomes of 20-25g gave the highest yield per hectare (29.41 t/ha) and
the highest dry recovery (19.67%) simultaneously — a genuinely useful
finding where both optimization targets point the same direction,
unlike several other density/size trade-offs this project has recorded
elsewhere [2]. Planting date mattered dramatically in the same Karnataka
trial: May planting gave 17.09 t/ha versus only 4.98 t/ha for August
planting — more than a 3x yield difference attributable to planting
timing alone, among the largest single-variable yield effects this
project has recorded in its spice/herb research [2]. Weed competition is
a serious, quantified threat: uncontrolled weed growth can reduce yield
by up to 76%, with a critical weeding window at 8-16 weeks after
planting [2].

**Finding 5 — ginger's disease complex overlaps substantially with
turmeric's (shared genus-level vulnerability to rhizome rot), but adds
a second, distinctly named bacterial disease this project has not found
in its turmeric research, plus a specifically quantified heat-treatment
protocol for seed-rhizome disinfection.** The major diseases are soft
rot (*Pythium* species), bacterial wilt (*Ralstonia solanacearum*),
yellows (*Fusarium*), and nematode damage (*Pratylenchus coffeae*),
alongside *Phyllosticta* leaf spot and storage rots [8]. Bacterial wilt
is a disease category this project has not found in its turmeric
research (which found only *Pythium* rot and a separate leaf-blotch
disease) — a genuine point of difference between these two sibling
rhizome crops' disease profiles, not merely a naming variation of the
same threat [8]. As with turmeric, nematode infestation is specifically
noted to aggravate rhizome rot severity, and infected seed rhizomes are
named as the primary disease-spread vector, making disease-free seed
selection the foundational management practice [8]. Several specific,
quantified management protocols were found: hot-water seed-rhizome
treatment at 51°C for 10 minutes followed by 1% Bordeaux mixture for 15
minutes (one source) or 50°C for 10 minutes specifically against
nematodes (a separate source) [8]; soil solarization raising soil
temperature up to 12.2°C above ambient, described as among the better
methods against both bacterial wilt and soft rot [8]; raised beds (30cm
height, 1m width) for drainage [8]; and biological controls
(*Trichoderma*, *Pseudomonas fluorescens*) alongside chemical options
(streptocycline specifically for bacterial wilt; a combination fungicide
evaluated in a 2011 trial against the *Pythium*/*Fusarium*/*Ralstonia*
complex together) [8]. Mustard oil cake soil application (40kg/ha before
sowing) is separately recommended specifically against nematodes [8].

**Finding 6 — ginger's pungency chemistry is well-characterized at the
compound level, with a genuinely sourced fresh-vs-dried flavor-chemistry
difference, though the specific optimal drying-temperature claims found
this pass come from a lower-authority source and are flagged
accordingly.** Fresh ginger's characteristic bite comes primarily from
gingerols, phenolic compounds; a foundational 1969 re-examination
established that shogaols and zingerone form from gingerol specifically
under the action of heat or alkali — meaning dried ginger's flavor
profile is chemically distinct from fresh ginger's by a well-understood
transformation mechanism, not merely a concentration difference from
moisture loss [6]. A 2017-era review adds that shogaols are reported to
be more pungent than the gingerols they derive from, though no specific
potency-ratio figure was found this pass [6]. One drying study
specifically tracked 6-gingerol and 6-shogaol levels through the sliced-
ginger drying process, finding that higher drying temperatures produced
lower final moisture content in shorter times [6]. A separate, lower-
authority product-catalogue source (flagged as garbled/unverified)
claims hot-air drying near 150°C for about 6 hours maximizes 6-shogaol
content, with higher temperatures instead degrading it, and claims sun-
or freeze-drying better preserves the original gingerols rather than
converting them — these specific temperature/time figures are not
independently corroborated by a higher-authority source this pass and
are recorded with that caveat.

**Finding 7 — ginger's interaction with the anticoagulant warfarin is a
genuinely disputed, mixed-evidence hazard.** A proposed platelet-
aggregation-inhibition mechanism (in-vitro evidence) and a separate in-
vitro finding that ginger inhibits CYP1A2 (not yet reported in humans)
together motivate concern, but the strongest available human evidence
is a single 2007 prospective observational study of 171 warfarin
patients, finding ginger independently associated with increased self-
reported bleeding risk (odds ratio 3.20) — though that same study found
no significant interaction specifically with supratherapeutic INR, and
did not record the quantity or preparation of ginger consumed by its
subjects [7]. A single case report (INR of 7 in a 76-year-old woman)
loosely implicated ginger without establishing the link, an earlier
(2000) review concluded evidence was simply lacking for a garlic-or-
ginger/warfarin interaction at all, and pharmacokinetic studies in
healthy subjects found no effect of ginger on INR or on warfarin's own
pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics [7]. This mixed-evidence shape
closely parallels the kind of disputed food-product/warfarin
interaction this project has found for other crops, despite ginger's
differing proposed mechanism.

**Finding 8 — ginger's trade and medicinal history is deep and well-
attested at a high level, though several specific claims (precise
ancient dates, a Marco Polo connection explicitly sought this pass)
were either unverifiable or simply absent from the sources opened.**
Ginger's precise geographic origin is described as genuinely uncertain
even in otherwise-confident sources, though Oceanic/Southeast Asia is
the most commonly named region [10]. It was among the earliest spices
to move along long-distance trade routes, with one account describing
the trade as originally Arabic-controlled before Roman demand
(attributed to Emperor Augustus) broke that monopoly — this specific
narrative detail comes from a single lower-authority source and was not
independently cross-verified [10]. Medicinal use is attested by the
Greek physician-pharmacologist Pedanius Dioscorides (c. 40-90 CE), and
separately, Arabian physicians during the Islamic Golden Age are
credited with documenting over 50 distinct medicinal uses, including
treatments for nausea, arthritis, and respiratory conditions — the
nausea/antiemetic application specifically connects to ginger's modern,
better-evidenced use as an antiemetic, though this document did not
independently research modern antiemetic clinical evidence this pass
[10]. Chinese textual attestation is dated inconsistently across
sources: one gives 400 BCE, while a specific named text is separately
dated to the first century CE by another source — these are not
reconciled [10]. A concrete medieval European record survives: King
Henry III's apothecary accounts for 1242-43 record a ginger conserve
given to the king for medicinal reasons [10]. No source opened this pass
mentioned Marco Polo specifically in connection with ginger, despite
being explicitly searched for — this document records that absence
rather than asserting a connection exists elsewhere. No reliable source
on ginger's specific storage/shelf-life duration was found this pass;
the sources opened only offered vague characterizations ("remarkable
preservative qualities" vs. a separate claim of "perishable nature,"
genuinely contradictory framings from different sources) without a
quantified figure for either the fresh or dried product.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical perennial rhizome, cultivated as an
  annual, same general pattern as turmeric. Single harvest per planting
  cycle — rhizomes lifted once at the end of the annual growing cycle.
- **Climate:** Tropical, warm-humid, partial-shade-tolerant — 19-28°C
  optimal (13-32°C tolerance range), no frost tolerance at all,
  1,500-3,000mm rainfall, 70-90% relative humidity, sea level to 1,500m
  (optimum 300-900m) (Finding 3) — the partial-shade tolerance
  specifically distinguishes this crop from turmeric, which had no
  comparable finding.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — unlike turmeric, fresh ginger rhizome
  is a standard, widely-used raw/lightly-processed culinary form (fresh
  ginger, pickled ginger, ginger paste) in addition to the dried/ground
  product — a genuine, sourced structural difference from turmeric,
  since turmeric's flavor compound doesn't meaningfully exist until
  processing while ginger's gingerol-based pungency is already present
  fresh.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable — propagation is 100% vegetative in
  commercial practice. Ginger is fertile at the chromosomal level
  (unlike turmeric's genuine triploid-sterility question) but fails to
  set seed in practice due to meiotic irregularities with pollen
  sterility of 60-84% (Finding 1); no source describes seed propagation
  as commercial practice.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 15,000-25,000 kg/ha fresh rhizome
  (general range across multiple regional sources). Exceptional yield
  ~29,410 kg/ha (Karnataka trial, 20-25g seed-piece size, also the
  highest-dry-recovery treatment in the same trial). Planting density
  and seed-piece size both show large, directionally consistent effects
  across independent regional trials (Nigeria, Peru, India); planting
  date showed the single largest effect found this pass (>3x yield
  difference, Finding 4) — timing may matter more than any other single
  sourced variable for this crop.
- **Water use:** No formal crop-coefficient curve was found. Drought
  tolerance is estimated low from the rainfall requirement and explicit
  no-waterlogging-at-any-time finding, not an independently sourced
  drought-tolerance figure itself. Waterlogging tolerance is low —
  waterlogging is explicitly flagged as harmful at any time during the
  crop cycle, with direct rotting risk; soft rot (*Pythium*) is the
  dominant disease and is wet-soil-favored (Finding 3/5) — the same
  acute drainage-sensitivity pattern this project has found for
  turmeric and saffron.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative rhizome division — the standard,
  effectively sole commercial propagation method (Finding 1); unlike
  turmeric, no modern seedling-based rapid-multiplication efficiency
  technique was found for ginger this pass — a genuine sourcing gap
  relative to turmeric's own sourced 4-5x seed-material-reduction
  finding. Seed rhizomes planted in raised beds for drainage; planting
  date is a dramatically sourced-significant variable (>3x yield
  difference between May and August planting in one trial).
- **Labor:** Weed control is a quantified, serious labor requirement —
  uncontrolled weeds can cut yield up to 76%, with a critical 8-16-week
  post-planting weeding window (Finding 4); no per-hectare labor-hour
  figure was sourced for weeding, harvesting, or any post-harvest
  operation. Seed-rhizome heat treatment (hot water, 51°C/10min or
  50°C/10min depending on target pathogen, followed by Bordeaux mixture)
  is a named, quantified pre-planting operation (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** pH optimal 6.0-6.5 (one source); min/max not independently
  sourced beyond that range. Planted in raised beds for drainage,
  similar to turmeric; specific planting depth not independently
  sourced.
- **Stand life:** Cultivated as an annual; no reconciled total-cycle-
  length figure was found — the same general gap this project has
  flagged for turmeric's own "duration class" sourcing problem.
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Pythium* soft rot, *Ralstonia solanacearum*
  bacterial wilt, *Fusarium* yellows, *Pratylenchus coffeae* nematode
  damage, and *Phyllosticta* leaf spot (Finding 5).
- **Genome:** Comparatively well-converged across independent studies
  (~1.57-1.80 Gb haploid), a clear contrast with turmeric's and
  saffron's own flagged multi-way disagreements (Finding 2). Chromosome
  number (2n=22 for the main sequenced/typical form) is also
  comparatively well-agreed, though named variety types show real
  variation (2n=24, 30, 66 reported across sources/varieties) (Finding
  1).
- **Cultivars:** One named Indian improved cultivar, with a sourced
  yield figure under integrated nutrient management (Finding 4), and
  named Nigerian/giant/small/red ginger variety types (differing in
  reported chromosome count per Finding 1) are the only named cultivar-
  level distinctions found this pass — a similar sourcing gap to
  turmeric's own cultivar finding.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — the whole rhizome clump is lifted once at
  the end of the growing cycle, same pattern as turmeric. Single window
  — one annual harvest per planting cycle; no multi-flush pattern
  sourced.
- **Storage:** Dry recovery figure of 19.67% specifically sourced from
  one Karnataka trial (the same trial that found this seed-piece-size
  treatment gave the highest yield too, Finding 4) — a single precise
  figure, in contrast to turmeric's own three-way-disputed 15-30% range.
  No quantified storage/shelf-life figure was found for either fresh or
  dried product (Finding 8).

## Hazards

Soft rot (*Pythium* spp.), bacterial wilt (*Ralstonia solanacearum*) —
a disease this project has not found in its turmeric research —
yellows (*Fusarium*), and nematode damage (*Pratylenchus coffeae*), with
nematode infestation specifically aggravating rhizome rot severity
(Finding 5).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — ginger's disputed, mixed-evidence interaction
with the anticoagulant warfarin (Finding 7) is a genuine human-health
hazard finding that is deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed rhizomes planted (raised beds, drainage) → grown out
  through the season → rhizome clump lifted once at season's end →
  used fresh, or dried/ground, or distilled into essential oil. Fresh-
  vs-dried product chemistry differs: drying converts gingerols to
  shogaols and zingerone under heat.

## Open research questions

- Specific optimal-drying-temperature claims (150°C for ~6 hours
  maximizing 6-shogaol) come from a single lower-authority, partially-
  garbled product-catalogue source and were not independently
  corroborated.
- Ginger's modern antiemetic clinical evidence was not independently
  researched this pass — the historical Islamic Golden Age nausea-
  treatment reference connects suggestively to this well-known modern
  use, but this document did not pursue the modern clinical literature.
- No source opened this pass mentioned Marco Polo in connection with
  ginger, despite being explicitly searched for — recorded as a genuine
  absence from the sources opened, not as evidence no such connection
  exists in better sources.
- No quantified storage/shelf-life figure was found for either fresh or
  dried ginger, and the characterizations found were genuinely
  contradictory ("remarkable preservative qualities" vs. "perishable
  nature") rather than merely absent.
- No modern seedling-based rapid-multiplication efficiency technique
  was found for ginger, in contrast to turmeric's own sourced 4-5x
  seed-material-reduction finding from the same general research
  context (Indian agricultural institutes).
- No per-hectare labor-hour figure was sourced for weeding, harvesting,
  or any post-harvest operation — only the weed-loss-percentage and
  weeding-window figures were sourced, not an actual labor-time cost.
- Total growing-cycle length is not reconciled to a specific figure by
  any source opened this pass.
- No named cultivar with a fuller sourced trait-delta profile (beyond
  one named cultivar's yield figure and the chromosome-count variation
  across named variety types) was found.
- No soil-chemistry (boron, chloride, micronutrient, heavy-metal)
  figures sourced beyond a single pH range.
- Precise ancient-history dating conflicts are unreconciled: Chinese
  textual attestation dated to both 400 BCE and "first century CE" (for
  a specific named text) across different sources, and the Arabic-
  trade-monopoly/Roman-Augustus narrative detail comes from a single
  lower-authority source not independently cross-verified.
- No source opened this pass described a specific religious/ritual role
  for ginger comparable to turmeric's deep ritual significance in some
  traditions — a genuine, notable absence relative to its closest
  sibling crop, despite ginger's otherwise deep and well-attested
  medicinal history.

## Estimates and derivations

A growing-cycle length of 240-300 days (roughly 8-10 months) was chosen
consistent with the general "well-distributed rainfall across 8-10
months" framing found in Finding 3's own climate sourcing, treating that
rainfall-distribution window as a loose proxy for the growing-season
length itself, since no source opened this pass gave a single reconciled
total-growing-cycle-length figure for ginger (similar to a gap this
project has found for turmeric). A dedicated agricultural-institute
variety-release document giving exact day-counts would replace this
estimate; plausible range 210-330 days.
