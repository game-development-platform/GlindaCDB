# Allspice (*Pimenta dioica*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-03

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Pimenta dioica* (L.) Merr., commonly called allspice
or pimento/pimenta — both names refer to the same species, and no
conflicting second-plant claim under either name was found this pass
(though this is not claimed as an exhaustive look-alike check). No close
sibling in this project's own research shares this species' genus or
family (Myrtaceae); the closest structural comparisons are other
dioecy-and-disputed-pollination tree-crop research this project has done.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the species name itself asserts dioecy ("dioica"), and one
historical source gives a specific cultivation practice built on that
premise (alternate-row male trees plus honeybee colonies for pollen
transfer), but a separate, more recent database source instead describes
the species as hermaphrodite and insect-pollinated, with a third source
(a government fact sheet) stating the pollination mechanism is simply not
known.** A 1960s Jamaican agronomic study recommended planting or
budding male trees as alternate trees in alternate rows specifically to
pollinate the bearing (female) trees, and separately recommended placing
honeybee colonies in the plantings to transfer pollen to receptive
stigmas [1] — a specific, actionable cultivation practice built directly
on an assumed dioecious reproductive system, consistent with the
species' own binomial name. A plant database instead describes the
species as hermaphrodite, with insect pollination [1] — directly
conflicting with the dioecy premise underlying that cultivation advice.
A government fact sheet separately states pollination is simply not
known for this species [1] — a third, genuinely different position
(neither affirming dioecy nor hermaphroditism, just declining to
characterize the mechanism at all). This is one of the more structurally
severe reproductive-system-level disputes this project has encountered
in its spice/herb tree-crop research — notably, if the hermaphrodite
claim is correct, the species' own Latin binomial ("dioica") would itself
be a historical misnomer, a possibility not resolved here but flagged
explicitly.

**Finding 2 — propagation is reported genuinely difficult from seed,
with a claim that many historical attempts to propagate from seed were
reported and all failed, while a separate government fact sheet states
the opposite.** One encyclopedia-style source states that many attempts
at growing allspice from seed were reported, and all failed [2] — a
strong, specific claim of historical propagation difficulty, not merely
slowness or inefficiency. A government fact sheet instead states the
species is propagated from seed [2] — directly conflicting with the
"all failed" claim, without this document being able to determine which
source is more current or reliable at the level consulted. The
alternate-row male-tree recommendation (Finding 1) implies budding/
grafting is used at least for establishing specific male pollinator
trees in a known ratio, though this is not extended here into a
confirmed general-propagation-method finding for the species as a whole,
since that context is specifically about sex-ratio management, not
general nursery practice.

**Finding 3 — yield figures scatter across a wide range with a specific,
sourced per-tree value from two independent measurement framings, plus a
separate, even higher historical outlier figure, and all located yield
data is per-tree, not per-hectare.** An agricultural database entry
reports young trees aged 10-15 years may give 23-60 kg of green berries
in a good year, with an average of about 5 kg per tree [3] — a specific
age-banded range alongside a separate, much lower "average" figure, a
genuinely wide spread (good-year maximum over 10x the stated average). A
separate, older source states a bearing tree may produce 20 lb of
berries per year, with yields of 150 lb for one tree having been recorded
[3] — a specific historical outlier figure (roughly 68 kg, broadly
consistent with the database entry's own upper "good year" bound of 60
kg, though not identical; the two figures are not merged into one
reconciled number since they come from different sources using different
units and framings). No per-hectare planting-density-based yield figure
was located despite a dedicated search — a genuine absence relative to
some other tree crops this project has researched in this family.

**Finding 4 — the species is cleanly and consistently reported diploid
(2n=22) across two independent sources, with a separate, broader 2008
flow-cytometry study of Neotropical Myrtaceae finding 28 species diploid
at the same chromosome count, though that broader study's own snippet
does not explicitly name this species among its 28 tested species.** A
handbook entry and a chromosome-count database (under the synonym
*Pimenta officinalis* Lindl.) both independently report 2n=22 [4] — a
genuinely convergent, consistent chromosome-count finding. A separate
2008 flow-cytometry study of Neotropical Myrtaceae reports that 28
tested species were found diploid at 2n=2x=22 [4] — broadly consistent
with, though not claimed to directly confirm, the handbook/database
finding specifically for this species, since the available material did
not explicitly list it among the 28 tested. No nuclear genome-size (2C
or 1C) value was located for this species itself despite checking both
the accepted name and its synonym [4] — a value from a different,
similarly-named but unrelated species was deliberately not substituted
after an initial search hit for that unrelated genus was correctly
excluded.

**Finding 5 — rust and anthracnose are confirmed the dominant diseases
by a 2025 peer-reviewed phytopathology review specifically covering
Mexican production, with biological control named as the paper's own
focus, while pest pressure is separately reported genuinely low.** A
2025 peer-reviewed review states that Mexican allspice production
specifically is threatened by rust and anthracnose, with the paper's own
stated focus on antagonistic microorganisms as a control strategy [5] —
no specific incidence percentage was located for either disease. A
separate extension source separately names rust as the main disease
concern, consistent with the review, and states fruit flies, scale and
moths only rarely affect the crop, depending on region [5] — a
genuinely milder, more occasional pest picture than most other tree
crops this project has researched; the disease claim is corroborated by
the peer-reviewed review, but the pest-rarity claim is not independently
corroborated by a second source.

**Finding 6 — postharvest drying is specifically quantified by two
sourced findings: a target moisture threshold (below 12%) from a trade-
standards sheet, and a separate, older source's own weight-loss figure
(roughly one-third of fresh weight lost during drying).** A trade-
standards sheet states storage is good when moisture is below 12%, and
that excess moisture harms aroma, with poorly-dried berries additionally
able to lose weight through continued evaporation in storage [6] — a
specific, actionable moisture target. A separate, older source states
drying removes roughly one-third of the fresh berry's weight [6] — a
specific, quantified fresh-to-dry weight-loss figure (fresh:dry roughly
3:2 by this reading), though this figure is not sourced with the same
rigor (no named author, institution, or publication year located) as
some other fresh-to-dry ratios this project has sourced elsewhere, so it
is treated as usable but comparatively weaker-sourced.

**Finding 7 — eugenol is confirmed the compound of concern for pet
toxicity by multiple secondary, non-veterinary sources, with a specific,
if unverified, dose claim, and a separately sourced, more authoritative
human-side reference explicitly stating the species is not generally
associated with toxicity except at high concentrations.** Several
pet-care blog sources name eugenol as the compound of concern, with one
specifically claiming the dried berries and ground allspice can cause
liver toxicity in dogs via eugenol, though this specific claim comes
from a single, non-veterinary blog source and is not independently
corroborated [7]. A separate pet-care source gives a specific, though
similarly unverified, dose claim: significant toxicity is unlikely with
small amounts, specifically less than 1/4 teaspoon [7] — recorded here
explicitly as unverified pet-blog-level material, not a sourced
veterinary threshold. Cat-specific evidence is separately described as
thin, with one source instead drawing an analogy to clove oil (also
eugenol-rich) causing liver toxicity, vomiting, seizures, and staggering
in cats — this analogy is not adopted here as a direct allspice finding,
since it is explicitly drawn by the secondary source itself, not a
direct allspice-specific claim. A more authoritative human-side
reference states allspice is not generally associated with toxicity, but
that eugenol can be toxic in high concentrations [7] — treated here as
the most reliable single toxicity-framing statement located this pass,
though it is a human-health, not pet-specific, reference. Essential-oil
production is separately confirmed: berry oil is obtained by steam
distillation of the dried, fully grown but unripe, berry-like fruits,
with leaf oil reported as a separate product [7] — a genuinely organ-
level product split (berry oil vs. leaf oil), though no sourced
composition difference between the two oils was found this pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree. No specific time-to-ultimate-
  height or productive-life figure was sourced this pass.
- **Climate:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Food status:** Not eaten fresh; berries are picked unripe/green and
  dried before use as a spice.
- **Pollination:** Genuinely disputed whether required at all (Finding
  1) — one source assumes a dioecious system requiring cross-
  pollination (with a specific male:female planting ratio and honeybee
  colonies); a second describes the species as hermaphrodite and insect-
  pollinated (self-fertile by implication, though not stated
  explicitly); a third says the mechanism is simply not known.
- **Yield / composition:** Per-tree only — 23-60 kg green berries/tree
  in a good year (trees aged 10-15 years), average ~5 kg/tree per a
  separate framing within the same source; a separate historical source
  gives 20 lb/year typical, with a 150 lb outlier recorded for one tree
  (Finding 3). No per-hectare figure located despite a dedicated search.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Propagation method genuinely disputed (Finding 2) —
  one source states many historical seed-propagation attempts all
  failed; a second states the species is propagated from seed; budding/
  grafting is implied at least for male pollinator-tree establishment.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** No specific time-to-ultimate-height or productive-life
  figure was sourced this pass. The yield figures are banded by tree age
  (10-15 years), not treated as a first-bearing-age claim.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Rust and anthracnose confirmed dominant by a
  peer-reviewed review; pest pressure (fruit flies, scale, moths)
  reported genuinely low and only occasional (Finding 5).
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n=22, confirmed by two independent sources, with
  a broader flow-cytometry study finding the same count across 28
  related species (though not confirmed to explicitly include this
  species by name). No nuclear genome-size (2C/1C) figure was found
  despite a dedicated, genus-and-synonym-aware search (Finding 4).
- **Cultivars:** No specific named commercial cultivar with sourced
  trait deltas was found this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — berries are picked at one maturity stage
  (green/unripe), then dried. Single window — no multi-flush or
  multiple-harvest-per-year finding sourced this pass.

## Hazards

- **Eugenol (pet toxicity)**: confirmed the compound of concern by
  multiple, mutually consistent (though non-veterinary) secondary
  sources; dose claims explicitly unverified. No authoritative
  veterinary reference (comparable to an ASPCA-style entry) was located
  for this species despite a dedicated search (Finding 7).
- A more authoritative human-side reference states the species is not
  generally associated with toxicity except at high eugenol
  concentrations (Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** green/unripe berries → picked → dried (moisture target
  below 12%; drying removes roughly one-third of fresh weight) → dried
  berries (spice). Berry oil is separately obtained by steam distillation
  of the dried, unripe berries; leaf oil is a separate product, with no
  sourced composition difference between the two found this pass
  (Finding 7).
