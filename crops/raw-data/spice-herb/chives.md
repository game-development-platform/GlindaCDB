# Chives (*Allium schoenoprasum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/chives.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Allium schoenoprasum* L. No close sibling in this
genus (onion, garlic, leek) has yet been researched in this project's
own format, so chives are researched here independently from primary
sources rather than grounded against a sibling.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — chives' chromosome number is genuinely unresolved across
sources, with both a diploid and a tetraploid-looking count reported for
the same species, and no reliable species-specific genome size was
found this pass.** A chromosome database (citing a 1979 study) lists two
distinct sporophytic counts for *A. schoenoprasum* (including one named
variety): 2n=16 and 2n=24 [1] — 2n=16 fits a diploid baseline for the
genus, while 2n=24 suggests either a polyploid cytotype or a different
ploidy series entirely; these two counts are not reconciled here. A
separate, more recent (2023) study of 12 Allium taxa reports chromosome
numbers varying 2n=16 or 2n=32 across the taxa studied, but that study's
*A. schoenoprasum*-specific count (if any) was not isolated from the
broader taxon list in this pass's own search depth. No reliable,
clearly-attributed genome-size (pg or Gb) figure specific to *A.
schoenoprasum* was found — a genus-level diploid-Allium range of
7.8-30.0 pg/1C was located, but this is far too broad to use as a
species-specific figure [1].

**Finding 2 — chives' breeding system is reported inconsistently across
source types: modern plant databases call the species self-fertile,
while the dedicated historical pollination literature finds insect (bee)
pollination essential for practical seed set.** A plant database and a
seed-catalog listing both describe chives as self-fertile [2]. However,
a dedicated pollination handbook citing a 1969 Czech study concludes
chives need bee pollination for seed set, much like onion, naming honey
bees as the main pollinator and separately noting a shortage of bees
visiting older plants specifically as a practical seed-production
problem [2]. The same handbook describes chive flowers as weakly
protandrous (pollen released slightly before the stigma becomes
receptive) — a mechanism that makes true single-flower self-pollination
unlikely without an insect vector, even though the plant as a whole may
be self-fertile via geitonogamy (pollen moving between flowers on the
same umbel/plant) [2]. One government agricultural research source
separately states that alliums generally cannot self-pollinate for this
same protandry-driven reason and depend on insects or hand-pollination
[2] — a direct institutional source conflicting with the "self-fertile"
database claims above. Which framing is correct is not resolved here.

**Finding 3 — chives are a documented poor seed producer with a
specific, sourced floret-count figure, and commercial propagation in
practice relies mainly on transplants or clump division rather than
direct field seeding.** The pollination handbook gives chives only
25-100 florets per seed head, with many seeds shattering before or
during harvest, and recommends harvesting seed heads once dry but
before shattering [2] — a specific, sourced seed-production-difficulty
figure. A seed-saving guide separately recommends an isolation distance
of 800 feet to half a mile with a population of 20-50 plants for seed
purity [2], consistent with Finding 2's insect-pollination-dependence
framing regardless of the self-fertility question. In practice, one
extension guide lists transplants from seed and root division as the
main commercial methods, explicitly noting direct field seeding is not
recommended commercially [3] — this project models both seed and clump
division as propagation routes, but division is flagged as the dominant
practical route.

**Finding 4 — no kg/ha cultivation yield figure was found for common
chives specifically; the only quantitative per-hectare figure located
in this pass is for the closely related but different species Chinese
chive, which is not transferred across species here.** A Thai study of
Chinese chive (grown for green production) reports seven harvests
annually yielding 55.96-58.29 t/ha/year depending on propagation method
(seed vs. clump division) and year [3] — a genuinely large, specific
figure, but for a different Allium species with a different growth
habit (Chinese chive has flat, not hollow, leaves and is grown at a
different commercial scale), so this is not used as a stand-in for *A.
schoenoprasum*. For common chives specifically, sources agree only on a
qualitative harvest-and-regrowth cycle: cut 3-5 cm above the ground,
never remove more than one-third of the plant at once, with new
harvestable growth within 4-6 weeks [3], and that a well-managed stand
remains productive for 2-3 years before yields decline and division/
replanting is needed [3]. Hardiness zone is also disputed across
sources: one extension source gives zone 3 specifically, while a
retailer gives a wider zones 3-9 range [3] — not reconciled.

**Finding 5 — chives' disease list is longer and more varied than its
"generally pest/disease-free" reputation suggests, including two
separately-named rust species/complex, and one literature summary
specifically flags stem and bulb nematode as a serious susceptibility
for this crop.** One extension source names thrips and rust as the most
significant problems locally, with varieties differing in rust
susceptibility [4]. Rust naming is itself inconsistent across sources —
variously given as one species pairing or a different species pairing
in one list [4], a genuine naming disagreement not resolved here. Downy
mildew (the same pathogen as onion downy mildew), onion white rot,
onion smut, and damping-off (especially in wet soil) are all separately
reported [4]. One literature summary specifically flags stem and bulb
nematode as a serious susceptibility for chives [4] — a notable,
specific finding worth flagging rather than folding anonymously into a
generic pest list, since it contradicts one authoritative horticultural
source's own general "pest-free" framing for the same crop [4]. That
same horticultural source separately notes birds sometimes pull up
chive plants grown from sets, a minor but concretely sourced
establishment hazard.

**Finding 6 — an authoritative pet-safety reference's own listing
confirms chives toxic to dogs, cats, and horses via the same N-propyl-
disulfide hemolytic-anemia mechanism shared across the whole edible
*Allium* genus, with chives specifically ranked as the mildest of the
four common edible alliums while still being unambiguously toxic.** That
reference's own page names N-propyl disulfide as the toxic principle,
with clinical signs of vomiting, hemolytic anemia, hematuria, weakness,
tachycardia, and panting [5] — a direct, confirmed, species-specific
listing. A secondary veterinary review ranks relative potency across the
genus as garlic > onion > chives/leek, explicitly still describing
chives as toxic, not safe, despite being the mildest [5] — "mildest of
the genus" and "non-toxic" are treated here as genuinely different
claims, not conflated. The same review notes the hazard applies across
fresh, cooked, dried, and powdered forms, with cumulative/repeated
exposure mattering, not just single large doses [5] — this is the same
genus-wide mechanism that would apply to onion, garlic, and leek once
those crops are researched in this project's format.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Cool-season hardy perennial; hardiness zone
  disputed across sources (zone 3 specifically per one extension
  source, zones 3-9 per a retailer) (Finding 4).
- **Climate:** Temperate, cool temperate (by analogy to the disputed
  zone-3-to-9 range above, not independently cross-checked).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — a fresh culinary herb; also an edible
  flower used as a minor garnish/infused-vinegar ingredient, not
  separately covered in detail here.
- **Pollination:** Disputed — modern plant databases call chives self-
  fertile, while dedicated pollination literature finds bee pollination
  essential for practical seed set (weakly protandrous flowers)
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** No kg/ha cultivation yield figure found for
  common chives specifically. A Chinese-chive (*A. tuberosum*, a
  different species) study reports 55.96-58.29 t/ha/year across 7
  annual harvests — not transferred to common chives (Finding 4).
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass — no crop-
  coefficient figure or drought/waterlogging-tolerance rating found.
- **Establishment:** Seed (poor producer, usually transplanted) or
  clump/bulb division (at least 5 bulbs per division, spring or ~6
  weeks before first frost; also the standard method to rejuvenate
  aging stands every 3-4 years) (Finding 3). Division is flagged as the
  dominant practical commercial route, not merely a cultivar-fidelity
  alternative to an adequate seed route. Birds pulling up set-grown
  plants is a minor establishment hazard (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** A well-managed stand remains productive 2-3 years
  before yields decline, at which point division/replanting is the
  standard remedy (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Thrips and rust (naming disputed across
  sources) are the most significant named problems in at least one
  regional extension source (Finding 5). Downy mildew, onion white rot,
  onion smut, and damping-off also reported. Stem and bulb nematode
  specifically flagged as a serious susceptibility by one literature
  summary, contradicting another source's general "pest-free" framing.
  Birds sometimes pull up set-grown plants.
- **Genome:** No reliable, species-specific genome-size figure found;
  chromosome number itself is unresolved (2n=16 vs. 2n=24 reported for
  the same species) (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** Three named cultivars were located this pass as
  profiled garden selections, but with no sourced eating-quality,
  yield, or disease-resistance delta beyond the name itself — a notably
  thinner cultivar-sourcing outcome than most other spice/herb crops
  this project has researched.
- **Harvest:** Repeat-cut — cut 3-5 cm above ground, never more than 1/3
  of the plant at once, 4-6 week regrowth. No separate seed-harvest
  stage was modeled as a distinct harvest stage this pass, despite
  chives' seed-head harvest having its own specific before-shattering
  timing requirement (Finding 3).

## Hazards

- **N-propyl disulfide (hemolytic anemia)**: an authoritative pet-safety
  reference's own listing confirms chives toxic to dogs, cats, and
  horses via this compound — the same mechanism shared across the whole
  edible *Allium* genus (garlic, onion, chives, leek), with chives
  ranked mildest of the four but still unambiguously toxic (Finding 6).
  The hazard applies across fresh, cooked, dried, and powdered forms,
  with cumulative/repeated exposure mattering.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh leaf → repeat-cut harvested → used fresh (dominant
  culinary use) or dried (commonly freeze-dried commercially for long-
  term storage).

## Estimates and derivations

A dried-leaf shelf life of roughly 730 days (plausible range 365-1095
days) is carried over by analogy to other dried leafy-herb products on
the reasoning that chives' dried product is a cut, dried hollow leaf
broadly similar in form and moisture behavior to other dried culinary
leaf herbs. This analogy is weaker than usual because most commercial
dried chives is specifically freeze-dried (a different process from
simple air/dehydrator drying used for the analogy crops), which may
behave differently in storage. A favorable (though not independently
confirmed) enclosed-space/hydroponic tractability rating is carried from
general reasoning about scallions/green onions (a related but different
Allium growth form) commonly being grown hydroponically at commercial
scale due to their shallow root systems; chives share the shallow-rooted
Allium-family trait and have an even shorter harvest-to-regrowth cycle
(4-6 weeks), which should make them, if anything, a good hydroponic
candidate by the same reasoning — though this is not independently
verified against a primary hydroponic-chives source.
