# Malabar Tamarind (*Garcinia gummi-gutta*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

*Garcinia gummi-gutta* (L.) N. Robson, synonym *Garcinia cambogia*
(Gaertn.) Desr. — the name under which it is best known in the global
dietary-supplement trade — family Clusiaceae. Also called Malabar
tamarind, kudampuli, cambodge, brindleberry, "pot tamarind," and,
confusingly, "fish tamarind" (a name also applied regionally to
unrelated plants). Not to be confused with true tamarind (*Tamarindus
indica*, Fabaceae), an entirely different family and crop.

This project's own kokum research was read in full as this crop's direct
sibling — same genus (*Garcinia*), same Western Ghats origin, same basic
dried-rind souring-agent product, and (per kokum's own research) the
same commercially troublesome 50-60% unproductive-male-seedling
fraction. The two crops diverge sharply on two points treated as this
document's own headline findings: pollination mechanism (wind-pollinated
and comparatively well-characterized here, versus kokum's own disputed,
possibly-apomictic mechanism) and the hydroxycitric-acid (HCA)
hepatotoxicity question (confirmed species-specific here, where kokum's
own research found the evidence applied only to this species, not to
kokum itself).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — pollination is wind-mediated, dioecious, and comparatively
well-characterized, a genuinely cleaner finding than kokum's own
disputed mechanism.** A dedicated 2018 reproductive-biology study
confirms the species is dioecious with wind pollination (not insect-
mediated), with flowering for both male and female plants occurring in
March-April in the populations studied [1]. The same study found
artificial (hand) pollination gave 96% fruit set [1]. A separate, older
(2002) Kerala Agricultural University thesis found cross-pollination is
the main mode of reproduction and that self-pollination gives no fruit
set at all; its own open-pollination fruit set was only 35%, against 60%
for hand cross-pollination, and it concluded that sufficient pollen must
be present around female trees for good fruit set, fruit size, and
retention [2]. Together these sources point to pollen supply, not pollen
compatibility, as the limiting factor — a cleaner, more internally
consistent picture than kokum's own research, where a single study's two
results (pollen-dependent fruit set vs. no detected zygotic embryo) were
in direct tension and left unresolved. No insect pollinator was found
reported for this species in either search pass; by contrast, a related
species was found to have floral visitors only on its male flowers,
which were explicitly confirmed not to be pollinators and were never
observed on female flowers [2] — flagged as background on a different
species, not evidence for *G. gummi-gutta* itself.

**Finding 2 — the same commercially troublesome unproductive-male-
seedling fraction already found for kokum recurs here independently,
confirming this is a genuine cross-species pattern in cultivated
dioecious *Garcinia*.** A Kerala cultivation guide states that if
seedlings are planted, 50-60% will be male and bear no fruit, and that
even a bearing female seedling takes 10-12 years to reach fruiting —
considerably longer than kokum's own grafted-tree figure of 4-5 years
[3]. Grafting is recommended specifically to avoid this loss and to
preserve the mother tree's early-bearing trait [3]. This document flags
this as a genuine, independently-confirmed cross-genus commercial
pattern rather than coincidence, since it recurs with a near-identical
percentage range in both *Garcinia* crops this project has researched.

**Finding 3 — yield, like kokum's own, is reported almost exclusively
per-tree, with no sourced per-hectare figure or standard commercial
spacing located.** A 2019-2022 Tamil Nadu field trial across eleven
accessions found the maximum marketable yield was 128.00 kg/tree (one
named accession), followed by 107.43 kg/tree (a second accession); the
same study identified fruit count per tree, rind thickness, and tartaric
acid content as traits significantly correlated with yield per tree [4].
No source gave a direct kg/ha figure or a standard commercial planting
density; an agroforestry database entry suggests roughly 10 m spacing
(implying roughly 100 trees/ha), but this document could not
independently verify that figure or confirm it is a commercial-orchard
recommendation rather than a generic agroforestry placeholder. Unlike
kokum's own research, this document does not attempt a derived kg/ha
extrapolation, since no comparably-sourced spacing recommendation was
found to anchor one.

**Finding 4 — a genus-wide seed-biology picture emerges cleanly across
two independently-sourced studies, both finding seed-coat removal
substantially improves germination, a finding this project did not have
for kokum's own more thinly-sourced seed-biology section.** A dedicated
germination study found 94.67% seed viability by tetrazolium testing,
and found that seeds with the seed coat removed reached the highest
germination percentage (93.25%); the same study confirmed seed storage
significantly reduces germination performance, consistent with the
genus's generally recalcitrant seed habit [5]. A separate, older Kerala
study found that seed-coat removal combined with a 12-hour soak in 500
ppm GA3 gave early, higher germination, reaching about 90.7% [5]. Despite
this comparatively strong seed-germination sourcing, softwood grafting
on seedling rootstock remains the dominant commercial propagation route,
specifically to bypass the Finding 2 male-fraction problem, not because
seed germination itself is poor [3][5].

**Finding 5 — the hydroxycitric-acid (HCA) dietary-supplement
hepatotoxicity signal, which kokum's own research found could not be
confirmed as species-specific, is confirmed here: this species is the
one actually implicated across the regulatory and case-report
literature.** A national health regulator's review of 93 international
case reports concerns *Garcinia gummi-gutta*/HCA by name specifically,
finding a possible link to liver injury and planning hepatotoxicity
warnings on product monographs [6]. A 2025 pharmaceutical-biology review
identified more than 200 adverse liver-injury events tied to Garcinia
consumption, and a UK toxicology advisory committee's 2026 draft opinion
separately found that (–)-HCA caused testicular toxicity (Sertoli-cell
effects) in rodent studies, with the data insufficient to confirm or
exclude genotoxicity [6]. A national drug-regulatory authority issued a
parallel safety alert [6]. The mechanism is reported as idiosyncratic —
not dependent on dose or duration of use — with onset typically within
weeks, though in some cases extending to 12 months [6]. Some older
sources push back: a 2009 article found no evidence HCA causes
hepatotoxicity, and a 2013 commentary concluded available data show no
liver toxicity from Garcinia cambogia extract/HCA — both notably older
than, and at odds with, the newer regulatory findings, and this document
treats the newer regulatory consensus as the stronger current evidence
[6]. A separate agricultural-research-institute listing reports HCA
content reaching up to 30% in dried rind, though this figure comes from
a supplement-oriented source and was not independently verified against
a dedicated analytical study [7]. This finding directly resolves the
forward pointer left open in this project's own kokum research — this
species, not kokum, is the one correctly excluded from kokum's own
hazards section.

**Finding 6 — traditional rind processing is a multi-step smoke-curing
sequence, distinct from kokum's own simple sun-drying, and is well-
documented across multiple consistent commercial and traditional
sources despite none being peer-reviewed research.** The rind is
removed from the ripe fruit, sun-dried, then smoked over several days
over a fire or in smoky chambers until it turns black, after which it is
rubbed with coconut oil and salt for preservation and softening and
stored in large ceramic pots [8]. This smoke-curing step is the key
processing difference from kokum's own rind, which is simply sun-dried
without smoking. Salt addition during curing is reported to extend
storage life, and commercial sellers claim the dried, salted rind can
remain usable for years if kept sealed and dry [8] — this document
treats that specific multi-year claim as an unverified commercial claim,
not a controlled storage-trial finding, consistent with how kokum's own
dried-rind shelf life was treated (an analogy, not a sourced figure).

**Finding 7 — conservation status is reported inconsistently across
sources for reasons that trace to varietal rather than species-level
confusion, and this document does not resolve which variety the
"endangered" claim actually concerns.** One Western Ghats botanical-
garden source states a variety of this species was placed in an
"endangered" category due to its restricted distribution in small
scattered populations, while the same source's own table separately
lists the wild nominate variety as "Common" [9]. A separate
environmental-information-system record gives the species an overall
global threat status of "Lower Risk, near threatened" [9]. This document
does not identify which named variety the "endangered" claim specifically
concerns from the available snippet-level sourcing, but notes the
nominate, commercially-relevant variety itself is reported common within
its Western Ghats range — endemic to Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka,
Kerala, and Tamil Nadu, occurring as a frequent understorey tree in
semi-evergreen to evergreen forest from 50-1,800 m elevation [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial — evergreen understorey tree,
  Western Ghats endemic. Perennial bearing (flowering January-April in
  Western Ghats populations per one source [2], a separate study's own
  populations flowering March-April [1] — not reconciled as a single
  range, but both fall within a broadly consistent early-year window;
  fruit ripening May-July per general cultivation-guide sourcing, not a
  dedicated study).
- **Climate:** Tropical, humid — endemic to the Western Ghats of India
  (Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu); understorey tree in
  semi-evergreen to evergreen forest, 50-1,800 m elevation [9]; no
  formal hardiness-zone classification was found.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh fruit eaten [8].
- **Pollination:** Required — wind-pollinated (Finding 1); self-
  pollination gives no fruit set at all [2], a cleaner and more
  confidently sourced finding than kokum's own disputed mechanism.
- **Yield / composition:** No sourced kg/ha figure exists; per-tree
  figures from an 11-accession Tamil Nadu trial run up to 128.00
  kg/tree (best accession) [4]; no commercial spacing recommendation
  was confidently sourced to derive a per-hectare estimate. Rind
  composition figures from a general consumer source (tannin 1.7%,
  pectin 0.9%, fat 1.4%, moisture 80.0 g/100g, protein 1%, sugars 4.1%)
  could not be verified against primary data [7].
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Both seed and vegetative (grafting), with grafting
  dominant commercially for the same reason as kokum — seedling
  populations run 50-60% unproductive male trees [3], directly
  mirroring kokum's own finding. Seed is recalcitrant (sensitive to
  desiccation and chilling, stores only a few weeks) [5], but
  germination itself is comparatively well-sourced and strong: 94.67%
  viability by tetrazolium test, 93.25% with seed coat removed [5].
  Softwood grafting on seedling rootstock is the dominant commercial
  route, specifically to bypass the 50-60% unproductive-male-seedling
  fraction. No standard commercial spacing figure was confidently
  sourced. One study found *G. gummi-gutta* performed poorly as a
  rootstock for a different species' intraspecific cleft grafting
  (complete failure, versus 93% success on a different rootstock
  species) [5] — this document notes this is evidence of *G. gummi-
  gutta*'s own poor performance as a rootstock for a different scion
  species, not evidence about grafting onto *G. gummi-gutta* as a scion
  (the commercial direction used for propagating this crop itself), and
  does not conflate the two.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour figure was sourced for any operation this
  pass. Rind processing is well-documented operationally: rind removed
  from fruit, sun-dried, then smoked over several days until black,
  rubbed with coconut oil and salt, stored in ceramic pots [8] (Finding
  6).
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Seedling females reported to take 10-12 years to bear
  fruit [3] — no grafted-tree figure or orchard-lifespan figure was
  sourced.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No dedicated postharvest disease or
  storage-pest study was located for either the fresh fruit or the
  cured rind.
- **Genome:** No species-specific chromosome count or genome-size
  (C-value) figure was located — only a genus-level base number (x=8)
  and a genus-wide count of roughly 100-400 species-level estimates from
  a standard plant-resources reference, explicitly noting the genus's
  taxonomy is still under revision [11] — a genuinely weaker genomic-
  sourcing outcome than even kokum's own disputed-but-at-least-species-
  level 2n=54/2n=48 figures.
- **Cultivars:** 11 numbered accessions were tested in a single Tamil
  Nadu field trial, with the top two by marketable yield at 128.00
  kg/tree and 107.43 kg/tree; fruit count per tree, rind thickness, and
  tartaric acid content were the traits found significantly correlated
  with per-tree yield [4] — no named, released commercial variety
  (comparable to kokum's or mangosteen's own named local types) was
  located this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — ripe fruit is the sole harvested stage
  sourced this pass. Single window — one annual fruiting season
  (flowering January-April or March-April depending on source, fruiting
  May-July).
- **Storage:** Fresh fruit — no dedicated shelf-life figure was sourced
  this pass; a ready-to-serve-beverage study found refrigerated storage
  preserved vitamin C better than ambient over 3 months, with a slight
  microbial-load increase over storage, but this concerns a processed
  beverage blend, not the whole fresh fruit itself [10]. Smoked/salted
  rind — estimated by analogy (see Estimates and derivations).

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the hydroxycitric-acid (HCA) dietary-supplement
hepatotoxicity signal is confirmed species-specific for this crop
(Finding 5), with a mechanism reported as idiosyncratic (not dose- or
duration-dependent), onset typically within weeks (sometimes up to 12
months), and supported by multiple national regulatory reviews — a
genuine human-health hazard, deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

No authoritative pet-safety listing or dog/cat toxicity finding was
located in either search pass. No dedicated postharvest disease or
storage-pest study was located for the fresh fruit or rind.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fruit harvested → rind removed, sun-dried, then smoked over
  several days until black → rubbed with coconut oil and salt → stored
  in ceramic pots. Smoke-curing is the key processing difference from
  kokum's own simply sun-dried rind.

## Estimates and derivations

A smoked/salted rind shelf life of 1095 days is estimated by direct
analogy to other heavily salted/smoked/low-moisture preserved spice
products this project has researched, on the reasoning that the
combination of smoking, salting, and oiling described in Finding 6
should give a comparably long shelf life to other preserved condiments,
while treating commercial sellers' "lasts for years" claims as
directionally consistent but not independently verified. Plausible
range: 730-1825 days. A dedicated storage-trial source for cured
kudampuli would replace this estimate.
