# Cilantro / Coriander (*Coriandrum sativum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/cilantro.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources

## Naming and scope

This covers both the leaf and seed harvest as a single crop/species,
since "cilantro" (fresh leaf, North American usage) and "coriander"
(dried seed, and the whole plant internationally) are the same species
— not two separate crops. This parallels a pattern this project has
found for other single-species, multiple-named-product crops, though
here the two names also map to two different plant parts rather than
processing stages of the same part.

No close sibling in this project's own research shares this species'
genus or even family (Apiaceae) among the spice/herb crops researched so
far at the time of this writing.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — coriander's own genome is unusually well-resolved by
primary sequencing, with a real, specific chromosome-level reference
assembly, though older flow-cytometry estimates still disagree with each
other and with the assembly by a wide margin.** A 2020 high-quality
genome assembly anchored the sequence to all 11 chromosomes (2n=22,
basic number x=11, independently confirmed diploid across a separate
2024 study of 14 populations), with a total assembled length of
2,118.68 Mb and a k-mer-based size estimate of 2,130.29 Mb — closely
agreeing with each other, and finding 70.59% of the genome is repetitive
sequence [1]. This is a genuinely strong, specific genomic finding
relative to many other spice/herb crops this project has researched.
However, older flow-cytometry-based estimates do not agree with the
assembly or each other: one Indian study gives approximately 1.5 Gb
(notably smaller than the assembly); a 2024 study instead reports
4.03-4.27 pg (mean 4.15 pg) across 14 populations; and an even earlier
2007 estimate gives 5.08 pg [1] — none of these pg-based figures
converted to Mb and reconciled against the assembly's own 2,118.68 Mb in
this pass, since the standard pg-to-Mb conversion factor was not
independently sourced here.

**Finding 2 — coriander is reported cross-pollinated (allogamous), a
genuinely different reproductive biology from several other mint-family
herbs this project has researched, though this project's own search did
not surface a detailed self-compatibility mechanism or pollinator-
dependence figure.** Multiple sources independently describe *C.
sativum* as a "cross pollinated annual herb" and, separately, as
"herbaceous, annual, and allogam[ous]" [2] — a consistent cross-
pollination finding across two independent sources, though neither
source gives a specific self-incompatibility mechanism, pollinator
species, or quantified self-vs-cross seed-set percentage. No seed-
isolation distance figure was found this pass.

**Finding 3 — coriander's own famous "soapy taste" dislike is the best-
characterized single-gene flavor-perception finding this project has
sourced for any spice/herb crop, anchored in a large, peer-reviewed
genome-wide association study rather than casual secondary sourcing.** A
2012 genome-wide association study (14,604 participants of European
ancestry reporting a soapy taste, replicated in a separate 11,851-
participant cohort reporting liking/disliking the herb) identified a SNP
near a cluster of olfactory receptor genes, pointing to OR6A2 as the
likely candidate receptor [3] — OR6A2 is reported to have high binding
specificity for several of the aldehydes responsible for coriander's own
characteristic aroma, directly linking the genetic finding to the
plant's own specific chemistry [3]. This is simultaneously one of the
best-sourced and most explicitly caveated findings: researchers
themselves describe the evidence as short of certainty ("some
researchers suspect a particular olfactory receptor gene, but that's as
certain as it gets"), and separately note the dislike may stem from a
combination of genes rather than one [3]. Reported dislike rates vary
sharply by population — 3-21% depending on location — with an
independently sourced cultural/exposure confound: populations from
cultures with frequent culinary use of the herb (Mexican, Indian) report
far fewer dislikers, and at least one source separately notes
unfamiliarity alone (not genetics) may explain some individual dislike
[3] — a genuinely two-factor (genetic and cultural/exposure) finding,
not reduced to genetics alone despite the strong GWAS anchor.

**Finding 4 — seed yield figures scatter across roughly a 4-5x range
depending on country/variety/sowing-timing, with a sourced, large,
actionable sowing-date effect and a separate, large seed-rate difference
between seed-crop and leaf-crop cultivation of the same species.** Indian
cultivar guidance lists a 500-1,400 kg/ha seed-yield range across
varieties with 80-140 day durations, with a specific named cultivar
reaching 1,200-1,400 kg/ha over 110-115 days [4]. A Bulgarian study
found the highest seed and essential-oil yields (2,302 kg/ha seed; 26.8
kg/ha oil) specifically from an October sowing, with the lowest yields
from an April sowing [4] — a sourced, large sowing-date effect, though
the April figure itself was not captured as a specific number in this
pass. A Romanian study separately reports up to 2,209 kg/ha for a
Moroccan variety [4] — broadly consistent in magnitude with the
Bulgarian October figure, though grown in a different country/climate,
not independently cross-validated. Seed rate differs sharply by end use:
Indian guidance recommends 10-12 kg/ha (irrigated) or 20-25 kg/ha
(rainfed) for seed production, while Bangladeshi trials for leaf/foliage
production used a much higher rate, around 50 kg/ha [4] — a genuinely
sourced, large (2-5x) seed-rate difference tied directly to which
product (seed vs. leaf) is the cultivation target, a dual-purpose-crop
finding worth recording explicitly.

**Finding 5 — bolting is reported temperature-triggered with a sourced,
wide genotype-dependent range, and here bolting is the path to the seed
crop, not a failure mode, unlike crops where early bolting is purely a
hazard.** A Bangladeshi genotype trial found days-to-bolting ranging
38-60 days across different genotypes [5] — a notably wide (>50%)
genotype-dependent spread for a single developmental trigger. High
temperature is separately reported to cause poor vegetative growth,
reduced plant height, and premature bolting, together reducing both
green-leaf yield and its quality [5] — a dual yield-and-quality
temperature effect. For cilantro/coriander, early bolting specifically
hurts the leaf (cilantro) harvest while being necessary for the seed
(coriander) harvest — the same single-species dual-harvest tension
already flagged in Finding 4's own seed-rate contrast, now extended to
bolting timing as well.

**Finding 6 — disease pressure is dominated by stem gall, explicitly
named the single most important disease of this crop by extension
sources, with a sourced moisture-and-shade interaction, alongside
powdery mildew (reported universal across 38 tested genotypes in one
study) and a specifically-named bacterial pathogen causing blight.**
Stem gall (tumor-like swellings on veins, stalks, stems, and fruit) is
explicitly described by extension material as "the most important
disease of this crop," with excess soil moisture under shade
specifically making the tumors more numerous — a sourced environmental
interaction (moisture × shade), with resistant cultivars and captan/
thiram seed treatments named as management options [6]. Powdery mildew
(*Erysiphe polygoni*) is separately described as one of the most
important diseases, with one study finding none of 38 tested genotypes
free of it [6] — a notably universal susceptibility finding. Bacterial
blight, caused by *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *coriandricola*, is
separately documented with a specific pathological mechanism:
blackening of leaf veins and surrounding tissue, with bacteria found
inside the xylem vessels of infected stems [6] — a specific,
mechanistically-described bacterial pathogen. One extension document
separately lists bacterial black spot, bacterial soft rot, and a
*Peronospora*-caused downy mildew [6] — whether "bacterial black spot"
is the same condition as the bacterial blight above or a distinct
disease was not independently cross-checked here. No aphid-specific
pest finding was located this pass despite a dedicated search.

**Finding 7 — the plant is confirmed non-toxic to pets via a directly-
located authoritative primary listing, with the genuine hazard instead
located in common co-ingredients rather than the herb itself.** An
authoritative pet-safety reference's own entry, located directly, lists
cilantro/coriander as non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses [7] — a
definitively negative finding, in contrast to the toxic-plant findings
this project has established for several other mint-family herbs it has
researched. Several lower-quality secondary sources separately flag that
common kitchen co-ingredients often served alongside cilantro/coriander
— specifically onion and garlic — are independently toxic to both cats
and dogs [7], a genuinely different kind of hazard (preparation-context,
not plant-intrinsic).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** True annual, herbaceous — the entire crop
  cycle (leaf harvest through optional seed harvest) completes within
  one growing season (80-140 days to seed maturity).
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure was found
  despite a dedicated search — a frost-tolerance or minimum-growing-
  temperature figure may be the more relevant equivalent for this annual
  crop, but this was not resolved.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh leaf eaten raw/as garnish; dried
  seed used whole or ground as a spice.
- **Pollination:** Not required for leaf harvest; required for seed
  harvest — cross-pollinated (allogamous), though mechanism and
  pollinator are not detailed in the sources found (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Seed 500-2,302 kg/ha across multiple
  countries/varieties/sowing dates (a roughly 4-5x spread, highest from
  an October-sowing Bulgarian trial) (Finding 4). Essential oil (from
  seed) up to 26.8 kg/ha in the same best-treatment Bulgarian trial. No
  leaf (cilantro) yield-per-hectare figure was independently sourced
  this pass — only the higher seed rate used for leaf production (~50
  kg/ha) was found, not the resulting leaf yield itself.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, the sole propagation route found
  this pass — no vegetative propagation route was sourced or expected
  for this true annual. Seed rate is product-dependent: lower for
  seed-crop cultivation (10-25 kg/ha), much higher for leaf-crop
  cultivation (~50 kg/ha) (Finding 4).
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the moisture-x-
  shade stem-gall interaction.
- **Stand life:** True annual — not a perennial/biennial the way some
  other spice/herb crops are.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Stem gall (explicitly "the most important
  disease"), with a sourced moisture-x-shade interaction, resistant
  cultivars and captan/thiram seed treatments as management (Finding 6).
  Powdery mildew, reported universal across 38 tested genotypes in one
  study. Bacterial blight, a specifically mechanistically-described
  xylem-colonizing pathogen. Separately-listed bacterial black spot,
  bacterial soft rot, and downy mildew, not cross-checked against the
  above for possible overlap. No aphid-specific finding located despite
  a dedicated search.
- **Genome:** Well-resolved by a real, specific 2020 chromosome-level
  assembly (2,118.68 Mb assembled; 2,130.29 Mb by k-mer estimate;
  70.59% repetitive sequence) (Finding 1). Older flow-cytometry
  estimates (1.5 Gb; 4.03-4.27 pg; 5.08 pg) disagree with the assembly
  and with each other, not reconciled to a common unit this pass.
  Chromosome number well-converged: 2n=22 (x=11), independently
  confirmed diploid across a separate 2024 study of 14 populations.
- **Cultivars:** One named cultivar with a specific sourced yield range
  (1,200-1,400 kg/ha over 110-115 days) (Finding 4). A Moroccan variety
  is separately named in a Romanian trial context (up to 2,209 kg/ha)
  without further characterization. No disease-resistance cultivar
  comparison was sourced despite the powdery-mildew study testing 38
  genotypes — the study found universal susceptibility rather than a
  resistant-cultivar finding.
- **Harvest:** Two distinct products from the same plant at genuinely
  different developmental stages — leaf (pre-bolting, repeated cutting)
  and seed (post-flowering/fruiting, single harvest). Repeat-cut for
  leaf; single stage, single window for seed (one bolting/flowering
  event per annual cycle). The same plant can theoretically supply both
  products sequentially if leaf-cutting stops early enough to allow seed
  maturation, though this tradeoff was not independently quantified this
  pass.

## Hazards

No human- or animal-health hazard was found this pass for the plant
itself — an authoritative pet-safety reference's own listing confirms
cilantro/coriander non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses (Finding 7). The
genuine hazard risk comes from common kitchen co-ingredients (onion,
garlic), not the herb itself — a separate concern from this crop.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (leaf):** fresh leaf → repeat-cut harvested pre-bolting → used
  fresh/raw. **Chain (seed):** plant allowed to bolt/flower/fruit →
  single harvest post-flowering → dried → used whole or ground as a
  spice (coriander), or distilled into essential oil.
