# Cumin (*Cuminum cyminum*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-02

listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced

## Naming and scope

"Black cumin" is a genuinely confused name across at least three
different plants, none of which this document treats as
interchangeable: (a) a *Cuminum cyminum* cultivar with small, dark,
rather sweet fruits, grown in Pakistan and Iran [1]; (b) *Nigella
sativa* (kalonji/black seed), a completely different genus and family
(Ranunculaceae, not Apiaceae); (c) *Bunium persicum* (black zira/kala
zeera), a separate Apiaceae genus. This document covers only true cumin
(*Cuminum cyminum*, including its own named "Black" cultivar) — *Nigella
sativa* and *Bunium persicum* are not covered here.

This project's closest grounding for cumin is its own prior research
into cilantro/coriander and fennel — all three are Apiaceae seed-spice
crops with a broadly similar single-seed-harvest shape, though cumin's
own climate and water-use profile (arid, dry, cool, sunny) is a
genuinely different cultivation niche from either.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — cumin's own chromosome number is cleanly sourced as
diploid (2n=14), but genome-size figures scatter across roughly a 50%
range even within the same cultivar across different studies, a
genuinely messier outcome than this project has found for some other
Apiaceae crops.** Cumin is reported a diploid with somatic chromosome
number 14 [1]. Genome size (2C value) is reported to vary across Indian
cultivars from 1.74 pg up to 2.66 pg [1] — a notably wide range even
allowing for genuine cultivar variation. Critically, the same named
cultivar ('GC-4') is reported at two different figures across different
studies: 2.51 pg in one study and 2.66 pg in another [1] — a within-
cultivar disagreement (not merely a cross-cultivar range), not
reconciled in this pass.

**Finding 2 — cumin is reported protandrous with cross-fertilization as
the general rule, but with a sourced, surprisingly high self-pollination
rate as an exception.** A general botanical/agronomic reference
describes cumin flowers as protandrous (pollen matures before the stigma
becomes receptive), with cross-fertilization described as "the rule,"
but also notes self-pollination may sometimes reach as high as 70% [2]
— a genuinely two-sided finding (predominantly cross-pollinating, but
capable of substantial self-pollination under some circumstances). No
pollinator species or isolation-distance figure was found this pass.

**Finding 3 — essential-oil content and composition are reported to
vary sharply by irrigation regime at the flowering/seed-formation stage
specifically, with the dominant compound itself shifting between two
different chemicals depending on water management.** Oil content is
reported widely, from 1.16-1.98% in one cross-country comparative study,
up to 3.0% (dry-weight basis) in an Indian seed study, with a general
agricultural reference giving a broader general range of 2.5-5% for
dried fruits [3] — already a notably wide scatter. More strikingly, a
2024 irrigation study found that cutting off irrigation specifically at
the seed-formation stage shifted the dominant essential-oil compound to
cuminal (20.7%), while other irrigation regimes instead produced gamma-
terpinene (20.8-25.84%) as the dominant compound [3] — a genuinely
dramatic finding where water management at one specific developmental
stage changes which compound dominates, not merely how much oil is
produced. The Indian seed study's own reported dominant compound,
cuminaldehyde (49.4%), is a third distinct figure, not obviously
reconcilable with either irrigation-regime compound above without
knowing that study's own irrigation practice — this document does not
assume cuminaldehyde, cuminal, and gamma-terpinene are simply
alternative names for the same compound, treating them as the genuinely
different reported dominants that the sources present them as.

**Finding 4 — yield figures are tightly clustered around 450-650 kg/ha
across multiple sources and countries, with a specific, quantified
named-cultivar yield advantage and a national productivity figure both
independently sourced.** Rajasthan coordinated trials report the
national check variety at roughly 469 kg/ha, with a newer variety
reaching a weighted mean of 566 kg/ha — a sourced, quantified 22.8%
yield advantage for the newer cultivar over the check [4]. India's own
national productivity figure is reported at 647 kg/ha for 2024-25 [4] —
a genuinely recent, specific national-level statistic. A Turkish trial
separately reports roughly 59.88 kg/da (approximately 600 kg/ha) [4] —
broadly consistent with the Indian figures despite a different growing
country, a notably tight cross-country yield agreement compared with
some other Apiaceae crops this project has researched.

**Finding 5 — cumin requires a specifically arid, dry, cool, sunny
cultivation niche with a defined sowing window and a sourced moderate
seed rate.** The crop is reported "mostly adapted to dry cool sunny
areas with low humidity," specifically favoring sandy loam soils in
semiarid and arid regions [4] — a genuinely distinct climate requirement
from the heat-avoidance framing or general Mediterranean framing this
project has found for some sibling Apiaceae crops; cumin instead seems
to actively need dry, low-humidity conditions as part of its own
cultivation profile, not merely tolerate them. Sowing in the Indian
plains runs from the first week of November through the first week of
December, with maturity reported at 110-115 days depending on variety
[4] — a specific, dated sowing-window figure. Seed rate is reported at
12-15 kg/ha depending on sowing method [4] — notably lower than the
higher leaf-crop seed rate this project has recorded for a sibling
Apiaceae crop grown for both leaf and seed, but broadly similar to that
crop's own seed-crop rate, consistent with both crops sharing a seed-
harvest (not leaf-harvest) cultivation target. No USDA-equivalent
hardiness-zone figure was found this pass, and no propagation method
beyond direct seeding was found or expected (cumin is a true annual).

**Finding 6 — Fusarium wilt is the dominant, most damaging disease by a
wide margin, with a specific sourced yield-loss range and causal
organism, alongside two other named fungal diseases and a multi-species
aphid-pest complex.** Fusarium wilt, caused by the soil-borne fungus
*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *cumini*, is reported to cause 35-80% yield
loss [5] — a dramatically high ceiling, among the most severe named,
quantified disease-yield-loss figures this project has sourced for any
spice/herb crop. Alternaria blight (caused by *Alternaria burnsii*) and
powdery mildew (caused by *Erysiphe polygoni* — the same pathogen this
project has found for some other Apiaceae and spice crops) are
separately named as major diseases [5]. Aphids are reported the key
insect pest, with three named species plus thrips [5] — across the full
disease-and-pest complex together, one source reports combined losses
of 10-90% [5], though this document does not interpret this wide
combined range as additive on top of Fusarium wilt's own already-severe
35-80% figure.

**Finding 7 — no cumin-specific authoritative pet-toxicity listing
could be located this pass, with the search instead surfacing a
genuinely confusing name collision: an authoritative pet-safety
reference's own "Persian Cumin" entry is actually caraway, not true
cumin.** A direct search for an authoritative pet-safety listing for
cumin returned only that reference's own caraway entry, which lists the
alternate common names "Meridian Fennel" and "Persian Cumin" [6] —
caraway (*Carum carvi*) is rated toxic to dogs, cats, and horses by that
reference, but this is not a finding about true cumin (*Cuminum
cyminum*), despite the name overlap [6]. Several secondary sources make
directly contradictory claims about true cumin's own toxicity status
(one claims a mild GI-irritant rating for dogs; another claims no toxic
listing at all) [6], neither independently verifiable. This document
does not write up a conclusion on this contradictory, unverified basis.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual, herbaceous — the same true-annual
  seed-harvest shape this project has found for a sibling Apiaceae
  crop, but with a genuinely different, specifically arid/dry/cool/
  low-humidity climate requirement (Finding 5).
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure was found this
  pass — the crop's own qualitative requirement (dry, cool, sunny,
  low-humidity, arid/semiarid) is sourced but not mapped to a hardiness-
  zone or degree-day figure.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in the usual sense — the seed is used
  as a dried/ground spice, not eaten raw; no fresh-leaf product was
  sourced for this crop.
- **Pollination:** Required for seed set — protandrous, predominantly
  cross-pollinating but capable of up to 70% self-pollination under some
  circumstances (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** 469-647 kg/ha across multiple Indian and
  Turkish sources — a notably tight range compared to many other
  Apiaceae crops this project has researched (Finding 4). A named
  cultivar shows a quantified 22.8% yield advantage over the national
  check cultivar. Essential-oil content 1.16-5% of dried fruit depending
  on source and method, with the dominant compound itself shifting
  between cuminaldehyde/cuminal/gamma-terpinene depending on irrigation
  regime and study (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance is implied high by the crop's own
  arid/semiarid cultivation niche, though not independently quantified
  with a specific crop coefficient this pass. A specific, actionable
  irrigation finding: cutting off irrigation at the seed-formation stage
  shifts the essential oil's own dominant compound to cuminal; other
  regimes instead favor gamma-terpinene (Finding 3) — a genuinely
  different kind of water-management lever than a simple yield effect.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding only (the sole route found or
  expected for this true annual). Seed rate 12-15 kg/ha depending on
  sowing method (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** Sandy loam soils specifically favored, in semiarid/arid
  regions (Finding 5) — no specific pH or nutrient-rate figure sourced
  this pass.
- **Stand life:** True annual, 110-115 days to maturity — no dual-
  harvest leaf/seed tension the way some sibling Apiaceae crops this
  project has researched have, since no leaf product was sourced for
  cumin.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fusarium wilt (*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp.
  *cumini*), the dominant disease, 35-80% yield loss — among the most
  severe quantified disease-yield-loss figures this project has sourced
  for any spice/herb crop. Alternaria blight and powdery mildew (the
  same pathogen species this project has found for some other spice
  crops) also named. Aphids (three named species) and thrips are the
  key insect pests; combined disease-and-pest losses reported 10-90%.
- **Genome:** 1.74-2.66 pg (2C) across Indian cultivars, with the same
  cultivar reported at two different figures (2.51 pg and 2.66 pg)
  across different studies, not reconciled. Chromosome number well-
  converged: diploid, 2n=14.
- **Cultivars:** A national check variety, with a disputed genome-size
  figure across two different studies of the same cultivar (Finding 1),
  and a newer variety with a quantified 22.8% yield advantage over the
  check (Finding 4). A "Black" cultivar (small, dark, rather sweet
  fruits, Pakistan/Iran) is separately named — not the same plant as
  *Nigella sativa* or *Bunium persicum*, both also commonly called
  "black cumin."
- **Harvest:** Single stage — one seed harvest per planting, no leaf
  product or multi-stage system sourced for this crop. Single window
  per planting season.

## Hazards

Fusarium wilt (*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *cumini*), the dominant
disease, 35-80% yield loss. Alternaria blight (*Alternaria burnsii*) and
powdery mildew (*Erysiphe polygoni*) also named. Aphids (three named
species) and thrips are the key insect pests; combined disease-and-pest
losses reported 10-90%.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — no cumin-specific authoritative pet-toxicity
listing could be located; a direct search surfaced only an unrelated
entry (caraway, confusingly also called "Persian Cumin"), and secondary
sources on true cumin's own toxicity status directly contradict each
other and were not independently verified. A dedicated, more targeted
search is deferred until this project has a clearer sourcing path for
this kind of pet-health data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed harvested at maturity (110-115 days) → dried/cured →
  used whole or ground as a spice, or distilled/extracted into essential
  oil whose dominant aromatic compound varies with irrigation regime.
