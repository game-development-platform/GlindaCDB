# Saffron (*Crocus sativus*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/saffron.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

No existing crop this project has researched shares saffron's structure
(a sterile, vegetatively-propagated perennial corm/geophyte grown for a
single floral organ). This document loosely grounds against this
project's own prior research into other extremely labor-intensive, low-
yield, high-value crops for the shared pattern of extreme hand-labor
intensity producing a tiny mass of an extraordinarily high-value
product, and against general corm/bulb propagation conventions used
elsewhere in this project where applicable — but saffron's core biology
(triploid sterility, single-organ harvest, flower-to-spice ratio) has no
close analogue in this project's research and is treated as standalone.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — saffron is a sterile autotriploid that cannot exist in the
wild, with its exact evolutionary origin still disputed between two
independent 2019 genomic studies that nonetheless agree on the parent
species.** *Crocus sativus* is in family Iridaceae, genus *Crocus*. It
is male-sterile with 2n = 3x = 24 chromosomes and produces no viable
seed; it has been propagated exclusively by vegetative corm division
since its origin [1][5]. Two independent 2019 studies (one using
chloroplast genomes and genotyping-by-sequencing; the other using genome
sequencing plus FISH chromosome mapping) both trace saffron's origin to
the wild species *Crocus cartwrightianus* of Attica (mainland Greece)
and the Aegean islands, assigning 99.3% of saffron's alleles to that
species in one study [5]. The two studies differ only in framing: one
describes saffron as an autotriploid arising from the combination of two
genotypes of the same wild species, while the other describes it as a
hybrid fusion of two *C. cartwrightianus* cytotypes with slightly
different chromosome structures — both point to the same parent species,
but use different technical language for how the triploid state arose
[5]. Because it is sterile and clonal, every *Crocus sativus* plant on
Earth is part of one worldwide clonal lineage, descended from this
single ancient origin event — the most extreme case of genetic
uniformity this project has encountered in its spice/herb research so
far [5]. Separately, some origin accounts (lower-authority sources)
instead suggest Iran, Mesopotamia, or even Kashmir as the origin region
— these are kept visible as competing claims, but the two primary 2019
genomic studies both independently support the Greek/Aegean origin
specifically [1].

**Finding 2 — saffron's own cultivation-climate tolerance is wide and
well-characterized by at least one peer-reviewed source, in clearer
contrast to several other crops this project has researched where even
basic climate figures disagreed across nearly every source.** A peer-
reviewed Turkish study reports saffron resistant to cold temperatures
down to -18°C and to summer temperatures up to 45°C [1]. It thrives in
Mediterranean maquis-type and semi-arid climates with hot, dry summers
[1]. Soil requirements are more consistently sourced than in several
sibling crop entries: the Turkish study recommends clay loam soil, and
multiple sources independently emphasize drainage — *Crocus sativus*
shows marked susceptibility to root and corm rot in poorly drained soil,
making drainage, rather than a specific soil texture, the dominant
sourced requirement [1]. Full sunlight is also specified [1]. Planting
depth figures do disagree across lower-authority sources (10-15cm vs.
15-20cm), and are treated with the usual caution applied to commercial-
guide-only figures.

**Finding 3 — corm planting density, depth, and replanting cycle
interact in a genuinely complex, only partially-resolved way across
multiple agronomic studies, with real trade-offs between daughter-corm
yield, flower yield, and corm size.** Daughter-corm output rises with
planting density — one study recorded the highest daughter-corm counts
(614 and 591 corms/m² across two years) at the highest density tested,
while a 2025 study instead found the largest individual corms and the
highest proportion of big corms at the lowest density tested (67
corms/m²) — these are not contradictory findings but two different
optimization targets (more, smaller corms vs. fewer, bigger corms) [2].
Central Italian growers favor a medium density (111-119 corms/m²)
specifically because it balances similar stigma yield against the
highest daughter-corm production compared to higher densities [2].
Planting depth findings genuinely conflict: one Italian trial found 15cm
depth gave the best yield; a separate source states shallow planting
produces more daughter corms (since more buds sprout from shallow-
planted corms); and the 2025 study found depth had minimal impact on
corm production at all, recommending a shallow 8cm depth at a density of
100 corms/m² [2]. Each mother corm is exhausted and replaced after one
season, yielding 1 to 10 daughter cormlets depending on the original
mother corm's size — this document did not find a single reliable
annual multiplication-factor figure consistent across regions, since
Iranian and Italian trial conditions are not directly comparable [2].
Separately, a commercial guide (lower authority) describes planting
bunches of 2-3 corms 10-15cm apart within rows, at roughly 3 tons of
corms per hectare, while a university extension factsheet gives
density-by-replanting-regime figures: 5-7 corms/ft² for longer-term beds
vs. 10-13 corms/ft² where Italian growers replant every 1-2 years [2].

**Finding 4 — harvest and stigma-separation labor figures are the most
sharply conflicting numbers found this pass, spanning nearly an order of
magnitude, almost entirely because the sources are commercial sellers
rather than agronomic literature.** Estimates of flowers needed per
kilogram of dried saffron are comparatively consistent, roughly
120,000-200,000, most commonly cited around 150,000 [3]. Labor-hour
figures per kilogram of dried saffron disagree far more sharply: one
commercial source gives approximately 40 hours (ambiguously worded,
possibly per pound rather than per kilogram in one phrasing), while
others give 370-480 hours per kilogram — a roughly tenfold spread — with
the higher figures appearing more consistent across multiple independent
commercial sources and therefore treated as more representative, though
none of these sources is a peer-reviewed or agricultural-extension
citation [3]. The most specific per-worker figures come from a Kashmir-
focused commercial source (lower authority): picking 1,000 flowers takes
45-55 minutes, and separating stigmas from that same 1,000 flowers takes
a further 100-130 minutes [3]. A university extension workshop
presentation (somewhat higher authority, though still a secondary slide
deck) states a picker can gather 8-10 kg of flowers per day, with
harvesting beginning before sunrise and completed within 4-5 hours —
this time-of-day constraint (flowers must be picked before they open
fully in daylight, to protect stigma quality) is the most operationally
load-bearing fact in this finding, independent of the disputed hour
totals [3]. The drying-weight-loss figure is comparatively well-agreed:
fresh stigmas are roughly 80% water, and saffron loses 80-85% of its
weight during drying [3].

**Finding 5 — saffron's grading standard (ISO 3632) measures three
specific chemical markers, but the standard's own accuracy has been
directly challenged by subsequent research, and adulteration is a
severe, well-documented, ongoing problem in the real-world trade.** ISO
3632-1:2011 sets Category I/II/III thresholds via UV absorbance of an
aqueous extract at three wavelengths: picrocrocin at 257nm (≥70/55/40
for categories I/II/III), safranal at 330nm (20-50, a fixed band rather
than a category-specific floor), and crocin at 440nm (≥200/170/120), all
per 1cm cell on dry matter [4]. Several lower-authority retail sources
describe additional tiers (an "Extra Class" above Category I, and a
four-grade I-IV scheme) that do not match the official 2011 standard's
actual category structure — kept visible as a genuine source-authority
conflict, with the official ISO structure treated as authoritative [4].
Critically, peer-reviewed research has directly challenged the
standard's own safranal measurement: one study found the ISO method is
not an accurate way to measure safranal and recommends HPLC instead, and
a separate paper notes that certain crocetin esters (mainly cis-isomers)
can interfere with the ISO absorbance reading [4]. Picrocrocin is a
glycoside that breaks down during drying into safranal — fresh flowers
contain almost no safranal, meaning the aroma compound genuinely does
not exist until the post-harvest drying/curing step chemically creates
it, a real and sourced "the product's own flavor doesn't exist until
processing" fact distinct from (though echoing) this project's own
prior research into how a related curing-dependent flavor compound
forms in another crop [4]. A 2021-2022 EU-wide testing campaign found
11% of tested saffron (within the broader spices category) was
fraudulent, mostly by adulteration with cheaper spices or synthetic
dyes; one survey found only 40% of samples genuinely met ISO Category I,
with average adulteration in the remainder at 36.25% [6]. Safflower
stigma-like florets are a specifically documented adulterant chosen for
their visual similarity to real saffron threads [6]. The ISO 3632
standard itself has a documented detection gap: adulteration with
safflower, marigold, or turmeric up to 20% (w/w) was found to be
undetectable by standard ISO 3632 testing — meaning official grading
does not, by itself, guarantee authenticity [6]. Newer detection methods
(DART mass spectrometry, detecting turmeric/safflower adulteration down
to 3-5%; ¹H-NMR with chemometrics, down to 2.5%; low-cost microscopy
with machine learning; DNA fingerprinting) are active current research
areas, not yet standard trade practice [6].

**Finding 6 — the two best-documented disease pathogens (*Fusarium*
species and, separately, *Sclerotium rolfsii*) both relate to corm rot,
and one study links a specific, long-standing agronomic practice
directly to disease severity.** *Fusarium oxysporum* and *F. gladioli*
are named as serious threats in a Jammu & Kashmir study, and an Italian
survey separately identifies *Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *gladioli*
(corm-transmitted) as the major cause of severe yield loss in Italian
saffron [7]. A different Kashmir report instead names *Sclerotium
rolfsii* as affecting 70-80% of saffron plants across 25 surveyed
commercial fields — a notably high prevalence figure for a single
pathogen, though from a regional survey rather than a controlled trial
[7]. The same Kashmir study explicitly links disease severity to an
agronomic practice: growers using an unusually long planting cycle (up
to 15 years before replanting), combined with non-graded corms and
continual reuse of diseased planting material, is cited as a direct
cause of frequent corm-rot outbreaks — meaning the long replant interval
itself is a documented disease-risk factor, not merely an economic or
labor-scheduling choice [7]. One biological-control trial found
*Trichoderma viride* used as a corm dressing, combined with vermicompost
and neem cake soil application, achieved 98% control of saffron corm rot
under a 2-year planting cycle — the most concrete disease-management
figure found in this project's saffron research [7]. No chilling-
requirement or formal dormancy-trigger figure was found this pass,
though one source notes that improper corm storage (high humidity or
extreme temperature) can disrupt the dormancy cycle and reduce future
flowering — a real finding, but without a quantified threshold [7].

**Finding 7 — national/regional yield figures (kg dried stigma per
hectare) are reasonably consistent across the world's major producers,
and Iran's own yield has measurably declined over recent decades, a
genuinely unusual trend relative to most crops this project has
researched, which more typically show rising yields from breeding or
management improvement over time.** Iran, which produces roughly 90% of
world saffron, shows a yield decline from about 5.1-6 kg/ha three to
four decades ago to roughly 3-3.5 kg/ha more recently (various sources,
2017-era figures) [8]. Other major producers cluster in a similar 4-5
kg/ha range: Spain (La Mancha) at about 4.4 kg/ha (2018), Kashmir
(India) at about 4.6 kg/ha, and Afghanistan grouped at about 4.2 kg/ha
in the same comparison table, while Greece (Kozani) is a notable outlier
at only about 1.5 kg/ha (one source, ambiguous table entry) [8].
Afghanistan's saffron sector has grown substantially in absolute terms
regardless of the per-hectare figure, producing over 67 tonnes in 2023
and ranking second globally behind Iran [8]. No single sourced cause for
Iran's yield decline was found this pass (candidates might include
disease pressure from the long-replant-cycle problem in Finding 6,
climate change, or aging corm stock, but none of these was directly
linked to the decline by a source opened this pass) — flagged as a
genuine open item rather than resolved by inference.

**Finding 8 — saffron's genome size is measured with genuinely
conflicting results across studies, a three-way (not merely two-way)
disagreement.** Chromosome number is well-agreed (2n = 3x = 24, first
established in 1926 and confirmed by modern studies) [9]. Genome size by
flow cytometry, however, disagrees across at least three different
framings: a Turkish study of 15 accessions found 2C nuclear DNA content
of 10.45-10.9 pg; a symposium paper gave 3C nuclear DNA of 9.77 ± 0.02
pg (~4.82 × 10⁹ bp as "1C genome size," with near-identical values in
tissue-culture-derived plants, 9.74 ± 0.02 and 9.73 ± 0.04 pg, confirming
genome-size stability through tissue culture specifically); a draft-
genome paper instead gives a haploid size of about 3.44-3.5 Gb from flow
cytometry and k-mer analysis; and a chromosome-sequencing study
describes the full sterile-triploid genome at roughly 10.5 Gbp [9]. These
values are not necessarily contradictory on close reading — they may
reflect different counting conventions (1C/2C/3C ploidy levels, haploid
vs. triploid totals) — but no single source in this pass reconciled all
of them into one consistent figure, so they are recorded as a genuine,
unresolved multi-way disagreement rather than picked apart definitively.

**Finding 9 — saffron has an exceptionally deep, well-documented history
of both genuine medicinal use and market fraud, with Bronze Age
pictorial evidence that may actually depict a wild relative rather than
the cultivated crop itself.** Minoan Bronze Age frescoes from Santorini
and Knossos depict saffron — one fresco shows a goddess overseeing
saffron-based remedies, another shows a woman treating a bleeding foot
with saffron, and a Knossos fresco depicts a saffron-gathering scene —
but some sources specifically note that the Minoan depictions are now
thought to show *Crocus cartwrightianus* (the wild ancestor species from
Finding 1), not the cultivated, sterile *C. sativus* itself [10].
Persian cultivation at Isfahan and Khorasan is dated as far back as the
10th century BCE, with saffron threads also woven into royal textiles
and carpets offered to divinities — though this specific "royal
offering" detail comes from a lower-authority source and was not
independently cross-verified [10]. A modern pharmacological review
describes saffron as having the largest number of traditional
applications among all medicinal plants, citing use across roughly 90
distinct medical indications historically, including in a historical
medical canon [10]. Saffron's adulteration is independently documented
as a problem spanning "over six centuries," making it one of the
longest continuously documented cases of food fraud this project has
researched so far [6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Autumn-flowering perennial geophyte (corm), no
  cultivar diversity possible since the species is a single sterile
  clonal lineage.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per flowering season, repeated
  annually across the corm's productive life — the flower crop is
  harvested once per autumn flowering window (roughly 15 days per
  Finding 4's sourcing pattern), each flower picked exactly once.
- **Climate:** Mediterranean/semi-arid, cold-hardy — resistant to cold
  down to -18°C and summer heat up to 45°C (Finding 2); full sun
  required.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in a culinary sense — the
  characteristic flavor/aroma compound, safranal, does not exist in the
  fresh stigma; it is created from picrocrocin breakdown only during
  drying (Finding 5); fresh stigmas are not a culinary product.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable — the crop is a sterile autotriploid,
  produces no viable seed, and has been propagated exclusively by
  vegetative corm division for its entire cultivated history (Finding
  1); this is among the clearest "pollination is structurally
  impossible, not merely difficult" cases this project has researched.
- **Yield / composition:** World average roughly 3-5 kg/ha dried stigma,
  sourced across multiple national figures (Finding 7); good yield
  roughly 4.4-4.6 kg/ha (Spain/Kashmir figures); poor yield roughly 1.5
  kg/ha (Greece/Kozani, one ambiguous table entry, lower confidence).
  Iran's own yield has measurably declined over recent decades (5.1-6
  kg/ha several decades ago to ~3-3.5 kg/ha more recently) with no
  sourced cause identified this pass — flagged as a genuine open item
  rather than assumed to be a breeding/management-improvement story
  like most other crops this project has researched (Finding 7).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance high (by analogy from the
  Mediterranean-maquis/semi-arid climate description and summer-heat
  tolerance to 45°C, Finding 2 — not an independently sourced drought-
  tolerance figure itself). Waterlogging tolerance low — marked
  susceptibility to root and corm rot in poorly drained soil; drainage
  is described as an essential prerequisite for planting, not merely
  beneficial (Finding 2/6).
- **Establishment:** Vegetative corm division is the only possible
  propagation method, since the crop produces no viable seed at all
  (Finding 1) — stronger and more absolute than most "propagated
  vegetatively" crops this project has researched, which usually retain
  seed viability as a theoretical (if commercially unused) alternative.
  Corms are planted in summer (typically June-August in most sourced
  regions) at a density and depth that trade off daughter-corm count,
  individual corm size, and flower yield against each other in ways not
  fully resolved across the agronomic literature opened this pass
  (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** Hand-picking rate roughly 8-10 kg flowers/picker/day,
  harvesting completed within 4-5 hours starting before sunrise (Finding
  4). Stigma-separation labor is a separate, substantial additional task
  (100-130 minutes per 1,000 flowers in one lower-authority source).
  Total labor-hours-per-kg-dried-saffron figures disagree by roughly 10x
  across sources (40 hours vs. 370-480 hours) and are not resolved this
  pass, with the higher range treated as more representative given
  multiple independent sources converge near it.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  clay-loam/drainage emphasis. Root/corm depth not sourced this pass —
  the corm sits below soil surface at a genuinely disputed planting
  depth, 8-20cm across sources (Finding 3), not resolved.
- **Stand life:** No single sourced economic stand-life figure was
  found. One Kashmir study explicitly names an unusually long replanting
  cycle (up to 15 years) as a disease-risk factor rather than treating
  longer stand life as simply more profitable, in contrast to most
  perennial crops this project has researched where a longer productive
  lifespan is unambiguously better; a biocontrol trial achieving 98%
  corm-rot control was specifically conducted under a much shorter 2-
  year planting cycle, suggesting (but not proving) that shorter cycles
  may be the real disease-management recommendation despite the extra
  replanting labor (Finding 6).
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Fusarium* corm rot and *Sclerotium rolfsii*
  corm rot (Finding 6); waterlogging-driven root/corm rot generally
  (Finding 2). No insect pest was sourced this pass — a gap, though
  possibly a genuine reflection of the crop's short above-ground
  flowering window limiting pest exposure relative to a full-season
  crop (not confirmed).
- **Genome:** Genuinely unresolved across at least three different
  framings (2C ~10.45-10.9 pg; 1C/3C ~9.7-9.8 pg per a tissue-culture-
  stability study; haploid ~3.44-3.5 Gb per a draft-genome paper; full
  triploid ~10.5 Gbp per a chromosome-sequencing study) (Finding 8).
  Chromosome number (2n = 3x = 24) is, by contrast, consistently and
  repeatedly confirmed from 1926 to the present.
- **Cultivars:** No cultivars in the conventional sense can exist —
  saffron is a single, globally uniform clonal lineage descended from
  one ancient triploid-formation event, per the genomic evidence in
  Finding 1. Regional production names (Iranian, Kashmiri/"Mongra,"
  Spanish/La Mancha, Greek/Kozani) describe geographic origin and, in
  some commercial contexts, grading/sorting tradition, not genetically
  distinct cultivated varieties.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — the whole flower is picked once, at the
  single moment it opens; there is no genuinely different downstream
  product depending on a later/earlier pick. Stigma separation is a
  separate post-harvest processing step applied uniformly to every
  picked flower, not a harvest-stage choice itself. Single window — one
  autumn flowering period per year, roughly 15 days long, with no second
  flush or bloom window sourced this pass.
- **Storage:** Dried threads lose 80-85% of fresh weight during drying
  (Finding 4); no specific target residual-moisture percentage was
  sourced for the finished dried product itself. See Estimates and
  derivations for the storage-duration estimate.

## Hazards

Corm rot from two documented pathogen groups — *Fusarium* species
(*F. oxysporum*, *F. gladioli*, and specifically *F. oxysporum* f. sp.
*gladioli* in Italy) and, separately, *Sclerotium rolfsii* (70-80% field
prevalence in one Kashmir survey) — both linked to excess soil moisture
and poor drainage, and specifically worsened by an overly long (up to
15-year) replanting cycle using non-graded, disease-carrying corms
(Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — *Colchicum autumnale* ("autumn crocus" or "meadow
saffron," a visually similar but taxonomically unrelated plant, family
Colchicaceae rather than Iridaceae) is a severe colchicine-poisoning
risk commonly confused with true saffron crocus due to shared bloom
season and overlapping common names. This is a human-health
misidentification hazard, deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — saffron itself (not a misidentified plant) has
genuine uterine-stimulant/abortifacient effects at doses well above
normal culinary use (commonly cited around 5g or more), with reported
serious-adverse-effect thresholds near 5g/day and potentially fatal
doses cited in the 12-20g range (sources explicitly caution these are
not precise LD figures). This dose-dependent hazard is likewise deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** corms planted in summer → autumn flowering (roughly 15
  days) → flowers hand-picked before full daylight each morning →
  stigmas separated by hand the same day → dried (80-85% weight loss) →
  graded by chemical marker thresholds (ISO 3632).
- Petal use (traditional saffron-petal tea and natural-dye applications)
  is a genuine real-world secondary use of this crop's primary byproduct,
  left unresearched this pass pending dedicated sourcing.

## Open research questions

- No single reliable annual corm-multiplication-factor figure was found
  (Finding 3) — daughter-cormlet count per mother corm (1-10) is
  sourced, but a consistent per-hectare annual multiplication rate was
  not found across the Iranian and Italian trial literature opened this
  pass, which use incompatible regional conditions.
- Planting depth genuinely conflicts across three distinct claims
  (Finding 3): 15cm optimal (one Italian trial), shallower-is-better for
  daughter-corm count (a separate source), and depth having minimal
  impact at all (a 2025 study, recommending 8cm regardless) — not
  resolved this pass.
- Labor-hours-per-kilogram figures disagree by roughly 10x (Finding 4)
  and all sourced figures are commercial/retail rather than peer-
  reviewed or extension-service sources.
- The official ISO 3632 category structure and various commercial
  "Extra Class"/four-tier grading schemes genuinely conflict (Finding 5)
  — not resolved; the official 2011 standard's I/II/III structure is
  treated as authoritative here, but commercial trade usage may follow
  different, non-standard tier names in practice.
- Saffron's actual economic stand-life/replanting-cycle recommendation
  is unclear and possibly in tension with itself: a long cycle (up to
  15 years) is identified as a disease-risk factor in one Kashmir study,
  while a much shorter 2-year cycle is the context for the most
  successful biocontrol trial found — but no source directly compared
  yield-over-lifetime economics between a long vs. short replanting
  cycle to settle which is actually more profitable once disease losses
  are accounted for.
- No insect pest was sourced for saffron this pass — possibly a genuine
  reflection of the crop's unusually short above-ground flowering
  window, but not confirmed by any source explicitly addressing pest
  pressure (or its absence) for this crop.
- No soil-chemistry (boron, chloride, micronutrient, heavy-metal)
  figures sourced — only the general clay-loam/drainage-emphasis finding
  from Finding 2.
- Saffron's short above-ground flowering window and below-ground
  dormant-corm growth habit may make it an unusually tractable
  controlled-environment/off-world candidate relative to other
  spice/herb crops this project has researched — flagged as a candidate
  for future research, not independently explored this pass.
- Iran's measured yield decline (5.1-6 kg/ha several decades ago to
  ~3-3.5 kg/ha recently) has no sourced cause (Finding 7) — candidates
  (disease pressure from Finding 6's long-replant-cycle problem, climate
  change, aging corm stock) were not directly linked to the decline by
  any source opened this pass.
- Genome size remains unresolved across at least three different
  measurement framings (Finding 8) — worth a dedicated comparative-
  genomics source that explicitly reconciles 1C/2C/3C-ploidy-level
  reporting conventions across these studies.
- Minoan fresco evidence may depict the wild ancestor, not the
  cultivated crop (Finding 9) — a genuine, sourced caveat on what is
  often cited as "the earliest depiction of saffron."
- The Persian royal-textile/divine-offering tradition detail comes from
  a single lower-authority source and was not independently cross-
  verified (Finding 9).
- Petal use (traditional saffron-petal tea and natural-dye applications)
  is a genuine real-world secondary use of this crop's primary byproduct,
  left unresearched this pass pending dedicated sourcing.
- No chilling-requirement or formal dormancy-trigger figure was found,
  though improper corm storage (high humidity or extreme temperature)
  is reported to disrupt the dormancy cycle and reduce future flowering,
  without a quantified threshold (Finding 6).

## Estimates and derivations

A grow-days figure of 90-120 days is estimated for time from summer
corm planting (typically cited as June-August across sources) to first
autumn flowering within the same season: no source opened this pass
gave a specific day-count figure. 90-120 days was chosen as a plausible
middle estimate consistent with the typical June/July-to-October/
November planting-to-flowering window implied narratively by multiple
sources, none of which gave the figure explicitly as a day-count. A
dedicated agronomic phenology study tracking corm-planting date against
first-flower date would replace this estimate; plausible range 60-150
days given how widely planting-month guidance itself varies by region.

A dried-saffron storage duration of 730 days (2 years) is estimated:
sourced figures from low-authority retail/consumer sources range from 6
months to 3-4 years, with "airtight, dark, cool, dry" agreed as the
storage regime but the duration itself unreconciled. 730 days was chosen
as a middle-of-range figure, broadly consistent with this project's
other long-shelf-life dried spice products. A food-science or spice-
industry extension source giving a tested shelf-life/quality-
degradation curve (ideally tied to the ISO 3632 chemical markers in
Finding 5, since picrocrocin/safranal/crocin content — not just moisture
— likely degrades over storage time) would replace this estimate;
plausible range 1-4 years given the spread in sourced claims.
