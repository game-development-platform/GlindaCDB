## Naming and scope

*Citrus hystrix* DC., family Rutaceae, the first species placed in
*Citrus* subgenus *Papeda* ("Mauritius papeda") [1][2]. Names: makrut
lime, Thai lime, kaffir lime, limau purut (Malay), jeruk purut
(Indonesian), combava (French, Indian Ocean islands) [1][2][3]. "Kaffir"
derives from the Arabic *kafir*, adopted as a racial slur in South
Africa, where "Thai lime" is now preferred [1]. This document uses
**makrut lime** as the primary name, with "kaffir lime" recorded only as
a historical alias. *C. micrantha* may be the same species; genomic work
is incomplete [1] — not treated as a separate crop here.

This is filed as a spice/herb rather than a fruit, since the leaf is the
main product everywhere the crop is farmed; the fruit is a by-product
that collectors take as a bonus [2][3]. This follows the same logic this
project applied to kokum and (separately) to Malabar tamarind — acidulant
rinds/leaves of fruit-family trees filed by their dominant commercial
product rather than by strict botanical family. This project's own lime
research (a different acid-lime *Citrus* crop, grown for juice rather
than leaf) was consulted for shared citrus disease, rootstock, and
water-use background, but makrut lime is a genuinely distinct crop by
product (leaf, not juice), harvest method (whole-canopy cutting with a
sickle), and planting system (a dense, closely spaced hedge).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — makrut lime is farmed as a cut-leaf hedge, not an
orchard, with two substantially different commercial stand designs
reported across two countries.** In one Indonesian production region
(about 2,080 ha, the only semi-intensive production center reported in
that country), grafted seedlings are planted 50 × 50 cm, about 40,000
per ha, on rain-fed land [2]. Leaves can be cut from the 4th month after
planting. Most farmers cut twice a year for labor efficiency, sometimes
delaying up to two months for price. The whole canopy is cut with a
sickle 10-30 cm above the ground, and the plant regrows for "another
period of six years" [2]. Yield: about 100 g of leaves per plant per
season, so about 4 t/ha per cut; a national citrus research institute
reports more than 10 t/ha from two seasons [2]. A Malaysian orchard
system is different: grafted trees at 2.4 × 3 m (about 1,380 per ha),
economic leaf harvests from year 5, picking every 3-4 months, about 4.5
t fresh leaves/ha/yr [3]. A pruning trial found 20% more growth and 22%
more yield when 30 cm of main stem was left at cutting rather than 10 cm
[10]. This is a cut-and-come-again woody perennial with two quite
different stand designs, both recorded here as cultivation paths.

**Finding 2 — the labor is concentrated in stripping the leaves from
the harvested stems, a distinct, piece-rate, largely female-labor
processing step.** Men cut and carry, paid a daily wage; women strip the
leaves from the spiny stems in groups of three to five, paid per 100 g
of fresh leaves, with gloves made from old clothes or bare hands [2].
Leaves are packed in 10 kg sacks and must be sold quickly: "the
postharvest life of kaffir lime leaves stored in the collector's houses
is only 24 hours" without cooling [2]. Stripped stems are sold as fuel
for the leaf-oil distilleries, and the ash goes back to the fields [2].

**Finding 3 — the leaf is processed three ways (sold fresh, dried into
leaf flour, or distilled into leaf oil), with oil-yield figures
disagreeing across sources, and the fruit is a secondary, price-tiered
by-product.** Leaf oil: steam distillation in home-industry furnaces
holding 400 kg of leaves, 6-7 hours, 0.5-0.6% oil (Indonesia) [2]; 0.8%
recovery and about 36 L oil per ha per year (Malaysia) [3]; 1.2-1.4% in
laboratory distillations [4] — three disagreeing yield figures, not
reconciled here. The oil is up to 80% (S)-citronellal [1] (61.7-72.5% in
Malaysian leaf oils [4]). Leaf flour: leaves sun-dried about 2 days
until they rustle, then ground [2]. Fruit: collected during the dry-
season leaf cut; "the more fruits that emerge, the less leaves produced
in a tree" — a direct fruit-vs-leaf production tradeoff. Small fruit
quantities are given free to the collector; above a threshold, fruit is
sold by weight [2]. The rind goes into Lao and Thai curry pastes,
combava zest in Réunion, Mauritius, and Madagascar, and candied rind in
Cambodia [1]; peel oil is reported at about 2.8% [4].

**Finding 4 — citrus greening disease drives both plant-health
management and, in the United States, a formal interstate trade-
quarantine rule.** The most threatening disease in the Indonesian
production region studied is citrus vein phloem degeneration (citrus
greening/HLB), spread by the Asian citrus psyllid; certified clean
seedlings from the national citrus research institute are the primary
defense [2]. In the United States, a 2017 federal regulatory notice
established a Federal Order allowing interstate movement of fresh,
mature makrut lime (and curry and bael) leaves for consumption out of
areas quarantined for citrus greening and the Asian citrus psyllid,
under compliance agreements, limited permits, federal certificates,
inspection, and labelling [5]. Malaysian growers separately report leaf-
miner (low levels) and citrus canker, controlled by pruning out infected
plants [3].

**Finding 5 — the fruit is phototoxic on skin contact followed by
sunlight exposure, yet is traditionally used as a hair and clothes
wash.** Peel and pulp contain "significant furanocoumarins," and
phytophotodermatitis has been documented after external use [1]. The
juice is traditionally used as a hair and clothes wash in Thailand and
Cambodia, and fruit slices go into Cambodian lustral water [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Thorny evergreen shrub or small tree, 2-11 m
  [1] (6-25 ft [7]); "double" leaves with a winged petiole [1]; kept as
  a cut hedge 10-30 cm high in leaf production [2]. Perennial.
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again (leafy stems cut with a
  sickle, twice a year in hedges [2], every 3-4 months in orchards [3]);
  fruit is a minor, separate perennial-bearing by-product.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical (frost-tender) [1][6]. USDA zones
  reported variously as 9-12 across grower/nursery sources; damaged
  below about 4°C (40°F), may survive briefly to −2°C (28°F) [6].
  "Mildly frost hardy... short, mild frosts" [7]. Lowland to 400 m
  (Malaysia) [3]; 90 m in the Indonesian production region studied [2].
  25-38°C [3]; a 5-6 month wet season is reported as needed for optimum
  growth [3]; one region's own rainfall is 1,717 mm/yr at 25.5°C mean
  [2].
- **Food status:** Not raw edible as food; the leaves are a flavoring,
  usually removed or shredded fine [1]. Not researched at the
  calorie/protein level (a flavoring leaf eaten in tiny amounts).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the leaf crop; fruit-set pollination
  mechanism not researched.
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh stripped leaves: roughly 4.5 t/ha/yr
  (Malaysian orchard figure) to about 4 t/ha per single cut in the
  Indonesian hedge system (40,000 plants × 100 g), with the same source
  reporting more than 10 t/ha from two harvesting seasons under good
  conditions. About 100 g leaves per plant per season in the hedge
  system. Hedge and orchard systems differ about 30-fold in plant
  density, yet produce similar per-hectare yields.
- **Water use:** No published crop-coefficient curve found specific to
  this crop; citrus-crop water-use patterns were consulted by analogy
  given the shared genus. Needs good drainage; one region's water table
  sits about 50 cm below the surface in the rainy season [3]. Dry-
  season (June-July) leaves are reported smaller, thinner, and lower
  quality, with more pests [2]. No irrigation scheduling method was
  sourced; fields in the primary Indonesian production region are rain-
  fed, sometimes watered by bucket or pump from rivers and ditches [2].
- **Establishment:** Land cleared, hand-hoed (watered to ease tillage),
  manure and dolomite added; planting 3 days after tillage [2]. Malaysian
  planting holes are 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 m [3]. Hedge system first cut at 4
  months, cut for about 6 years [2]; orchard system's economic harvest
  begins from year 5 [3]; productive life beyond that was not
  researched.
- **Labor:** Wage and piece-rate figures are sourced for the Indonesian
  hedge system (a daily wage for cutters, a per-100g piece rate for
  leaf-stripping labor), but specific labor-hour figures per operation
  were not independently sourced and would need to be estimated from
  these wage figures combined with assumed throughput rates, which this
  document does not attempt definitively.
- **Soil:** Fertilizer guidance from one source: roughly 20 g N, 10 g
  P2O5, 5 g K2O per tree, applied once after planting, sometimes again
  at month 3; manure, compost, and dolomite added at tillage [2]. Soil
  pH optimum reported at 6.0 [3], with the broader citrus pH range (used
  as an analogy given the shared genus) running roughly 5.0-7.5.
- **Stand life:** Hedge: first cut at 4 months, cut for about 6 years
  [2]; orchard: economic harvest from year 5 [3]; productive life beyond
  that not researched.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Reported in the primary Indonesian
  production region: leaf miner, mites, aphids, thrips, lime butterfly,
  a fungal gummosis, powdery mildew, citrus canker, sooty mould, scab;
  citrus greening (CVPD/HLB) is the most threatening disease [2]. A leaf
  blight fungal disease is separately reported in northern Thailand
  [13]. Dry-season leaves are small, thin, and "smutty" [2].
- **Establishment / propagation:** Vegetative — grafted seedlings in
  commercial plantings [2][3]; cuttings and layers also root readily
  [11]. Farmers do not use seed commercially [2], though one source
  reports fresh seed germinates in 2-4 weeks [11] and another states
  seeds are "not available" [7] — a direct source conflict, not
  resolved here. Named rootstocks used in Indonesia: Rough Lemon (*C.
  jambhiri*) and a second seedling rootstock [2]; Malaysian sources
  report "grafted seedling using resistant root stocks" without naming
  them [3]. Citrus greening travels in budwood and nursery stock, so
  certified clean seedlings are the primary defense against introducing
  it via planting material [2].
- **Cultivars:** No named commercial cultivars with sourced trait deltas
  were found; the variation found is between production systems (hedge
  vs. orchard) and seasons (wet vs. dry), not named cultivars. Three
  regional production/use types are recorded: a hedge type grafted on
  Rough Lemon/a second rootstock, cut 10-30 cm twice a year (Indonesia);
  a landrace grown for zest and rind rather than leaves (combava,
  Mauritius/Réunion); and a leaf-export-focused regional type (Thailand,
  reported as the largest producer of makrut leaf products, followed by
  Indonesia, Malaysia, and India) [12].
- **Harvest:** Single stage — the leaf and fruit are different
  harvested products, not maturity stages of the same product.

## Hazards

Furanocoumarins in the peel and pulp are a phototoxin: fruit, juice, or
peel oil on skin followed by sunlight exposure can cause
phytophotodermatitis, a hazard directly in tension with the crop's own
traditional use as a hair and clothes wash (Finding 5).

Thorns on the spiny stems are a physical hazard during hand stripping of
harvested leaves, traditionally done with gloves made from old cloth or
bare hands [2].

A regulatory hazard applies in trade: in the United States, interstate
movement of fresh makrut lime leaves is restricted by a citrus-greening/
Asian-citrus-psyllid quarantine and is only permitted under a specific
federal compliance framework [5].

No drug-interaction finding was located — makrut lime is a papeda, not a
pomelo hybrid, and the well-known grapefruit-type drug interaction is
not established for this species.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (leaf):** leafy stems cut by sickle → leaves stripped (stems
  used as distillery fuel) → sold fresh within 24 hours, chilled, or
  sun-dried about 2 days and ground to leaf flour.
- **Chain (leaf oil):** leaves → steam distillation, 6-7 hours → leaf
  oil, roughly 0.5-1.4% yield depending on source (up to 80% (S)-
  citronellal).
- **Chain (fruit):** fruit → rind (curry paste, combava zest; candied in
  Cambodia) and pulp (traditional hair and clothes wash) → rind
  sometimes steam-distilled for peel oil (~2.8%).

## Open research questions

- Several source conflicts were not resolved: planting system (40,000
  plants/ha hedge cut from month 4 vs. 1,380 trees/ha orchard with
  economic harvest from year 5); harvest frequency (twice a year vs.
  every 3-4 months); leaf-oil yield (0.5-0.6% vs. 0.8% vs. 1.2-1.4%
  laboratory); and propagation (grafted only, no farmer seed use, vs.
  cuttings/layers rooting easily vs. "seeds are not available").
- No named cultivar with sourced trait deltas was found.
- Leaf composition and per-tonne nutrient removal were not researched.
- Fruit yield specifically (as distinct from leaf yield) was not
  researched.
- Thai production statistics and the leaf-export trade were not
  researched in depth beyond one supplier/market-overview source.
- Leaf cold-storage regime and chilling injury were not fully resolved
  — sources disagree on refrigerated shelf life (1-2 weeks vs. 3-4 weeks
  wrapped/sealed vs. a 21-day chilling-storage study design vs. a 10-day
  patent figure at 5-10°C).
- Frost limits were sourced only from grower/nursery secondary sources,
  not a primary horticultural study.
- The pruning/nitrogen-fertilizer trial referenced could not be read in
  full (access blocked) — only its headline growth/yield-improvement
  figures were available.
- Whether the grapefruit-type drug interaction applies to this species
  was not established either way.
- No wild-harvest or wild-gathering practice was sourced for this
  species, despite its native range in tropical Southeast Asia — this
  document does not treat it as a wild/dual-mode-harvested crop absent
  such a source.
- Labor-hour figures per operation (harvesting, stripping, weeding,
  planting) were not independently sourced — only wage and piece-rate
  figures were found, from which hour estimates would need to be
  derived with an assumed throughput rate.
- A specific crop-coefficient curve for this crop was not sourced;
  general citrus water-use patterns were consulted only as a loose
  analogy given the shared genus.
