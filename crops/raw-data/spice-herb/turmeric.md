# Turmeric (*Curcuma longa*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/turmeric.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

No crop already researched in this project shares turmeric's structure
closely — ginger, its nearest taxonomic and structural relative (same
family, Zingiberaceae, same rhizome-geophyte growth habit, same boil/dry
curing chain), is this project's own closest sibling, grounded against
loosely for the general propagation convention (vegetative seed-rhizome
division), but turmeric's specific curing chain (boiling, sun-drying,
hand/drum polishing) and its chemistry (curcuminoid pigment/flavor
compounds) are treated as standalone research.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — turmeric is a sterile-to-partially-fertile polyploid
rhizome crop with a genuinely unresolved cytogenetic picture, more
complex even than saffron's own already-flagged multi-way genome-size
disagreement.** *Curcuma longa* belongs to family Zingiberaceae and is
a perennial rhizomatous herb, cultivated commercially as an annual,
native to tropical South Asia (the Indian subcontinent specifically)
[1]. Synonyms include *Curcuma domestica* and *Curcuma tinctoria* [1].
The plant grows 60-90cm tall, and the commercial product is the rhizome
after processing (boiling, drying, cleaning, polishing) [1].
Cytogenetically, turmeric is reported at 2n = 3x = 63 (triploid, basic
chromosome number x = 21) by the most commonly cited recent figure, but
the wider genus *Curcuma* shows an extraordinary spread of reported
chromosome counts across different studies — 2n = 22, 42, 63, >70, 77,
and 105 have all been reported across the genus, and even within
cultivated *C. longa* specifically, nuclear DNA content has been found
to vary by up to 15.1% across samples in one flow-cytometry study of
161 accessions [9]. Sterility is reported but not uniform: a Kerala
breeding study describes medium- and long-duration turmeric types as
sterile triploids (citing earlier secondary work), yet the same study
reports actual seed set in 3 of 12 attempted crosses, specifically only
via in-vivo stigmatic pollination (not stylar pollination), with
pollen-tube failure to reach the ovules proposed as the likely
mechanism limiting (not eliminating) fertility [5]. Open-pollinated
seedling progenies of a 2n=63 triploid variety showed chromosome counts
ranging from 2n=78 to 2n=84 (most frequently 84), and a separate study
found seedling-progeny counts spanning 2n=63 to 2n=86 — meaning seed-
derived progeny do occur in the field at some rate, contradicting a
simple "triploid, therefore completely sterile" model [5]. No source
opened this pass directly quantified pollen viability or an actual
seed-set percentage for turmeric — this sterility question is recorded
as a genuine, unresolved partial-fertility finding, not settled either
way.

**Finding 2 — genome-size estimates for turmeric disagree across
methods in a pattern now familiar from this project's saffron research,
though the disagreement here is somewhat narrower.** One genome-
sequencing paper cites a database estimate of 1.33 Gbp (at 2n=63),
reporting a wider literature range of chromosome-number variation from
2n=48 to 2n=64 across sources it reviewed; the same paper's own
sequencing effort instead estimated a smaller genome of 1.15 Gbp, with
its actual draft assembly coming out smaller still at 1.02 Gbp
(containing roughly 70% repetitive sequence) [9]. This three-way spread
(1.33 vs. 1.15 vs. 1.02 Gbp, all from within one source's own
reporting) is narrower in absolute terms than saffron's own multi-Gb-
scale disagreement, but reflects the same general pattern seen across
this project's recent spice/herb research: polyploid, vegetatively-
propagated spice crops with repetitive-sequence-heavy genomes are
consistently harder to assemble cleanly than simpler diploid crops.

**Finding 3 — turmeric's own cultivation-climate requirements are
comparatively well-agreed across Indian government extension sources, a
contrast with several of this project's earlier spice/herb entries
where even basic climate figures disagreed across nearly every source
opened.** Optimal temperature is given as 20-30°C by one extension
source and roughly 20-35°C across vegetative and rhizome-development
phases by another — broadly consistent rather than conflicting [1].
Altitude range differs only modestly across sources (sea level to
1,200m vs. sea level to 1,500m) [1]. Rainfall figures are likewise
consistent: 1,500mm/year or more by one source, 1,500-2,000mm (well-
distributed) by another, with one review specifically noting that
excess rainfall and poor drainage can be more damaging to the crop than
a moderate short-term moisture deficit — the same "drainage-matters-
more-than-total-rainfall" pattern already seen in this project's
saffron research [1][3]. Soil guidance favors fertile, well-drained
loamy soil with good organic content, with one lower-authority source
giving a pH range of 5.5-7.5 [1].

**Finding 4 — seed-rhizome requirements, planting density, and curing/
polishing recovery rates are all reasonably well-sourced from Indian
agricultural extension material, with one genuinely significant
efficiency finding: a modern seedling-based multiplication technique
can cut seed-rhizome requirements by roughly 80%.** The conventional
"present farmers' practice" seed rate is 2,000-2,500 kg of rhizomes per
hectare, but a university study reports that a rapid-multiplication
technique using seedlings instead of whole rhizome pieces needs only
about 500 kg of seed rhizomes to plant the same hectare — a roughly 4-5x
reduction in seed-material requirement, a genuinely significant
agronomic efficiency finding not flagged as disputed by any source
opened this pass [2]. Planting is done in raised beds, with one source
giving 30cm × 30cm optimum spacing and roughly 2,000 beds of 3m × 1m
prepared per hectare [2]. Fresh (green) rhizome yield figures cluster
reasonably tightly: 20-25 tonnes/ha by one source, 20,000-22,000 kg/ha
by another (essentially the same figure in different units), while one
specific research trial reported 33.85 t/ha fresh and 6.97 t/ha cured —
a notably higher fresh-yield figure than the general extension range,
from a single research-trial source rather than a general average [2].
Curing requires boiling the rhizomes — 45-60 minutes for finger
rhizomes, 90 minutes for mother rhizomes, with operators judging
completion by the appearance of froth and fumes rather than a fixed
timer; over-boiling damages color, under-boiling leaves the dried
product brittle [2]. Drying follows, either in the sun or artificially
at around 60°C [2]. Polished-turmeric recovery from raw cured material
is reported inconsistently: one source gives 15-25% as the "yield of
polished turmeric from raw material," a second gives 10-30% depending
on variety and location, and a third gives a narrower 19-23% — all
describing essentially the same dry-recovery concept but not reconciled
into one figure [2].

**Finding 5 — rhizome rot (Pythium) is the dominant disease across all
turmeric-growing regions sourced this pass, with a documented disease-
severity interaction with nematode infection, while the dominant insect
pest is a stem borer with inconsistent sourced chemical-control
recommendations.** *Pythium aphanidermatum* is identified as the cause
of rhizome rot, described by one source as "the major problem in all
turmeric growing areas," with losses of up to 30% [8]. One source
specifically notes that rhizome rot severity is aggravated by nematode
infection, making nematode-free planting material a documented disease-
management lever, not merely a general plant-health recommendation [8].
Biological control (*Trichoderma harzianum* seed treatment and soil
application, sometimes combined with neem cake) and chemical control
(metalaxyl-mancozeb seed dip and soil drench) are both recommended,
alongside two named partially-resistant/tolerant lines [8]. Leaf
blotch, caused by *Taphrina maculans*, is a second named disease,
typically appearing October-November, favored specifically by 80%
relative humidity and 21-23°C — a precise, sourced dose-response-style
trigger uncommon among this project's other disease findings — with
mancozeb spray (0.2%) and crop rotation as the recommended controls
[8]. The turmeric shoot borer (*Conogethes punctiferalis*) is described
as the crop's most serious insect pest, identifiable by a borehole in
the pseudostem with a withered central shoot; recommended insecticides
genuinely disagree across sources (dimethoate 0.05% or quinalphos 0.05%
in one source; malathion 0.1% reapplied at 30-day intervals from July
to October in another) [8].

**Finding 6 — curcumin, turmeric's signature bioactive/colorant
compound, has well-documented poor oral bioavailability, and the
widely-repeated claim that black pepper (piperine) dramatically boosts
its absorption rests on thinner, more mixed evidence than popular
framing suggests.** A 2019 pharmacological review lists multiple causes
of curcumin's poor absorption: low water solubility, weak intestinal-
membrane permeability, chemical instability at alkaline pH, and rapid
metabolism [6]. The famous "2,000% increase" figure traces to a single
1998 rat-and-human study, with a national food-safety regulator's own
review of that work reporting a 154% increase in rats but up to 2,000%
in the human arm — a figure widely repeated by commercial sources
without this context [6]. The proposed mechanism is that piperine
inhibits glucuronidation (a liver/intestinal metabolic pathway),
reducing curcumin's breakdown rather than directly improving its
absorption [6]. A more recent (2023) human pilot study, explicitly
described by the regulator as limited to three volunteers, found black
pepper roughly doubled curcumin's plasma half-life (2.2 to 4.5 hours)
and increased urinary excretion — the regulator's own assessment
explicitly notes that few other human studies directly confirm the
piperine-boosts-curcumin-bioavailability claim at all [6]. Formulation-
based approaches (micelles, nano/microemulsions, nanoparticles,
liposomes, solid dispersions) are described as alternative, more
heavily-engineered ways to improve oral bioavailability, distinct from
the simple "cook turmeric with black pepper" folk practice [6]. This
finding is recorded for culinary/pharmacological context; this project
does not model bioavailability or supplement formulation.

**Finding 7 — turmeric adulteration with lead chromate is a severe,
well-documented, ongoing South Asian public-health problem, a
genuinely more acute food-safety hazard than any adulteration issue
flagged elsewhere in this project's research (including saffron's own
documented, but non-toxic, adulteration problem).** Researchers report
that turmeric processors in Bangladesh began adulterating the crop with
lead chromate (a substance banned as a food additive) around the
1980s, reportedly beginning after floods dulled crop color, as a cheap,
fast way to restore a desirable bright-yellow appearance [7]. A 2024
study analyzing 356 samples across South Asia found turmeric lead
levels exceeding 1,000 μg/g in Patna (Bihar, India) and in Karachi and
Peshawar (Pakistan) — an extreme contamination level, not a marginal
exceedance [7]. Unlike saffron's adulteration problem (economic fraud
via cheaper spices or synthetic dyes, generally not acutely toxic
itself), lead chromate is directly poisonous: it acts as a neurotoxin,
and its hexavalent-chromium component is independently recognized as
carcinogenic by Indian health authorities [7]. Documented health
effects from prolonged consumption include damage to the brain, nervous
system, liver, and kidneys, impaired reproductive health, and stunted
physical/cognitive development in children [7]. One cited study used
lead-isotope fingerprinting to identify adulterated turmeric as the
most likely source of elevated lead levels found in affected
populations' blood [7]. India's food-safety regulator has declared lead
chromate use in food illegal, and researchers have proposed import-
inspection screening using X-ray detection devices as a mitigation
measure [7].

**Finding 8 — turmeric's religious and cultural significance in
Hinduism is deep and well-documented for its ritual role, though
several specific claims (Vedic-period dating, the "golden spice of the
sun" association) come from lower-authority sources and are flagged
accordingly.** Turmeric (Sanskrit/Ayurvedic name *haridra*) is central
to Hindu weddings and worship — the *haldi* ceremony applies turmeric
paste to the bride and groom before marriage, and temple practice
includes anointing deity idols and offering turmeric in *puja*, with
one source specifically describing its use in South Indian Amman-
temple traditions [10]. An early-20th-century scholarly reference
(1913) documents turmeric and saffron's joint use in Hindu marriage
ceremonies as an already long-established custom by that point [10].
Lower-authority sources additionally claim a Vedic-period association
between turmeric and the sun (as "the golden spice") and attribute its
main indigenous ritual uses to fertility and spiritual-purification
rites — these specific claims were not independently cross-verified
against a primary Vedic-literature source this pass and are flagged
with lower confidence [10]. Ayurvedic medicinal use is dated
inconsistently across sources: one gives "at least 2,500 years,"
another gives establishment in Ayurveda "by around 500 BCE" — both
describing the same general antiquity without a precise reconciled
date [10]. Classical Ayurvedic applications include wound cleaning
(referenced in a classical Hindu text), joint-pain poultices, and
turmeric milk as a cold remedy [10]. Unlike this project's own saffron
religious-use findings (significance without a sourced production
rule), this pass likewise found no sourced constraint on how turmeric
must be grown, harvested, or processed for religious use — the sourced
material concerns the finished product's ritual application, not farm-
level production requirements.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical perennial rhizome, cultivated as an
  annual (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per planting cycle — rhizomes are
  lifted once at the end of each annual growing cycle, not harvested
  repeatedly from a standing plant.
- **Climate:** Tropical, warm-humid — 20-30°C (one source) to 20-35°C
  across growth phases (another); sea level to 1,200-1,500m altitude;
  1,500-2,000mm annual rainfall, well-distributed (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in the standard commercial sense —
  the commercial product is defined by its curing process (boiling,
  drying, polishing); fresh raw rhizome is not the standard culinary
  form, though a distinct fresh-rhizome culinary use does exist in some
  regional cuisines and is flagged as a possible future consideration.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable — propagation is 100% vegetative in
  commercial practice. Fertility is genuinely partial/disputed rather
  than fully sterile (Finding 1), but no source opened this pass
  describes seed propagation as a commercial practice; occasional seed-
  derived progeny appear to occur in the field/breeding context only.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 20,000-25,000 kg/ha (fresh/green
  rhizome, general Indian extension range); exceptional yield ~33,850
  kg/ha (fresh, one research trial, not a general average, lower
  confidence as a representative figure) (Finding 4). The general
  20,000-25,000 kg/ha fresh-rhizome extension range is used as the
  primary representative figure; the research trial's 33,850 kg/ha
  figure is recorded separately as a research-trial outlier, not
  assumed representative of ordinary farm yields.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance assumed low-to-moderate by analogy
  from the general rainfall requirement, not an independently sourced
  drought-tolerance figure. Waterlogging tolerance low — excess rainfall
  and poor drainage are specifically noted as potentially more damaging
  than a moderate moisture deficit; rhizome rot (Pythium) is the
  dominant disease and is a wet-soil-favored pathogen (Finding 3/5).
- **Establishment:** Vegetative rhizome division is the standard
  commercial propagation method; a modern seedling-based rapid-
  multiplication technique can reduce seed-rhizome requirement by
  roughly 80% (500 kg/ha vs. 2,000-2,500 kg/ha conventional) (Finding
  4) — one of the more significant sourced agronomic-efficiency
  findings in this project's recent spice/herb research. Seed rhizomes
  planted in raised beds (30cm × 30cm spacing in one sourced
  recommendation, ~2,000 beds of 3m × 1m per hectare).
- **Labor:** Boiling (45-60 min for finger rhizomes, 90 min for mother
  rhizomes, judged by froth/fume appearance rather than a timer), sun-
  or 60°C-artificial drying, and hand- or drum-polishing are all
  distinct, sourced post-harvest labor operations (Finding 4); no per-
  hectare labor-hour figure was sourced for any of these operations.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced beyond a single lower-
  authority 5.5-7.5 range. Rhizome planted in raised beds; specific
  planting depth was not independently sourced, unlike saffron's own
  (disputed but at least sourced) corm-depth figures.
- **Stand life:** Cultivated as an annual regardless of the plant's
  perennial biology; varieties are described by "short/medium/long
  duration" naming in Indian extension sources, but no source opened
  this pass gave a reconciled day-count figure for each category.
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Pythium aphanidermatum* rhizome rot, the
  dominant disease across all turmeric-growing regions sourced, up to
  30% crop loss, aggravated by nematode infection (Finding 5). Leaf
  blotch (*Taphrina maculans*), with a precise sourced humidity/
  temperature infection trigger (80% RH, 21-23°C). Turmeric shoot borer
  (*Conogethes punctiferalis*), the most serious insect pest.
- **Genome:** Genuinely unresolved across at least three framings from
  within a single source's own reporting (1.33 Gbp database estimate
  vs. 1.15 Gbp from the same paper's own sequencing vs. 1.02 Gbp draft-
  assembly size, ~70% repetitive sequence) (Finding 2) — narrower in
  absolute terms than saffron's own multi-Gb-scale disagreement, but
  following the same general pattern. Chromosome number is also
  genuinely unresolved at the genus level (2n = 22, 42, 63, >70, 77,
  105 variously reported across *Curcuma* species) even though 2n=63
  is the most commonly cited figure specifically for cultivated *C.
  longa* (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** Named short/medium/long "duration" variety groupings
  exist in Indian extension sources, but no specific named cultivar
  with sourced trait deltas (color intensity, curcumin content, disease
  resistance) was found this pass beyond the two partially rot-tolerant
  lines named in Finding 5 — a genuine sourcing gap relative to black
  pepper's well-documented cultivar lineage.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — the whole rhizome clump is lifted once at
  the end of the growing cycle; no genuinely different downstream
  product depends on an earlier/later lift within the same season.
  Single window — one annual harvest per planting cycle; no second
  flush or multi-harvest pattern was sourced this pass.
- **Storage:** Not confidently sourced this pass — no dedicated source
  for dried/polished turmeric shelf life was opened. Dry recovery from
  cured raw material disagrees across sources (15-25%, 10-30%, 19-23%)
  (Finding 4), not reconciled into one figure.

## Hazards

*Pythium aphanidermatum* rhizome rot, the dominant disease across all
turmeric-growing regions sourced, up to 30% crop loss, aggravated by
nematode infection (Finding 5). Leaf blotch (*Taphrina maculans*), with
a precise sourced humidity/temperature infection trigger (80% RH, 21-
23°C). Turmeric shoot borer (*Conogethes punctiferalis*), the most
serious insect pest.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — widespread lead chromate adulteration in South
Asian turmeric supply chains is an acutely toxic (neurotoxic,
carcinogenic) food-safety hazard: a 2024 study found turmeric lead
levels exceeding 1,000 μg/g in several South Asian cities, with
documented health effects including damage to the brain, nervous
system, liver, and kidneys, impaired reproductive health, and stunted
physical/cognitive development in children (Finding 7). This is
deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data — the first adulteration hazard this project has recorded that is
deliberately introduced and acutely toxic, rather than a dose-dependent
property of the food itself or a misidentification risk.

Curcumin's own poor oral bioavailability and the thinly-evidenced
piperine-synergy claim are recorded as a culinary/pharmacological note,
not a safety hazard (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed rhizome or multiplication seedling planted in raised
  beds → single annual harvest, rhizome clump lifted → boiled (45-90
  minutes depending on rhizome type) → dried (sun or ~60°C artificial)
  → hand- or drum-polished → ground into the finished spice.

## Open research questions

- Turmeric's fertility status is genuinely unresolved (Finding 1):
  sterility is commonly cited but the same source reporting it also
  documents actual (if low-rate, hand-pollination-context-only) seed
  set; no source opened this pass directly quantified pollen viability
  or a field seed-set percentage.
- Fresh-rhizome culinary use (as distinct from the cured/dried/polished
  commercial product) was not independently researched this pass —
  some regional cuisines reportedly use fresh turmeric rhizome
  directly; flagged as a possible future addition.
- No per-hectare labor-hour figures were sourced for boiling, drying,
  or polishing.
- No dedicated finished-product (dried/polished turmeric) shelf-life
  source was found — a genuine gap; this document does not even have
  the kind of disputed-but-present figures saffron had for its own
  finished-product storage life.
- "Short/medium/long duration" variety-class day-counts are not
  reconciled to a specific figure by any source opened this pass.
- No named cultivar with sourced trait deltas (beyond the two partially
  rot-tolerant lines) was found — a genuine sourcing gap relative to
  black pepper's well-documented cultivar lineage.
- No soil-chemistry (boron, chloride, micronutrient, heavy-metal)
  figures sourced beyond a single lower-authority pH range.
- Genome size and chromosome number are both genuinely unresolved at
  the genus level, though less severely than saffron's own flagged
  multi-way disagreement in absolute Gb-scale terms.
- Vedic-period dating and the "golden spice of the sun" association
  come from lower-authority sources and were not independently cross-
  verified against primary Vedic literature.

## Estimates and derivations

A grow-days figure of 210-300 days is estimated: Indian extension
sources describe turmeric varieties by "short/medium/long duration"
naming without this document finding a single reconciled day-count
figure for each category. 210-300 days (roughly 7-10 months) was chosen
as a plausible range consistent with the general framing of turmeric as
an annual crop with a multi-month-long growing season, cross-checked
loosely against the general pattern that most Zingiberaceae rhizome
spice crops run a similar multi-month cycle. A dedicated variety-release
document from an Indian agricultural research institute giving exact
day-counts per duration class would replace this estimate; plausible
range 180-330 days given how widely "duration class" framing varies by
source and region.
