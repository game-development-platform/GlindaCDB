# Lavandin (*Lavandula × intermedia*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/lavandin.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *Lavandula × intermedia*, the sterile natural/cultivated
interspecific hybrid of true lavender (*L. angustifolia*, covered
separately in this project's own research) and spike lavender (*L.
latifolia* Medik., not independently researched as its own crop entry).
It does not re-cover true lavender's own findings except by direct
comparison — see this project's true lavender research for that crop's
own full detail. Most findings below are framed as deltas from that
research.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the lavender/lavandin distinction is a genetically real,
sterile-hybrid-vs-species split, not a cultivar-level variant, with
cultivar-lineage, plant-form, and world-production-volume evidence.**
Lavandin is explicitly described as a hybrid species resulting from
crossing *L. angustifolia* and *L. latifolia* [1] — the same basic
finding this project's own true lavender research already recorded, but
this pass's scoping research added several further independent lines of
evidence. Lavandin stems reach up to 80cm with two lateral branches, and
one named cultivar specifically is described as an evergreen shrub
"much larger than true lavender" [2] — a real, visible plant-size
difference, not merely a chemistry-level distinction. Four named
cultivars have their own independent development history: 'Abrial' (the
first lavandin cultivar, developed in the 1920s), 'Grosso' (developed by
Pierre Grosso in the 1970s specifically for higher yield, prolificacy,
and multi-climate hardiness), 'Super' (distinguished by high linalyl-
acetate content), and 'Sumian' [2] — a cultivar lineage independent of
true lavender's own cultivar history, since lavandin cultivars are
themselves hybrid lines, not selections within *L. angustifolia*. At the
world-production-volume level, one source gives roughly 1,000 tonnes of
lavandin oil produced annually worldwide against roughly 200 tonnes of
true lavender oil [3] — lavandin is the dominant commercial essential
oil of the two by volume, not a minor variant.

**Finding 2 — lavandin's oil yield figures disagree substantially across
sources, in a now-familiar pattern for this project's spice/herb yield
findings, but all sourced ranges agree lavandin yields multiple times
true lavender's own oil output, and the finished oil's chemistry is
consistently and independently reported as sharper/more camphorous
across every source opened this pass.** One Irish extension source gives
lavandin oil yield as 40-220 kg/ha and dried-flower-stem yield as
500-1,000 kg/ha, both explicitly dependent on location, management, and
cultivar [4] — a notably wider range than true lavender's own already-
cited 80-150/100-200 kg/ha figures. A Provence trade-press article
instead gives 150-200 kg/ha [4], and a training-document source gives up
to 120 kg/ha [4] — three sources, three different ranges, none fully
reconciled. All of them, however, agree the figure is multiple times
true lavender's own 15-40 kg/ha range — a consistent multiplier finding
even where the absolute figures disagree. Separately, and independent of
the yield-quantity disagreement, lavandin oil's chemistry is reported
consistently across every source opened this pass: substantially higher
camphor, borneol, and 1,8-cineole content give it a sharper, more
"camphorous" scent versus true lavender's delicate, powdery floral
fragrance [3] — directly consistent with true lavender's own camphor-
content finding, but this pass additionally found that lavandin oil is
explicitly reported as not a pharmacopeial material, unlike true
lavender (and spike lavender) oil [3] — a genuine formal regulatory/
market-status difference.

**Finding 3 — a Turkish field study directly compared micropropagation
against cutting propagation for lavandin specifically, finding
micropropagated plants outyielded cutting-propagated plants in both
floral yield and oil characteristics.** The same study reported fresh
stem/flower yields of up to 7,791 kg/ha from micropropagated plants in
one trial year, with essential-oil content ranging narrowly between
roughly 1.2% and 1.3% of fresh stem flowers (and 6.9-7.4% of dried
flowers) across the two years and plant-origin groups studied [4] — a
notably tight oil-content range compared to some other wide
disagreements this project has found for essential-oil content in other
crops. Harvest is reported as mid-summer generally, with exact timing
varying by cultivar and region [4]; one source specifically notes
flowers should be dry at harvest time for best oil-processing quality
[4] — a genuinely different harvest-timing-to-quality concern from true
lavender's own "degree of blossoming/time of day/weather all affect oil
quality" finding, though not independently cross-checked against that
finding this pass.

**Finding 4 — lavandin's propagation is reported as obligately
vegetative, with no viable seed route at all — a stronger, cleaner
finding than true lavender's own cuttings-primary-but-seed-possible
propagation profile, directly consistent with Finding 1's sterility
finding.** One source states lavandin can only be propagated by
cuttings, "making human intervention essential to its cultivation" [4] —
directly confirming Finding 1's genetic-sterility finding at the
practical-cultivation level, and a genuinely stronger, unqualified
finding than true lavender's own "cuttings strongly preferred, seed a
real secondary route" profile. This document did not find a seed-crop or
isolation-distance figure for lavandin, consistent with it having no
seed-propagation route to source such a figure for at all.

**Finding 5 — lavandin's cold-hardiness is reported, across multiple
sources, as combining true lavender's own cold tolerance with spike
lavender's own heat/humidity tolerance — a genuinely interesting hybrid-
vigor climate-tolerance finding this project has not previously recorded
for any other hybrid crop — though the specific zone-range figures
disagree across sources and one extension source takes the opposite
view.** Two independent sources describe lavandin as combining "the
cold-hardiness of English lavender" with "the heat-tolerance of
Portuguese lavender" (an alternate common name for spike lavender) [5] —
a genuinely different hardiness profile-shape than simply being
somewhere between its two parent species on a single axis, since cold-
tolerance and heat/humidity-tolerance are two different stress axes
being inherited from two different parents. However, one Ontario
extension guide takes the opposite view, stating lavandins are generally
less hardy than other lavenders [5] — directly disagreeing with the
"best of both parents" framing, and not reconciled this pass. Specific
USDA zone figures also disagree across sources: 5-8 for one named
cultivar, 5-9 from a general retailer source, and 5-10 from another [5]
— a wider disagreement spread than true lavender's own single commonly-
cited 5-8 range. One source specifically advises that in Zone 5, where
winter hardiness is less reliable, one named cultivar (itself noted
elsewhere as more accurately an English-lavandin hybrid than a true
lavandin) benefits significantly from a sheltered location and winter
protection [5].

**Finding 6 — lavandin's soil, drainage, and drought-tolerance
requirements closely parallel true lavender's own already-established
findings, with one source giving an even more strongly worded
waterlogging-intolerance statement, and pest/disease findings were
comparatively thin this pass.** Drainage is reported as the single most
consistent requirement, with one source stating lavandin "strongly
rejects wet feet, especially in winter" and a second (Ontario extension)
source stating the plant "will not survive in saturated soils" [6] — at
least as strongly worded as true lavender's own already-established
acute waterlogging intolerance. Soil pH guidance is similarly disputed
across sources as true lavender's own: one gives 6.5-7.5, while a
horticultural society instead lists lavandin as tolerating acid,
alkaline, or neutral soils, and a separate source recommends well-
drained, sandy or gravelly soil with neutral-to-slightly-alkaline pH [6]
— not reconciled. Drought resistance is explicitly confirmed by the
horticultural society source, and established plants are reported to
generally not require irrigation [6] — directly paralleling true
lavender's own established-plant drought-tolerance finding. Pest/disease
findings were comparatively thin this pass: one source notes rabbits and
deer avoid the plant entirely, but that cultural stress from humidity or
poor drainage invites fungal issues, with soil-texture improvement
(adding sand/grit) given as a remedy for root rot specifically [6] —
directly consistent with true lavender's own water-management-driven
disease-complex finding, though this pass did not find a lavandin-
specific pathogen name the way this project's true lavender research
did — no dedicated insect-pest information was found at all this pass.

**Finding 7 — lavandin's end-use split from true lavender is
independently confirmed across multiple sources this pass, but the
specific claim of soap/detergent/industrial-cleaning use could not be
confirmed from any source opened this pass.** Lavandin is reported as
used primarily in cosmetics, fragrance, and antiseptic/aromatherapeutic
applications, with multiple sources agreeing its flavor profile does not
suit culinary use as well as true lavender's own [3] — directly
consistent with this project's own true lavender research (an unresolved
culinary-use dispute there), but this pass's sources lean more
decisively toward "lavandin is not the preferred culinary choice" than
toward the contested "might be acceptable in small quantities" framing
some of that research's own sources offered. A 2020 botanical-
adulterants prevention program bulletin specifically reports that true
lavender essential oil competes directly against, and in trade practice
is sometimes adulterated with, lavandin oil [3] — independent, dated,
named-organization confirmation that lavender and lavandin are
understood in the real marketplace as two distinct, substitutable-but-
differently-valued commercial products, not synonyms or simple cultivar
labels of one product. No source found this pass specifically confirmed
soap, detergent, or industrial-cleaning-product use by name.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial subshrub, Mediterranean-
  climate-adapted, larger habit than true lavender — stems to 80cm,
  "much larger than true lavender" for one cultivar specifically
  (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, by analogy to true lavender's
  own provisional value — sources also describe a single mid-summer
  harvest per season (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate to warm temperate, Mediterranean (USDA
  zones disputed 5-8/5-9/5-10 across sources, Finding 5) — reported
  hybrid-vigor climate tolerance (parentage-derived cold and heat/
  humidity tolerance) disputed against one source's opposite "generally
  less hardy" claim.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — lavandin's dried flower is
  explicitly not recommended for culinary use per multiple sources
  (Finding 7).
- **Pollination:** Not applicable in the reproductive sense (sterile
  hybrid, no viable seed produced at all, Finding 1/4) — not
  independently sourced this pass whether lavandin flowers still
  attract/reward pollinators for nectar the way true lavender's own
  bumblebee-dominated finding described.
- **Yield / composition:** Oil 40-220 kg/ha (widest sourced range) /
  120-200 kg/ha (two narrower competing ranges) — disputed across three
  sources (Finding 2), all agreeing the figure is several times true
  lavender's own 15-40 kg/ha. Dried flower-stem yield 500-1,000 kg/ha
  (one source). A dedicated Turkish propagation-comparison study gave an
  unusually tight oil-content range (1.2-1.3% of fresh stem/flower
  mass) across its own two study years (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance high (once established; established
  plants generally need no irrigation). Waterlogging tolerance very low
  — "strongly rejects wet feet"; "will not survive in saturated soils"
  — at least as strongly worded as true lavender's own already-
  established finding (Finding 6). pH disputed (6.5-7.5 per one source;
  acid/alkaline/neutral-tolerant per a horticultural society; neutral-
  to-slightly-alkaline per a third) — a similar spread to true
  lavender's own disputed range.
- **Establishment:** Obligate vegetative propagation (cuttings or
  micropropagation) — no viable-seed alternative exists at all (Finding
  1/4), a stronger, cleaner finding than true lavender's own cuttings-
  primary-but-seed-possible profile. A Turkish field study found
  micropropagated plants outyielded cutting-propagated plants in both
  floral yield (up to 7,791 kg/ha fresh stem/flower) and oil
  characteristics (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** pH disputed, roughly neutral with acid/alkaline tolerance
  reported by one source — see water block above.
- **Stand life:** Woody perennial subshrub, by analogy to true
  lavender's own finding; no lavandin-specific productive-lifespan or
  replanting-cycle figure was sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fungal issues from cultural stress
  (humidity, poor drainage), with root rot specifically remediable by
  improving soil texture (sand/grit addition) — directly consistent with
  true lavender's own water-management-driven disease-complex finding,
  though no specific lavandin pathogen name was sourced this pass.
  Rabbits and deer reported to avoid the plant entirely (one source). No
  specific insect pest information was found. No human- or animal-
  health hazard (toxin, allergen, drug interaction) was found for
  lavandin itself this pass.
- **Cultivars:** Four named, independently developed cultivar lines
  sourced this pass: 'Abrial' (first lavandin cultivar, 1920s), 'Grosso'
  (developed by Pierre Grosso in the 1970s for higher yield/prolificacy
  and multi-climate hardiness — the dominant commercial cultivar per
  this project's own prior lavender research's own yield citations),
  'Super' (high linalyl-acetate content, a chemistry delta from the
  others), and 'Sumian' (named but not independently characterized this
  pass). A separate cultivar is noted elsewhere in this project's own
  research as more accurately an English-lavandin hybrid cultivar than a
  true lavandin, not one of this document's own four primary cultivars.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — one practical mid-summer harvest/maturity
  point per season; no distinguishable immature-vs-ripe harvest-stage
  choice sourced this pass. Single window — one annual flowering/harvest
  period per established planting, by direct analogy to true lavender's
  own finding.

## Hazards

Fungal issues from cultural stress (humidity, poor drainage), with root
rot specifically remediable by improving soil texture — directly
consistent with true lavender's own water-management-driven disease-
complex finding, though no specific lavandin pathogen name was sourced
this pass (Finding 6). Rabbits and deer reported to avoid the plant
entirely. No human- or animal-health hazard was found for lavandin
itself this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** flowering stems cut mid-summer (dry at harvest time, best
  for oil-processing quality) → used fresh, or dried, or distilled into
  essential oil (substantially higher camphor, borneol, and 1,8-cineole
  content than true lavender oil, not a pharmacopeial material).

## Open research questions

- This document's own flowers-dry-at-harvest finding was not
  independently cross-checked against true lavender's own broader
  "degree of blossoming/time of day/weather" harvest-quality finding
  this pass.
- No seed-crop or isolation-distance figure was sourced for lavandin —
  not a gap so much as a structural non-applicability, since lavandin
  produces no viable seed at all.
- The disputed cold-hardiness framing was not resolved: two sources
  describe lavandin as combining true lavender's cold tolerance with
  spike lavender's heat/humidity tolerance, while a separate extension
  source states lavandins are generally less hardy than other lavenders
  — a direct disagreement, not reconciled.
- No specific lavandin pathogen name was sourced this pass (e.g. an
  equivalent to true lavender's own named fungal pathogen findings) —
  only a general "fungal issues from cultural stress" finding.
- No specific insect-pest information was found for lavandin this pass.
- The claim that lavandin is used in soap, detergent, or industrial-
  cleaning products could not be confirmed from any source opened this
  pass — a dedicated follow-up search on lavandin's specific
  industrial/household-product uses (as distinct from fragrance/
  cosmetic/aromatherapy use generally) would fill this gap.
- No lavandin-specific years-to-peak-yield or productive-lifespan figure
  was sourced this pass — this document's own field data uses an
  analogy to true lavender's own 2-4-year establishment curve as a
  provisional placeholder, not an independently sourced figure for
  lavandin itself.
- Whether lavandin flowers still attract and reward pollinators for
  nectar despite being reproductively sterile was not independently
  sourced or ruled out this pass — true lavender's own bumblebee-
  dominated pollinator-community finding was not independently re-
  confirmed for lavandin specifically.
- No specific storage-days figure was sourced for either the fresh or
  dried lavandin flower product.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any lavandin
  operation.
- This document did not independently confirm whether each new lavandin
  planting is propagated from an existing lavandin stock plant (true
  clonal propagation of an already-established hybrid line) or re-
  crossed from the two parent species fresh each generation — the four
  named cultivars' own long commercial histories (since the 1920s for
  'Abrial') suggest ongoing clonal propagation of established lines is
  the norm, but this was not independently confirmed from a dedicated
  source this pass.
- 'Sumian' was named as a fourth lavandin cultivar but not independently
  characterized this pass beyond its name.
- Lavandin's light requirement was not independently sourced this pass
  — carried over from true lavender's own general Mediterranean-sun-
  loving assumption by analogy, not a lavandin-specific source.
- Lavandin's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass, with the same added relevance here
  given this crop's own acute waterlogging sensitivity.
- This document did not independently re-verify whether any of its own
  four named cultivars were among the genotypes studied in this
  project's own true lavender research's cited 2021 cytogenetic survey
  (which covered 82 lavender genotypes including 15 hybrids) — a
  dedicated re-read of that study's own per-genotype table would likely
  resolve this.

## Estimates and derivations

A fresh-flower-to-dried-flower yield-chain fraction of 0.25-0.35 is
estimated by direct analogy to true lavender's own identical fresh-to-
dried-flower yield chain, reasoning that both crops' flowers are similar
general flower-tissue products with comparable water content; no source
this pass gave a direct lavandin-specific dry-recovery percentage.

A fresh-flower-to-oil yield-chain fraction of 0.003-0.02 is derived as
follows: three sources disagree substantially on lavandin's own oil
yield per hectare (40-220, 150-200, and up to 120 kg/ha), with the
widest-range source also giving a dried-flower-stem yield of 500-1,000
kg/ha. Converting that dried-stem-basis figure through this document's
own fresh-to-dried yield chain (0.25-0.35 retained fraction) implies a
fresh-flower mass base of roughly 1,430-4,000 kg/ha; dividing the 40-220
kg/ha oil range by that implied fresh-mass range gives a fresh-flower
oil fraction of roughly 0.01-0.15 by direct arithmetic — this document
explicitly did not use that raw arithmetic result directly, since it
chains three already-individually-uncertain figures together and
compounds their disagreement. Instead, this document widened and lowered
the final range to 0.003-0.02, reasoning that lavandin's own
consistently-reported 3-7x yield multiplier over true lavender (Finding
2) should scale true lavender's own already-derived 0.001-0.004 fresh-
flower-to-oil range by a comparable order of magnitude, rather than by
the much larger multiplier the raw three-source chained arithmetic
above implied.
