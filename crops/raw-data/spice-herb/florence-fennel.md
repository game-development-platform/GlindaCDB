# Florence Fennel / Bulb Fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare* var. *azoricum*, "finocchio")

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/florence-fennel.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *F. vulgare* var. *azoricum* ("Florence fennel," "finocchio,"
"bulb fennel"), grown for its swollen, overlapping leaf-base ("bulb") —
a genuinely different harvested organ from this project's seed/herb
fennel research, with an opposite agronomic relationship to bolting (a
crop failure here, the intended endpoint there). The two varieties
differ in harvested organ, failure mode, yield class (17-40 t/ha bulb
vs. seed fennel's own 0.2-1.9 t/ha seed), market identity (a fresh
vegetable vs. a dried spice), and in having dedicated breeding programs
organized specifically around bulb productivity and bolt resistance.

Botanical/cytogenetic findings (chromosome number, genome size) are the
same species as this project's seed/herb fennel research and are not
re-sourced independently in this document. Pest/disease findings largely
carry over from that research (same species, same general pathogen
exposure), with bolting-specific agronomy sourced fresh for this
variety since that is its own defining hazard.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — bolting is this variety's own central, defining agronomic
hazard, with water/nutrient stress, heat, and photoperiod all
independently implicated as triggers, and named cultivars bred
specifically for bolt resistance showing measurably different outcomes
under the same conditions.** A university extension service names water
stress as the main trigger for bolting, specifically advising growers to
water regularly and never let the soil dry out between irrigations
(roughly 1-2 inches/week depending on soil type, with drip irrigation
recommended to keep soil consistently moist) [1]. A horticultural
society makes the identical point: regular water keeps plants growing
strongly and is the key defense against bolting [1]. Separately, excess
nitrogen is reported (across several blog-level sources, flagged as
anecdotal rather than peer-reviewed) to cause lush foliage at the
expense of bulb size, while the same extension service states the crop
needs moderate nitrogen but low phosphorus and potassium [1] — a
genuinely different, more specific fertility profile than the general
richer-soil Apiaceae framing this project has used for seed/herb
fennel. Heat and long summer days are independently implicated: the
crop is reported to grow best under shorter days and cooler
temperatures, with summer heat causing poor bulb development [2][3]. A
Washington State University cultivar trial found several cultivars had
very low bulb productivity in both trial years specifically due to
bolting, while several other named cultivars performed measurably better
under the same conditions — a genuinely sourced, named cultivar-level
bolt-resistance delta [3]. A 1990 Finnish trial on a named late-bolting
cultivar found bolting onset tied to a specific bulb-width threshold
(60-70mm), with short-day treatment (16-hour dark period) retarding but
not preventing it, and the latest/summer-planted lot showing the highest
bolter rate (8%) [3].

**Finding 2 — bulb yield figures are thinly and inconsistently sourced,
spanning roughly 17-40 t/ha across very different kinds of sources (one
commercial breeder claim, one 1990 research trial), a notably wider and
less reconciled spread than even this project's own seed/herb fennel
seed-yield range.** A commercial fennel breeder's industry statement
puts average commercial output at roughly 40 t/ha of rough bulb,
additionally claiming 95% of cultivated Florence-fennel varieties are
now F1 hybrids [3]. A 1990 Finnish trial on a named cultivar estimated
yields of 170-360 kg/are (roughly 17-36 t/ha by straightforward unit
conversion) at the third and fourth harvests, varying by planting date
and growing-season length [3]. Neither figure is from a controlled,
peer-reviewed yield trial specifically isolating variety/management/
region effects; this document treats the 17-40 t/ha range as indicative
only, not a reconciled world-average figure.

**Finding 3 — Florence fennel's own seed supply is reported to be
overwhelmingly F1-hybrid, produced via a three-line cytoplasmic-male-
sterility (CMS) breeding system, a genuinely more industrialized seed-
production pathway than this project has found for most other spice/
herb crops, including seed/herb fennel's own non-hybrid seed-saving
framing.** A peer-reviewed paper on fennel breeding describes hybrid
seed as typically produced via three lines — a cytoplasmic male-sterile
seed parent, a fertile maintainer line that perpetuates that seed
parent, and a fertile pollinator line [4]. The male-sterile line itself
is built by repeated backcrossing, using one inbred as the recurrent
parent and a CMS source as the donor [4]. A separate patent on
cytoplasmic-male-sterile parsley (a related Apiaceae crop covered
separately in this project's own research) notes that no fertility-
restoring genes are known in fennel specifically, which the patent cites
as the reason fennel serves well as a CMS donor source for other
Apiaceae crops, not only for its own hybrid seed [4] — a genuinely
interesting cross-crop breeding-resource finding. Molecular markers are
reported to assist across the entire breeding pipeline, from building
parental lines through hybrid evaluation to variety registration, with
hybrid heterozygosity/vigor reported to rise as parental lines become
more genetically dissimilar [4]. No commercial-scale isolation-distance
or field-ratio figure specific to fennel hybrid-seed production was
found — this document explicitly flags the 95%-hybrid industry claim
[3] as a single commercial source, not independently cross-checked
against a second source.

**Finding 4 — storage and harvest-timing figures are reasonably well
sourced from extension/seed-industry material, with a genuine
disagreement between an extension source and a seed-industry source on
storage duration, and a distinct, named optional field-blanching
practice not shared with this project's own seed/herb fennel research.**
A Canadian agricultural-ministry reference lists bulb storage at 0-2°C
and 90-95% relative humidity for roughly 14-21 days [5]; a commercial
seed-company source gives a more conservative figure of up to 1 week at
0°C/95% humidity [5] — an unreconciled roughly 2x disagreement in
storage life between an agricultural-extension source and a seed-
company source, not resolved this pass. Full-size bulbs are harvested
when the base thickens to roughly 5-6 inches diameter (3 inches for baby
bulbs), cut just above the taproot with foliage trimmed 1-2 inches above
the bulb, before seed stalks form [5] — the harvest timing criterion is
thus explicitly the opposite of seed/herb fennel's own seed-harvest
timing, which specifically waits for seed stalks to form and dry. The
same agricultural-ministry reference recommends harvesting during the
cooler part of the day and removing field heat quickly after harvest to
limit moisture loss [5]. Field blanching is reported as an optional
practice: hilling soil over the bulb base once it reaches roughly egg
size, then harvesting 2-3 weeks later for a whiter, sweeter bulb [5] — a
genuinely distinct named field operation not shared with this project's
other spice/herb research.

**Finding 5 — days-to-maturity and hardiness-zone figures scatter widely
across seed-company and extension sources, with the scatter plausibly
reflecting real cultivar-to-cultivar variation rather than simple
sourcing noise, and this variety's own cultivation-method preference
(direct-seeding strongly favored) is more strongly and consistently
sourced than this project found for seed/herb fennel.** Days-to-maturity
figures range from roughly 60 days (a low-end extension estimate) to 115
days (a seed-company high-end estimate for some cultivars), with most
seed-company figures clustering around 80-100 days [6]. USDA hardiness-
zone listings range from zone 4-9 to 5-10 depending on source, with one
Canadian agricultural-ministry reference listing zone 6 specifically [6]
— broadly similar in shape to seed/herb fennel's own disputed zone range,
though this document did not independently reconcile the two varieties'
figures against each other. Direct-seeding is more strongly and
specifically recommended for this variety than extension sources
recommend for seed/herb fennel: the crop is reported to be especially
sensitive to temperature swings and transplant shock, with direct
seeding specifically cited as producing better results because the crop
dislikes root disturbance [6] — consistent with (and a more specific,
bulb-variety-targeted version of) the Washington State trial's own
finding that direct-seeded bulb fennel required 32 fewer days to harvest
than transplanted bulb fennel. When transplanting is used, seed-company
guidance recommends starting indoors in individual peat or coir pots
roughly 4 weeks before the last frost date, specifically to minimize
root disturbance at transplant [6].

**Finding 6 — pest, disease, and pet/human toxicity profiles are the
same species as this project's seed/herb fennel research and are not
independently re-sourced in this document; this variety's own primary
disorder (poor bulb formation/splitting) is a physiological response to
the same water/nutrient stresses identified in Finding 1, not a separate
pathogen-driven disorder.** This project's own seed/herb fennel research
already documents this species' general disease complex (powdery
mildew, downy mildew, Cercospora leaf blight, rust, general root-rot
susceptibility, aphids, caterpillars, cutworms, root-knot nematodes) and
an authoritative pet-safety reference's own non-toxic listing for
*Foeniculum vulgare* generally (explicitly naming "Florence Fennel,
Finocchio" in its own listing text) — this document does not re-derive
those findings independently. This variety's own specific physiological
disorder, bulb splitting from irregular watering, and small/stunted bulb
formation from water or nutrient stress generally [1][2], is the bulb-
specific expression of the same underlying water/nutrient-stress
mechanism already identified in Finding 1 as this variety's bolting
trigger — not a separate pest or pathogen, and not independently
documented for the seed/herb variety's own foliage/seed harvest (which
has no comparable "organ quality" failure mode tied to the same stress
inputs).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Grown as a cool-season annual; the perennial/
  self-seeding framing used for seed/herb fennel does not apply to this
  variety's own commercial cultivation cycle, since bulbs are harvested
  before the plant would flower/set seed/persist (Finding 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — a single bulb per planting,
  harvested once and done — genuinely different from seed/herb fennel's
  own dual single-harvest/cut-and-come-again pattern, since there is no
  repeated-cutting foliage-harvest stream for this variety's own primary
  commercial product.
- **Climate:** Days to maturity 60-115 (widely scattered across
  sources, plausibly reflecting real cultivar variation). USDA zone 4-10
  depending on source (figures scatter: 4-9, 5-9, 5-10, one reference
  specifically zone 6) — grows best under cooler temperatures and
  shorter days; summer heat causes poor bulb development (Finding 1).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — eaten raw/sliced or cooked/braised, a
  genuinely different culinary use from seed/herb fennel's own fresh-
  frond/dried-seed products.
- **Pollination:** Not required — the bulb is harvested before
  flowering; this variety's own commercial product has no dependency on
  pollination at all, a genuine simplification relative to seed/herb
  fennel's own seed-production pollination dependence.
- **Yield / composition:** 17-40 t/ha bulb, thinly and inconsistently
  sourced (one commercial breeder claim, one 1990 research trial)
  (Finding 2) — not comparable to seed/herb fennel's own seed-yield
  figures (230 kg/ha - 1,864 kg/ha), confirming the two varieties'
  yield-class distinctiveness.
- **Water use:** No crop-coefficient figures were independently sourced
  this pass; the sourced finding that matters most for this variety is
  qualitative, not a crop coefficient: water stress is the primary
  bolting trigger, with extension guidance specifically against ever
  letting soil dry out between irrigations (roughly 1-2 in/week)
  (Finding 1). Drought tolerance is inferred low by this water-stress-
  triggers-bolting finding — lower than the medium rating this project
  has used for seed/herb fennel, a genuine, sourced-in-spirit contrast
  between the two varieties, though not independently assigned a numeric
  figure this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeding strongly preferred — more
  specifically and consistently sourced for this variety than for
  seed/herb fennel's own type: root-disturbance sensitivity and
  transplant-shock susceptibility are explicitly named reasons (Finding
  5). When transplanting is used, individual peat/coir pots started ~4
  weeks before last frost are recommended specifically to minimize root
  disturbance. ~95% of cultivated varieties reported as F1 hybrids per a
  single commercial-breeder source, produced via a three-line CMS system
  (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for most operations,
  except a genuinely distinct named field operation, optional blanching
  (hilling soil over the bulb base at roughly egg size, harvested 2-3
  weeks later) (Finding 4) — not shared with seed/herb fennel's own
  labor profile at all.
- **Soil:** pH disputed across sources, ranging 6.0-7.0, 6.0-7.5, and
  6.5-8 across different extension/grower sources (Finding 1). Moderate
  nitrogen, low phosphorus/potassium requirement per one extension
  source — more specific than the general "richer soil" assumption used
  for seed/herb fennel; excess nitrogen specifically implicated (lush
  foliage, small bulbs) across several anecdotal/blog sources, not
  independently peer-reviewed-confirmed this pass.
- **Stand life:** Not applicable in the usual sense — grown as a cool-
  season annual, harvested once per planting before flowering. Does not
  persist as a perennial the way seed/herb fennel's own framing does.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Same species as seed/herb fennel — not
  independently re-sourced for this variety specifically. This
  variety's own specific disorders are bulb splitting (irregular
  watering) and small/stunted bulb formation (water or nutrient stress)
  (Finding 6) — physiological disorders tied directly to the same stress
  mechanism as bolting, not independent pathogen-driven disorders.
- **Cultivars:** Named cultivars with a sourced, measured bolt-
  resistance delta under identical trial conditions: several cultivars
  showing better bulb productivity under bolting pressure vs. several
  others showing poor bulb productivity and being bolting-prone
  (Finding 1). A separate cultivar is named as "very slow to bolt,"
  suited to early planting. Two further cultivars (one late-bolting,
  one standard) were specifically studied in the 1990 Finnish trial,
  with bolting onset tied to a 60-70mm bulb-width threshold and short-
  day treatment found to retard but not prevent it. This is among the
  most densely cultivar-characterized crops in this project's own
  spice/herb research, directly a consequence of commercial breeding
  programs being organized specifically around this variety's own
  defining hazard (bolting).
- **Harvest:** Single stage — the bulb is harvested at a single target
  size window (roughly 5-6 inches diameter for full-size bulbs, 3 inches
  for baby bulbs) (Finding 4), before seed stalks form. Genuinely
  simpler than seed/herb fennel's own dual-stage (foliage + seed)
  framing, since this variety's own commercial product has no comparable
  secondary harvest stream. Single window — one bulb harvested per
  planting, a true single-harvest crop.
- **Storage:** Disputed between sources: 14-21 days at 0-2°C/90-95%
  relative humidity per one agricultural-ministry reference, vs. a more
  conservative "up to 1 week" at 0°C/95% humidity per a seed-company
  source (Finding 4). Field heat should be removed quickly after
  harvest to limit moisture loss; harvesting during the cooler part of
  the day is recommended.

## Hazards

Bolting is this variety's own central, defining hazard — triggered by
water stress (primary), excess nitrogen, heat, and long summer days
(Finding 1). Bulb splitting from irregular watering, and small/stunted
bulb formation from water or nutrient stress generally — the bulb-
specific physiological expression of the same stress mechanism as
bolting, not a separate disorder (Finding 6). General disease/pest
complex is the same species as seed/herb fennel — see that crop's own
research entry; not independently re-sourced in this document. The
plant itself is non-toxic per an authoritative pet-safety reference,
which explicitly names "Florence Fennel, Finocchio" in its own
*Foeniculum vulgare* listing text — see this project's seed/herb fennel
research for that finding (its own estragole-sourcing gap applies
equally here, since it is the same species; no separate estragole search
was run for this variety).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** bulb grown to target size (roughly 5-6 inches diameter) →
  cut above the taproot, foliage trimmed → optionally field-blanched
  (soil hilled over the bulb at egg size, harvested 2-3 weeks later) →
  field heat removed quickly → sold fresh, or stored cool (0-2°C,
  90-95% relative humidity).

## Open research questions

- Bulb yield (17-40 t/ha) is thinly and inconsistently sourced — one
  commercial-breeder industry claim and one 1990 research trial, not a
  reconciled, controlled multi-site yield-trial figure.
- No commercial-scale isolation-distance or field-ratio figure specific
  to fennel hybrid-seed production was found for the three-line CMS
  breeding system described in Finding 3.
- The ~95%-of-cultivated-varieties-are-hybrid claim is single-sourced
  (one commercial breeder's industry statement) and was not
  independently cross-checked against a second source.
- kcalPerKg/proteinGPerKg for raw bulb were not independently verified
  against a primary nutrition-database lookup this pass — a commonly
  cited real-world figure of roughly 31 kcal/100g (~310 kcal/kg) exists
  in general nutrition references, but this document deliberately does
  not present it as sourced without having opened and verified a primary
  database entry this pass.
- Water-use figures (crop coefficients, seasonal mm) were not
  independently sourced this pass beyond the qualitative "never let soil
  dry out" extension guidance.
- Waterlogging tolerance was not independently sourced this pass.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any operation other
  than the qualitatively-described optional blanching practice.
- Soil pH optimum is disputed across sources (6.0-7.0, 6.0-7.5, 6.5-8),
  not reconciled.
- Bulb storage duration is disputed roughly 2x between an agricultural-
  extension source (14-21 days) and a seed-company source ("up to 1
  week"), not reconciled.
- Controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not independently
  assessed this pass — this variety's own root-disturbance sensitivity
  may make it a poorer transplant/compact-system candidate than seed/
  herb fennel, though it avoids that variety's own mature-plant-size
  limitation since it is harvested before flowering.

## Estimates and derivations

Bulb yield of 17-36 t/ha is derived from a 1990 Finnish trial's own
reported 170-360 kg/are figure, using the standard unit conversion 1 are
= 100 m² = 0.01 ha (170 kg/are ÷ 0.01 ha/are = 17,000 kg/ha = 17 t/ha;
360 kg/are ÷ 0.01 ha/are = 36,000 kg/ha = 36 t/ha). This is a
straightforward unit conversion of the source's own reported figure, not
an independent estimate of the crop's productivity; the range itself
reflects real variation by planting date and growing-season length per
the source.
