# Fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare* var. *dulce*, "sweet/herb/seed fennel")

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/fennel.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *F. vulgare* var. *dulce* ("sweet fennel"), grown for fresh
foliage and seed — a type for which bolting/seed-set is the intended
successful endpoint, not a failure. Florence/bulb fennel (var.
*azoricum*) is a genuinely distinct, widely-known vegetable product with
an opposite agronomic relationship to bolting (a crop failure, not a
success) — covered as its own, separate crop entry elsewhere in this
project. Bitter fennel (var. *vulgare*) was not independently evaluated
this pass.

This project's own research on parsley, rosemary, sage, and thyme
provides useful grounding: parsley for real botanical family (fennel is
Apiaceae, not Lamiaceae like rosemary/sage/thyme), and
rosemary/sage/thyme for general cultivation shape.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — fennel's chromosome number is well-sourced and converges
tightly across independent studies, a notably cleaner cytogenetic
picture than this project has found for several Lamiaceae herbs, though
a 2023 polyploidy finding complicates it slightly.** Multiple sources,
including a Turkish study of six separate populations, agree on 2n=22
[1]; a karyotype analysis of Ankara-province material gives the specific
formula 18 median + 4 submedian chromosomes [1]; a Moroccan study
separately confirms 2n=22 specifically for var. *dulce* (this document's
own variety) [1]. A 2023 peer-reviewed paper complicates this clean
picture by reporting tetraploid cytotypes in both wild and cultivated
fennel, proposing spontaneous sexual polyploidization as the likely
origin [1] — not reconciled with the otherwise-unanimous diploid count,
and not resolved to a specific ploidy-level product/chemistry
difference. Genome size is thinly sourced: the only figure found is a
2C DNA range of 18.17-18.93 pg from an Indian botanical-survey
chromosome datasheet (microspectrophotometry), the same datasheet
showing an internally inconsistent ploidy field alongside its own 2n=22
count [1] — flagged as a likely formatting artifact rather than genuine
ploidy variation, but not independently resolved. No primary reference-
genome assembly was found.

**Finding 2 — fennel seed-yield field trials span a notably wide
0.2-1.9 t/ha range depending on sowing date, seeding rate, and plant
density, a management-driven spread rather than a clean single figure,
with essential-oil content and its dominant compound (trans-anethole)
both independently variable across the same trials.** A Turkish
seeding-rate trial found fruit yields of 230-791 kg/ha, highest at 15
kg/ha seeding rate, with essential oil content 1.60-2.46% and trans-
anethole 84.48-97.79% of the oil [2]. An Iranian sowing-date trial found
a substantially higher best seed yield of 1,864.3 kg/ha with essential-
oil yield of 91.3 kg/ha from an early-April sowing, with anethole share
falling sharply (to 58.2%) when sowing was delayed to late June [2] — a
genuine sowing-date-driven chemistry shift, not just a yield shift. An
Iranian organic/biofertilizer trial found a best treatment of 29.9 L/ha
essential oil at 69.7% anethole [2]. A 1992 Pakistani nitrogen/
phosphorus trial found foliar N/P sprays decreased anethole while
increasing fenchone, leading the authors to recommend foliar feeding
specifically for perfumery/confectionery-grade oil rather than culinary-
grade [2] — a genuinely actionable fertilizer-practice-to-product-
quality finding. A Turkish plant-population trial found the best seed
and essential-oil yield combination at 15 cm row spacing with 10 kg/ha
seeding [2]. No source reconciles these widely scattered figures to a
single representative world-average yield.

**Finding 3 — fennel's pollination biology is unusually well peer-
reviewed-characterized for an Apiaceae crop, with a specific, sourced
insect-dependence percentage not found for most other crops this project
has researched.** Fennel flowers are hermaphrodite and protandrous, a
floral arrangement favoring cross-pollination, with flowering moving in
waves through the umbel (outer umbellules maturing first, the pattern
repeating across umbel orders) [3]. A French study found that
pollination without insects leads to reproductive failure, estimating
insect-pollination dependence at 86% at the field level [3] — a notably
specific, sourced dependence figure, a rarer level of precision than
most other crops this project has researched achieve for pollination
dependence. Honey bees are reported as the dominant pollinator in Indian
field studies (nearly 80% of insect-pollination contribution in one
study; other studies give combined bee/wasp and fly contributions
totaling over 90% of visits) [3] — though a review of Apiaceae more
broadly notes both wind and insects are considered active in
pollination generally, a caveat against treating fennel as purely
insect-pollinated. Self-fertilization is reported as possible within the
same umbel or between umbels on the same plant, with fennel nonetheless
classed as predominantly allogamous (cross-pollinating) [3] — not a
clean self-incompatibility finding. No direct fennel-dill cross-
pollination study was found; the closest sourced material was general
varietal isolation-distance guidance (two fennel varieties recommended 1
km apart, or 500 m with a hedge barrier) [3], which does not itself
confirm or deny any cross-pollination concern between the two species.

**Finding 4 — fennel's disease and pest profile is broadly consistent
with other Apiaceae crops this project has researched, but adds powdery
mildew as a distinct primary hazard.** Named fungal diseases include
Cercospora leaf blight, powdery mildew, and rust [4]; a plant-disease
database specifically names the powdery-mildew pathogen as *Erysiphe
heraclei* and a downy-mildew pathogen as *Peronospora umbellifarum*, the
latter reported to mainly affect younger plants and spread via
consistently wet foliage [4]. A horticultural-society source separately
flags general susceptibility to root rots [4] — no specific causal
organism was named for fennel in the sources opened this pass. An Indian
regional integrated-pest-management resource names region-specific pest/
pathogen identities (an aphid species, a caterpillar species, and a
different powdery-mildew species name, *Erysiphe polygoni* — an
unreconciled regional-taxonomy disagreement with the first source's
*E. heraclei*) [4]. Aphids are the most consistently named insect pest
across sources generally, with caterpillars (armyworm, named as heavily
skeletonizing foliage), cutworms, and root-knot nematodes also reported
[4]. A genuinely interesting beneficial-insect finding: fennel's own
flowers are reported to attract bees, wasps, lacewings, butterflies, and
syrphid flies, with one source explicitly recommending organic pest
control specifically to preserve this beneficial-insect value [4] —
directly consistent with Finding 3's own insect-pollination-dependence
finding, since the same visiting insects serve both roles.

**Finding 5 — fennel (fresh, small quantities) is non-toxic to pets per
an authoritative pet-safety reference, but this pass could not
independently source a fennel-specific concentrated-oil hazard compound
(estragole), leaving this crop's own essential-oil risk profile
genuinely incomplete rather than merely weakly sourced.** An
authoritative pet-safety reference's own plant-toxicity listing names
*Foeniculum vulgare* (Florence Fennel, Finocchio) as non-toxic to dogs,
cats, and horses, with no toxic principle identified [5]. A secondary
pet-care source similarly describes fresh fennel in small quantities as
non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, while separately advising against
concentrated fennel essential oil for dogs, citing a lack of
manufacturing/quality-control standards for essential-oil products
generally [5] — a weaker, non-compound-specific caution rather than a
sourced toxicology finding. No estragole-specific toxicity data (human
or pet) was found this pass despite estragole being a well-known
potential hepatocarcinogen compound present in basil, tarragon, and
fennel essential oils in the broader toxicology literature. This
document explicitly does not claim an estragole finding it did not
source.

**Finding 6 — fennel's hardiness/naturalization profile is broad, and
fennel is independently reported as naturalized/invasive in parts of the
real world, with a plausible historical wild-foraging basis.** Hardiness
is reported across USDA zones 3-10 depending on source (most commonly
4-9 or 5-9) [6] — comfortably broader/hardier than rosemary's own
disputed range. Fennel is reported to self-seed freely in gardens, with
extension advice to remove spent flower heads before seed forms to
control this [6]. Separately, and more significantly, fennel is reported
as naturalized and locally invasive in parts of California, Washington
state, and Hawaii, where it displaces native vegetation [6] — though one
commercial seed-company source disputes the invasive characterization
specifically, agreeing only that it self-seeds aggressively [6], an
unreconciled disagreement. Separately from this pass's own sourcing,
wild fennel ("finocchietto selvatico") is well known in Mediterranean
(particularly Sicilian) cuisine as a foraged wild green/seed, a
historical-use basis worth noting though not independently re-sourced in
this pass's own search set.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial (biennial/short-lived-perennial
  behavior reported by some sources, self-seeds freely) (Finding 6).
- **Climate:** USDA zone 3-10 depending on source, most commonly 4-9/
  5-9 — broader/hardier than rosemary's own range.
- **Harvest pattern:** Dual — foliage is cut-and-come-again through the
  season, while the seed harvest itself is a single terminal event per
  planting. Not independently re-verified against a dedicated multi-cut
  foliage source this pass.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh fronds, like dill; seed used as a
  dried spice.
- **Pollination:** Not required for foliage harvest; required for seed
  production specifically — 86% insect-pollination dependence at field
  level is sourced for seed set (Finding 3), a notably more precise
  figure than this project typically achieves for pollination
  dependence.
- **Yield / composition:** Seed yield 230 kg/ha - 1,864 kg/ha depending
  on sowing date/density/management across several field trials (Finding
  2) — a genuinely wide, management-driven range, not reconciled to a
  single world-average figure. No independently-sourced foliage-yield
  figure was found this pass.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass. Drought tolerance
  is partially corroborated by a bolting-under-moisture-stress finding
  ("fennel has a tendency to bolt if stressed by a lack of irrigation"),
  though that finding describes bulb fennel's own bolting-as-failure
  framing, not this variety's own bolting-as-success framing — flagged
  as a cross-variety sourcing caveat.
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded; no cuttings/division alternative
  route was sourced or expected for this crop — fennel's own seed-saving
  is a stated primary purpose for this variety, not merely one
  propagation option among several.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Self-seeding, biennial-to-short-lived-perennial
  behavior reported (Finding 6); naturalization/local invasiveness
  reported in parts of California, Washington state, and Hawaii,
  disputed by at least one commercial source.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A genuinely wide pathogen list (5+ named
  fungal/pest organisms across sources) with at least one unreconciled
  regional-taxonomy disagreement on the powdery-mildew causal species
  (Finding 4).
- **Cultivars:** No *F. vulgare* var. *dulce*-specific cultivar trait-
  delta was independently sourced this pass — named cultivars found
  during this research belong to bulb-fennel cultivar trials, not to
  this variety's own seed/herb type.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for foliage (cut as needed through the
  season); a separate terminal stage for seed (umbels harvested once
  seed has set and dried on the plant). Single window per planting for
  each of the two harvest streams (foliage: continuous through the
  season; seed: one terminal event).
- **Genome:** 2C DNA 18.17-18.93 pg per a single datasheet, internally
  inconsistent with its own stated ploidy field — flagged as likely a
  formatting artifact, not independently resolved (Finding 1). No
  primary reference-genome assembly found.

## Hazards

Powdery mildew (named pathogen *Erysiphe heraclei* per one source,
*Erysiphe polygoni* per a regional Indian source — an unreconciled
regional-taxonomy disagreement), downy mildew (*Peronospora
umbellifarum*, mainly affecting younger plants in consistently wet
foliage conditions), Cercospora leaf blight, and rust (Finding 4).
General root-rot susceptibility, no specific causal organism named in
the sources opened this pass. Aphids, caterpillars (armyworm), cutworms,
and root-knot nematodes. The plant itself (fresh, small amounts) is
considered non-toxic per an authoritative pet-safety reference; a
concentrated essential-oil hazard compound (estragole, a known
hepatocarcinogen in the broader toxicology literature for related
Apiaceae/aromatic plants) was not independently sourced for fennel this
pass (Finding 5).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — a targeted follow-up on fennel essential oil and
estragole-specific toxicity (human or pet) is deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data; this is a
genuine open gap, not a "nothing to find" conclusion.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (foliage):** fresh fronds → cut as needed through the season
  → used fresh. **Chain (seed):** plant allowed to bolt/flower/fruit →
  umbels harvested once seed has set and dried on the plant → used
  whole or ground as a dried spice.

## Open research questions

- Fennel's own genome-size figure comes from a single datasheet that is
  internally inconsistent with its own stated ploidy field — likely a
  formatting artifact given every other source's unanimous 2n=22 count,
  but not independently resolved. No primary reference-genome assembly
  was found.
- Seed-yield field trials span a wide 230-1,864 kg/ha range driven by
  sowing date, seeding rate, and plant density, not reconciled to a
  single representative world-average figure.
- No direct fennel-dill cross-pollination study was found; the closest
  sourced material (varietal isolation-distance guidance) does not
  itself confirm or deny any cross-pollination concern.
- No specific causal organism was named for fennel's own root-rot
  susceptibility in the sources opened this pass.
- No estragole-specific toxicity finding (human or pet) was
  independently sourced for fennel this pass despite estragole being a
  known potential hepatocarcinogen in the broader Apiaceae/aromatic-
  plant toxicology literature.
- Fennel's naturalization/invasiveness status is disputed — reported as
  locally invasive in parts of California, Washington state, and Hawaii
  per several sources, while a commercial seed-company source disputes
  the "invasive" characterization specifically.
- Wild/foraged fennel ("finocchietto selvatico" in Mediterranean,
  particularly Sicilian, cuisine) is worth noting on a historical-use
  basis, but was not independently re-sourced in this pass's own search
  set.
- Water-use figures (crop coefficients, seasonal mm, drought/
  waterlogging tolerance) were not independently sourced this pass.
- The bolting-under-moisture-stress finding sourced this pass was found
  in the context of bulb fennel (var. *azoricum*), not independently
  re-verified for this variety's own var. *dulce* — a cross-variety
  sourcing caveat, since the two varieties have opposite stakes in
  whether bolting is desirable.
- Fennel seed's own storage life was not independently sourced this
  pass.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any fennel
  operation this pass.
- No fennel-specific (as opposed to generic varietal) seed-isolation-
  distance figure was sourced.
- No *F. vulgare* var. *dulce*-specific cultivar trait-delta was
  independently sourced this pass.
- Fennel's light requirement was not independently sourced this pass.
- Fennel's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass.
