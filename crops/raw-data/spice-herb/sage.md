# Sage (*Salvia officinalis*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/sage.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers common/garden sage, *Salvia officinalis*. Clary sage
(*Salvia sclarea*) and Anatolian/Greek sage (*Salvia fruticosa*) surfaced
repeatedly in this pass's yield research as different species with their
own cultivation/yield literature — not covered here, flagged as a
possible future candidate crop entry, not researched directly.

Sage and rosemary are now literally the same genus (*Salvia*, following
rosemary's own 2017 reclassification from *Rosmarinus officinalis* to
*Salvia rosmarinus*), making this project's own rosemary research the
closest sibling by a wide margin, genuinely closer than lavender (same
family, different genus). This document re-verifies and extends two
cross-crop comparisons against rosemary (broader cold-hardiness than
rosemary; a real seed-vs-cutting propagation divergence between the two
genus-mates) rather than taking them on faith.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — sage's chromosome number and base ploidy are comparatively
well-sourced and agree closely across sources, in genuine contrast with
rosemary's own unresolved 2n=20-vs-24 disagreement, but sage also has a
documented natural autotetraploid form with measurably different
chemistry — a cytogenetic complexity this project's other Lamiaceae
herbs did not show.** Cultivated *S. officinalis* is reported diploid at
2n=2x=14, with one cytogenetic study describing a karyotype of 14
chromosomes measuring 0.99-2.38 μm with varied morphology [1]. A
colchicine-induced autotetraploid (2n=4x=28) has been produced and
directly compared against diploid plants: 2C DNA content was measured at
1.10 pg for diploids and 2.12 pg for tetraploids (confirmed by both flow
cytometry and chromosome counting) [1] — giving sage a directly species-
specific genome-size figure, unlike rosemary's own species-specific 1.17
Gb assembly (still a meaningfully different measurement basis, pg vs.
Gb, not converted this pass) or lavender's own genus-wide-only range.
The same study found the autotetraploids had lower total phenolic
content and rosmarinic acid than the diploids [1] — a sourced ploidy-
affects-chemistry finding this project has not recorded for any other
crop's own polyploidy variants (contrast with basil's own ploidy-varies-
but-chemistry-link-unestablished finding). Across the wider genus
*Salvia*, basic chromosome numbers vary substantially (x = 6, 7, 8, 9,
11, 13, 15, 17, 19) with ploidy from diploid to octoploid [1], and
molecular phylogenies place *S. officinalis* close to clary sage [1] —
the same species flagged above as a possible future candidate crop
entry.

**Finding 2 — sage's fresh/dried-leaf and essential-oil yield literature
is comparatively thin and scattered across several different Salvia
species' own trials, a genuinely worse cross-species sourcing-
conflation problem than this project found for rosemary's own (still
internally disagreeing, but at-least-same-species) yield figures.** A
Turkish nitrogen-rate trial on Anatolian/Greek sage (a different
species) found dry-leaf yield rising with nitrogen up to 150 kg N/ha,
while essential-oil yield peaked at a lower 100 kg N/ha (oil content
itself fell as nitrogen increased past that point) [2] — this document
does not treat this as common sage's own figure, flagging it only as a
directional management pattern. A semi-arid Mardin, Turkey trial
specifically on *S. officinalis* recommends roughly 50,000 plants/ha
with 120 kg N/ha for optimal fresh/dry herb and dry-leaf yield under
semi-arid conditions, but the search snippet opened this pass did not
give the resulting absolute kg/ha yield figures themselves [2]. A Polish
organic-vs-conventional trial (one named cultivar) found herb yield
highest in specific site/management combinations, with essential-oil
content and composition not depending on location [2] — a management-
driven yield / location-independent-quality split this document did not
find paralleled for rosemary. A Ukrainian drip-irrigation trial on clary
sage (a different species again) reported ~14.5-15.0 t/ha inflorescence
yield and ~51 kg/ha essential oil [2] — explicitly not adopted as a
common-sage figure, kept separate per honesty conventions. No single
source this pass gave a clean *S. officinalis*-specific kg/ha figure for
either fresh/dried leaf or essential oil — a notably weaker yield-
sourcing outcome than rosemary's own (still-disagreeing but at least
same-species) three-source fresh-yield convergence.

**Finding 3 — sage's propagation picture directly confirms a real
divergence from rosemary, but this pass's sources are less unanimous
than initially assumed, since cuttings are independently described as at
least as dominant commercially.** Seed germinates in 7-21 days at 65-
70°F (18-21°C) per a commercial seed-company growing guide, with sage
seed specifically described as light-sensitive (should not be covered
with soil) [3] — a mechanistically specific finding this project did not
have for rosemary's own seed route. Seed viability is reported at 3-5
years [3]. However, cuttings are also independently and repeatedly
described as the easier commercial route: one source calls sages "some
of the easiest plants to propagate by stem cuttings," with softwood
cuttings recommended in late spring/summer just after stems begin
turning brown, and a university-extension-adjacent source states "most
cultivars are propagated by division or stem cuttings" [3] — directly
complicating a clean "sage=seed, rosemary=cuttings" divergence framing.
This document does not resolve which route actually dominates
commercial common-sage production, recording both as genuinely viable
rather than picking one. No commercial germination-percentage or
cutting-rooting-success figure was found for sage this pass, the same
gap this project found for rosemary's own cuttings.

**Finding 4 — sage's hardiness-zone literature disagrees across sources
but consistently rates it measurably more cold-hardy than rosemary.**
Sources variously give USDA zones 5-8, 4-8, or (for warmer-climate use)
up to zone 10 depending on cultivar [3] — all of these ranges sit
measurably colder than rosemary's own disputed zone 6-10/6-9/7-11 range,
confirming this cross-crop comparison still holds under this pass's
independently-sourced figures. One source specifically notes that
cultivars with colorful/mottled foliage (contrast with plain-green
culinary types) are less hardy and slower-growing than the plain type
[3] — directly feeding this document's own cultivar notes below, a
genuinely sourced trait-delta this project did not have for rosemary's
own cultivar comparison (which remained encyclopedic/blog-level only).

**Finding 5 — sage's pollination mechanism (the Salvia-genus "staminal
lever") is substantially better mechanistically characterized in the
peer-reviewed literature than rosemary's own pollination findings,
though most of the opened studies concern other Salvia species, not
*S. officinalis* itself, a genuine cross-species-generalization caveat
this document flags rather than silently extends.** In Salvia flowers,
two stamens are modified into a lever mechanism; a visiting bee pushing
toward the nectar at the flower's base swings the lever, depositing
pollen onto the bee's body [4]. A peer-reviewed study of meadow sage
(a different Salvia species) describes the lever as a "pollen-
dispensing device" whose force interaction with the visiting bee
determines how much pollen is deposited [4]. Sage flowers are reported
protandrous (male parts mature before female), a timing-separation
mechanism reducing self-pollination and promoting cross-pollination,
sourced from a study of a different Salvia species (Jerusalem sage) [4]
— directly paralleling rosemary's own sourced protandry finding, making
protandry now a two-species-confirmed pattern within this project's
*Salvia* genus coverage (though each finding's primary source is a
different species within the genus, not the two species studied
directly against each other). A 2024 study on a different Salvia
species tested whether a degraded lever reduces pollinator foraging time
and found it did shorten visits but did not significantly improve
pollination efficiency — the hypothesis that a weaker lever boosts self-
pollination via faster visits remained unconfirmed [4]. New World,
hummingbird-pollinated Salvia species are reported to have lost the
lever entirely once pollen became freely accessible without it [4] — a
genuinely interesting genus-wide mechanism-loss finding this document
records for completeness, though not applicable to *S. officinalis*
itself.

**Finding 6 — sage's disease complex includes powdery mildew as a
genuinely distinct dominant hazard not shared with rosemary's own
Phytophthora-dominated profile, alongside an overlapping but less severe
root-rot risk.** Powdery mildew is reported by multiple sources, with an
Argentine greenhouse study identifying one named pathogen species and
reporting 85-90% disease incidence during its observation period, and a
2020 peer-reviewed paper separately reporting a different named pathogen
species causing powdery mildew on common sage in Mexico [5] — two
different named powdery-mildew pathogens across two independent
studies, not reconciled to a single causal agent. Root rot and foliar
blight are also reported: a 2021 Turkish study specifically identifies a
named pathogen as, per the authors, the first global report of this
pathogen on common sage, while a separate source attributes sage root
rot more generally to soil oomycetes (*Phytophthora* spp. or *Pythium*
spp.) [5] — this latter oomycete finding does overlap with rosemary's
own dominant disease category, but is explicitly framed as a secondary
rather than primary hazard for sage, unlike rosemary's own primary-
hazard framing. A major horticultural society additionally lists honey
fungus (rarely), verticillium wilt and foot/root rots, plus named pests
(rosemary beetle, sage leafhopper, capsid bug), with other sources
adding Japanese beetles, whiteflies, spider mites, and aphids [5] — a
notably longer named-pest list than this project found for rosemary
(which had none sourced). Sources disagree on overall severity, with one
calling sage generally pest/disease-light and another comparing its
disease load to ordinary herbaceous annuals [5] — not reconciled,
consistent with honesty conventions.

**Finding 7 — sage itself (plant/culinary leaf) is non-toxic to pets per
a major pet-safety organization, directly paralleling rosemary's own
plant-non-toxic finding, but the human essential-oil hazard (thujone) is
a genuinely distinct neurotoxicity profile this project has not recorded
before.** That organization's own plant page lists *Salvia officinalis*
(Common/Garden Sage) non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses [6]. A medical
review found no reported cases of acute intoxication from *S.
officinalis*, with toxic effects described mainly in chronic use of
concentrated extracts [6] — a notably cleaner "plant safe, product-
dependent hazard" framing than rosemary's own finding. The essential
oil's hazard is centered on thujone: reported at roughly 18-43% alpha-
thujone depending on source, with thujone and camphor both recognized as
CNS-active/neurotoxic [7]. A 1981 rat study found sage oil's convulsant
effect to be centrally mediated, with convulsions above ~0.5 g/kg and
lethality around ~3.2 g/kg [8] (an animal dose, not directly
transferable to a human threshold). Human case reports describe tonic-
clonic seizures in a newborn and a toddler following sage essential-oil
exposure [7]. Sources advise against oral essential-oil use at all, and
against concentrated sage supplements/extracts during pregnancy
(possible uterine effects) and breastfeeding (possible reduced milk
supply); culinary leaf amounts are considered acceptable [7][9]. Dose-
ceiling figures disagree sharply and are not reconciled this pass: one
source gives 3-6 g dried leaf/day, while another cites a regulatory-
sourced ~5 mg/day infusion limit, and a third states no safe oral dose
for isolated thujone has been established [7][9] — flagged per honesty
conventions rather than picking one. No sage-specific animal essential-
oil toxicity finding was sourced this pass, unlike rosemary's own
dedicated finding.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial subshrub, Mediterranean-
  climate-adapted but measurably more cold-hardy than rosemary (Finding
  4).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, matching rosemary's own
  evergreen-adjacent continuous-cutting framing — not independently re-
  verified against a dedicated multi-cut-per-season source this pass,
  same caveat flagged for rosemary.
- **Climate:** USDA zones 5-8, 4-8, or up to 10 depending on cultivar
  across disputed sources (Finding 4) — measurably broader/more cold-
  hardy than rosemary's own disputed zone 6-10/6-9/7-11 range.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — culinary seasoning use, not eaten in
  bulk.
- **Pollination:** Not required for food/leaf yield (harvested as
  foliage); required for seed production specifically — protandrous
  flowers reduce self-pollination, pollinated via the genus's staminal-
  lever mechanism (Finding 5, sourced from related Salvia species, not
  *S. officinalis* directly).
- **Yield / composition:** Not reconciled to a clean *S. officinalis*-
  specific kg/ha figure for either fresh/dried leaf or essential oil
  this pass — the clearest sourced figures found were for different
  Salvia species, kept separate per honesty conventions (Finding 2).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance assumed very high by analogy to
  rosemary (not independently re-verified this pass — plausible given
  the shared genus and Mediterranean-herb profile, but flagged as
  carried by analogy, not newly sourced). Waterlogging tolerance assumed
  low by analogy; this pass's own disease findings (root rot from soil
  oomycetes, Finding 6) are consistent with but do not independently
  confirm this figure.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed (primary per a commercial seed-company
  growing guide; 7-21 days at 65-70°F/18-21°C; light-sensitive, should
  not be covered with soil; viability 3-5 years) (Finding 3). An
  alternative route of vegetative softwood cuttings (late spring/summer
  as stems begin turning brown) is independently described as at least
  as dominant commercially as seed by several sources (Finding 3) — this
  document does not resolve which route actually dominates, a genuine
  complication of a cleaner seed-vs-cuttings divergence framing.
  Pollinator-dependent for seed set (protandrous, staminal-lever bee
  pollination mechanism, sourced from related Salvia species not *S.
  officinalis* directly) (Finding 5).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced this pass. A semi-arid Turkish
  trial recommends ~50,000 plants/ha with 120 kg N/ha for optimal yield
  under semi-arid conditions specifically (Finding 2) — a more specific,
  genuinely sourced fertilizer-rate figure than this project found for
  rosemary's own soil/fertility notes.
- **Stand life:** Woody perennial subshrub; no specific maximum
  productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was sourced this pass,
  the same gap flagged for rosemary.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Powdery mildew, two different named causal
  pathogens across independent studies, not reconciled — a genuinely
  distinct dominant disease category from rosemary's own Phytophthora-
  dominated profile (Finding 6). Root rot/foliar blight, including a
  first-global-report pathogen and a secondary soil-oomycete
  (*Phytophthora*/*Pythium*) risk that does overlap with rosemary's own
  primary hazard, though framed as secondary for sage. Named pests:
  rosemary beetle, sage leafhopper, capsid bug, Japanese beetles,
  whiteflies, spider mites, aphids — a notably longer named-pest list
  than rosemary's own (which had none sourced).
- **Genome:** 2C DNA 1.10 pg (diploid) / 2.12 pg (autotetraploid)
  (Finding 1) — a directly species-specific figure, though measured in a
  different basis (pg) from rosemary's own 1.17 Gb assembly figure, not
  converted/reconciled this pass.
- **Cultivars:** Cultivars with colorful/mottled foliage (contrast with
  plain-green culinary types) are reported less cold-hardy and slower-
  growing than the plain type (Finding 4). One named cultivar is
  described as a vigorous, large-leaved, strong-flavored clone that
  rarely flowers, rated among the hardiest; a second named cultivar
  (soft grey-purple leaves) and a third (green with yellow-variable
  edges) are described as milder-flavored; a fourth (pink stems/leaf
  stalks/shoot tips) is described as the least hardy of the four and
  intolerant of crowding, wet, or cold — sourced but encyclopedic/
  nursery-level, not independently peer-reviewed-sourced.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — foliage cut as needed/at usable size
  through the growing season, no distinguishable immature-vs-ripe
  harvest-stage choice sourced, matching rosemary's own framing. Single
  window — cut-and-come-again continuous cutting through the growing
  season, matching rosemary's own framing, in contrast with lavender's
  own single annual flowering window.
- **Storage:** Dried sage storage-life sources disagree sharply (6
  months to 3 years depending on source), with "peak potency within the
  first 3-6 months, usable longer" the most common framing — sourced but
  low-quality, the same low-quality-content-farm-domain caveat flagged
  for rosemary's own storage figures. Fresh-to-dried ratio commonly
  cited at 3:1 by volume — same caveat.

## Hazards

Powdery mildew (two different named causal pathogens across independent
studies, not reconciled) — a genuinely distinct dominant disease
category from rosemary's own Phytophthora-dominated profile. Root
rot/foliar blight, including a first-global-report pathogen and a
secondary soil-oomycete (*Phytophthora*/*Pythium*) risk. Named pests:
rosemary beetle, sage leafhopper, capsid bug, Japanese beetles,
whiteflies, spider mites, aphids (Finding 6).

The plant itself (fresh/dried leaf) is non-toxic to dogs/cats/horses per
a major pet-safety organization.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the concentrated essential oil carries a thujone-
driven neurotoxicity risk: human case reports describe tonic-clonic
seizures in a newborn and a toddler following sage essential-oil
exposure, and sources advise against oral essential-oil use at all, and
against concentrated sage supplements/extracts during pregnancy and
breastfeeding (Finding 7). This hazard is deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** foliage cut cut-and-come-again through the growing season →
  used fresh or dried as a culinary seasoning, or distilled into
  essential oil (thujone-bearing, a hazard distinct from the plant
  itself).

## Open research questions

- Clary sage and Anatolian/Greek sage surfaced repeatedly in this pass's
  yield research as distinct species with their own cultivation/yield
  literature — flagged as possible future candidate crop entries, not
  researched directly.
- Sage's own genome-size figure (2C DNA 1.10/2.12 pg) uses a different
  measurement basis from rosemary's own 1.17 Gb chromosome-level
  assembly figure — not converted or reconciled to allow a direct cross-
  crop comparison this pass.
- No clean *S. officinalis*-specific kg/ha figure was found for either
  fresh/dried leaf or essential-oil yield this pass — the clearest
  sourced figures found were for different Salvia species, kept separate
  per honesty conventions. A semi-arid Turkish trial recommends a
  specific planting density/N-rate combination for *S. officinalis*
  itself but the search snippet opened this pass did not give the
  resulting absolute yield figures.
- This document does not resolve whether seed or vegetative cuttings
  actually dominates commercial common-sage propagation — both are
  independently and repeatedly described as viable/standard.
- No commercial germination-percentage or cutting-rooting-success figure
  was found for sage this pass — the same gap this project found for
  rosemary's own cuttings.
- The sourced convulsant/lethal oral-dose figures for sage essential oil
  (0.5 g/kg convulsant, 3.2 g/kg lethal) are from a 1981 rat study, not
  independently translated to a human-equivalent threshold this pass.
- No sage-specific animal (pet) essential-oil toxicity finding was
  sourced this pass, unlike rosemary's own dedicated finding.
- This document's own pollination-mechanism findings (protandry,
  staminal lever) are all sourced from related Salvia species, not from
  *S. officinalis* directly — a genuine cross-species-generalization
  caveat, not independently verified against an *S. officinalis*-
  specific pollination study this pass.
- Dried-sage storage-life and fresh-to-dried-ratio figures came almost
  entirely from the same low-quality content-farm domain already flagged
  for rosemary's own storage findings.
- No specific maximum productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was
  sourced for this perennial crop, the same gap flagged for rosemary.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any sage operation
  this pass.
- No seed-yield-per-hectare or isolation-distance figure was sourced for
  sage's own seed-propagation route, despite seed being a genuinely
  viable (possibly co-primary) route, a different situation from
  rosemary's own markedly-secondary seed route.
- The named-cultivar trait-delta claims are nursery/encyclopedic-level,
  not independently peer-reviewed-sourced — though this is still a more
  specific, better-sourced cultivar finding than this project found for
  rosemary's own cultivars.
- Sage's light requirement was not independently sourced this pass — a
  full-sun requirement was assumed by analogy to every other
  Mediterranean Lamiaceae herb this project has researched.
- Sage's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass — this crop's apparently lower
  waterlogging/root-rot emphasis relative to rosemary may make it a
  genuinely more tractable candidate, not independently confirmed.
