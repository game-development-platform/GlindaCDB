# Basil (*Ocimum basilicum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/basil.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as sweet basil, *Ocimum basilicum* — the species behind
essentially all mainstream culinary basil cultivars (Genovese, Italian
large-leaf, Thai, lemon, purple/opal, etc.), treated here as cultivar-
level variation within one species. This file does NOT cover holy basil
/ Tulsi (*Ocimum tenuiflorum*, syn. *O. sanctum*), a genuinely distinct
species within the same genus, primarily grown for Hindu religious/
ritual use and traditional medicine rather than as a culinary herb, with
a different clove-like flavor profile and often a woodier, more
perennial growth habit in tropical climates. Holy basil is a separate
crop this project has researched elsewhere (Finding 9).

Basil shares the mint family (Lamiaceae) with several cold-hardy woody
perennial subshrub herbs this project has also researched, but is not
treated as a close sibling to them the way some other crop pairs in this
family are compared to each other. Those other Lamiaceae herbs are real
Mediterranean woody perennial subshrubs, broadly cold-hardy, harvested as
repeat-cut foliage from an established, multi-year planting. Basil is
instead a frost-tender true annual — chilling injury begins around 12°C
and severe damage occurs below 10°C, which rules out the same evergreen,
multi-year-stand model entirely. This is a genuine, load-bearing point
of difference from those other mint-family herbs, not a minor cultivar-
level distinction.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — basil's chromosome number and ploidy are genuinely
unresolved across the literature, while the genome size itself is
comparatively well-anchored by a real reference assembly.** Reported 2n
values for *O. basilicum* span 48, 50, 52, 56, 72, and 74 across
different studies [1]. A 2010 cytological study of 20 genotypes found
2n=48, but two genotypes of the purple-leaf variety instead showed
2n=72 — a ploidy difference within the species, not merely between
studies [1]. A separate Thai study instead treats *O. basilicum* as the
tetraploid member of a polyploid series on a base number of x=13
(2n=4x=52), while other authors treat the genus's base number as x=12
and describe basil as tetraploid on that basis instead — two different
base-number conventions producing different "tetraploid" chromosome
counts for the same ploidy level [1]. One review explicitly concludes
that basil's many cultivars and high morphological variation have
prevented researchers from reaching a clear, single chromosome-number
figure for the species as a whole [1]. By contrast, genome size is
comparatively well-anchored: flow-cytometry 1C estimates range 1.46-2.32
Gbp, but a reference genome assembly for one cultivar gives a concrete,
sequenced tetraploid genome size of 2.13 Gbp across 12,212 scaffolds
(>90% of the assembly placed) [1] — a real sequenced anchor point this
project's other genome-size disputes in this family have often lacked.

**Finding 2 — basil's leaf and essential-oil composition is organized
around named chemotypes, not merely cultivar-level taste variation.** A
review of chemotype classification names two chemotypes as European, one
as tropical, and one as a Réunion-Island chemotype [2]. A 34-cultivar
study found linalool the most abundant essential-oil constituent
overall, dominating in 28 of the 34 cultivars at concentrations of
24.2-81.1%, while methyl chavicol (estragole) reached up to 82.0% in
some samples instead [2]. Purple-leaf basil's oil was found in one
source to contain 57.3% methyl chavicol, while a different variety
instead had 68.0% linalool — showing the chemotype split cuts across,
not along, the obvious purple/green leaf-color cultivar distinction [2].
Enzyme-level work on sweet basil found that in the high-estragole
chemotype specifically, the O-methyltransferase enzyme was highly
specific for chavicol, while eugenol was essentially not methylated at
all — a genuine biosynthetic-pathway-level explanation for why
chemotypes diverge the way they do, not just an unexplained
compositional difference [2]. Lemon basil (*O.* × *citriodorum*) is
additionally identified as a genuine interspecific hybrid between *O.
basilicum* and *O. americanum*, not simply a flavor cultivar of *O.
basilicum* itself [2] — a taxonomic wrinkle flagged here rather than
silently folded into the same species.

**Finding 3 — basil's yield and time-to-harvest figures disagree across
sources in ways this document could not fully reconcile.** One
cultivation guide reports dried-leaf yield of 2.5-7.5 t/ha or fresh-leaf
yield of 15-25 t/ha, with essential-oil yield around 10-20 kg/ha [3]. A
separate Indian processing document instead gives oil yield as 10-23
kg/ha, noting that the figure depends on variety, season, and origin
[3]. Time to first harvest likewise disagrees: one extension guide gives
60 days, while an Indian source instead gives 90-95 days for the first
harvest, with subsequent harvests every 65-75 days; a Turkish field
study instead reports roughly 4-5 weeks between harvests and 10-15 days
between growth periods, and a grower guide separately estimates 10-14
days for post-pinch regrowth specifically in warm summer weather [3].
These figures are not averaged away, and the full spread is recorded
rather than picking one source silently.

**Finding 4 — basil's harvest-quality dynamics are driven by a repeat-
cut pinching regime where delaying harvest, not merely under-
harvesting, actively degrades the product.** Pinching/cutting before
flowering is reported to keep the plant bushy and productive, while
leaving the plant unpinched (harvesting only a few bottom leaves) leaves
it tall and prone to bolting [4]. Once the plant flowers, leaves are
reported to become smaller and more bitter as the plant's energy shifts
toward seed production — flowers specifically form in the leaf axils,
directly trading off against further leaf yield at the same nodes [4].
Bolting triggers are reported as long days (over roughly 14 hours of
light), temperatures above 32°C (90°F), drought stress, or the plant
becoming root-bound [4] — a notably more varied set of bolting triggers
(photoperiod and heat and water stress and root confinement) than most
bolting-prone crops typically show from a single dominant trigger.

**Finding 5 — basil carries two well-studied fungal/oomycete disease
problems with genuinely different resistance-breeding strategies, and
the disease-resistance literature for one of them (downy mildew)
specifically disagrees across trials.** Basil downy mildew (*Peronospora
belbahrii*) resistance is described by one extension source as only
partial — resistant cultivars are not fully immune [5]. Trial results
genuinely conflict: one university study found four of five "resistant"
cultivars became infected at the same time as a known-susceptible
control, with only one cultivar remaining symptom-free, while a separate
trial at a different institution instead found several different
cultivars showing only limited symptoms [5] — the two trials do not
agree on which resistant cultivars actually held up, probably reflecting
different sites, seasons, or pathogen races, per the researchers' own
framing. A 2025 preprint specifically proposes a differential panel to
track pathogen races, directly paralleling the race-overcoming-
resistance problem documented in related downy mildews generally [5]. A
2015 resistance-screening study found most *O. basilicum* entries highly
susceptible to downy mildew, while most entries belonging to several
wild relative species — including holy basil itself — were highly
resistant, with the study's authors concluding that interspecific
crossing with these wild relatives is a feasible breeding path [5] — a
direct, sourced connection between this document's culinary-basil
disease-resistance finding and holy basil's own disease profile. Fusarium
wilt (*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *basilici*), by contrast, is
controlled primarily through resistant cultivars bred via conventional
genetics rather than wild-species crossing: one cultivar is named as the
first released resistant cultivar, another is described as an Italian
large-leaf cultivar with intermediate resistance, and the pathogen is
noted to survive in soil for many years, making resistant cultivars the
primary practical control [5]. A 2006 study found resistance inheritance
consistent with two resistance alleles, while a 2022 mapping study
instead found a single QTL with a candidate gene under investigation —
two different genetic models for the same trait, not independently
reconciled here.

**Finding 6 — basil's seed is both a propagation input and a directly
marketed human food product in its own right, with a seed-biology trait
(mucilage gel formation) notably different from most spice/herb crop
seeds.** Basil is reported as insect-pollinated and genuinely cross-
pollinating between varieties — one extension seed-saving guide
recommends roughly 150 feet (~45m) of isolation for true-to-type seed,
while a separate French seed-production guide argues 100m is not always
sufficient and that commercial seed production sometimes calls for
several hundred metres [6] — a real, sourced disagreement on the
isolation distance needed, left unresolved. A Brazilian protected-
cultivation study found that excluding pollinating insects with nets
severely restricted natural pollen flow, directly confirming the
pollinator dependency rather than merely assuming it from the isolation-
distance recommendation [6]. Separately, basil seed forms a mucilaginous
gel on contact with water — a microscopy study describes mucilage
emerging as spiral filaments immediately on wetting, with a measured
water-holding capacity of 97.5±2.4 g water per gram of mucilage, and
identifies the polysaccharide involved as glucomannan, the same general
category of hydrocolloid chia seed is known for [7]. This gelled seed
(sabja/tukmaria/falooda seed) is eaten directly as a food and drink
ingredient across South and Southeast Asian cuisines, and separately
studied as a candidate natural food-thickening/gelling/stabilizing
hydrocolloid, reported to retain its gelling behavior even under high-
temperature conditions [7] — a genuinely dual-purpose seed commodity
(propagation stock and a directly consumed food product). Germination
targets are reported at 80-95% in one production guide, with growers
advised against planting seed lots testing below 70% — a separate guide
gives the same 70% cutoff independently [6]. Basil additionally needs a
long, hot growing season to produce viable seed at all, making seed-
saving explicitly difficult in cooler regions per one extension source
[6] — directly relevant to this crop's own frost-tender, warm-season-
only cultivation profile (Finding 7).

**Finding 7 — basil's cold sensitivity is well documented through
storage/postharvest studies, though direct field frost-tolerance figures
were not found.** Basil is described by one review as highly sensitive
to cold temperatures, a trait attributed to its tropical origins [8].
Injury onset is placed at 12°C (54°F), with temperatures below 10°C
(50°F) inducing severe-to-total injury according to one review,
independently corroborated by a separate source citing the same
12°C/54°F threshold [8]. Symptoms include leaf necrosis (spotting or
browning) and loss of leaf turgidity/decay [8]. A storage study found
more severe chilling injury and greater aroma-volatile loss at 5°C
compared to 10°C or 15°C — meaning cold storage itself measurably
degrades basil's defining culinary trait (aroma), not just its visual/
textural quality [8]. One grower source states that sweet basil
specifically is the least cold-sensitive type among basil's many
cultivars, implying the other named types (Thai, holy basil's close
relatives, etc.) may be even more cold-tender — not independently
verified this pass. These figures are postharvest/storage findings
(applying to harvested or potted plants held in cold conditions), not
field frost-tolerance figures — the sources consulted did not give a
direct field frost-kill temperature threshold the way a typical annual-
vegetable production guide would.

**Finding 8 — basil's soil and water requirements are comparatively
mundane, but the specific pH range found this pass disagrees moderately
across sources.** Most sources converge on pH roughly 6.0-7.0, with the
most authoritative source opened this pass giving a narrower 6.0-6.5,
while a separate source instead describes basil as tolerant of a much
broader 5.5-8.0 range [9]. Soil is consistently described as wanting to
be well-drained and organically rich, with one source noting basil does
not strictly require fertile soil but performs best with added organic
matter [9]. Water guidance calls for evenly moist soil without
waterlogging, with one blog source suggesting roughly 1-1.5 inches/week
as a rough rule of thumb (explicitly flagged here as a lower-authority
figure) [9]. Light requirement is given as 6-8 hours of direct sun daily
by one source [9]. A targeted search for authoritative provincial/state
extension-service production guides did not surface one directly in the
results opened this pass.

**Finding 9 — holy basil (Tulsi, *Ocimum tenuiflorum*) is a genuinely
distinct species from sweet basil, not merely a flavor cultivar, with
its own primary use case (Hindu religious ritual) that this document
scopes out rather than silently absorbing.** Sources use two names for
the same plant — *Ocimum sanctum* L. and *Ocimum tenuiflorum* L.
(treated as a synonym) [10]. Hindus revere the plant as a manifestation
of the goddess Lakshmi (as Tulsi), consort of Vishnu; daily worship
includes offering a garland of Tulsi leaves, and many households
maintain a dedicated courtyard structure (tulsi vrindavan) for the
living plant [10]. Tulsi Vivah — a ritual marriage between the Tulsi
plant and a Saligram stone (representing Vishnu or his avatar Krishna,
both framings used across sources) — is performed between Prabodhini
Ekadashi and Kartik Purnima, marking the end of Chaturmas and the start
of the Hindu wedding season [10]. Dead plants' woody stems are reported
to be made into prayer beads (japa mala) [10]. Whether "Krishna Tulsi"
(purple leaves) and "Rama Tulsi" (green leaves) are two color varieties
of the same species or something more taxonomically distinct is
unsettled in the sources opened this pass — only blog/shop-level sources
distinguish them, and no authoritative botanical database confirmation
either way was found. Separately and significantly, Finding 5's disease-
resistance study found holy basil among the wild-relative species highly
resistant to basil downy mildew, where most sweet basil entries were
highly susceptible [5][10] — a genuinely useful, double-sourced
connection between this species split and this document's own disease
finding.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season true annual, frost-tender —
  chilling injury onset ~12°C, severe/total injury below 10°C (Finding
  7) — a genuine point of difference from other mint-family herbs this
  project has researched, all real cold-hardy perennials.
- **Climate:** Warm temperate, subtropical, tropical (as a warm-season
  annual; no true cold-hardiness the way some sibling mint-family herbs
  have).
- **Food status:** Raw edible as a fresh culinary herb, the dominant
  culinary use.
- **Pollination:** Required for seed production only, not for leaf
  harvest. Insect-pollinated and cross-pollinating, confirmed by a
  pollinator-exclusion-netting study showing severely restricted seed
  set without pollinators (Finding 6) — a genuine point of difference
  from some other spice/herb crops this project has researched that are
  far more self-fertile/clonally propagated.
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh-leaf 15,000-25,000 kg/ha; dried leaf
  2,500-7,500 kg/ha (one cultivation guide); essential oil 10-23 kg/ha
  (two independent sources broadly agreeing) (Finding 3). Harvest-timing
  and harvest-interval figures disagree moderately across sources.
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-style crop-coefficient curve specific to
  basil was found. Drought tolerance is low — basil is explicitly
  described as intolerant of water stress, and drought is independently
  named as one of several bolting triggers (Finding 4/8). Waterlogging
  tolerance is low — well-drained soil is explicitly called for.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed; needs a long, hot growing season to
  set viable seed — explicitly noted to make seed-saving difficult in
  cooler regions (Finding 6). Likely chilling-sensitive from germination
  onward given the crop's overall cold intolerance (Finding 7), though
  this was not independently sourced for the seedling stage specifically.
- **Soil:** pH optimum disputed across three framings: 6.0-6.5 (narrower,
  more authoritative source) vs. 6.0-7.0 (broader consensus) vs. 5.5-8.0
  (one source's wider tolerance claim). Does not strictly require
  fertile soil but performs best with added organic matter; well-drained
  preferred (Finding 8).
- **Stand life:** True annual — the entire cultivation cycle is one warm
  season, ending at first frost or at bolting if harvest/pinching is
  neglected; no multi-year stand concept applies, a genuine structural
  contrast with other mint-family herbs this project has researched, all
  real perennials.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Basil downy mildew (*Peronospora
  belbahrii*), partial-resistance-only, conflicting cultivar-trial
  results across at least two independent university studies, active
  pathogen-race-tracking research ongoing (Finding 5). Fusarium wilt
  (*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *basilici*), soil-persistent for many
  years, controlled primarily via resistant cultivars, genetic basis
  disputed between a two-allele model (2006) and a single-QTL model
  (2022) (Finding 5).
- **Genome:** Reported 2n values span 48, 50, 52, 56, 72, and 74 across
  different studies, with ploidy conventions genuinely unresolved
  (Finding 1). Genome size is comparatively well-anchored: flow-cytometry
  1C estimates range 1.46-2.32 Gbp, with a sequenced reference assembly
  for one cultivar giving a concrete tetraploid genome size of 2.13 Gbp
  across 12,212 scaffolds.
- **Cultivars:** Several named cultivar groups exist within *O.
  basilicum* with sourced chemical/trait deltas. **Genovese/Italian
  large-leaf types**: dominant culinary-pesto type, not independently
  chemotyped this pass. **Thai basil types**: not independently
  chemotyped this pass either. **Purple-leaf (purpurascens-type)
  basil**: sourced at 2n=72 in two genotypes vs. 2n=48 in the species'
  more typical genotypes, and at 57.3% methyl chavicol in one source's
  oil analysis vs. 68.0% linalool in a different variety's — showing the
  chemotype split does not track cleanly with the purple/green leaf-
  color distinction (Finding 1/2). **Lemon basil** (*O.* × *citriodorum*):
  a genuine interspecific hybrid with *O. americanum*, not simply a
  flavor cultivar of *O. basilicum* (Finding 2). **Disease-resistant
  lines**: a first released Fusarium-wilt-resistant cultivar and a
  second cultivar with intermediate Fusarium resistance, plus several
  disputed-efficacy downy-mildew-resistant lines named in Finding 5.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — leaf is picked at one practical maturity
  point (pre-flowering, by pinching), with flowering itself marking the
  end of productive-quality harvest rather than a second harvestable
  stage. Single window — one continuous repeat-cut harvest period per
  annual planting, from first cut to frost-kill or bolting; no multi-
  flush/separate-cohort pattern sourced.

## Hazards

No human- or animal-health hazard (toxin, allergen, drug interaction)
was found this pass for basil itself. The disease hazards (basil downy
mildew, Fusarium wilt) are agronomic, not food-safety or toxicological,
concerns, covered above under Disease/pest profile.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh leaf → harvested by repeat-cut pinching → used fresh
  (dominant culinary use) or dried (dry-recovery not directly sourced
  for this crop) or distilled into essential oil. Seed is both saved for
  propagation and, when soaked into a mucilaginous gel, eaten directly
  as a food and drink ingredient (sabja/tukmaria/falooda seed) across
  South and Southeast Asian cuisines, and separately studied as a
  candidate natural food-thickening/gelling/stabilizing hydrocolloid
  (Finding 6).

## Open research questions

- Whether lemon basil (a genuine interspecific hybrid) should be
  researched as its own separate entry rather than folded into sweet
  basil's own cultivar notes.
- Yield and time-to-harvest figures disagree across at least four
  independent sources (60 vs. 90-95 days to first harvest; varying
  subsequent-harvest intervals) — not reconciled.
- Seed-isolation distance for true-to-type seed production is disputed
  (roughly 45m per one extension source vs. "several hundred metres" per
  a seed-production guide) — not reconciled.
- Sweet basil is reported as the least cold-sensitive basil type by one
  source, implying other named types may be more cold-tender — not
  independently verified or quantified this pass.
- No direct field frost-kill temperature threshold was sourced — the
  chilling-injury figures found this pass are postharvest/storage
  findings, not field production guidance.
- Soil pH optimum is disputed across three source framings, and a
  targeted search for an authoritative production guide from a major
  extension service did not surface one directly in the results opened
  this pass.
- Whether "Krishna Tulsi" (purple leaf) and "Rama Tulsi" (green leaf) are
  two color varieties of the same species or something more
  taxonomically distinct is unsettled in the sources opened this pass.
- No specific storage-days figure was sourced for fresh basil leaf
  beyond the chilling-injury temperature thresholds themselves.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any basil operation
  (planting, pinching, harvesting, drying).
- No seed-yield-per-hectare figure (distinct from seed quality/
  germination rate) was sourced for seed-crop production specifically.
- No chemotype or named-cultivar-group essential-oil composition was
  independently sourced for Genovese or Thai basil specifically.
- Basil's prevalence as a commercial hydroponic/vertical-farm and
  spaceflight/closed-system-agriculture trial crop is general knowledge
  this pass did not independently source with a citation.

## Estimates and derivations

A fresh-to-dried-leaf yield-chain fraction of roughly 0.10-0.20 is an
estimate: no source gave a direct basil-specific dry-recovery
percentage, and the range is drawn by analogy to other dried leafy herbs,
reasoning that tender leafy herbage typically loses roughly 80-90% of
its fresh mass as water on drying. A fresh-leaf-to-oil yield-chain
fraction of roughly 0.001-0.003 is derived from one source's fresh-leaf
yield (15,000-25,000 kg/ha) and essential-oil yield (roughly 10-20
kg/ha) from the same general cultivation context, implying an oil
fraction of approximately 0.0004-0.0013, widened to additionally cover a
second, independent source's 10-23 kg/ha oil figure, which was not
stated over a directly comparable fresh-leaf-mass base — a reasoning
step combining two partially-independent sourced figures, not a single
directly sourced dry-recovery percentage.
