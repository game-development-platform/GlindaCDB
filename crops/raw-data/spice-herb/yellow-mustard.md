# Yellow / White Mustard, condiment seed (*Sinapis alba*)

## Naming and scope

This covers the condiment seed use of *Sinapis alba* — the mild seed of
American ready-made ("ballpark") mustard — a different species from
brown mustard (*Brassica juncea*), not merely a different color or
processing of the same plant. This document does not re-derive brown
mustard's own findings; where a direct comparison is useful, it cites
this project's own brown mustard research explicitly by finding number.

This project's own brown mustard research was read in full as the
closest sibling — same general family (Brassicaceae), same condiment-
seed use category, but a genuinely different species with its own
distinct genome, breeding system, and chemistry, not a minor variant.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — Sinapis alba is a true diploid (2n=24, SS genome), a
genuinely simpler ploidy story than brown mustard's own allotetraploid
(AABB, 2n=36) hybrid-origin story, with a real draft genome assembly
already published, though — like brown mustard's own assembly — it is
a sequence-based figure, not an independent cytometric measurement.**
Multiple sources agree on 2n=24 with an "SS" genome designation,
describing *Sinapis alba* as a diploid species [1] — a simple, non-
hybrid diploid genome, in direct contrast to brown mustard's own
allotetraploid AABB origin from two different parent species. A draft
genome assembly gives approximately 459 Mbp of sequence, covered across
403,423 contigs; a separate genome-database entry lists a full assembly
of 449,668,332 bp [1] — these two figures broadly agree with each other
(within about 2%), a notably tighter internal agreement than brown
mustard's own single-assembly figure (which had no second figure to
compare against). Both remain sequence-based assembly lengths, not
independent flow-cytometry measurements, carrying the same caveat
already flagged for brown mustard's own assembly.

**Finding 2 — Sinapis alba is reported an obligate out-crossing species
due to a sporophytic self-incompatibility (SI) breeding system — the
exact opposite reproductive-biology finding from brown mustard, which
was confirmed self-compatible with no SI system found at all.** One
source describes the species as "an obligate out-crossing crop due to
its self-incompatibility reproduction system," adding that "yellow
mustard has a sporophytic self-incompatibility reproductive system
which makes it difficult to develop homozygote parental and recombinant
inbred lines" [2] — a direct, specific, and genuinely contrasting
finding from brown mustard's own finding (self-compatible, no SI system
found, largely self-pollinated with only ~11-24% outcrossing). This
document treats the two mustard species' own breeding systems as
genuinely, substantively different, not a minor variation on the same
theme: brown mustard breeds true largely on its own, while yellow/white
mustard requires cross-pollination to set seed at all, with the
sporophytic SI mechanism specifically complicating inbred-line
development for breeders [2]. No isolation-distance figure was sourced
for yellow/white mustard this pass, a genuine gap relative to brown
mustard's own sourced 50-100 m figures.

**Finding 3 — yellow/white mustard's own pollinator-dependency finding
is quantified with a specific yield-increase percentage from a
dedicated field study.** A Russian field study identified insect
pollinators across three orders (Hymenoptera, 7 species; Diptera, 7
species; Lepidoptera, 1 species) visiting seed crops of white mustard,
and a summary of that same work reports pollinators provide
"sufficiently high utility," specifically increasing seed yield by
31.8% [3] — a directly quantified pollinator-value figure, stronger
evidence than dill's own caged-exclusion qualitative finding (which
showed poor seed set without pollinators but gave no percentage) or
brown mustard's own merely-claimed, not measured, pollinator
attraction. This is directly consistent with, and provides the missing
quantitative support for, Finding 2's own obligate-outcrossing claim: a
species that must cross-pollinate to set seed would be expected to show
a large, measurable yield response to pollinator presence, exactly as
this study found.

**Finding 4 — Sinapis alba's own dominant glucosinolate is sinalbin,
reported at over 90% of total glucosinolate content in cotyledon
tissue across multiple tested populations — a cleaner, more dominant
single-compound chemistry than brown mustard's own sinigrin finding,
and a genuinely different compound from brown mustard's, consistent
with these being different species with different pungency chemistry,
not merely different concentrations of the same compound.** A study of
low-seed-glucosinolate populations of *S. alba* reports that sinalbin
makes up over 90% of the total glucosinolates in the cotyledons of the
three populations tested [4] — a notably cleaner, more dominant single-
compound finding than brown mustard's own sinigrin-dominant-but-not-
quantified-as-a-percentage-of-total-glucosinolates finding (which
quantified only the hydrolysis yield of sinigrin to allyl
isothiocyanate, not sinigrin's own share of total glucosinolate).
Sinalbin and sinigrin are different glucosinolate compounds (sinalbin
hydrolyzes to p-hydroxybenzyl isothiocyanate, a comparatively milder
compound, while sinigrin hydrolyzes to the more pungent allyl
isothiocyanate) — this document does not merely assert this chemical
distinction from general background knowledge, but treats it as the
most likely explanation, consistent with both species' own sourced
findings, for yellow/white mustard's own well-known milder culinary
character relative to brown mustard's own sharper pungency; no direct
side-by-side pungency-comparison study was independently sourced this
pass to confirm the magnitude of this difference.

**Finding 5 — yellow/white mustard shows a sourced, large sowing-date
yield penalty from an independent study (Polish), structurally similar
to brown mustard's own October-sowing finding but with a specific,
quantified magnitude brown mustard's own research lacked, and a
separate large-scale Romanian yield figure broadly consistent with
brown mustard's own cross-study range.** A Polish study found that
sowing late (after 10 May) cut seed yield by 38-79% compared to sowing
in the first ten days of April [5] — a specific, quantified, actionable
sowing-date penalty, a stronger figure than brown mustard's own finding
(which sourced only the direction of a sowing-date effect, not its
magnitude). A 2025 Romanian field trial separately reports a maximum
seed yield of 2,134 kg/ha from an early-March sowing at 25 cm row
spacing with N80P80 fertilization [5] — broadly within the same order
of magnitude as brown mustard's own 572-3,080 kg/ha cross-study range,
though this document does not merge the two species' own yield ranges,
since they remain separate species with separately sourced figures.
The crop is separately described as a "short-season crop" with "high
adaptability to environmental conditions" by one source, and a Polish
study found higher yield specifically when the growing period was
"moderately long" and precipitation was higher [5] — no specific day-
count figure was captured for either claim this pass.

**Finding 6 — pest pressure is reported lower than a related oilseed
crop (*Brassica napus*) in a dedicated comparative study, a genuinely
favorable relative-resistance finding paralleling brown mustard's own
relative-resistance finding against a different Brassica relative,
though the diseases named for yellow/white mustard specifically include
one (bacterial leaf spot) not named for brown mustard, and the overall
disease list otherwise overlaps substantially.** A peer-reviewed study
found *Sinapis alba* less susceptible to damage by insect pests than
*Brassica napus*, tested against four insect pests (a flea beetle, a
pollen beetle, and two aphid species) across low-seed-glucosinolate
populations of *S. alba* [6] — directly paralleling brown mustard's own
finding that a related Brassica group is more aphid-susceptible than
brown mustard itself, both mustard-family crops showing a favorable
relative-resistance profile against a different Brassica relative. A
Slovak crop guide separately lists flea beetles (*Phyllotreta* spp.),
aphids, cabbage seed weevils, and pollen beetles as pests, and names
Alternaria leaf spot, downy mildew (*Peronospora brassicae* — note: a
different named species from the genus-unspecified "downy mildew"
listed for brown mustard), bacterial leaf spot, and white mold
(*Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*) as the most common diseases [6] — three of
these four diseases (Alternaria, downy mildew, Sclerotinia) directly
parallel brown mustard's own four named diseases, though white rust is
not named for yellow/white mustard in this source, and bacterial leaf
spot is named for yellow/white mustard but not for brown mustard — this
document does not assume the two species share an identical disease
profile merely because they are both mustards, and records the
genuine, partial overlap and partial divergence as sourced.

**Finding 7 — no listing specifically naming Sinapis alba could be
located from a major pet-safety organization, and the closest-named
listing used for brown mustard's own toxicity finding is explicitly
scoped to "Brassica species," a different genus from Sinapis.** This
document does not extend brown mustard's own pet-safety finding to this
species, treating the genus mismatch as a real, substantive gap rather
than a naming technicality, while separately sourcing a livestock-
specific hazard (stubble/pod/seed poisoning in cattle, and a topical
skin-blistering effect from the seed oil) from an independent source. A
direct search for that organization's own scientific-name attribution
for its "White/Black/Yellow Indian Mustard" page (the same page used
for brown mustard's own finding) found the page's own scientific name
given as "Brassica species," in the family Brassicaceae — not Sinapis,
a different genus entirely [7]. Despite the page's own title including
the word "White," this document does not assume that title refers to
*Sinapis alba* specifically, since the scientific name given is
explicitly Brassica-genus, and multiple white-seeded cultivars exist
within *Brassica juncea* and other Brassica species as well — the
title's "White" may simply denote a white-seeded Brassica cultivar
group, not this document's own species. This is flagged as a genuine,
unresolved naming ambiguity, not resolved by assumption either way.
Separately, an Australian weed-database source reports that cattle may
be poisoned by the stubble of White Mustard crops, or by pods and seeds
included in feed meals, though field cases in Australia are described
as rare; the same source separately reports that the oil of White
Mustard can cause blistering and ulceration of the skin [7] — a
genuinely different hazard shape from brown mustard's own
isothiocyanate-GI-irritation finding: here the primary sourced
livestock risk is specifically tied to stubble/feed-meal context (not
simply ingesting the growing plant), and the skin-blistering effect is
a topical, not ingestion, hazard from the seed oil specifically.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual, herbaceous — true annual, described
  as a "short-season crop" by one source, with no specific day-count
  figure captured this pass.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — a single seed harvest per
  planting, the same general shape as brown mustard's own crop pattern.
- **Climate:** Not independently sourced as a USDA-equivalent figure
  this pass.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible in the usual sense — the seed is used
  as a ground/whole condiment spice (American "ballpark" mustard,
  pickling); no fresh product was sourced or expected.
- **Pollination:** Required — genuinely obligate; sporophytic self-
  incompatibility means the species cannot reliably set seed without
  cross-pollination (Finding 2), with a directly quantified 31.8% yield
  increase attributable to pollinator presence (Finding 3) — a
  stronger, quantified pollinator-dependency finding than any companion
  mustard-family or Apiaceae crop this project has researched in this
  pass.
- **Yield / composition:** 2,134 kg/ha maximum in one 2025 Romanian
  trial (early-March sowing, 25cm rows, N80P80 fertilization) (Finding
  5) — broadly within the same order of magnitude as brown mustard's
  own 572-3,080 kg/ha range, though not merged with it since these are
  separate species. Late sowing (after 10 May vs. first ten days of
  April) cuts yield by 38-79% in a Polish study — a specific, quantified
  sowing-date penalty, stronger than brown mustard's own merely
  directional finding.
- **Water use:** A Polish study found higher yield specifically under
  higher precipitation and a moderately long growing period (Finding
  5) — no specific crop coefficient or irrigation-regime figure sourced
  this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, the sole propagation route found
  or expected for this true annual. Seeding rate tested across 1.5-3.5
  million viable seeds/ha with 25cm or 50cm row spacing in one dry-
  steppe study, with seed yield reported to respond markedly to plant-
  density changes — no single recommended rate figure was captured this
  pass. Genuinely obligate cross-pollination via sporophytic self-
  incompatibility (Finding 2) — no isolation-distance figure sourced
  this pass, a gap relative to brown mustard's own sourced 50-100m
  figures.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** True annual, described as "short-season" with no
  specific day-count sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Less susceptible to insect-pest damage than
  the related oilseed crop *Brassica napus* in a dedicated comparative
  study (flea beetle, pollen beetle, two aphid species tested) — a
  favorable relative-resistance finding paralleling brown mustard's own
  finding (Finding 6). Named pests: flea beetles, aphids, cabbage seed
  weevils, pollen beetles. Named diseases: Alternaria leaf spot, downy
  mildew (*Peronospora brassicae*), bacterial leaf spot, white mold
  (*Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*) — genuine partial overlap with brown
  mustard's own four named diseases, not identical (white rust not
  named here; bacterial leaf spot not named for brown mustard).
- **Genome:** A real draft genome assembly exists, with two
  independently reported figures in close agreement (~459 Mbp;
  449,668,332 bp) (Finding 1) — both remain sequence-based assembly
  lengths, not independent cytometric measurements. Chromosome number
  well-converged: diploid, 2n=24, SS genome — a genuinely simpler, non-
  hybrid ploidy story than brown mustard's own allotetraploid origin.
- **Cultivars:** No specific named cultivar with sourced trait deltas
  was found this pass for the condiment-seed use specifically — the
  low-seed-glucosinolate populations referenced in Finding 4 and
  Finding 6 are research populations, not named commercial condiment
  cultivars.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — one seed harvest per planting; no leaf
  product or multi-stage system sourced for this crop. Single window
  per planting season; not independently characterized as a multi-
  window crop this pass.
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced this pass.

## Hazards

Less susceptible to insect-pest damage than the related oilseed crop
*Brassica napus* in a dedicated comparative study — a favorable
relative-resistance finding paralleling brown mustard's own finding
(Finding 6). Named pests: flea beetles, aphids, cabbage seed weevils,
pollen beetles. Named diseases: Alternaria leaf spot, downy mildew
(*Peronospora brassicae*), bacterial leaf spot, white mold
(*Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*).

No listing specifically naming *Sinapis alba* was located from a major
pet-safety organization; the genus-mismatched "Brassica species"
listing used for brown mustard's own toxicity finding is explicitly not
extended to this species — this species' own pet-toxicity status is
left genuinely unresolved, not assumed either way (Finding 7).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — cattle may be poisoned by White Mustard stubble,
or pods/seeds in feed meals (rare in practice); the seed oil can cause
skin blistering and ulceration on topical contact (Finding 7). This
livestock/dermal hazard is deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

- **Chain:** direct-seeded → single seed harvest → seed used as a

## Open research questions

  own assembly.

  own seed-crop production — a genuine gap relative to brown mustard's
  own sourced 50-100m figures, despite this species' own obligate
  cross-pollination (which, if anything, suggests isolation may matter
  more here, not less — not resolved this pass).

  (yellow/white mustard) and sinigrin (brown mustard) was independently

  Mustard" page is explicitly scoped to "Brassica species," a different
