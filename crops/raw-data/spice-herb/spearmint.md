# Spearmint (*Mentha spicata*)

## Naming and scope

This covers *Mentha spicata*, the carvone-dominant commercial mint. It
does not cover peppermint (*M. × piperita*, a separate entry in this
project's own research) or the wider *Mentha* genus generally.

This project's own peppermint research was read in full as the closest
sibling — same genus, same general cultivation shape (rhizomatous
herbaceous perennial, vegetatively propagated, essential-oil-focused),
but a genuinely different species with its own disputed hybrid-origin
story and different dominant chemistry.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — spearmint, like peppermint, has a genuinely disputed
cytogenetic story, but the two species' own disputes take different
shapes: peppermint's dispute is mainly about which ploidy/parentage
account is correct, while spearmint's own sources report multiple
genuinely different ploidy levels coexisting within the species itself,
a cytotype-diversity finding not paralleled by peppermint's own single-
hybrid-lineage story.** A ploidy database lists spearmint at 24
chromosomes, classified as polyploid, with a named subspecies also at 24
[1]. A 1950s-era British cytogenetic study reported two different
chromosome counts occurring within the species itself: 2n=36 and 2n=48
[1] — not a single disputed figure across different studies the way
some of peppermint's own older literature disagreed, but an explicit
within-species finding by one study. An Iranian cytogenetic study
independently found diploid, triploid, and tetraploid genotypes among
nine spearmint accessions it examined [1] — a genuinely sourced, multi-
cytotype finding within a single study, the clearest within-species
ploidy diversity result this project has found to date for any crop.
Separately, genomic work describes commercial spearmint cultivars as
"two unbalanced polyploid cultivars," and states spearmint itself arose
as a hybrid of two diploid parent species [1] — directly paralleling
peppermint's own disputed-hybrid-origin story, but naming a completely
different parent pair, confirming the two crops are not simply "the
same taxonomic situation twice." No reference-genome assembly or
genome-size (Mbp/Gbp) figure was found for spearmint this pass — a
weaker genomic-sourcing outcome than peppermint's own 2024 chromosome-
level assembly.

**Finding 2 — field yield and oil-chemistry figures are better cross-
source-corroborated for spearmint than they were for peppermint, with a
genuinely actionable, sourced harvest-time-of-day finding (not just
harvest stage) uniquely characterized for this crop.** A 2020 Pantnagar
(India) trial of five cultivars found two named cultivars giving the
highest herb and oil yields, at 124.10 and 116.20 q/ha herb (12,410 and
11,620 kg/ha by the standard 1 q = 100 kg conversion, see Estimates and
derivations) and 68.88 and 70.58 kg/ha essential oil respectively [2] —
a notably tighter, more internally consistent yield range than
peppermint's own scattered figures, from a single well-documented
cultivar trial. Carvone content varied measurably by cultivar within the
same trial, with one named cultivar showing the highest at 64.35% [2] —
the first sourced cultivar-level chemistry (not just yield) delta in
this project's mint-genus research. A separate Argentine study found
essential-oil yield 0.8-1.3 mL/100g dry matter, with carvone 60.72-
68.09% and limonene 14.23-16.41% as the main components [2] — broadly
consistent in magnitude with the Pantnagar trial's own carvone figures.
A genuinely unique, sourced harvest-timing finding not paralleled for
any other crop this project has researched: a Wyoming study found
flowering spearmint harvested around 9:00 AM gives the best overall
essential-oil yield, while harvesting at 9:00 PM instead gives oil with
a higher carvone concentration [2] — a true time-of-day (not just
harvest-stage or season) tradeoff between yield and chemical quality. A
Turkish study separately found the highest fresh herbage, dry herbage,
and dry-leaf yield specifically when harvesting after full flowering
stage [2] — consistent in shape with (though not the same variable as)
the Wyoming time-of-day finding.

**Finding 3 — propagation and general agronomic practice mirror
peppermint's own findings closely (same genus, same general cultivation
shape), with nitrogen fertilization again identified as a specific,
actionable yield driver, though this document could not independently
source spearmint-specific disease findings the way it could for
peppermint.** A Canadian provincial extension guide — the same source
cited in this project's own peppermint research — states commercial
spearmint (like peppermint) cannot be grown from seed and is propagated
by division or rhizome cuttings [3] — confirming this is a genus-wide,
not peppermint-specific, propagation constraint. The same source cites
an Indiana-derived nitrogen optimum of 168 kg/ha specifically for
spearmint, while explicitly cautioning that research/recommendations
from outside its own province do not necessarily apply to its own
growing conditions [3] — a genuinely transferable-with-caution figure,
flagged as such by the source itself. No USDA hardiness-zone figure
specific to spearmint was found this pass — only a Canadian zone-3
figure from that provincial extension guide, which this document does
not treat as a USDA-equivalent value. No spearmint-specific disease
finding (Verticillium, rust, leaf spot, or otherwise) was independently
sourced this pass — a genuine sourcing gap relative to peppermint's own
well-characterized Verticillium/rust findings; this document does not
assume spearmint shares peppermint's own disease profile without a
dedicated source, given the two species' already-demonstrated
differences in ploidy/origin (Finding 1) and chemistry (Finding 2).

**Finding 4 — pet-toxicity sourcing for spearmint resolves more cleanly
than it initially did for peppermint, since a major pet-safety
organization's own primary listing covers the genus as a whole, though
spearmint-specific secondary sourcing remains thin and at least one
source explicitly concedes it cannot name the specific toxic substances
involved.** That organization's own "Garden Mint" (*Mentha sp.*)
listing, located directly during this pass, names the genus (not a
specific species) as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, with essential
oils as the toxic principle and vomiting/diarrhea as the signs after
large ingestions [4] — this resolves a primary-source-location gap this
project's own peppermint research had flagged as open. A plant-
identification site specifically names spearmint as toxic to both cats
and dogs, but explicitly states the specific toxic substances in
spearmint have not been cited, even by that source itself [4] — a
genuinely more honest gap-acknowledgment than most of the low-quality
secondary sources found for peppermint's own toxicity claims. Several
sources separately claim spearmint is milder than peppermint and that
cats are more vulnerable than dogs [4] — plausible, consistent with the
general CNS-active-terpene/cat-liver-enzyme framing this project has
established for several other crops, but from low-quality sourcing not
independently verified this pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, herbaceous, rhizomatous — same
  general growth habit as peppermint (by analogy, not independently re-
  sourced for spearmint specifically beyond the shared propagation-
  constraint finding, Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, by analogy to peppermint's
  own framing, not independently re-verified for spearmint specifically.
- **Climate:** No USDA hardiness-zone figure independently sourced this
  pass — only a Canadian zone-3 figure, from a provincial extension
  guide, not treated as a USDA-equivalent value.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh leaves used in culinary contexts,
  notably more widespread culinary use than peppermint's own more
  oil/confectionery-skewed framing, not independently re-verified in
  this document.
- **Pollination:** Not required — foliage/oil harvest; vegetatively
  propagated (Finding 3).
- **Yield / composition:** Herb 11,620-12,410 kg/ha, essential oil
  68.88-70.58 kg/ha (best two of five cultivars in a single well-
  documented Indian trial) (Finding 2) — a tighter, more internally
  consistent range than peppermint's own scattered figures. Carvone
  content 60.72-68.09% (Argentine study) to as high as 64.35% for a
  specific cultivar in the Indian trial. Oil yield 0.8-1.3 mL/100g dry
  matter (Argentine study, a different unit basis, not converted to
  kg/ha without a sourced density figure).
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  propagation/fertilization findings in Finding 3.
- **Establishment:** Not seed-propagated commercially — division/
  rhizome cuttings only, the same genus-wide constraint as peppermint,
  confirmed by the same extension source for both crops (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Nitrogen optimum ~168 kg/ha (Indiana-derived figure, cited
  with an explicit cross-region-applicability caveat by the extension
  source) (Finding 3). No pH figure independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Perennial, rhizomatous — by analogy to peppermint's
  own framing, not independently re-sourced for spearmint's own specific
  stand-life or rotation guidance this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No spearmint-specific disease (Verticillium,
  rust, leaf spot) finding was independently sourced this pass — not
  assumed to share peppermint's own disease profile without a dedicated
  source.
- **Genome:** No reference-genome assembly or genome-size figure was
  found for spearmint this pass — a weaker genomic-sourcing outcome than
  peppermint's own 2024 chromosome-level assembly. Chromosome number
  itself is disputed within the species (24 per one database; 2n=36 and
  48 reported within a single older study; diploid/triploid/tetraploid
  cytotypes found within a single Iranian study) (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** Two named cultivars sourced with the highest herb/oil
  yields in a single trial; a third named cultivar sourced with the
  highest carvone content (64.35%) in the same trial (Finding 2) — the
  first sourced cultivar-level chemistry delta (not just a resistance or
  yield delta) in this project's mint-genus research.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, with a genuinely unique time-of-day sub-
  dimension (Finding 2's own 9:00 AM vs. 9:00 PM harvest-timing
  tradeoff) not characterized as a formal multi-stage system this pass.
  Single window per planting season, cut-and-come-again through the
  season (by analogy to peppermint's own framing, not independently re-
  verified for spearmint specifically).
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced this pass — the same genuinely-
  different-from-dried-leaf value-chain framing as peppermint (oil-
  focused, not dried-leaf-storage-focused), not independently re-
  verified for spearmint's own specific storage needs.

## Hazards

Pet toxicity resolved via a genus-level listing from a major pet-safety
organization (toxic to dogs/cats/horses, essential oils as toxic
principle) (Finding 4). No spearmint-specific disease (Verticillium,
rust, leaf spot) finding was independently sourced this pass — not
assumed to share peppermint's own disease profile without a dedicated
source.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** rhizome/cutting establishment → cut-and-come-again foliage
  harvest → used fresh for culinary purposes, or distilled into
  carvone-dominant essential oil. No menthol-crystallization step
  applies — no sourced finding this pass indicates spearmint's own
  carvone-dominant oil undergoes an equivalent crystallization step to
  peppermint's own menthol crystallization.

## Estimates and derivations

Herb yield of 12,410 and 11,620 kg/ha is derived from the Pantnagar
trial's own reported 124.10 and 116.20 q/ha figures (Finding 2): a
straightforward unit conversion using the standard 1 quintal (q) = 100
kg. Arithmetic: 124.10 q/ha × 100 kg/q = 12,410 kg/ha; 116.20 q/ha × 100
kg/q = 11,620 kg/ha — not an independent productivity estimate.
