# Black Cardamom (*Amomum subulatum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/black-cardamom.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Amomum subulatum* Roxb. specifically — Indian/
Nepalese/Himalayan "large cardamom" or "black cardamom" (badi elaichi,
kali elaichi). This explicitly does NOT cover the related but genuinely
distinct *Amomum tsao-ko* (Chinese black cardamom, cao guo), which
several sources this pass conflated with *A. subulatum* under the shared
English name "black cardamom" — every instance where a source's species
identity was unclear is flagged below, and *A. tsao-ko* findings are
treated as out of scope rather than silently merged in (Finding 1,
Finding 6, and several open questions below). *A. tsao-ko* is a possible
future research candidate, not covered here.

This is deliberately not treated as a close sibling of true/green
cardamom (*Elettaria cardamomum*, a separate crop this project has
researched), despite both being commonly called "cardamom" and both
being in the ginger family (Zingiberaceae). The two are different genera
with a different harvested-organ processing path (smoke-cured vs. sun-
cured/bleached), different chromosome counts, and different flavor,
culinary identity, and production geography (Eastern Himalayas vs. South
India/Guatemala). Where a finding below deliberately parallels a green-
cardamom-style finding, this is said explicitly rather than assumed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — black cardamom's chromosome number is comparatively well-
agreed, but a genome-size-ranking finding this pass could not be
corroborated with a direct genome assembly for this specific species,
and one genuinely useful sequenced relative turned out to be the wrong
species.** The most commonly reported chromosome count is 2n=48,
confirmed by a dedicated genus-wide phylogenomics study across most
*Amomum* accessions examined, including *A. subulatum* [1]. A separate
database entry lists a conflicting 1998-99 count of 2n=54, recorded here
as unreconciled rather than discarded. The same genus-wide phylogenomics
study found *A. subulatum* to have the smallest 2C genome size measured
across the entire *Amomum sensu stricto* group examined (2C = 3.54 pg)
— a comparative/ranking finding, not an absolute-value dispute. No
chromosome-level genome assembly was found for *A. subulatum* itself
this pass; the closest match in the search results was instead a
chromosome-level assembly of *A. tsao-ko* (24 chromosomes, 2.08 Gb total
length) — but the source explicitly notes *A. tsao-ko* has a different
chromosome number from *A. subulatum*, so this assembly cannot be used
as even a rough proxy for this crop's own genome size.

**Finding 2 — black cardamom's climate, elevation, and soil requirements
are reasonably well-sourced from regional extension material, and a
2023 peer-reviewed study provides a genuinely useful, sourced cultivar-
by-elevation performance dataset.** Cultivation occurs at 1,000-2,000m
under forest shade, with 3,000-3,500mm annual rainfall [2]. Soil is
reported as organic-matter- and nitrogen-rich, pH roughly 4.5-6.0,
loamy/deep/well-drained preferred, with waterlogging explicitly harmful
[2]. A nitrogen-fixing tree species is named as the most common shade
tree, and productivity is reported higher where supplemental irrigation
is available [2]. A 2023 peer-reviewed study surveying 41 sites
(975-2,069m elevation) in a major Himalayan production region found that
cultivar performance genuinely depends on elevation band: two low-
elevation cultivars perform best below 975m; a third cultivar instead
performs best above 1,515m; and three named cultivars together yielded
better at mid-elevations (975-1,515m) than at high elevations [2] — four
named cultivars with a sourced, elevation-dependent yield-performance
delta, a genuinely strong cultivar-data finding.

**Finding 3 — black cardamom's yield figures, while still disagreeing
across sources, show notably better cross-source agreement than some
other cardamom-family yield disagreements this project has sourced.**
One region's 2017-18 figures (17,735 ha under cultivation, >4,385 tonnes
produced) imply roughly 250 kg/ha by calculation from the two official
totals, not a figure stated directly by the source [3]. A separate
neighboring district's 2015-16 official figures are more directly
stated: 3,305 ha planted, 2,829 ha yielding, 848.84 tonnes produced, for
an official average productivity of 300.5 kg/ha [3] — a figure within
roughly 20% of the first region's derived estimate. Farm-level anecdotes
vary far more widely (one farmer recalling an equivalent of ~278 kg/ha
from the late 1990s on a specific plot; a different farm reporting 700
kg/year with no area given), but the two official district-level figures
are treated as the more reliable comparison points. No country-specific
kg/ha figure was found for one major producing country despite that
country reportedly supplying roughly 68% of global market share — a
genuine sourcing gap given its apparent production dominance.

**Finding 4 — black cardamom's post-harvest processing is built around
smoke-curing, a distinct processing method from plain drying.** The
traditional "bhatti" method cures fresh pink/red capsules by smoking
them on bamboo mats over an open wood fire, which both dries the capsule
(reducing moisture from ~85% to ~10% for storage) and turns the capsule
from pink to black while imparting a defining smoky flavor [4]. This is
mechanistically different from a simple "fix the color, then dry"
sequence — here, the same single step both dries the product and
defines its characteristic flavor/color. An alternative "flue-pipe"
curing system instead keeps the capsule's pink color, avoiding the smoke
flavor entirely — a genuine alternative processing path, but with no
sourced yield, price, or market-share split between the two methods.
Open-fire burning is specifically noted to lose roughly two-thirds of
its energy as smoke, and a research-institute-developed gasifier design
uses less fuel while producing a higher-quality dried product — a real
efficiency-improvement finding with no adoption-rate figure sourced [4].
A separate technology profile describes a licensed-but-not-yet-
commercialized treatment (controlled aeration, steam, or mild washing)
to remove an "undesirable smoky odour" that the bhatti process is
explicitly noted to sometimes leave on capsules [4].

**Finding 5 — black cardamom's propagation carries a disease-
epidemiology risk, with a narrower mitigation path than some other
cardamom-family crops this project has researched.** Suckers — a mature
tiller plus 2-3 immature tillers/vegetative buds — are the standard
commercial planting unit, explicitly described as breeding true to type
and giving high productivity when collected from high-yielding, disease-
free plants [5]. The same source explicitly warns that collecting
suckers from diseased plants transmits infection (chirke/foorkey, see
Finding 6) directly to the new planting [5]. Unlike some other
cardamom-family crops (where seed propagation is a real, if slower,
alternative specifically used in disease-endemic areas), this crop's
seed-propagation route is itself unreliable: one source states plainly
that no known reliable method exists for seed-germination propagation,
while a separate agricultural extension source instead describes
propagation as occurring "through seeds and suckers" without reconciling
the contradiction [5] — this conflict is recorded rather than resolved.
Modern tissue-culture micropropagation from rhizome explants is an
established alternative route, with at least one dedicated published
protocol found [5]. Planting is timed for June-July, when soil moisture
is adequate [5]. Time to first harvest is reported as up to 4 years by
one university extension source, with fruiting following flowering by
about 5 months, putting the harvest in late autumn [5] — no source this
pass gave a reconciled, narrower range.

**Finding 6 — black cardamom's essential oil composition is reasonably
well-characterized at the compound level, but the specific figures
sourced this pass mix *A. subulatum* (this crop) and the related but
genuinely distinct *A. tsao-ko* (Chinese black cardamom) without full
disentanglement.** One source gives Indian/Nepalese *A. subulatum*'s
steam-distilled seed oil yield at 2.8%, with a characteristic cineole
smell [6]. A separate, more detailed source instead describes *A.
tsao-ko* seed oil at 3% yield, dominated by 1,8-cineole (typically
~70%), with smaller and more variable amounts of alpha-terpinyl acetate,
limonene, terpinene, terpineol, and sabinene (citing a 1970 paper), and
separately notes the pericarp has much less oil with a reduced cineole
fraction compared to the seed [6]. Terpinene is confirmed as a minor
constituent in both species' oil by at least one source each, with
1,8-cineole the major compound in both [6]. This document models black
cardamom's oil yield as a 2.5-3.5% range spanning both species' sourced
figures specifically because they were not independently disentangled
this pass, rather than picking one species' figure and silently
discarding the other.

**Finding 7 — black cardamom's disease complex includes two specifically
named, aphid-vectored viral diseases with a genuinely confirmed vector-
species finding from dedicated transmission studies, alongside one
unreconciled lower-authority claim this document explicitly does not
treat as settled.** 'Foorkey' is caused by cardamom bushy dwarf virus
(CBDV), described by one source as a nanovirus and separately as genus
*Babuvirus* — these terms are not standard synonyms in viral taxonomy,
and this inconsistency is recorded rather than silently resolved [7].
'Chirke' is caused by large cardamom chirke virus (LCCV), a
macluravirus [7]. Both are aphid-transmitted. A 2015 study found a
specific aphid species colonizes in significantly higher numbers on
CBDV-infected plants compared to healthy plants across several regional
plantations, with a companion study describing the specific behavioral
mechanism: the aphid migrates to infected plants, colonizes and acquires
the virus there, and then migrates away to healthy plants once the
diseased plants decline [7]. A 2016 transmission study specifically
confirmed that only this one aphid species, and not two other aphid
species examined, could transmit CBDV to large cardamom — a direct,
controlled-experiment vector confirmation, not merely a correlational
colonization observation [7]. A 2015 genetic survey of 148 infected
plants additionally found that aphid-species community composition
changes across the elevation gradient, meaning the disease-risk aphid
community itself may vary by the same elevation bands Finding 2's
cultivar-performance data already distinguishes [7]. One lower-authority
regional extension article instead names a different vector for chirke
(the corn aphid) and calls the chirke agent a "Mosaic Streak Virus" — a
classification that does not match LCCV's macluravirus identity in the
peer-reviewed literature; the peer-reviewed vector-confirmation studies
are treated as more reliable here, but the conflicting claim is recorded
rather than omitted [7]. CBDV is specifically confirmed to affect large
cardamom across the Himalayan foothills of Northeast India, Nepal, and
Bhutan — the same broad production geography already established in
Finding 2/3 [7].

**Finding 8 — black cardamom's culinary and cultural identity is
genuinely distinct from green cardamom's, anchored by its role in garam
masala and a comparatively minor, species-ambiguous role in Chinese
five-spice blends.** Black cardamom (specifically *A. subulatum*) is
described as a key garam masala ingredient across Indian, Pakistani,
Nepalese, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan, and Afghan cuisines [8]. Its role in
Chinese five-spice is smaller and more ambiguous: one specialist spice-
reference source states that outside India and Nepal, the spice has
"some regional importance" in central and southern China specifically,
where ground seeds are an optional ingredient to five-spice powder —
but this appears to primarily describe the related *A. tsao-ko* rather
than *A. subulatum* itself, another instance of the two species'
identities blurring in the available sources [8]. One source describes
the crop as cultivated "for centuries" on cool, moist Eastern Himalayan
forested slopes, and another separately notes a traditional-Chinese-
medicine use for digestive and respiratory complaints — neither claim
carries a specific date or primary-source citation, a markedly thinner
historical sourcing than this project has found for some other spice
trade-history questions. No source opened this pass described a formal
religious/ritual production rule for black cardamom.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical/subtropical perennial, shade-grown
  under forest canopy; multi-year stand (Finding 2).
- **Climate:** Subtropical/temperate-Himalayan, shade-obligate, monsoon-
  rainfed — 1,000-2,000m elevation, 3,000-3,500mm annual rainfall
  (Finding 2).
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — the smoke-cured (or flue-cured)
  dried capsule is the marketed product; no source described fresh
  capsules as a culinary form (Finding 4).
- **Pollination:** Not independently sourced this pass — no pollination-
  biology study specific to *Amomum subulatum* was opened.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield roughly 250-300 kg/ha dried
  (derived/stated from two official district-level production totals,
  which agree within roughly 20% of each other) (Finding 3). No
  exceptional or poor yield tier was confidently sourced this pass
  (farm-level anecdotes reach higher, but without a stated area base).
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-style crop-coefficient curve specific to this
  species was found. Drought tolerance is likely low, inferred from the
  monsoon-rainfed, high-rainfall (3,000-3,500mm) cultivation finding,
  though not independently sourced as a drought-tolerance figure.
  Waterlogging tolerance is low — explicitly flagged as harmful (Finding
  2), the same acute drainage-sensitivity pattern this project has found
  for several other rhizomatous spice crops.
- **Establishment:** Sucker-piece planting (mature tiller + 2-3 immature
  tillers/vegetative buds) timed for June-July soil moisture; suckers
  from diseased parent plants transmit chirke/foorkey to the new
  planting (Finding 5, Finding 7). Seed-propagation reliability is
  directly contradicted across two sources (one says no reliable seed-
  germination method is known; a separate agricultural extension source
  says propagation occurs "through seeds and suckers") — this conflict
  is not resolved here. Tissue-culture micropropagation from rhizome
  explants is an established modern alternative (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** pH optimum roughly 4.5-6.0 [2]. Soils described as organic-
  matter- and nitrogen-rich where this crop is grown — no specific
  micronutrient-deficiency association was found this pass, a genuine
  point of difference from some other cardamom-family soil-chemistry
  findings this project has sourced.
- **Stand life:** Time to first harvest up to 4 years by the one source
  found this pass, with no competing figure to triangulate against.
  Productive life was not sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** 'Foorkey', cardamom bushy dwarf virus (CBDV),
  aphid-vectored specifically by one confirmed aphid species, confirmed
  by a dedicated transmission study (Finding 7). 'Chirke', large
  cardamom chirke virus (LCCV), aphid-vectored (vector species disputed
  between the peer-reviewed literature and one lower-authority extension
  source naming a different aphid) (Finding 7).
- **Genome:** *A. subulatum* has the smallest 2C genome size (3.54 pg)
  measured across the *Amomum sensu stricto* group in one genus-wide
  study — a comparative/ranking finding, not an absolute multi-way
  dispute. No genome assembly exists for this specific species; the only
  assembly found (for the related *A. tsao-ko*) is explicitly a
  different species with a different chromosome number (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** Four named cultivars with a sourced, elevation-dependent
  yield-performance delta from a single 2023 peer-reviewed 41-site
  survey (Finding 2): one performs best above 1,515m; two others perform
  best below 975m; and three named cultivars together yielded better at
  mid-elevations (975-1,515m) than at high elevations — a genuinely
  strong cultivar-data finding relative to most other spice/herb crops
  this project has researched.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — capsules are picked at one maturity point
  (late autumn, ~5 months post-flowering); no distinguishable immature-
  vs-ripe harvest-stage choice was sourced this pass. Single window —
  one annual harvest per bearing season; no multi-flush pattern sourced,
  and no source confirmed or ruled out multiple picking rounds per
  season the way labor studies have implied for some other cardamom-
  family crops.

## Hazards

No human- or animal-health hazard (toxin, allergen, drug interaction)
was found this pass for black cardamom itself. The disease hazards
(foorkey, chirke) are agronomic, aphid-vectored viral diseases, covered
above under Disease/pest profile.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh pink/red capsule → bhatti smoke-curing (dries the
  capsule from ~85% to ~10% moisture while turning it black and
  imparting a smoky flavor) or flue-pipe curing (dries without the smoke
  flavor, retaining pink color) → dried capsule (spice — key garam
  masala ingredient across several South Asian cuisines; a smaller,
  species-ambiguous role in Chinese five-spice blends, Finding 8).
  Essential oil separately distilled from the seed (Finding 6).

## Open research questions

- Several sources this pass conflated *Amomum subulatum* (this crop)
  with the related but genuinely distinct *Amomum tsao-ko* (Chinese
  black cardamom) under the shared English name "black cardamom" — a
  dedicated taxonomic/trade-literature review specifically separating
  the two species' figures would strengthen several findings here.
- No country-specific kg/ha yield figure was found for one major
  producing country, despite that country reportedly supplying roughly
  68% of global market share.
- Seed-propagation reliability is directly contradicted across two
  sources — not resolved here.
- CBDV (foorkey's causal agent) is described inconsistently as both a
  "nanovirus" and genus "*Babuvirus*" across sources — not independently
  verified which classification is current/correct.
- Chirke's vector is disputed between peer-reviewed transmission studies
  (confirming one aphid species) and one lower-authority extension
  article naming a different aphid and a different virus
  classification.
- Black cardamom's "cultivated for centuries" and traditional-medicine
  claims carry no specific date or primary-source citation.
- The essential-oil composition and yield figures sourced this pass mix
  *A. subulatum* and *A. tsao-ko* without full disentanglement.
- No source confirmed or ruled out multiple picking rounds per bearing
  season.
- No pollination-biology study specific to this species was found or
  opened this pass.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any black cardamom
  operation.
- Productive stand life was not sourced this pass — only the up-to-4-
  year first-harvest figure was found.
- Black cardamom's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass — likely shares the same shade-
  obligate tractability concern as other shade-grown spice crops this
  project has researched, but this is an inference, not independently
  researched.

## Estimates and derivations

A dry-recovery yield-chain fraction of roughly 0.14-0.20 is derived
using a simple mass-balance method: fresh capsules are ~85% moisture,
dried to ~10% for storage; treating 1 kg fresh-equivalent as 15% dry
matter + 85% water, the dried product at 10% moisture retains dry mass
fraction = 0.15 / (1 - 0.10) ≈ 0.167, widened slightly to 0.14-0.20 for
natural variation — a reasoning step using simple mass-balance
arithmetic, not a directly sourced dry-recovery figure. A time-to-first-
harvest range of roughly 3-4 years is an estimate: the only sourced
first-harvest figure this pass was "up to 4 years" from one university
extension source, with no competing figure to triangulate a narrower
range from; the lower bound (3 years) was chosen by analogy to some
other multi-year Zingiberaceae perennial stands with a broadly similar
flowering-to-harvest structure — the weaker half of this estimate.
