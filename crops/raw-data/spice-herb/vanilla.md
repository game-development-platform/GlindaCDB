# Vanilla (*Vanilla planifolia*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/vanilla.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *Vanilla planifolia* only, the overwhelmingly dominant
commercial species (Bourbon/Madagascar vanilla and most of the global
crop) [1]. Family Orchidaceae, subfamily Vanilloideae — a climbing,
hemiepiphytic vine, not a tree or shrub like several other crops this
project has researched [1]. Two other species with real commercial
presence and a genuinely different aroma chemistry — Tahitian vanilla
(heliotropine-like floral notes) and a third related species — are not
covered by this document; see Open research questions for a proposed
future entry, since both show markedly different dominant aroma
compounds from *V. planifolia*'s vanillin-centered chemistry [8].

This project's own black pepper research is the closest structural
sibling (a hand-pollination-dependent tropical vine with an elaborate
post-harvest curing chain), and this project's own cinnamon research
shares the same "sparse dedicated literature, apply pest/disease facts
by analogy where needed" caveat pattern.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — taxonomy and native range are well-sourced and
uncontested, but the crop's own cultivation-climate figures disagree
across sources, the same pattern seen in this project's clove
research.** *V. planifolia* is native to the neotropical lowlands from
southern Mexico through Central America, Colombia, and the northern
Brazilian Amazon; the IUCN lists it as Endangered in the wild due to
habitat loss [1]. It requires a humid, warm tropical climate, growing
best at 20-30°C in high humidity [1]. Rainfall figures disagree: one
source gives a 2,000mm/year minimum, while an Indian agricultural
source gives 2,000-3,000 mm/year (200-300 cm) [1][5]. Altitude
tolerance also disagrees: one source gives a natural range of 150-900m,
another an upper limit of 1,500m [1]. Flowering specifically requires a
roughly 3-month dry spell as a trigger [1] — the same kind of "stress
triggers a reproductive response the plant otherwise can't tolerate"
pattern already noted for clove's dry-season flowering trigger. Soil
guidance is comparatively consistent: well-drained, humus-rich sandy
loam, pH 6-7, with waterlogging and root rot flagged as a real risk
[1][5].

**Finding 2 — commercial vanilla depends entirely on hand pollination,
a historically significant and labor-defining fact, with the underlying
flower anatomy well-characterized.** In the wild (in its native range,
where its specialist pollinator — likely a *Melipona* bee or related
insect — occurs), only about 1% of vanilla flowers are naturally
pollinated; everywhere vanilla is commercially grown outside that
native range, this pollinator is absent, making hand pollination a
practical necessity rather than a yield-boosting option [1][2]. The
flower's own anatomy explains why: the rostellum, a flap-like structure
derived from the stigma, physically separates the anther from the
stigma — the same anther/stigma-separation mechanism common across
orchids generally, preventing accidental self-pollination [6]. Hand
pollinators lift the rostellum with a thin stick, splinter, or blade,
bringing the anther into contact with the stigma [2][6]. The technique
is specifically credited to Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy on Réunion,
in 1841; accounts describe him using a thin stick or blade to lift the
rostellum, and one account connects his insight to having been taught
hand-pollination technique on watermelon by his enslaver, recognizing
the analogous flower anatomy [2]. A slower, less practical hand-
pollination method had been developed earlier by a European botanist
[2]. Each flower opens for roughly 24 hours, with the ideal pollination
window given as 06:00-13:00 and the stigma remaining receptive for up
to 24 hours [2]. A 2024 study found that mechanical alternatives to the
traditional method do produce fruit, but at a lower set rate and with
inferior-quality fruit compared to hand pollination [2] — a genuine,
sourced reason the traditional manual method persists commercially
rather than being automated away.

**Finding 3 — curing is a multi-stage, multi-week process converting
glucovanillin to free vanillin, with real measured trade-offs between
methods, and sources disagree on exact stage durations.** Curing
proceeds through killing (enzyme activation), sweating, drying, and
conditioning [3][4]. Killing is a hot-water dip of 10 seconds to 3
minutes, performed within 3 days of harvest, which activates the
enzymatic hydrolysis of glucovanillin to free vanillin — the chemical
reaction underlying the whole curing process [3]. A Réunion study found
vanillin content rising in stages through each phase of sweating, then
holding steady once drying began, with total vanillin actually
decreasing during the drying stage itself [3] — a real, sourced loss
during what is otherwise framed as a "drying" step, not pure water
removal. A 2007 Australian study quantified a genuine method trade-off:
continuously-cured, non-blanched beans at 35°C achieved about 90%
glucovanillin hydrolysis, while beans blanched at 67°C for 3 minutes
before curing achieved only about 70% conversion [3] — blanching trades
some conversion efficiency for (presumably) a different handling or
safety benefit not further explored this pass. Sweating duration is
reported inconsistently: "up to two weeks" by one commercial source,
versus the Australian study's much shorter 4 days (45°C) or 5 days
(35°C) for blanched beans specifically [3] — these may not be directly
comparable given the blanching difference, but are kept visible as
reported rather than reconciled. Drying itself runs 3-4 weeks by one
source, or a more specific 20-25 days by an Indian government guide,
continuing until beans reach roughly one-third of their initial weight
[3][4]. Conditioning follows, in closed wooden boxes, during which
vanillin crystallizes visibly on the bean surface [3][4].

**Finding 4 — yield figures vary enormously by region and management,
spanning roughly two orders of magnitude, and per-vine and per-hectare
figures do not cleanly cross-check against each other.** Indian
extension sources give 1,800-3,600 kg/ha of green beans (300-600 kg/ha
cured), with a conversion ratio of about 6 kg green per 1 kg cured [7].
A standard reference handbook, covering traditional Mexican plantings,
gives a much lower 50-500 kg/ha green, averaging 200 kg/ha [7] —
roughly an order of magnitude below the Indian figures. A Malaysian-
oriented source gives a per-vine figure instead: a 5-year-old vine
producing 1.5-3 kg of pods, rising to as much as 6 kg after further
years, alongside a separate claim of 16 t/ha/year green yield for
"intensive plots" [7] — this per-hectare figure is dramatically higher
than either the Indian or Mexican figures and was not cross-checked
against a stated planting density, so is recorded with caution rather
than treated as representative. A UN food-agency's own post-harvest
guidance sets a planning target of 2 t/ha green beans [7] — closer to
the Indian figures than to the Mexican or the 16 t/ha outlier. A drip-
irrigation vendor's planning default of 2,000 kg/ha at 0.5 kg/plant
(20,000 plants/ha) is noted but flagged as a design assumption, not
field data [7].

**Finding 5 — propagation, flowering age, and stand life depend heavily
on cutting length and growing conditions, and the economic lifespan is
clearly bounded by Fusarium disease pressure.** Vanilla is propagated
almost exclusively from stem cuttings taken from a mother vine that has
not been allowed to flower — true seed propagation is not used
commercially [9]. Cutting length creates a genuine, sourced trade-off:
longer cuttings (around 90-100cm, "meter-long") flower sooner (1-2
years, one source says up to 2 years) but grow more slowly; shorter
cuttings (around 30cm) take longer to first flower (3-5 years) but grow
faster once established [9][10]. A separate, more general source
instead frames first flowering as occurring in year 2-3 regardless of
cutting length [9][10] — this is a real inconsistency across sources on
a basic timing fact, kept visible rather than picked. Economic
productive lifespan is comparatively well-agreed: roughly 10-15 years
across the most authoritative sources (~10 years; 12-14 years; 10-15
years across three separate sources) [10]. *Fusarium* wilt is named as
the dominant disease threat and a major driver of that lifespan limit —
Hawaiian growers call it "the scourge of vanilla"; one documented case
describes a farm growing vanilla successfully for five years before
Fusarium began attacking the planting, with replanting at a different
site failing to stop it once established [9]. Chemical management (soil
drenching with carbendazim at 0.1%) is the only sourced control measure
found this pass; no resistant planting material or cultural-control
strategy specific to vanilla Fusarium was found [9]. Vanilla vines are
grown against a living "tutor tree" companion, planted at least six
months ahead of the vanilla cuttings in organic systems, providing both
physical support and shade [9] — a living-support-tree pattern already
seen for black pepper's own companion-tree system.

**Finding 6 — vanilla's genome is genuinely difficult to characterize,
with a real, sourced gap between flow-cytometry and sequence-assembly
estimates, and recent (not ancient) autopolyploidy documented in
cultivated material.** The diploid *V. planifolia* genome is estimated
at about 4.09 Gb across 16 chromosome pairs (2n≈32) by flow cytometry
(2C ≈ 4.18 ± 0.08 pg for a Réunion cultivar) [11]. An earlier sequence
assembly came out at only about 1.5 Gb — well short of the flow-
cytometry figure — and this gap has been traced to partial
endoreplication (a high-copy-number tissue effect that makes the genome
unusually hard to assemble), compounded by aneuploidy [11]. A 2008
study of Réunion accessions found three distinct 2C-value groups
(averaging about 5.03, 7.67, and 10.00 pg), interpreted as diploid,
autotriploid, and autotetraploid types within cultivated material —
explicitly read by the study's authors as evidence of recent
autopolyploidy, not an ancient whole-genome-duplication event [11].
Root-cell chromosome counts varied widely (16 to 54) depending on the
accession group studied, and in Tahitian vanilla (itself a natural
hybrid) pollen was found to be euploid while root tissue was aneuploid
— a genuinely unusual within-plant ploidy mismatch [11]. A 2024 study
reported successful haploid *V. planifolia* lines, confirmed by both
chromosome counts and flow cytometry [11]. No source opened this pass
addressed a deeper, ancient polyploidy event in vanilla's evolutionary
history.

**Finding 7 — Totonac cultural and historical use is reasonably well-
documented for the colonial-era export trade, but direct evidence for
pre-Columbian wild gathering is explicitly described as thin by the
sources themselves.** The Totonac people of the Papantla region
(Veracruz) were the first and, for nearly two centuries, the only
vanilla exporters in the world, beginning in the mid-to-late 18th
century, and still rely on the crop economically and culturally today
[12]. The Totonac treated vanilla as a sacred herb from roughly 1000 CE
(dates for this vary slightly across sources), using it in ritual
offerings, perfume, and medicine [12]. A well-known legend attributes
the vine's origin to the transformed blood of two forbidden lovers,
declared a sacred gift to the gods [12]. Vanilla moved into the wider
Mesoamerican economy as Aztec tribute, sent from Totonac territory to
Tenochtitlán [12]. However, sources are explicit that direct
archaeological evidence for actual pre-domestication wild-gathering is
limited — one source hedges that "the ancient Maya and even the Olmecs
before them may have been the first peoples to engage in wild gathering
or even small-scale cultivation," inferred mainly from later
Mesoamerican cultural-practice analogy rather than a primary
archaeological find [12].

**Finding 8 — vanilla's own flavor-chemistry literature cleanly
distinguishes the three major commercial species by their dominant
aroma compound, giving a genuinely different chemical signature from
this document's own species to its two close relatives.** A 2024 odor-
analysis study found vanillin to be the key odor-active compound
especially in *V. planifolia* samples, while a related species was
found to be mainly rich in 4-methoxybenzyl alcohol instead [8]. The
same study found 4-methoxybenzyl alcohol and 4-methoxybenzaldehyde
showing higher odor-detection-threshold factors than vanillin in that
species, and partially in Tahitian vanilla [8]. A 2021 study of 30
accessions found anisyl alcohol entirely absent from all 13 *V.
planifolia* varieties tested, but present in most close relatives
tested [8] — a clean, sourced species-level chemical marker. A 2006
review separately describes Tahitian vanilla's aroma as flowery,
perfumey, and heliotropine-like, explicitly noting that Bourbon (i.e.
*V. planifolia*) vanilla lacks these particular notes [8]. Chemistry
also varies meaningfully within *V. planifolia* itself: the 2021
accession study found a clear gradient in total aromatic content across
different genotypes of the same species, and the 2006 review separately
notes that growing conditions, soil nutrition, harvest maturity, and
the specific curing method used all further shape the final aroma
profile [8] — meaning even a single-species model has real, sourced
reasons to vary flavor outcome by growing/curing choices, not just by
cultivar.

**Finding 9 — vanilla carries a genuine, if uncommon, human-health
contact-sensitization hazard that is specifically tied to occupational
exposure during growing and processing, not just consumer use of the
finished flavoring.** Allergen-database reviews describe documented
contact-sensitivity cases from vanilla exposure in a lip salve, in a
child with recurrent lip dermatitis, and from a hair lotion containing
vanilla tincture — true food/skin allergy to vanilla is described as
uncommon, with reactions most likely delayed-type (Type IV) rather than
immediate [13]. Critically, occupational contact dermatitis is
separately documented specifically from growing or processing vanilla
(not just from consumer exposure to the flavoring), as well as from
vanillin manufacturing and among bakers and beverage-makers; one case
was traced specifically to ethylvanillin [13]. A commercial vanillin
safety data sheet names this syndrome "vanillism" (allergic dermatitis
from prolonged/repeated contact), while separately noting on closer
reading that some historical reports attributed reactions to a mite
present in raw vanilla rather than to vanillin itself — a genuine
causal ambiguity in that specific source, kept visible rather than
resolved [13]. A commercial Bourbon vanilla safety data sheet formally
classifies the product as EU Skin Sensitization Category 1 (H317, "may
cause an allergic skin reaction"), with ethyl vanillin separately
classified Skin Sens. 1A [13]. A double-blind oral-challenge study in
11 children with severe atopic dermatitis produced eczematous reactions
in 9 of them and urticaria in 1 — a notably high reaction rate in that
specific, small, already-atopic study population, not representative
of the general population [13]. Most of the underlying case evidence
is dated (1940s-1990s), and no recent large-scale prevalence data was
found.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical, perennial — a climbing,
  hemiepiphytic orchid vine (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per pod, repeated across the
  harvest season — pods are picked individually at a specific partial-
  yellowing maturity stage, roughly 8-9 months after each flower's hand-
  pollination; the vine itself bears for 10-15 years (Finding 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical — 20-30°C, high humidity; rainfall figures
  disagree (2,000mm vs. 2,000-3,000mm minimum across sources) (Finding
  1); a roughly 3-month dry spell is specifically required to trigger
  flowering.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — green beans have essentially no
  vanilla flavor or aroma until cured; the flavor is entirely a product
  of the curing process (Finding 3).
- **Pollination:** Required — hand-pollination (commercially universal
  outside the native range; natural fruit set without it is only ~1%)
  (Finding 2) — one of the most clearly necessity-driven, rather than
  yield-optimizing, hand-pollination requirements this project has
  researched.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 1,800-3,600 kg/ha green / 300-600
  kg/ha cured (Indian extension figures) (Finding 4). The Indian 1,800-
  3,600 kg/ha green-bean range, close to a UN food agency's own 2 t/ha
  planning target, is used as the primary representative figure; the
  much lower Mexican traditional-planting figures (50-500 kg/ha green,
  averaging 200 kg/ha) and the much higher, uncross-checked 16 t/ha
  "intensive plot" figure are both recorded separately as bounding
  outliers under very different management regimes.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance low-to-moderate, with a genuine
  nuance: the crop needs high ambient humidity generally, but also
  requires a roughly 3-month dry spell specifically to trigger flowering
  (sourced for the flowering-trigger fact; the general drought-tolerance
  rating itself is an analogy from the humidity requirement, not
  independently sourced) (Finding 1). Waterlogging tolerance low — root
  rot from overwatering/poor drainage is explicitly flagged.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative cuttings — stem cuttings from a non-
  flowered mother vine are the overwhelmingly dominant commercial
  method; true seed is not used commercially (Finding 5). Propagated
  from stem cuttings planted against a living "tutor tree" companion
  (established at least six months ahead of the vanilla cuttings in
  organic systems) that provides both physical support and shade — the
  same living-support-tree pattern already seen for black pepper's own
  companion-tree system.
- **Labor:** Hand-pollination labor scales directly with planted area —
  one source states a single worker can hand-pollinate 1.5 ha [2]; for
  Madagascar's SAVA region, one source gives roughly 133 labor-days/ha/
  year at a cost of about $370/ha [2] — this SAVA figure likely covers
  the full annual labor cycle (planting, training, pollination,
  harvest), not pollination alone, and was not broken down by task in
  the source opened. Pinching the top 7.5-10cm of vine 6-8 months before
  the flowering season, and pruning older fruiting branches, are both
  named operations that encourage flower-bud initiation and production
  [9].
- **Soil:** pH 6-7 optimal (Finding 1). Root architecture (depth,
  aerial-root proportion) not independently sourced this pass — the
  vine is hemiepiphytic with aerial roots along its climbing stem in
  addition to soil roots.
- **Stand life:** Economic productive life roughly 10-15 years across
  the most authoritative sources opened, with *Fusarium* disease
  pressure as the main sourced driver of vines being abandoned before
  natural senescence (Finding 5).
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Fusarium* wilt, the dominant named disease
  threat and economic-lifespan limiter — root infection leading to vine
  decline and death, with chemical soil-drenching (carbendazim 0.1%)
  the only sourced control measure and no resistant planting material
  or cultural control found (Finding 5). Root rot from waterlogging/
  poor drainage. No insect pest specific to vanilla was found this pass
  — a genuine gap relative to most other crops this project has
  researched, which at minimum have a named insect pest or pathogen
  beyond the primary disease.
- **Genome:** Genuinely unresolved between flow-cytometry (~4.09 Gb,
  2n≈32) and sequence-assembly (~1.5 Gb) estimates, the gap traced to
  partial endoreplication and aneuploidy; recent (not ancient)
  autopolyploidy is documented within cultivated Réunion accessions
  (diploid/autotriploid/autotetraploid types coexisting) (Finding 6) —
  one of the most genuinely complicated genome-size pictures this
  project has researched so far, not a simple single-figure gap.
- **Cultivars:** No named *V. planifolia* cultivars (as distinct from
  the species-level Bourbon/Madagascar-vs-Mexican-vs-Indian production-
  region framing) were found this pass. This is a genuine gap relative
  to black pepper's well-documented cultivar lineage — commercial
  vanilla appears to be grown largely as unselected or regionally-
  adapted clonal stock rather than as named, registered cultivars.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for the pod/bean itself — pods are picked at
  one specific partial-yellowing maturity stage, roughly 8-9 months
  after hand-pollination. Unlike black pepper's green/ripe split or the
  two cinnamons' bark-maturity question, there is no genuinely different
  downstream product depending on pod maturity at harvest; the entire
  flavor difference comes later, from curing (Finding 3), not from
  harvest-stage choice.
- **Storage:** Moisture content 15-38% across sources; described as the
  primary quality/grading factor, with "very dry" (15-20%) and "moist"
  (35-38%) as the two ends of the commercially recognized range — no
  standardized export grading system was found for vanilla specifically,
  unlike clove's or cinnamon's own (looser) grading tiers — a genuine,
  sourced absence, not a research gap. See Estimates and derivations
  for the storage-duration estimate.

## Hazards

*Fusarium* wilt, the dominant named disease threat and economic-
lifespan limiter — root infection leading to vine decline and death,
with chemical soil-drenching (carbendazim 0.1%) the only sourced
control measure and no resistant planting material or cultural control
found (Finding 5). Root rot from waterlogging/poor drainage.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — occupational contact dermatitis ("vanillism") is
documented specifically among vanilla growers, curing workers, vanillin
manufacturers, bakers, and beverage-makers, distinct from the uncommon
general food/skin allergy risk to consumers of the finished product
(Finding 9). This human-health hazard is deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** stem cuttings established against a living tutor tree →
  hand-pollinated flowers (each open ~24 hours, best window 06:00-13:00)
  → pods harvested individually ~8-9 months later at partial-yellowing
  maturity → cured through killing (hot-water dip), sweating, drying
  (3-4 weeks, to ~1/3 original weight), and conditioning (vanillin
  crystallizes on the bean surface).

## Open research questions

- A proposed future entry for Tahitian vanilla and a third related
  species: both show genuinely different dominant aroma chemistry from
  *V. planifolia* (anisyl-alcohol/4-methoxybenzyl-alcohol-dominant
  rather than vanillin-dominant), which would likely warrant separate
  treatment if researched — flagging rather than researching now, since
  this pass was scoped to *V. planifolia* only.
- The 16 t/ha "intensive plot" yield figure was not cross-checked
  against a stated planting density (Finding 4) — dramatically higher
  than every other sourced yield figure this pass; should be verified
  against a primary agronomy source before being treated as
  representative of anything beyond a single cited claim.
- No resistant planting material or cultural control strategy was found
  for *Fusarium* wilt, only a chemical soil-drench recommendation from
  one Indian extension source (Finding 5) — given *Fusarium*'s
  documented role as the main economic-lifespan limiter for this crop,
  a dedicated plant-pathology source on vanilla Fusarium management
  would be a high-value follow-up.
- No source addressing ancient (deep evolutionary) polyploidy in
  vanilla's genome was found (Finding 6) — all sourced ploidy
  information this pass concerns recent autopolyploidy within
  cultivated material, not the genus's or family's deeper evolutionary
  history.
- The underlying source for pre-Columbian wild-gathering explicitly
  hedges the claim ("may have been") (Finding 7) — worth strengthening
  with a dedicated Mesoamerican archaeobotany source if one becomes
  available.
- No recent (post-1990s) large-scale prevalence data was found for
  vanilla contact dermatitis/"vanillism" (Finding 9) — most underlying
  case evidence is dated 1940s-1990s; a modern occupational-dermatology
  survey of vanilla growing/curing regions would be a valuable follow-
  up given the scale of the modern industry.
- C3 photosynthesis pathway is assumed by analogy and carries above-
  average uncertainty: CAM photosynthesis is common enough across
  Orchidaceae generally that this analogy should be checked against a
  Vanilla-specific or Vanilloideae-specific source in a future pass,
  rather than treated as settled.
- The SAVA-region (Madagascar) labor-day figure (~133 days/ha/year)
  likely covers the full annual labor cycle, not hand-pollination
  specifically — the source opened did not break this figure down by
  task.
- Root architecture (depth, aerial-root proportion) was not
  independently sourced — vanilla's hemiepiphytic growth habit makes
  this a genuinely more complex question than a single root-depth
  figure would capture for a typical soil-rooted crop.
- No insect pest specific to vanilla was found — a genuine gap relative
  to most other crops this project has researched, which at minimum
  name one insect pest beyond the primary disease/pathogen.
- No named *V. planifolia* cultivars were found — commercial vanilla
  appears to be grown largely as unselected or regionally-adapted
  clonal stock rather than named, registered cultivars, a genuine
  contrast with black pepper's well-documented cultivar lineage.
- No shade-tolerance or daily-light-integral figure was sourced —
  vanilla's need for a shading tutor tree implies real shade-tolerance
  characteristics that were not independently quantified this pass.
- Madagascar's SAVA-region vanilla theft, violence, and price-
  volatility dynamics are a real, well-documented (if dated, mostly
  2017-2021 sources) regional security/economic phenomenon tied to this
  crop's high value-to-weight ratio and harvest-window vulnerability —
  recorded descriptively only, out of scope for this project's
  agronomic research.

## Estimates and derivations

A vanilla cutting-stock shelf life of 7 days is estimated: no source
opened this pass gave a specific viability window for a harvested
vanilla cutting prior to planting. 7 days was chosen by loose analogy
to other vegetatively-propagated tropical perennial spice crops this
project has researched (cinnamon's own propagule stock, 10 days; black
pepper's cutting stock, 14 days) as a plausible middle estimate for a
soft-stemmed orchid cutting, which would be expected to desiccate
somewhat faster than a woody cutting. A dedicated nursery-propagation
source giving vanilla cutting viability specifically would replace this
estimate; plausible range 3-14 days.

A cured-bean shelf life of 1,095 days (3 years) is estimated: no
dedicated spice-storage-handbook source specific to cured vanilla beans
was opened this pass. Commercial vanilla-bean and extract retailers
commonly advertise multi-year shelf life for properly stored whole
cured beans (general market knowledge, not independently verified this
pass), and 3 years was chosen as consistent with that general claim and
with this project's other long-shelf-life dried spice products (e.g.
cinnamon bark, 4 years; clove, 2 years). A dedicated postharvest source,
if one exists for vanilla specifically, would replace this estimate;
plausible range 2-5 years depending on moisture content (Finding 3's
grading note) and storage conditions.

A green-to-cured yield chain fraction of 0.19-0.21 is derived
arithmetically from a curing study cited in Finding 3, which found total
weight loss plus remaining moisture together accounted for 79.1-81.1%
of the original green weight — meaning 100% minus that range leaves
18.9-20.9% of the green weight remaining after curing. This is this
document's own calculation from the study's reported figures, not a
ratio the study itself states directly. A dedicated, explicitly-stated
green-to-cured weight-loss ratio from a processing source (rather than a
derived figure) would be a cleaner replacement; plausible range 15-25%
given the Indian extension sources' own implied ~17% figure (1 kg cured
per ~6 kg green, Finding 4) sits close to but not exactly within this
derived range.
