# Caraway (*Carum carvi*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/caraway.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Carum carvi* L. Cumin and fennel, both umbellifer-
family (Apiaceae) seed-spice crops this project has also researched, are
the closest siblings — all three share a broadly similar single-seed-
harvest shape. Caraway's own genuinely distinct feature among this
family's crops is that the commercial type is biennial (rosette and
taproot in year 1, bolt/flower/seed in year 2 after overwintering), not
a true annual like cumin or dill — closer in cycle shape to carrot or
parsnip (both real Apiaceae root crops) than to any other spice/herb
sibling this project has researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — caraway's chromosome number is cleanly sourced as diploid
(2n=20) from a large 2020 genotyping study, but that same study found
the real genome roughly 4-5x smaller than an older cytophotometry
estimate.** A 2020 peer-reviewed study genotyping 137 caraway accessions
confirms *Carum carvi* diploid at 2n=2x=20 [1], consistent with a
chromosome-count database's own listing for a taxonomic synonym. For
genome size, the same 2020 study measured 35 of its accessions by flow
cytometry and found an average 2C value of 4.282 pg (±0.0096 SE) [1] —
explicitly and substantially smaller than an older Feulgen-
cytophotometry estimate of 4C = 19.051 pg from a single prior accession,
which the 2020 authors themselves judge a clear overestimate [1].
Converting the 2020 figure at roughly 978 Mb/pg gives an implied genome
of about 4.2 Gb per diploid cell (approximately 2.1 Gb per 1C) — this
document's own arithmetic, not independently stated by the source. This
is a genuinely larger correction (the older figure is roughly 4-5x too
high once unit conventions are normalized) than this project has found
for most other Apiaceae crops' own genome-size disagreements.

**Finding 2 — caraway is strongly protandrous and predominantly insect-
pollinated, with a sourced, unusually low self-fertilization ceiling.**
Caraway florets are protandrous and strongly dichogamous, each floret's
own male and female phases together lasting 7-15 days, with nectar
produced from the stylopodia for 4-12 days [2]. Isolation experiments
found self-fertilization within the same umbel to be very low, with one
source giving a specific exceptional ceiling of only 6-7% [2] — a
markedly lower self-pollination ceiling than this project has sourced
for some other Apiaceae crops' own exceptions, making caraway a more
clearly outcrossing-dependent crop, though no source states a formal
genetic self-incompatibility mechanism (the evidence found is
behavioral/statistical, not genetic) [2]. An extension-style pollination
handbook states caraway pollen is not windblown and must be insect-
transferred, with bees named the primary pollinator [2], though a
separate source notes wind may play an additional role, a caveat not
resolved here. No specific insect-pollination-dependence percentage or
isolation-distance figure was found for caraway this pass.

**Finding 3 — caraway's biennial commercial cycle produces sharply
conflicting yield figures across countries and even within a single
Finnish trial's own first-vs-second-year results.** One agricultural
database gives biennial caraway types roughly 0.5-1 t/ha versus 1-1.6
t/ha for annual types, with biennial crops needing about 440-460 days
sowing-to-fruit-ripening [3]. One provincial extension guide reports
seed yields starting around 100 kg/acre (roughly 250 kg/ha) in the first
productive year, rising to over 1,120 kg/ha (500 lb/acre) in the
following year or two [3]. A Finnish peer-reviewed trial found the
opposite direction for its own two measured years: 3,200 kg/ha at the
first harvest, falling to only 800 kg/ha (25% of the first-year figure)
in the second year [3] — these two sources' own opposite first-vs-
second-year trends are not reconciled here, since it is unclear whether
"first harvest"/"first year" means the same calendar stage in both
sources; a separate provincial extension page gives a flat average of
approximately 800 kg/ha for the biennial type, with possible stands
persisting into a third year depending on how many plants set seed in
year 2 [3] — this third-year-regrowth possibility is flagged but not
treated as a confirmed multi-year harvest pattern here. Seed rate is
reported around 10-13 kg/ha across sources [3][4], broadly similar to
some other Apiaceae seed-spice crops' own seed-rate ranges.

**Finding 4 — caraway's essential-oil chemistry is dominated by two
compounds (carvone and limonene) with an unusually wide and strongly
negatively correlated retail-sample spread.** A study of 20 retail
caraway products found carvone ranging 44.5-95.9% and limonene
1.5-51.3%, strongly negatively correlated with each other, with only 4
of the 20 samples meeting European Pharmacopoeia standards [5].
Commercial trade data separately shows a narrower, more typical 53-57%
carvone / 42-45% limonene(+beta-phellandrene) split [5], while older
chromatography tables show a broader 43-80% carvone / 10-54% limonene
range depending on growing origin [5]. A Serbian study reports total oil
content averaging 3.95% of the fruit, with carvone and limonene together
making up 97.69-98.62% of that oil [5] — this study's own oil-content
figure (how much oil) is treated as a separate, non-conflicting
measurement from the retail-sample studies' own oil-composition figures
(what's in the oil), not reconciled into one number. A regulatory
pharmacopoeia standard sets a floor of oil content exceeding 30 g/kg
(>3%) and at least 50% carvone in the oil [5] — this regulatory floor
and the Serbian average together are used here as the primary oil-yield
basis (3.0-3.95%), explicitly not the much wider 44.5-95.9% carvone-
share range, which describes composition variability rather than total
extractable-oil quantity.

**Finding 5 — caraway's disease list spans at least six named fungal
genera with one specific, severe infection-rate figure and a declining-
vs-rising pathogen-importance trend documented by a direct before/after
comparison, plus a named gall mite with no known effective control.**
Named fungal pathogens include several Fusarium species (one
specifically implicated in Polish seedling-infection tests), and
several other named fungal genera, with monoculture stands reported at
higher risk generally [6]. In the Polish pathogenicity tests, one
Alternaria species was the single most aggressive species tested,
causing 95% seedling infection, a specific and severe figure [6]. A
Czech source gives a genuinely dated before/after finding: one fungal
pathogen, once considered caraway's most serious pathogen, has declined
in importance, while three others — including a powdery-mildew species
also found on fennel in this project's own research — have increased
[6]. For pests, a Czech review names two dominant species: a moth and
the caraway gall mite, the latter reported to cause serious yield loss
with no effective control method currently known [6] — a more severe,
more specific single-pest finding than this project has sourced for
other Apiaceae crops. A home-gardening source separately names the
carrot rust fly as a pest (consistent with caraway's real carrot-family
relationship), noting it can also vector aster yellows [6]. No aphid
species specific to caraway were found in this pass's own search
results, despite a dedicated search — caraway is not assumed to share
other Apiaceae crops' own named aphid species without a caraway-
specific source.

**Finding 6 — an authoritative pet-safety reference's own caraway
listing is independently confirmed here as caraway's own direct
listing: toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, with named toxic principles
and specific clinical signs.** That reference's own plant-toxicity
listing for *Carum carvi* (alternate common names include two
confusingly cumin- and fennel-adjacent names) names it toxic to dogs,
cats, and horses, with toxic principles given as oils, carvone, and
limonene, and clinical signs of mild vomiting and diarrhea [7]. A
secondary pet-care source corroborates the dog-toxicity finding while
noting the reaction is typically mild, and separately flags concentrated
caraway essential oil (undiluted or in excess) as the greater risk [7]
— directly paralleling a pattern this project has found for several
other essential-oil-bearing herbs, where concentrated essential oil is a
materially greater hazard than the whole plant or spice in small
culinary amounts. One secondary source makes a contradictory claim that
the reference lists caraway as non-toxic in small amounts, which is
treated as unreliable against the primary listing.

**Finding 7 — caraway's root is an independently documented, if minor,
food product (eaten boiled or roasted like parsnip), but the leaf's own
edibility is directly contradicted across sources.** Two encyclopedia-
style sources both describe the caraway root as a winter root vegetable,
prepared like parsnip [8]. Separately, the leaves are described by one
source as edible, used fresh in salads and in a named Norwegian dish (a
leaf-and-cabbage soup) [8], while another source instead describes
caraway leaves as "mildly toxic," with symptoms including vomiting and
diarrhea [8] — a direct, unreconciled contradiction between a
traditional-food-use claim and a toxicity claim for the same plant
organ, not resolved here. The root, by contrast, is corroborated across
two independent source types without a competing toxicity claim. A
German-language source separately reports that caraway fruit finds from
Neolithic pile dwellings make it a candidate for the oldest spice used
in Europe, though the exact dating is not clearly stated in the snippet
reviewed [8] — flagged, not asserted as a precise date. Today's leading
caraway-producing countries are reported as the Netherlands, Poland, and
Germany [8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Biennial (the commercial type) — genuinely
  distinct cycle shape from some other Apiaceae crops' own true-annual
  pattern; closer to root-crop biennials like carrot and parsnip
  (Finding 3). An annual type is separately reported with a higher
  per-year yield range (1-1.6 t/ha vs. 0.5-1 t/ha for biennial) but was
  not independently characterized this pass.
- **Climate:** Reported inconsistently across sources — USDA zones
  3-11, 4-10, and 3-7 variously given; one extension source gives
  4a-9b — not reconciled. Frost-tolerant; foliage reported to die back
  after first frost and regrow in spring.
- **Food status:** Root: raw edible, eaten boiled or roasted like
  parsnip (Finding 7). Seed: used as a dried/ground spice, not eaten
  raw. Leaf edibility is contradicted across sources and not resolved
  here.
- **Pollination:** Required for seed set — strongly protandrous/
  dichogamous, predominantly insect-pollinated (bees), with a sourced,
  unusually low self-fertilization ceiling (6-7% in exceptional cases
  within the same umbel) (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Seed yield figures conflict sharply across
  sources and even within a single trial's own two measured years (one
  database: 0.5-1 t/ha biennial vs. 1-1.6 t/ha annual; one extension
  guide: ~250 kg/ha rising to >1,120 kg/ha; one Finnish trial: 3,200
  kg/ha falling to 800 kg/ha; another extension source: flat ~800 kg/ha
  average) (Finding 3). Essential-oil content ~3.0-3.95% of dried fruit
  (regulatory floor / Serbian study average), with composition (carvone
  44.5-95.9%, limonene 1.5-51.3%) varying far more widely across retail
  samples than total oil content (Finding 4).
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the crop's
  own frost-hardy, cold-tolerant framing — no crop-coefficient or
  drought-tolerance rating found.
- **Establishment:** Seed only (direct seeding); does not transplant
  well, taprooted. Germination reported notoriously slow. Seed rate
  ~10-13 kg/ha (Finding 3). Rotation guidance: avoid sowing caraway in
  the same field for 4 years.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass for pH or fertility
  beyond the general 4-year rotation guideline.
- **Stand life:** Biennial commercial cycle: rosette and taproot in year
  1 (foliage dies back after first frost, regrows in spring), bolt/
  flower/seed in year 2 (Finding 3). One older source reports stands can
  persist and set a reduced seed crop again in year 3, depending on how
  many plants bolted in year 2 — flagged but not independently re-
  verified this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** At least six named fungal genera, with one
  species causing 95% seedling infection in Polish pathogenicity tests,
  the most aggressive species tested (Finding 5). A documented pathogen-
  importance shift over time among several named species. The caraway
  gall mite causes serious yield loss with no known effective control
  method — the single most severe uncontrolled-pest finding this
  project has sourced for any Apiaceae seed-spice crop. A named moth is
  separately a dominant pest, and the carrot rust fly is reported as a
  vector for aster yellows. No caraway-specific aphid species were found
  this pass.
- **Genome:** 2C DNA average 4.282 pg (±0.0096 SE) across 35 accessions
  in a 2020 flow-cytometry study, substantially smaller than an older
  4C = 19.051 pg cytophotometry estimate the 2020 authors themselves
  judge an overestimate (Finding 1). Chromosome number well-converged:
  diploid, 2n=20.
- **Cultivars:** No specific caraway cultivar names or trait deltas were
  sourced this pass. An annual type is separately reported alongside the
  biennial commercial type, with a genuinely different yield profile,
  but was not independently characterized as a named cultivar or line
  this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for the seed harvest (year 2, timed by seed
  color — swath at ~3/4 seeds darkened, then combined at ≤10% seed
  moisture). The root, if taken instead, is also a single stage (end of
  year 1) — the two are mutually exclusive cultivation paths on the same
  plant, not two stages of one harvest stream. Single window per
  biennial cycle for the seed harvest; a possible third-year reduced
  re-harvest from plants that did not bolt in year 2 is reported by one
  older extension source but not independently re-sourced this pass.

## Hazards

- **Essential-oil toxicity (carvone, limonene)**: an authoritative
  pet-safety reference's own listing names the species toxic to dogs,
  cats, and horses, with toxic principles named as oils, carvone, and
  limonene, and clinical signs of mild vomiting and diarrhea (Finding
  6). Concentrated essential oil is the greater risk compared to whole
  plant/spice in culinary amounts.
- **Leaf edibility**: directly contradicted across sources (edible-in-
  salad/soup-ingredient vs. "mildly toxic") — not resolved here.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → harvested (swathed at ~3/4 seeds darkened, combined
  at ≤10% moisture) → cured by natural air drying/aeration (hot-air
  drying should be avoided) → dried seed (spice) or distilled into
  essential oil. Root, if taken instead of seed, harvested at end of
  year 1 and eaten boiled or roasted like parsnip (Finding 7).
  Commercial storage beyond about a year is not recommended — carvone
  is volatile and buyers prefer fresh product, though consumer/retail
  sources separately (and more loosely) claim 3-4 years for whole seed.

## Estimates and derivations

An implied genome size of ~4.2 Gb per diploid cell (~2.1 Gb per 1C) is
computed from the 2020 flow-cytometry study's own reported 2C = 4.282 pg
value, using the general pg-to-bp conversion factor of approximately
978 Mb per pg (a standard conversion constant, not caraway-specific).
This arithmetic is not stated directly by the source; it is offered here
only as a size-order sanity check against the older, since-revised
4C = 19.051 pg estimate, and should be treated as a rough figure, not a
citable genome-size statistic, pending a dedicated reference-genome
assembly.
