# Kokum (*Garcinia indica*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/kokum.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *Garcinia indica* (Thouars) Choisy, filed under spice/herb
rather than as a fruit because the dried rind is a souring agent/
condiment, not a dessert fruit. This project's own mangosteen research
(same genus, *Garcinia*, Clusiaceae/Guttiferae) was read for the shared
genus and the shared seed-propagation/clonal-uniformity theme, but the
two species diverge sharply on sexual system — mangosteen is a
functionally all-female apomict with no male trees at all, while kokum
is dioecious/polygamodioecious with a documented, commercially
troublesome male-seedling fraction. No other *Garcinia* crop this
project has researched has a comparable dioecious/male-fraction problem,
so this document's own pollination and propagation findings are
grounded directly in the primary literature rather than by analogy.
Malabar tamarind (*Garcinia gummi-gutta*), a close congener, is covered
separately elsewhere in this project, with several direct cross-
references noted below.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the sexual system is genuinely unresolved across the
literature, and the unresolved male-tree fraction is the single biggest
commercial obstacle to kokum orchards.** Multiple sources describe
*G. indica* as polygamodioecious — male plants that bear no fruit at
all, high-yielding functional female plants, and low-yielding bisexual
plants [1] — while at least one other source calls the sexual system
itself "controversial," having been separately reported as either
polygamo-dioecious or gynodioecious [1]. A controlled-pollination study
found that flowers bagged without pollination set no fruit at all,
pointing to a genuine pollen requirement, yet the same study detected no
zygotic embryo or endosperm in pollinated flowers, suggesting the actual
fruit-set mechanism may be apomictic rather than sexual — the authors
explicitly state the mode of reproduction is still not confirmed [1]. A
Kerala Agricultural University thesis reports a graded cross-
pollination outcome: female trees crossed with "type II" male or
bisexual flowers gave 100% fruit set, crosses with "type I" male flowers
gave 60%, and selfed bisexual flowers gave only 50% [1] — a genuinely
richer, more structured pollination-type finding than this project has
recorded for most dioecious fruit crops, but one that coexists uneasily
with the same literature's own apomixis finding above; this document
does not resolve which mechanism dominates. Seedling populations are
commercially reported to run 50-60% unproductive male trees [2][3] —
directly mirroring the same male-fraction problem independently
reported for Malabar tamarind (*G. gummi-gutta*), a genuine cross-genus-
wide pattern in cultivated dioecious *Garcinia* species.

**Finding 2 — chromosome number disagrees across sources by a wide
margin, unusually for a single named species, and even the newest study
could not settle an old male-vs-female discrepancy.** Reported counts
split mainly between 2n=54 and 2n=48 [4]. The 2n=54 count traces to the
oldest located source (1949) [4]; a 2n=48 count is attributed to a
separate 1964 study and is also the figure given in a standard
Southeast Asian plant-resources handbook [4]. The most recent study
found this pass, a 2023 karyomorphology paper, found the same 2n=54
count in both male and female trees, but reported that the female trees
differed from the males in their number of satellite chromosome pairs
[4] — a genuine, specific sex-linked karyotype difference, though this
document could not independently verify from the available snippet-
level sourcing whether this difference has been replicated or is
itself disputed.

**Finding 3 — yield figures are reported almost exclusively per-tree,
not per-hectare, and the one per-hectare figure in this document is a
derived extrapolation, not a sourced field measurement.** A government
agricultural-research decision-support page and a separate agronomy
guide both give roughly 45-60 kg of fruit per tree per year as a typical
average [2][5]; a Karnataka field trial reported a maximum of 42.60
kg/tree against 31.82 kg/tree for an untreated control, and a separate
germplasm-evaluation study recorded 28.77 kg/tree/year for its best
accession [5]. No source gave a direct kg/ha figure. Using a named
agricultural university's own recommended sole-plantation spacing of
6×6 m (about 278 trees/ha) [2] and the 45-60 kg/tree range above, this
document derives roughly 12,500-16,700 kg/ha at full bearing — labeled
explicitly as this document's own extrapolation, not a measured orchard
yield, since no source reported an actual field-level per-hectare
harvest. Grafted trees are reported to begin bearing 4-5 years after
planting, while seedling trees take longer [2][5] — directly paralleling
mangosteen's own long-juvenile-period finding, though kokum's own
juvenile period is markedly shorter.

**Finding 4 — the crop is reported as remarkably disease- and pest-free
by commercial/extension sources, but this document found no dedicated
primary disease study for kokum specifically to confirm or qualify that
claim, and a tempting mangosteen-disease list must not be transferred
across the genus boundary.** One agronomy article states the tree "does
not require rainfall and [is] generally not affected by pest attack or
any diseases," and a separate organic-farming guide independently calls
it "remarkably pest and disease-free" [6]. However, neither of these is
a dedicated plant-pathology source, and this document explicitly
declines to borrow mangosteen's own named diseases onto kokum without
species-specific confirmation, since those are mangosteen-specific
findings from a different (though related) species. Postharvest shelf
life for the fresh fruit is reported inconsistently: one agricultural-
research publication gives 4-5 days at ambient temperature, a separate
research-linked ready-to-serve-beverage study gives "about one week,"
and a third journal study reports a maximum shelf life of 22.5 days for
treated fruit under an unspecified cold-storage treatment, with no
shriveling or spoilage observed under that regime [7] — this document
could not confirm the exact treatment conditions behind the 22.5-day
figure from the available snippet and flags it as the least-reliable of
the three.

**Finding 5 — most commercial dried-rind supply is reported wild/
forest-collected rather than orchard-grown, a genuine real-world
supply-chain fact that explains why this long-domesticated, well-known
spice crop has never become a mainstream commercial orchard species
despite steady demand.** A feature article on the crop's commercial
underdevelopment notes that most kokum comes from wild collection, which
the same source says makes rind drying especially difficult during the
rainy season [8]. Annual kokum seed production across the Western Ghats
(India) has been separately estimated at about 500 t [4], though this
document found no source directly stating what fraction of that total
is wild-collected versus orchard-grown.

**Finding 6 — a genuinely interesting cross-species horticultural
technique was found connecting kokum directly to a closely related
crop.** A 2025 agricultural-research journal article describes grafting
kokum onto Malabar tamarind (*Garcinia gummi-gutta*) stock so that both
spices can be harvested from a single tree, explicitly framed as a
sustainable model for high-value spice cultivation in land-constrained
settings [8][9]. Separately, in-situ or coppice grafting has been
reported as a technique to convert an existing unproductive male tree
into a fruiting female, directly addressing the Finding 1 male-fraction
problem without felling and replanting [9].

**Finding 7 — kokum butter's seed-to-fat yield figure comes only from a
commercial farming guide, not a research source, and should be treated
as weaker than this project's typical sourced yield-chain figures.**
Kokum butter — a solid, stable hard fat extracted from the dried seed by
cold-pressing or hot processing — is reported to come from the seed
specifically (not the pulp or rind) [8][9]. A commercial guide gives a
roughly 30-35% yield from dried seed weight [9], but this document found
no peer-reviewed or extension-research source independently confirming
that figure.

**Finding 8 — a hydroxycitric acid (HCA) dietary-supplement
hepatotoxicity signal exists in the literature, but every source found
implicates *Garcinia gummi-gutta*/"Garcinia cambogia" specifically, not
*G. indica* (kokum) by name, so this document does not assert the
finding for kokum itself.** Kokum fruit contains anthocyanins, fatty
acids, hydroxycitric acid (HCA), and garcinol, the last described as an
antioxidant with chelating activity [7]. Separately, and for the related
species *G. gummi-gutta* (the usual commercial source of "Garcinia
cambogia" weight-loss extract), a national health regulator reviewed 93
international case reports and found a possible link between HCA-
containing supplements and liver injury, planning to add hepatotoxicity
warnings to product monographs; a 2025 pharmaceutical-biology review
separately identified more than 200 adverse liver-injury events tied to
Garcinia consumption, and a UK toxicology advisory committee has a 2026
draft opinion noting (–)-HCA caused testicular toxicity (Sertoli-cell
effects) in rodent studies, with genotoxicity neither confirmed nor
excluded [10]. This document searched specifically for *G. indica*-named
evidence and found none — the hepatotoxicity literature is uniformly
about *G. gummi-gutta*/*G. cambogia* commercial extract products — so
this is explicitly flagged as a forward pointer for this project's own
Malabar tamarind research, not written into kokum's own hazards section.
No authoritative pet-safety listing or religious/ritual use was located
for kokum in either search pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial — evergreen tree, slow-growing,
  endemic to the Western Ghats of India. Perennial bearing (flowering
  November-February, fruit ripening April-June [1][4]).
- **Climate:** Tropical, humid [4] — endemic to and commercially
  concentrated in the Western Ghats (Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka,
  Kerala); also reported on the lower slopes of the Nilgiri hills, West
  Bengal, and Assam [4]; no formal hardiness-zone classification was
  found.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh fruit eaten out of hand [1].
- **Pollination:** Required (disputed mechanism) — likely insect-
  pollinated, mechanism unconfirmed. The controlled-pollination study's
  own "no fruit set without pollination" result and its own "no zygotic
  embryo detected" result are in direct tension and not resolved in this
  pass (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** No sourced kg/ha figure exists; per-tree
  figures run roughly 28.77-60 kg/tree/year across multiple studies
  [2][5]; a derived 12,500-16,700 kg/ha extrapolation from the
  recommended 6×6 m sole-plantation spacing is given as this document's
  own estimate, not a measured field yield (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  claim that the tree "does not require rainfall" (an unsourced-
  strength claim) (Finding 4).
- **Establishment:** Both seed and vegetative (grafting), with grafting
  dominant commercially. Dioecious/polygamodioecious; seedling
  populations run 50-60% unproductive male trees [2][3], directly
  motivating grafting from elite high-yielding female mother trees as
  the preferred commercial route [3][9]. Seed is reported recalcitrant,
  losing viability quickly if dried or frozen [9]. Grafted trees
  (softwood grafting from elite high-yielding female mother trees) are
  the dominant commercial planting material. Recommended sole-
  plantation spacing 6×6 m (~278 trees/ha) [2]. Root suckers also
  reported as a propagation option [9]. Successful grafting is reported
  season-dependent, though the specific season was not sourced in the
  available snippet.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour figure was sourced for any operation this
  pass. Rind processing is operationally documented: fruit is peeled,
  the rind sun-dried 5-7 days to below 15% moisture, then stored
  airtight [8][9]; the seed is recovered during this separation step.
- **Soil:** Farmyard manure @ 20-25 kg and NPK (19:19:19) in two split
  doses per one government agricultural-research recommendation; a named
  agricultural university recommends 20 kg farmyard manure, 500 g N, 250
  g P2O5, and 250 g K2O per mature tree after year 10 [2]. No pH range
  or soil-texture preference was independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Grafted trees bear from 4-5 years after planting;
  seedling trees take longer with no specific figure sourced. No
  orchard-lifespan or peak-yield-age figure was sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No dedicated kokum-specific pest/disease
  study was located; two non-research sources describe the crop as
  largely pest- and disease-free [6], but this is not independently
  confirmed and mangosteen's own named diseases are explicitly not
  transferred across the genus boundary without species-specific
  sourcing (Finding 4).
- **Genome:** No genome-size (C-value) figure was sourced this pass,
  though chromosome number itself is disputed between 2n=54 and 2n=48
  across sources, with the newest (2023) study finding 2n=54 in both
  sexes but a sex-linked satellite-chromosome-pair difference (Finding
  2).
- **Cultivars:** No named kokum cultivar or accession comparison beyond
  the unnamed Karnataka field-trial line (42.60 vs. 31.82 kg/tree
  control) and the unnamed best germplasm accession (28.77 kg/tree/
  year) was sourced this pass [5] — a notably thinner cultivar-sourcing
  outcome than this project's own mangosteen research, which found a
  named-local-type list.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — ripe fruit is the sole harvested stage
  sourced this pass. Single window — one annual fruiting season
  (flowering November-February, fruit ripening April-June) [1][4].
- **Storage:** Fresh fruit 4-5 days ambient; "about one week" (separate
  research-linked study); 22.5 days maximum under an unspecified cold-
  storage treatment, with no shriveling or spoilage observed (flagged as
  the least-reliable of the three) [7] (Finding 4). Dried rind and
  butter storage figures are estimated by analogy (see Estimates and
  derivations).

## Hazards

No dedicated kokum-specific pest/disease study was located; two non-
research sources describe the crop as largely pest- and disease-free
[6], but this is not independently confirmed and mangosteen's own named
diseases are explicitly not transferred across the genus boundary
without species-specific sourcing (Finding 4). No authoritative pet-
safety listing or dog/cat toxicity finding was located.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — a hydroxycitric-acid dietary-supplement
hepatotoxicity signal exists in the literature but is sourced only to
the related species *G. gummi-gutta*/"Garcinia cambogia," not to *G.
indica* by name (Finding 8) — flagged as a forward pointer for this
project's own Malabar tamarind research, deferred until this project has
a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (rind):** fruit peeled → rind sun-dried 5-7 days to below 15%
  moisture → stored airtight → used as a souring agent/condiment.
  **Chain (butter):** seed recovered during rind separation → dried →
  cold-pressed or hot-processed into kokum butter (a solid, stable hard
  fat), reported at roughly 30-35% yield from dried seed weight (weakly
  sourced).

## Estimates and derivations

A yield of 12,500-16,700 kg/ha is derived: no source gave a direct
per-hectare field yield. A named agricultural university's recommended
sole-plantation spacing for kokum is 6×6 m [2], which gives 10,000 m² /
36 m² ≈ 278 trees/ha. Multiplying by the sourced 45-60 kg/tree/year
range (two independent sources citing roughly the same figure) [2][5]
gives 278 × 45 = 12,510 kg/ha to 278 × 60 = 16,680 kg/ha, rounded to
12,500-16,700 kg/ha. This assumes full bearing, uniform spacing, and an
all-productive (fully female/bisexual) stand, none of which is confirmed
by a field study; it should be replaced by an actual orchard harvest-
weight record if one is ever located.

A kokum butter yield chain of 30-35% of dried seed weight is carried
directly from a commercial farming guide [9], not independently verified
against a research source. Plausible range: 20-40%, given the general
variability of seed-fat extraction yields across comparable tropical
seed butters in this project's prior research.

A dried rind shelf life of 365 days is estimated by direct analogy to
other sun-dried spice/condiment products this project has researched
with comparable low final moisture content. Plausible range: 270-545
days.

A kokum butter shelf life of 730 days is estimated by direct analogy to
other solid seed-fat/butter commodities this project has researched, on
the reasoning that kokum butter's own reported stability ("a solid,
stable hard fat") [8] should behave similarly to other tropical seed
fats in storage. Plausible range: 365-1095 days.
