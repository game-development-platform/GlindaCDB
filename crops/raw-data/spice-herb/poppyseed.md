# Poppy Seed, culinary use (*Papaver somniferum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/poppyseed.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-03

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is one species with two distinct use paths: this document covers
the culinary-seed/oilseed use only. The plant's medicinal/latex (opium)
production chain is a separate use path, outside the scope of this
project's public-safe research, and is not modeled here.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — chromosome number is well-converged at 2n=22 across most
sources, but a 2024 study of Afghan populations found a genuinely
different count (2n=20, with larger chromosomes) in one of seven
populations studied, and genome-size estimates disagree by a non-trivial
margin depending on measurement method.** A standard plant genome
database's own annotation describes the species as diploid with 2n=22
[1], and this is the standard, widely-repeated figure. However, a 2024
peer-reviewed study of Afghan populations found that while six of seven
populations studied had the standard 22 chromosomes, the seventh had
only 20, larger chromosomes [1] — a genuine within-species karyotype
variant, not resolved by this document. Genome-size estimates for a
named reference cultivar range from a highly contiguous sequenced
assembly of ~2.72-2.73 Gb to a k-mer-based estimate of 2.87 Gb to a
separate assembly summary's 3.37 Gb [1] — a roughly 20% spread across
methods applied to nominally the same reference cultivar, not fully
reconciled here. This document explicitly does not use a chromosome-
count/genome-size figure found for a related subspecies (a different
taxon/ploidy level entirely) as a stand-in for the cultivated species
[1].

**Finding 2 — poppy is predominantly self-pollinated with only modest,
sharply distance-attenuated insect-driven outcrossing, a genuinely
different and more precisely quantified pollination profile than most
of this project's other crops, directly measured via a transgenic field
trial's pollen-flow gradient.** A Tasmanian field study found self-
pollination is the dominant route to fertilization, with wind
contributing little and insects (mainly honeybees and flies) accounting
for most of the limited outcrossing that does occur [2]. The same study
measured actual pollen-mediated gene flow using a transgenic field
trial: 3.26% of seeds were transgenic (i.e., cross-pollinated from the
transgenic parent) at 0.1 m from the pollen source, dropping sharply to
0.34% at 5 m and 0.18% at 10 m [2] — a specific, directly measured
distance-decay curve, a notably stronger and more quantitative isolation
-distance basis than most of this project's other seed-crop pollination
findings, which typically rely on secondary "recommended isolation
distance" guidance rather than a primary gene-flow measurement.
Historical observations from Charles Darwin corroborate self-fertility
when the species is protected from insects [2]. Separately, inbreeding
effects on seed yield are reported to vary meaningfully by landrace in
Indian studies: some lines showed significant inbreeding depression on
selfing, while one named landrace showed none [2] — a genuine cultivar-
level difference in selfing tolerance, not reconciled into a single
figure here.

**Finding 3 — seed yield and seed oil content both vary widely by
cultivar and sowing timing, and seed yield is reported negatively
correlated with morphine content in low-alkaloid food-type cultivars, a
direct agronomic tradeoff between the crop's two uses that this
document's own culinary-only scope does not resolve but explicitly
flags.** Turkish trials report autumn-sown cultivars yielding roughly
523-1,520 kg/ha of seed versus 286-963 kg/ha for spring sowings — autumn
sowing clearly outyielding spring sowing in that trial [3]. A Croatian
oilseed-poppy study found top cultivars averaging 816-847 kg/ha with
seed oil content of 42.5-46.3% across cultivars [3]. A Slovak study of
newer food-type (low-alkaloid) cultivars reported 1.77-1.90 t/ha versus
1.76 t/ha for older control varieties, though the difference was not
statistically significant [3] — and the same Slovak study explicitly
states that seed yield is negatively correlated with morphine content,
and that food-type varieties were not even registered for cultivation in
Slovakia until 2019 [3], a specific, sourced regulatory-and-breeding-
history fact tying directly back to this document's own culinary-vs-
other-use split.

**Finding 4 — poppy seed can produce false-positive opiate results on
standard urine drug tests at surprisingly small ingested quantities, a
well-documented, food-safety-adjacent phenomenon distinct from any
toxicity finding this project has recorded, with the alkaloids coming
from latex residue on the seed coat rather than from the seed's own
interior.** A 1998 forensic-science journal study found that cake slices
containing about 4.69 grams of poppy seed caused four test subjects to
screen positive for up to 24 hours [4]; a secondary report cites as
little as one teaspoon of poppy seed pushing urine opiate levels to
1,200 ng/mL [4] — this document treats the teaspoon figure as a
secondary, less rigorous claim than the peer-reviewed cake-slice study,
but both point in the same direction. The alkaloids are picked up from
pod/latex residue adhering to the seed's exterior during harvest/
processing, not synthesized within the seed's interior itself [4] — a
specific, sourced mechanism this document flags since it directly
explains why thorough washing/processing could plausibly reduce, though
not reliably eliminate, the effect (one low-quality source's "cannot be
washed away" claim is explicitly flagged as unreliable and not relied
upon here [4]). Regulators have responded by raising the federal urine-
test opiate cutoff (a national drug-policy agency raised it to 2,000
ng/mL), though some hospitals still use lower thresholds (one example
reported at 300 ng/mL) [4] — this document records this as a real-world
food-safety-adjacent phenomenon worth flagging as a hazard, even though
it is a diagnostic-interference effect rather than a toxicity effect.

**Finding 5 — downy mildew is reported as poppy's single most
destructive disease, caused by two distinct oomycete species, with one
documented case of complete crop loss across multiple fields in a
single growing season, a notably more severe single-disease outcome than
most other crops this project has researched.** Downy mildew is
described as one of the most destructive diseases of this crop because
of the systemic nature of infection, caused by two oomycete species,
*Peronospora meconopsidis* and *Peronospora somniferi* [5]. One
Australian industry report documented complete crop loss in some fields
during the 2014/15 season specifically from the systemic form of this
disease [5] — a severity outcome this document flags as notably starker
than the typically partial, manageable disease impacts reported for most
other spice/herb crops this project has researched. Leaf blight
(*Helminthosporium papaveris*) is separately described as one of the
most destructive poppy diseases worldwide [5], and Indian research adds
collar rot, damping-off, root rot, and stem/capsule rot to the disease
list, alongside mosaic viral diseases [5]. Pest pressure is by contrast
reported as generally low: flea beetles perforate young-plant leaves and
aphids feed on flower-bud sap, with no severe outbreak reported in this
pass's own source set [5]. Seed treatments show some promise against
downy mildew transmission (one trial found physiochemical seed
treatments reduced seed-borne transmission without harming viability),
and resistance breeding is active, with two named Indian landraces
identified as resistant [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual; autumn- or spring-sown depending on
  climate, with autumn sowing outyielding spring sowing in at least one
  Turkish trial (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — capsules dried in the field,
  then threshed for seed.
- **Climate:** Temperate, Mediterranean, cool temperate.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — seed eaten directly as a baking topping/
  ingredient; also cold-pressed for culinary oil.
- **Pollination:** Required, but predominantly self-pollinated with
  modest, sharply distance-attenuated insect-driven outcrossing (3.26%
  transgenic-seed rate at 0.1 m down to 0.18% at 10 m in one field
  trial) (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** 523-1,520 kg/ha (Turkish autumn-sown trials)
  to 286-963 kg/ha (Turkish spring-sown trials); 816-847 kg/ha (Croatian
  top cultivars); 1.77-1.90 t/ha (Slovak food-type cultivars) vs. 1.76
  t/ha (older control varieties, difference not statistically
  significant) (Finding 3). Seed oil content 42.5-46.3% across Croatian
  cultivars. Seed yield reported negatively correlated with morphine
  content in low-alkaloid food-type cultivars — a direct cross-use
  agronomic tradeoff.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance/waterlogging tolerance not
  independently sourced this pass. No crop-coefficient figure sourced.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding; autumn sowing reported to outyield
  spring sowing in at least one trial (Finding 3). The same tiny, oil-
  rich seed is both the planting stock and the harvested culinary/oil-
  pressing product, the same pattern as caraway's own seed. Mostly self-
  pollinated; isolation distance implied by the sourced pollen-flow
  gradient above rather than a conventional recommended-distance figure
  (Finding 2).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Annual; single growing season per planting.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Downy mildew (two oomycete species) is the
  single most destructive disease, with one documented case of complete
  crop loss across multiple fields in a season (Finding 5). Leaf blight,
  collar rot, damping-off, root rot, stem/capsule rot, and mosaic viral
  diseases also reported. Pest pressure generally low (flea beetles,
  aphids).
- **Genome:** 2.72-3.37 Gb depending on measurement method for a named
  reference cultivar (a roughly 20% spread, not fully reconciled)
  (Finding 1). Chromosome number 2n=22 is the standard, well-supported
  figure, though one 2024 study found a 2n=20 variant in one of seven
  Afghan populations studied.
- **Cultivars:** One named Polish low-morphine food-type cultivar,
  unnamed Slovak food-type lines (registered for cultivation only from
  2019), and several unnamed Croatian oilseed cultivars (top performers
  at 816-847 kg/ha) were located this pass (Finding 3). No single
  cultivar's full eating-quality/yield/disease-resistance profile was
  fully characterized across all three axes simultaneously by any one
  source.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — capsules are dried in the field, then
  threshed; no separate maturity-stage-dependent product was sourced
  this pass. Single window; annual crop, single planting-to-harvest
  cycle per season.
- **Storage:** Estimated by analogy (see Estimates and derivations); no
  dedicated shelf-life source was found for the culinary seed.

## Hazards

Downy mildew (two oomycete species) is the single most destructive
disease, with one documented case of complete crop loss across multiple
fields in a season (Finding 5). Leaf blight, collar rot, damping-off,
root rot, stem/capsule rot, and mosaic viral diseases also reported.
Pest pressure generally low (flea beetles, aphids).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — poppy seed can cause false-positive opiate results
on standard urine drug tests from pod/latex residue on the seed coat,
not from the seed's own interior, at surprisingly small ingested
quantities — a genuine food-safety-adjacent hazard, deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 4).

No pet-toxicity finding was investigated this pass for the culinary-seed
path specifically — the plant's latex/alkaloid toxicity belongs to a
separate use path outside this project's scope.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** capsules dried in the field → threshed for seed → seed
  used directly as a culinary topping/ingredient, or cold-pressed for
  culinary oil (oil content 42.5-46.3% across sourced cultivars).

## Estimates and derivations

A culinary-seed shelf life of 365 days is estimated: no dedicated
shelf-life source was found for culinary poppy seed specifically. Other
dried whole-seed spice products this project has researched (caraway,
cumin, dill) all use longer figures, carried by analogy to a generic
"dried intact seed" storage behavior. This document instead uses a more
conservative 365 days for poppy seed specifically, reasoning that poppy
seed's sourced oil content (42.5-46.3%, Finding 3) is substantially
higher than those drier seed spices, and high-oil seeds are well-
established in general food-science terms to be more prone to
rancidity-driven quality loss during long storage than low-oil seeds.
This is a double analogy (borrowing the general "dried seed" storage
category, then adjusting down for a general oil-content/rancidity
principle) rather than a single direct analogy, and is correspondingly
weaker. Plausible range: 180-730 days. A dedicated poppy-seed storage-
trial source would replace this estimate entirely.
