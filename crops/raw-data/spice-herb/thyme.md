# Thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/thyme.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers common/garden thyme, *Thymus vulgaris*, the culinary
species. Creeping thyme (*Thymus serpyllum*, Wild/Breckland/Mother-of-
Thyme) surfaced this pass as a genuinely distinct, more ornamental-than-
culinary species with its own cultivar range and a sourced strong-bee-
attraction finding — flagged as a possible future candidate crop entry,
not researched directly. Lemon thyme (*T. x citriodorus*, itself a
hybrid) was not independently researched this pass.

This document follows the same three-way comparison this project has
used for rosemary and sage: thyme is real Lamiaceae like both, but a
different genus from either (unlike rosemary/sage's own same-genus
closeness).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — thyme's chromosome number and ploidy are comparatively
well-sourced and agree closely across sources, with a documented
natural polyploid series paralleling sage's own diploid/tetraploid
finding, though without that finding's own sourced chemistry-delta
between ploidy levels.** A 2023 study reports chromosome number
increasing from 2n=2x=30 in diploids to 2n=4x=60 in tetraploids,
confirmed by both cytogenetic and flow-cytometry analysis [1]; a
separate paper on Iranian *Thymus* species found 2n=2x=30, 2n=4x=56 or
60 across root-tip counts [1] — broadly consistent, with a minor
unreconciled 56-vs-60 tetraploid-count disagreement. The genus's base
chromosome number is reported as x=15 in most species, with one
exception (x=14) [1] — a genus-wide taxonomic data point this document
did not find paralleled for either rosemary's or sage's own genus-
context findings. Genome size: one study cites an earlier (1993) flow-
cytometry estimate of 2C DNA = 1.54 pg for diploid *T. vulgaris*,
implying roughly 750 Mbp per haploid genome by standard pg-to-Mbp
conversion, with other *Thymus* species in the same comparative study
ranging more widely (499-1182 Mbp) [1] — a directly species-specific
figure, though sourced secondhand from a 30-year-old estimate, not a
primary reference-genome assembly, a weaker sourcing basis than
rosemary's own direct 2024 chromosome-level assembly but comparable in
kind to sage's own pg-based figure.

**Finding 2 — thyme's yield literature is dominated by a different,
related species (Iranian/Denai thyme) rather than *T. vulgaris* itself,
a cross-species-sourcing problem this document flags explicitly rather
than silently adopting those figures as this crop's own.** An Iranian
harvest-stage trial on the related species found dry herbage yield of
2,564 kg/ha at 50% bloom versus 1,354 kg/ha pre-bloom, with essential-
oil yield of 43.4 kg/ha at 50% bloom versus 18 kg/ha pre-bloom [2] — a
genuinely useful harvest-timing finding in shape, but explicitly not
*T. vulgaris*'s own figure. The same species, across three years
(2006-2008) of a different Iranian trial, gave fresh/dry herbage yields
of 9,124/1,568, 10,250/2,176, and 11,337/2,750 kg/ha respectively, with
yield falling over successive cuttings within a season (first cut
highest) [2] — again not *T. vulgaris*, not adopted as this crop's
figure. Thymol content itself varies over a season in the same species
(69% first cutting, 76% second cutting) [2], while a Polish organic
trial specifically on common thyme found thymol share (up to 57.08%)
decreasing with consecutive cuts [2] — a directly opposite within-
season trend from the related species' figure, a genuine cross-species
inconsistency this document does not attempt to reconcile. The same
Polish trial reports protected (foil-tunnel) cultivation giving visibly
higher fresh-herb output than open field, in units of kg fresh weight
per 10 m² (not converted to kg/ha this pass) [2]. No source opened this
pass gave a clean *T. vulgaris*-specific open-field kg/ha figure for
either fresh/dried leaf or essential oil — paralleling sage's own
yield-sourcing weakness, though for a different underlying reason
(species conflation here vs. simple scarcity for sage).

**Finding 3 — thyme's propagation-reliability picture confirms a
rosemary < thyme < sage reliability gradient in its general shape (seed
genuinely less reliable than sage's), while cuttings are, as with both
rosemary and sage, uniformly described as the easier commercial
route.** Seed germination is repeatedly described as "slow and
unreliable" and "spotty," with growth also described as "quite slow"
even after germination [3]; one outlier source specifically reports
germination taking up to two years in some cases [3]. Cuttings are
described as the reliable, true-to-type route, rooting in roughly 6-8
weeks, with rooting in plain water specifically noted as a simple
viable method [3] — directly paralleling both rosemary's and sage's own
cuttings findings. Division is also named as a real propagation route
for thyme specifically (recommended in spring, from healthy plants at
least 3 years old, repeated roughly every 3 years) [3] — a propagation
method this document did not find sourced for either rosemary or sage,
a genuine point of difference within this project's four-crop Lamiaceae
herb group. No commercial germination-percentage or cutting-rooting-
success figure beyond the qualitative "slow/unreliable" framing was
found this pass, the same general gap flagged for rosemary's and sage's
own propagation sourcing.

**Finding 4 — thyme's hardiness-zone literature disagrees across
sources but, like sage's own finding, consistently rates common thyme
measurably more cold-hardy than rosemary.** Most sources converge on
USDA Zone 5 as the safe minimum, with some reporting Zone 4 survival
(occasionally even Zone 3) given winter protection/heavy mulch, and
named "German"/"winter" thyme types specifically described as hardier
variants suited to colder sites [4] — a comparable range to sage's own
disputed zone 4-8/5-8 findings, and measurably colder-hardy than
rosemary's own zone 6-10/6-9/7-11 range. This pass's own sourcing is
thus a third independent confirmation (after sage) that rosemary alone
is the outlier among this project's Mediterranean woody Lamiaceae
herbs, not a general "all kitchen herbs need Mediterranean warmth"
pattern.

**Finding 5 — thyme's disease complex includes gray mold/Botrytis as a
hazard this project's other three Lamiaceae herbs did not report,
alongside a genuinely multi-pathogen root-rot/damping-off complex that
partially overlaps with sage's own named pathogens.** An extension-
style source lists Rhizoctonia root rot, gray mold, and Alternaria
blight as thyme's most frequent disease problems, specifically noting
that thyme's preference for poor, well-drained soil makes dense/moist
soil conditions the trigger [5]. A 2017 Turkish pathogenicity study
found *Rhizoctonia solani* and *Macrophomina phaseolina* causing thyme
root rot [5] — *Rhizoctonia* directly overlapping with sage's own
secondary soil-oomycete root-rot risk, though as different specific
organisms (sage's own finding named *Phytophthora*/*Pythium*, not
*Rhizoctonia*). A later Egyptian study identified a four-pathogen
complex behind thyme damping-off, root rot, and wilt: *Fusarium
semitectum*, *F. solani*, *Rhizoctonia solani*, and *Macrophomina
phaseolina* [5] — a notably more multi-pathogen root-disease picture
than either rosemary's (Phytophthora-dominated) or sage's (powdery-
mildew-dominated) own findings. *Botrytis cinerea* gray mold is
separately named by a university extension source as a common thyme
problem, developing in cool, wet weather [5] — the first gray-mold
finding in this project's four-crop Lamiaceae herb group; notably, one
study found thyme oil itself shows efficacy against Botrytis and
several other soil/foliar fungi [5], a genuinely interesting "the
crop's own essential oil may suppress its own pathogen" finding not
recorded for any other crop this project has researched. Named pests
are limited to aphids and spider mites per the extension sources opened
[5] — a notably shorter named-pest list than sage's own seven-name
list.

**Finding 6 — thyme itself (plant/culinary leaf, small amounts) is
considered non-toxic by a major pet-safety organization, directly
paralleling both rosemary's and sage's own plant-non-toxic findings,
but the essential-oil hazard compound is thymol rather than rosemary's
camphor or sage's thujone — a third genuinely distinct concentrated-
oil-hazard compound in this project's four-crop Lamiaceae herb group.**
That organization considers thyme (the kitchen herb, small amounts)
non-toxic, with the concentrated essential oil specifically identified
as the real hazard [6]. In cats, thymol exposure is reported to cause
drooling, vomiting, oral and skin irritation, CNS depression, and
possible liver injury [6]. A systematic review of thymol in veterinary
medicine reports dose-dependent toxicity (mainly liver/kidney) at high
levels, explicitly stating no established safe/effective pet dose
exists across species [7] — directly paralleling the "no established
safe dose" framing this project found for sage's own thujone. Dog-
specific sensitivity claims conflict across sources (one says thyme oil
is unsafe for dogs, another says dogs are less sensitive than cats)
[6], not reconciled. On the human side, thymol is reported to have a
strong corrosive effect on tissue (capable of severe, possibly painless
chemical burns due to thymol's own local-anesthetic property), with
systemic absorption after ingestion or dermal exposure producing
agitation, confusion, seizures, coma, hypotension, cardiac
dysrhythmias, methemoglobinemia, hemolytic anemia, and anion-gap
metabolic acidosis [8] — a notably more severe-sounding systemic-
toxicity profile than this project found for either camphor or thujone,
though (as with those two) sourced mainly from poison-center/
toxicology-database material rather than a dedicated clinical dose-
response study for thymol specifically. An oral LD50 in mice is
reported around 640 mg/kg [8] (an animal figure, not a human/pediatric
threshold). General essential-oil-poisoning research (not thymol-
specific) found 63% of poison-center essential-oil cases occur in
children under 15, with a researcher warning that as little as 5 mL of
some oils can be life-threatening to a child [8] — a general framing
this document does not treat as thymol-specific.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial subshrub, low/creeping growth
  habit, Mediterranean-climate-adapted but measurably more cold-hardy
  than rosemary (Finding 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again, matching rosemary's and
  sage's own evergreen-adjacent continuous-cutting framing — not
  independently re-verified against a dedicated multi-cut-per-season
  source this pass, same caveat flagged for both.
- **Climate:** USDA zone 5 most common safe minimum, zone 4 (and
  occasionally 3) reported with winter protection; named hardier
  "German"/"winter" thyme types exist (Finding 4) — measurably broader/
  hardier than rosemary's own disputed zone 6-10/6-9/7-11 range, a third
  confirmation (after sage) that rosemary is the outlier among these
  herbs.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — culinary seasoning use, not eaten in
  bulk.
- **Pollination:** Not required for food/leaf yield (harvested as
  foliage); required for seed production specifically — no *T.
  vulgaris*-direct pollination mechanism study was opened this pass (the
  sourced pollinator-attraction finding below concerns the related
  creeping thyme).
- **Yield / composition:** Not reconciled to a clean *T. vulgaris*-
  specific open-field kg/ha figure for either fresh/dried leaf or
  essential oil this pass — the clearest sourced yield-timing-pattern
  figures found were for a different species, kept separate per honesty
  conventions (Finding 2). A Polish trial specifically on *T. vulgaris*
  gives a protected-cultivation fresh-yield figure in kg/10m², not
  converted to kg/ha this pass.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance assumed very high by analogy to
  rosemary/sage, not independently re-verified this pass; the extension
  source's own "thyme prefers poor, well-drained soil" framing (Finding
  5) is consistent with but does not independently confirm this figure.
  Waterlogging tolerance assumed low by analogy; this pass's own disease
  findings (multi-pathogen root rot triggered by dense/moist soil,
  Finding 5) are consistent with but do not independently confirm this
  figure.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed (primary; this pass's sources describe
  germination as "slow and unreliable"/"spotty," with one outlier
  report of up to 2-year germination) (Finding 3). Alternative routes:
  vegetative cuttings (6-8 week rooting, rooting in plain water viable)
  and division (spring, plants 3+ years old, repeated roughly every 3
  years) (Finding 3) — division is a genuinely new propagation route
  relative to rosemary's and sage's own findings, not sourced for
  either. Pollinator-dependent for seed set, mechanism not independently
  sourced for *T. vulgaris* directly this pass.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** pH not independently sourced this pass. Extension sourcing
  confirms thyme's real preference for poor, well-drained soil (Finding
  5).
- **Stand life:** Woody perennial subshrub, low/creeping growth habit;
  division recommended from plants 3+ years old, repeated roughly every
  3 years — the closest this project has to an actual replanting-cycle
  figure for any of its four Lamiaceae herbs, though framed as a
  propagation-maintenance practice rather than a maximum-lifespan figure
  per se.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Rhizoctonia/Macrophomina/Fusarium multi-
  pathogen root rot and damping-off complex, triggered by dense/moist
  soil — a notably more multi-pathogen picture than either rosemary's
  or sage's own findings (Finding 5). Gray mold/Botrytis cinerea, the
  first gray-mold finding in this project's four-crop Lamiaceae herb
  group — interestingly, thyme oil itself is reported to show efficacy
  against Botrytis and other fungi, a self-suppressing-pathogen finding
  unique to this crop. Named pests limited to aphids and spider mites —
  a shorter list than sage's own.
- **Genome:** 2C DNA 1.54 pg (diploid), implying ~750 Mbp per haploid
  genome by standard conversion (Finding 1) — a directly species-
  specific figure, though a notably older and less directly-verified
  basis than rosemary's own 2024 chromosome-level assembly.
- **Cultivars:** No *T. vulgaris*-specific cultivar trait-delta was
  independently sourced this pass — the cultivar names found this pass
  belong to the related, more-ornamental creeping thyme, not to common
  culinary thyme, and are recorded here only for completeness/species-
  distinctness context. One source does name "German"/"winter" thyme as
  a hardier type within *T. vulgaris* itself (Finding 4) — not
  independently characterized beyond the hardiness claim.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — foliage cut as needed/at usable size
  through the growing season, no distinguishable immature-vs-ripe
  harvest-stage choice sourced, matching rosemary's and sage's own
  framing. The related species' own yield-timing finding (Finding 2)
  suggests bloom stage meaningfully affects both dry-herbage and oil
  yield for that species — flagged as a possible future consideration
  for *T. vulgaris* itself, not adopted as a harvest-stage distinction
  this pass since it was not independently confirmed for this crop's
  own species. Single window — cut-and-come-again continuous cutting
  through the growing season, matching rosemary's and sage's own
  framing.
- **Storage:** Not independently re-sourced this pass — unlike rosemary
  and sage, no dedicated storage-life search was run for thyme this pass
  given time constraints.

## Hazards

Rhizoctonia/Macrophomina/Fusarium multi-pathogen root rot and damping-
off complex, triggered by dense/moist soil (Finding 5). Gray mold/
Botrytis cinerea — thyme oil itself is reported to show efficacy against
Botrytis and other fungi. Named pests limited to aphids and spider
mites.

The plant itself (fresh/dried leaf, small amounts) is considered non-
toxic per a major pet-safety organization.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the concentrated essential oil carries a thymol-
driven hazard distinct from rosemary's camphor and sage's thujone: in
cats, drooling, vomiting, oral and skin irritation, CNS depression, and
possible liver injury; in humans, thymol is reported to have a strong
corrosive tissue effect with systemic absorption producing agitation,
confusion, seizures, coma, hypotension, cardiac dysrhythmias,
methemoglobinemia, hemolytic anemia, and metabolic acidosis (Finding 6).
This hazard is deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed, cuttings, or division establishment → foliage cut
  cut-and-come-again through the growing season → used fresh or dried
  as a culinary seasoning, or distilled into thymol-bearing essential
  oil (a hazard distinct from the plant itself).

## Open research questions

- Creeping thyme and lemon thyme surfaced this pass as genuinely
  distinct species/hybrids with their own cultivar ranges and (for
  creeping thyme) a sourced strong-bee-attraction finding — flagged as
  possible future candidate crop entries, not researched directly.
- Thyme's own genome-size figure (2C DNA 1.54 pg, ~750 Mbp implied) is
  sourced secondhand from a 1993 flow-cytometry estimate, not a primary
  reference-genome assembly — a notably older/weaker basis than
  rosemary's own 2024 chromosome-level assembly.
- No clean *T. vulgaris*-specific open-field kg/ha figure was found for
  either fresh/dried leaf or essential oil this pass — the clearest
  sourced yield-pattern figures found were for a different species, kept
  separate. A Polish trial specifically on *T. vulgaris* gives a
  protected-cultivation figure in kg/10m², not converted to kg/ha.
- No numeric germination-percentage figure was independently sourced —
  this pass's sources describe germination only qualitatively ("slow
  and unreliable," "spotty," up to 2 years in one outlier report).
- No commercial germination-percentage or cutting-rooting-success figure
  was found for thyme this pass beyond the qualitative framing above —
  the same general gap flagged for rosemary's and sage's own propagation
  sourcing.
- The sourced severe systemic-toxicity profile for thymol (seizures,
  coma, cardiac dysrhythmias, etc.) is drawn from poison-center/
  toxicology-database material, not a dedicated clinical dose-response
  study — the mouse oral LD50 (640 mg/kg) is an animal figure, not
  independently translated to a human threshold.
- No pollination-mechanism study was opened for *T. vulgaris* directly
  this pass — the sourced bee-attraction finding concerns the related
  species creeping thyme, not independently confirmed for common thyme
  itself.
- Dried-thyme storage-life was not independently re-sourced this pass —
  unlike rosemary's and sage's own dedicated (if low-quality-sourced)
  storage searches, no equivalent search was run for thyme this pass
  given time constraints.
- Division's "repeat roughly every 3 years" framing is a propagation-
  maintenance recommendation, not independently confirmed as a true
  maximum-productive-lifespan figure for the crop as a whole.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any thyme operation
  this pass.
- No seed-yield-per-hectare or isolation-distance figure was sourced for
  thyme's own seed-propagation route.
- The thyme-honey species-naming claim (wild thyme, garden thyme, and
  mother-of-thyme all as recognized honey-source species) came from a
  commercial honey-retail source, not independently verified against a
  botanical/apicultural reference.
- No *T. vulgaris*-specific cultivar trait-delta was independently
  sourced this pass — the cultivar names found this pass belong to the
  related, more-ornamental creeping thyme, not to common culinary
  thyme.
- Thyme's light requirement was not independently sourced this pass — a
  full-sun requirement was assumed by analogy to every other
  Mediterranean Lamiaceae herb this project has researched.
- Thyme's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass — this crop's low, creeping growth
  habit (shallower roots, denser canopy) may make it a genuinely
  different substrate-bed/hydroponic-adjacent candidate than rosemary or
  sage, not independently confirmed.

## Estimates and derivations

No estimates were independently computed this pass. One arithmetic
conversion was performed by a source this document cites rather than by
this document itself: the ~750 Mbp-per-haploid-genome figure is the
cited source's own conversion from its 1.54 pg 2C DNA measurement using
the standard ~978 Mbp-per-pg factor, not an independent calculation
performed here.
