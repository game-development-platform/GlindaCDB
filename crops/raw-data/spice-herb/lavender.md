# Lavender (*Lavandula angustifolia*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/lavender.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers true/English lavender, *Lavandula angustifolia* Mill.
(synonyms *L. officinalis*, *L. vera*), the species behind the premium
culinary- and aromatherapy-grade essential oil and the dried culinary
flower bud. It explicitly does not cover lavandin (*Lavandula ×
intermedia*), a sterile natural hybrid of *L. angustifolia* and spike
lavender (*L. latifolia* Medik.) with a genuinely different commercial
profile (much higher oil yield, lower oil quality, industrial rather
than culinary/premium-aromatherapy use) — covered separately elsewhere
in this project's own research.

This document treats this project's own rosemary, sage, and thyme
research as genuinely close siblings, more so than either basil crop
could claim for them: lavender is a real cold-hardy Mediterranean woody
perennial subshrub, like all three, grown for a harvested aerial organ
from an established multi-year planting, needing excellent drainage and
tolerating drought once established.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — lavender's taxonomy and the lavender/lavandin distinction
are comparatively well-sourced and agree closely across sources, a
notable contrast with some of this project's other species-identity
questions.** *Lavandula angustifolia* subsp. *angustifolia* (fine
lavender) and *L. latifolia* (spike lavender) are both native to
southern Europe [1]. Lavandin is explicitly described as a sterile
natural hybrid obtained by crossing *L. angustifolia* and *L. latifolia*
[1] — directly analogous in structure to lemon basil's own interspecific
-hybrid status researched elsewhere in this project, though lavandin's
sterility is a further, genuinely distinct wrinkle neither lemon basil
nor any other hybrid taxon this project has researched was reported to
share. A 2021 cytogenetic study of 82 lavender genotypes (including 15
hybrids) found hybrid genome sizes falling between those of their parent
species, and all analyzed hybrid genotypes had even chromosome numbers
(2n=48 or 50), with two specific hybrid cultivars showing polyploidy or
aneuploidy instead ('Heavenly Angel' at 2n=100, 'Big Boy James' at
2n=66) [4] — a cleaner, more internally consistent cytogenetic picture
than this project found for either basil species' own ploidy disputes.
A separate Turkish study directly compared lavender and lavandin pollen
/seed traits, finding the lavandin cultivar had more nuclear DNA content
but lower pollen viability and very few viable seeds compared to true
lavender [4] — a sourced, mechanistic confirmation of lavandin's
reported sterility, not merely an assertion.

**Finding 2 — lavandin's oil yield is reported at roughly 3-7x true
lavender's own yield, but its oil quality is consistently described as
lower by the same practical marker (camphor content), and its culinary
use is explicitly contested across sources.** True lavender's essential-
oil yield is reported at 15-40 kg/ha, depending on altitude, cultivar
and climate [2], while lavandin's is reported at 100-200 kg/ha
(primarily one named cultivar) [2] — one French distillery source gives
a narrower 80-150 kg/ha (occasionally reaching 200 kg/ha) for lavandin
against "fine lavender" not exceeding 40 kg/ha [3]. Camphor content is
the key practical quality marker separating the two: one source gives
true lavender's oil as containing 0% camphor and 0% 1,8-cineole, versus
lavandin's 7-40% camphor and 6-20% 1,8-cineole [1]; other sources give
narrower but still-elevated lavandin camphor ranges (2-33% or roughly
7-12%) [1] — the exact figures disagree across sources, but all agree
lavandin's camphor content is substantially higher, and one source
explicitly states true lavender's oil is considered higher-quality
because of its lower camphor content [1]. Culinary use of lavandin is
explicitly disputed across sources: one source says specific lavandin
cultivars might be used in small quantities in some Herbes de Provence
blends, while a separate source states lavandins are generally not
recommended for culinary use specifically because of their higher
camphor content, and a third (a herb-society reference, via a secondary
blog citation) recommends one named cultivar — itself described
elsewhere as more accurately an "English lavandin hybrid" rather than a
true lavandin [5]. This document does not resolve the culinary-lavandin
dispute, but records it as additional evidence that the lavender/
lavandin product distinction is commercially real and contested, not
merely a minor technical note.

**Finding 3 — lavender's harvest timing and yield-building curve over
plant age are reasonably well-sourced and agree across sources, though
the specific oil-yield figures underlying different sources' percentage
claims do not fully reconcile.** Harvest timing varies substantially by
region and climate: end of June in southern France [2], versus August-
September in India's Kashmir Valley [2], with flowering starting earlier
in warmer, lower-altitude areas [2]. As a perennial, yield is explicitly
reported to build over plant age: production is "relatively low, and in
some cases almost zero" during the first two years, reaching an
"optimal peak" starting the 3rd or 4th year [2] — the first source this
project has found to give a specific near-zero-yield figure for the
establishment years of a perennial spice/herb crop, rather than just a
qualitative "lower yield" framing. Flowers are cut with short stems not
exceeding roughly 12cm, since essential oil is concentrated in the
flower tissue itself, with little or none in stem/leaf tissue [2] —
directly paralleling cardamom's own "harvest only the economically
relevant organ" framing, though for a genuinely different plant
structure (flower spike vs. capsule). Oil content is explicitly reported
to fluctuate through both the day and the growing season, with one
government source naming degree of blossoming, time of day, and
atmospheric conditions as all independently affecting final oil quality
[2] — a richer harvest-timing-to-quality relationship than this project
typically records for a single crop. One farming guide's internal
conversion (an average ~1.2% oil content of dried floral stems implying
~14kg/ha oil yield) was used in this document's own derived fresh-
flower-to-oil yield-chain estimate below, since no source gave a
directly-stated fresh-flower-to-oil percentage.

**Finding 4 — lavender's propagation reliability and pollination-
dependency findings each show a genuinely different shape from this
project's basil and holy basil research, making lavender a useful third
data point for propagation-reliability comparisons across Lamiaceae
crops.** Lavender is reported as highly heterozygous and will not grow
true to seed, with cuttings (specifically semi-hardwood cuttings taken
in late summer or early autumn) described as "the most promising
method" for propagating both true lavender and French lavender [4] —
directly extending this project's own rosemary/sage/thyme research with
a fourth Lamiaceae data point, though lavender's own position on that
propagation-reliability gradient (cuttings strongly preferred, seed a
real but secondary route) was not independently scored against the
other three this pass. Seed is viable as a route, described as a
"light germinator" needing only light soil coverage, with germination
taking 1-2 weeks under optimal conditions but sometimes stretching over
several months [4] — a published in-vitro study specifically treated
*L. angustifolia* seed with dormancy-breaking methods (half-strength MS
medium or distilled water with phytagel) [4], paralleling holy basil's
own sourced GA3 dormancy-breaking treatment, though via a different
specific method. Pollination is explicitly required for lavender to
reproduce (an agricultural-literacy resource specifically contrasts
lavender with self-pollinated field crops like soybeans/wheat/oats/
barley) [6] — directly paralleling sweet basil's own sourced insect-
pollination-dependency finding, though lavender's own pollinator
community is reported as bumblebee-dominated rather than honeybee-
dominated at the study sites found this pass, with bumblebees' longer
proboscises specifically cited as giving them an advantage reaching
lavender's nectar [6] — a genuinely different pollinator-community
finding from anything else in this project's spice/herb research so
far. Commercial growers are reported to place beehives in lavender
fields "for optimal essential oil production," though this specific
claim was not independently verified beyond one source this pass — and
lavender honey is reported to typically be a blended, multi-floral
product rather than a single-source "lavender honey" in the strict
sense, since bees visiting lavender fields also visit many other plants
[6].

**Finding 5 — lavender's climate and soil requirements agree closely on
the qualitative picture (well-drained, full sun, drought-tolerant once
established) but disagree moderately on the specific numeric pH range,
and hardiness is explicitly species/hybrid-dependent rather than
uniform across the genus.** English lavender (*L. angustifolia*, this
document's crop) is commonly rated USDA Hardiness Zones 5-8, while
Spanish lavender (a different species, *L. stoechas*, not covered by
this document) is rated Zones 7-10, and lavandin is commonly rated Zone
5-and-warmer [7]. Soil pH is disputed across three sources: one gives
6.4-8.2, a second gives 6.5-7.5, and a third recommends neutral-to-
alkaline (7-9.0) [7] — not reconciled. Drainage is repeatedly described
as the single most critical cultivation factor, with lavender preferring
dry conditions both above and below ground, sharp drainage specifically
called essential in wet winters to prevent crown rot, and raised beds or
mounded planting (raising the planting level 20-30cm) given as a common
remedy for heavy soil [7] — directly connecting to Finding 6's own
Phytophthora/Pythium root-and-crown-rot disease finding below. Drought
tolerance is reported as developing after 1-2 establishment seasons [7],
paralleling this crop's own yield-building-over-plant-age finding in
Finding 3.

**Finding 6 — lavender's disease complex is dominated by water-
management-sensitive root/crown pathogens, a genuinely different hazard
profile from both basil species' own foliar-disease-dominated findings,
and directly connects back to Finding 5's own drainage-criticality
finding rather than being an independent hazard.** 'Shab' (caused by
*Phoma* sp.) is described as causing twig dieback, and specifically
characterized as a weak pathogen likely to be ignored unless other
problems are already present [8]. *Septoria lavandulae* causes a
distinct leaf-spot disease, and a Bulgarian study specifically found
co-infection by both *Phoma lavandulae* and *Septoria lavandulae*
together to be more harmful than either pathogen alone [8] — a genuine,
sourced synergistic-coinfection finding this project has not previously
recorded for any spice/herb crop's disease complex. Phytophthora root
and crown rot is reported by a university extension source as an active
grower concern, with a separate plant-clinic finding implicating four
different pathogens (*Fusarium*, *Phytophthora*, *Pythium*,
*Rhizoctonia*) associated with lavender root rot generally, and symptoms
specifically noted to become obvious during dry-season water stress —
counterintuitively, since the underlying cause is typically
overwatering/poor drainage, not drought itself [8]. A separate Bulgarian
study specifically identified *Phytophthora nicotianae* (syn. *P.
parasitica*) causing grayish water-soaked stem-base lesions leading to
stem wilt and death [8]. Management guidance explicitly notes that
fungicides will not work against Phytophthora or Pythium specifically,
since these are technically oomycetes (water molds), not true fungi —
cultural control (sunny, well-drained sites) is emphasized instead [8],
directly reinforcing Finding 5's own drainage-criticality finding as the
primary practical lever for this crop's dominant disease risk.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial subshrub, Mediterranean-
  climate-adapted — directly comparable in general growth habit to this
  project's own rosemary/sage/thyme research.
- **Harvest pattern:** This document's own sourced harvest-timing
  findings (Finding 3) describe one annual flowering/harvest window per
  established planting, closer to a single-season-bearing shape than a
  true multi-cut-per-season pattern — flagged as a genuine open
  question, not resolved here.
- **Climate:** Cool temperate to warm temperate, Mediterranean (USDA
  zones 5-8 for this species specifically) (Finding 5).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — dried flower; fresh flower culinary use
  not independently sourced (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Required — lavender explicitly requires pollination
  to reproduce, bumblebee-dominated pollinator community at the sites
  studied (Finding 4).
- **Yield / composition:** Oil 15-40 kg/ha, true lavender specifically,
  altitude/cultivar/climate-dependent (Finding 2) — contrast with
  lavandin's own reported 100-200 kg/ha. No per-tier breakdown was
  sourced this pass; the 15-40 kg/ha oil-yield range is reasonably well
  -agreed across 2-3 independent sources for true lavender specifically,
  in contrast with some of this project's other, wider yield-figure
  disagreements — though the underlying fresh-flower-to-oil conversion
  basis required a derived estimate rather than a directly sourced
  fresh-flower percentage (Finding 3; see Estimates and derivations).
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance high (once established, 1-2
  seasons). Waterlogging tolerance very low — drainage explicitly called
  the single most critical cultivation factor; waterlogging directly
  implicated in the crop's own dominant disease complex (Finding 5/6) —
  among the strongest-worded waterlogging-intolerance findings in this
  project's spice/herb research. pH disputed across three sources
  (6.4-8.2 / 6.5-7.5 / 7.0-9.0, roughly neutral-to-alkaline across all
  three but not reconciled to one range) (Finding 5).
- **Establishment:** Semi-hardwood cuttings (late summer/early autumn)
  are the standard commercial route; true seed a real secondary route
  (highly heterozygous, light germinator, dormancy-breaking in-vitro
  methods documented) (Finding 4). 2-4 years to reach peak yield
  (Finding 3/4) — a notably longer establishment period than this
  project's annual spice/herb crops, though comparable in general shape
  to cardamom's own multi-year establishment.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** pH disputed, roughly neutral-to-alkaline — see water block
  above. No fertility note independently sourced this pass beyond the
  drainage-criticality finding above.
- **Stand life:** Woody perennial subshrub; yield explicitly near-zero
  in years 1-2, peaking from year 3-4 (Finding 3). No specific maximum
  productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** 'Shab' (*Phoma* sp.), a weak twig-dieback
  pathogen (Finding 6). *Septoria lavandulae* leaf spot, with a sourced
  co-infection-worsens-outcome finding alongside Phoma (Finding 6).
  Phytophthora/Pythium/Fusarium/Rhizoctonia root and crown rot, directly
  connected to drainage/waterlogging (Finding 5/6) rather than an
  independent risk — the dominant disease-hazard category for this
  crop.
- **Cultivars:** No individually named true-lavender cultivar with a
  sourced trait-delta was found this pass — this document's richest
  cultivar-level finding instead concerns the species/hybrid-level
  lavender-vs-lavandin distinction (Finding 1/2), plus two specific
  hybrid cultivars with anomalous ploidy named in the 2021 cytogenetic
  study ('Heavenly Angel' 2n=100, 'Big Boy James' 2n=66, Finding 1) —
  though these are lavandin-type hybrids, not true-lavender cultivars,
  and are recorded here only because they surfaced in the same genome-
  size study.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — flowers harvested at one practical
  maturity/blossoming point per season; no distinguishable immature-vs-
  ripe harvest-stage choice sourced this pass, though oil quality is
  explicitly reported to vary by degree of blossoming and time of day
  within that single stage (Finding 3). Single window — one annual
  flowering/harvest period per established planting; no multi-flush/
  separate-cohort pattern sourced.
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced for a specific storage-days
  figure this pass, for either the fresh or dried flower product.

## Hazards

'Shab' (*Phoma* sp.), a weak twig-dieback pathogen; *Septoria
lavandulae* leaf spot, with a sourced co-infection-worsens-outcome
finding alongside Phoma; Phytophthora/Pythium/Fusarium/Rhizoctonia root
and crown rot, directly connected to drainage/waterlogging rather than
an independent risk — the dominant disease-hazard category for this
crop (Finding 6). No human- or animal-health hazard (toxin, allergen,
drug interaction) was found this pass for lavender itself.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** flowers cut with short stems (not exceeding roughly 12cm)
  at one annual flowering window → used fresh (culinary use not
  independently sourced) or dried (culinary use) → or distilled into
  essential oil, with oil quality varying by degree of blossoming, time
  of day, and atmospheric conditions at harvest.

## Open research questions

- The lavender-vs-lavandin culinary-use dispute was not resolved this
  pass — sources disagree on whether any lavandin cultivars are
  acceptable for culinary use in small quantities, or whether lavandin
  should be avoided entirely for culinary purposes because of its
  higher camphor content.
- Lavender's own position on a rosemary/thyme/sage propagation-
  reliability gradient (cuttings strongly preferred, seed a real but
  secondary route) was not independently scored against those three
  Lamiaceae herbs' own sourced germination-percentage figures this
  pass.
- The claim that commercial growers place beehives in lavender fields
  "for optimal essential oil production" was found in only one source
  this pass and not independently cross-verified.
- No specific storage-days figure was sourced for either the fresh or
  dried lavender flower product this pass.
- No specific maximum productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure
  was sourced for this perennial crop beyond the 2-4-year establishment-
  to-peak-yield finding.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any lavender
  operation.
- No seed-yield-per-hectare, isolation-distance, or seed-
  multiplication-ratio figure was sourced for lavender's own (secondary)
  seed-propagation route.
- No individually named true-lavender cultivar with a sourced trait-
  delta was found this pass.
- Lavender's light requirement was described only qualitatively ("full
  sun") this pass, with no specific daily-light-integral or hours-of-
  direct-sun figure sourced, unlike sweet basil's own sourced 6-8-hours
  figure.
- Lavender's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass, though this crop's own acute
  waterlogging sensitivity (Finding 5/6) makes it a genuinely more
  consequential open question here than for most other crops this
  project has researched.
- The 2021 cytogenetic study's genome-size range (0.76-4.80 pg 2C⁻¹)
  covers the whole genus studied, not a species-specific figure for *L.
  angustifolia* alone isolated from that range this pass.
- Lavandin (*Lavandula × intermedia*) is covered separately elsewhere in
  this project's own research, as a genuinely distinct, sterile
  interspecific hybrid with its own commercial profile — 3-7x true
  lavender's own oil yield, consistently lower oil quality by camphor
  content, contested-at-best culinary use, and primarily industrial/
  fragrance rather than premium-aromatherapy or culinary use.

## Estimates and derivations

A fresh-flower-to-dried-flower yield-chain fraction of 0.25-0.35 is
estimated: no source this pass gave a direct lavender-specific dry-
recovery percentage. The range is an analogy to other dried flower/leaf
herbs this project has researched, reasoning that flower tissue is
generally somewhat less water-dense than tender leafy herbage (e.g.
basil's own leaf, estimated at 0.10-0.20 in this project's own basil
research), so a moderately higher retained-dry-mass fraction was used.

A fresh-flower-to-oil yield-chain fraction of 0.001-0.004 is derived:
one source gives true-lavender oil yield as 15-40 kg/ha, while a
separate government-cited Indian source gives oil content as roughly
1.2-1.5% of dried floral stems specifically (not fresh flower) with an
implied yield around 14-15 kg/ha. Converting that 1.2-1.5% dried-stem-
basis figure through this document's own fresh-to-dried yield chain
(0.25-0.35 retained fraction) gives an implied fresh-flower oil fraction
of roughly 1.2-1.5% × 0.25-0.35 ≈ 0.003-0.005 (a reasoning step chaining
two partially-independent sourced figures, not a single directly sourced
fresh-flower percentage). This was widened slightly downward to
0.001-0.004 to also plausibly cover the lower end of the 15-40 kg/ha
herb-yield-implied range against a fresh-flower-mass base that no single
source stated directly.
