# Cinnamon — Ceylon (*Cinnamomum verum*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-30

## Naming and scope

This covers *Cinnamomum verum* J. Presl (synonym *C. zeylanicum* Blume),
family Lauraceae — "true cinnamon," native to and still overwhelmingly
associated with Sri Lanka (historically Ceylon) [1][2]. Cinnamon —
Cassia (*C. cassia*/*aromaticum*), covered separately elsewhere in this
project, is a genuinely distinct species — different flavor (Ceylon
mild/sweet, cassia hot/sharp), different bark structure (Ceylon a soft
many-layered hand-rolled quill; cassia one thick hard curl), and a major
food-safety-relevant coumarin difference (cassia roughly 1% coumarin by
mass vs. Ceylon roughly 0.004%, about a 250-fold difference by one
commonly repeated figure) — so the two are kept as separate crop
entries. This is the first woody tree crop this project has researched
in the spice/herb category grown for its bark rather than a fruit, seed,
or leaf.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the crop is grown as a repeatedly coppiced stool, not a
standard tree, and the harvested product is the bark of young regrowth
shoots, not mature wood.** Cinnamon is cut back on roughly a 2-year
cycle, and it is specifically the flush of straight young shoots
produced by this coppicing that is peeled for quills [3]. Stems are cut
during the rainy season specifically to make peeling easier [3]. The
first harvest comes 3-4 years after initial planting, with quality and
yield improving over subsequent cutting cycles, before yields decline
after 10-12 years by one source's figure [3][4].

**Finding 2 — yield figures vary by more than an order of magnitude,
with planting density as the clearest explanatory variable found this
pass.** One agricultural reference gives a first-crop yield (3-4 years
after planting) of 50-120 kg/ha of quills, rising to 175-250 kg/ha in
subsequent crops [3]. A plant-use database gives a lower range, 56-67
kg/ha rising to 168-224 kg/ha [5]. A university study guide's snippet
(garbled, not independently confirmed) suggests a Sri Lankan average
around 470 kg/ha/yr of dry bark [6]. A 2025-era Indian intercropping
study, using a deliberately high-density pentagonal planting method
(0.6 x 1.2 m spacing, 7,290 plants/ha), reported 632 and 979 kg/ha in
its first and second harvests respectively — several times higher than
the other figures, plausibly explained by the much higher plant
population rather than a different biological yield ceiling [7]. A 1998
FAO figure puts total harvested area at 24,000 ha in Sri Lanka (12,000
t) and 3,400 ha in the Seychelles (600 t) [3] — both giving an implied
national average in the same rough 50-250 kg/ha-equivalent range as the
other figures, not the high-density trial's figure, suggesting the
high-density result is a genuine management outlier rather than
representative of typical practice.

**Finding 3 — the hand-peeling and rolling process is the single most
labor-intensive and skill-dependent step in the whole chain, and it has
its own detailed, sourced grading system.** Peeling is done by hand with
a small knife, removing the outer bark and then peeling the inner bark
in two longitudinal strips — described by a university source as the
skilled, time-consuming step [8][9]. Peeled bark strips are shade-dried,
then rolled on a board to tighten them into their characteristic many-
layered quill shape, then dried further (shade then sun) to about 14%
moisture or below, over roughly 4-7 days [8][9]. Grading is genuinely
more elaborate than most spice crops this project has researched: one
university source gives a detailed table with specific diameter and
count-per-kilogram figures — the finest grade at a maximum 6 mm diameter
and 45 quills per kg, down to the coarsest grade at 38 mm and only 7
quills per kg [9]; other sources give conflicting overall grade-count
figures (4 main groups by one practical-development source, 13 grades
grouped into finer and coarser bands by one commercial source, 10 grades
by a third citing an academic reference) [9][10] — these conflicting
counts are kept visible rather than resolved, with the university's own
detailed table treated as the most authoritative single source opened
this pass. "Quillings" — broken pieces and splits collected from all
quill grades during preparation — are a distinct, lower-value byproduct
used mainly for oil distillation rather than sold as sticks [9][10].

**Finding 4 — bark oil and leaf oil are chemically distinct products
from the same plant, with a third, much less certain root-bark-oil
fraction.** Bark oil (distilled from bark/quillings) is dominated by
(E)-cinnamaldehyde, with cited ranges spanning roughly 50-90% depending
on source, converging more narrowly around 65-80% in a review and
specifically measured at 69.89% in a Sri Lankan GC-MS study, alongside
eugenol as a clear secondary component (14.27% in the same study) [11].
A commercial specification requires cinnamaldehyde no less than 60% and
eugenol no more than 14% for genuine Ceylon bark oil [11]. Leaf oil is
chemically the opposite profile: eugenol-dominant (70-98% by one
patent's figure, 81-84.5% by a separate study) with cinnamaldehyde
present only as a minor component (roughly 1-10%) [11] — this bark/leaf
chemical inversion is itself the finding, not merely two separately-
sourced numbers. A third fraction, root bark oil, is reported by some
sources as camphor-dominant (60%, repeated by at least two sources that
may share a common original citation) [11], but a separate Sri Lankan
rootlet-specific study instead found much lower camphor (1.0% and 8.4%
in two samples) with cinnamaldehyde as the dominant constituent instead
[11] — a genuine, unresolved chemotype-or-sampling conflict, not
modeled as its own commodity this pass.

**Finding 5 — pollination biology is more precisely characterized than
most crops this project has researched, and resolves an apparent
contradiction between "self-incompatible" and "some self-pollination
occurs."** The species shows protogynous dichogamy with two distinct
flower types, opening their female and male phases at different times
of day (one flowering in the morning, the other in the evening) [12][13].
A breeding-oriented review describes near-complete temporal separation
between the male and female phases (almost a full day apart), framing
this as ensuring outcrossing [12]. A more recent (2022) floral-biology
study instead found the picture more nuanced: flowers are structurally
adapted for cross-pollination, with a dry stigma suggesting a
sporophytic self-incompatibility mechanism, yet some pollen was observed
to hydrate during deliberate self-pollination trials, and natural
close-pollination (as opposed to true self-pollination) did set some
fruit — about 8% fruit set under hand-pollination in a closed
environment, versus about 4% under insect pollination in the same trial
[12][13]. The same study's insect-pollination treatment specifically
used ants (alongside naturally-occurring bees/wasps, flies — specifically
hoverflies — and beetles as reported pollinators) [12]. The authors' own
overall framing — "largely outcrossing but not strictly self-
incompatible" — is the most precise single characterization available
and is adopted here rather than either simpler "self-incompatible" or
"self-fertile" label [12]. A separate regional morphology study notes
that partial overlap between the two flower types' phases may itself be
a mechanism allowing some self-pollination, consistent with the 2022
study's own nuanced finding [13].

**Finding 6 — the genome is thinly characterized compared to most of
this project's tree crops, with a chromosome count sourced only from a
single handbook entry.** A standard agricultural handbook entry gives
2n=24 for *C. verum*, but this is a single handbook citation rather than
an independently verified cytogenetic study [14]. No nuclear genome
assembly for *C. verum* itself was found this pass; the closest
available data points are a chromosome-level assembly for a related
species (approximately 1.06 Gb), and a separate chromosome-level
assembly for camphor tree, neither of which should be assumed to apply
directly to *C. verum* [14]. A chloroplast genome for *C. verum* itself
is available and reasonably precise: 152,765 bp [14].

**Finding 7 — climate, soil, and pH figures carry real cross-source
conflicts, and propagation method is genuinely disputed between
sources.** Climate sources agree the species needs warm, humid, frost-
free tropical conditions, but give different rainfall figures: one
source gives 2,000-2,500 mm annually, well-distributed; another gives a
wider 1,250-2,500 mm range [15][16]. Soil pH is commonly cited at
5.5-6.5 (tolerating 5-7) for field cultivation, though one houseplant-
care source gives a notably lower 4.5-5.5 range that likely reflects
container growing conditions rather than field agronomy [15][16] — kept
as a labeled, lower-confidence figure rather than discarded. Propagation
sources disagree on the preferred method: one authoritative source lists
softwood cuttings or seed without ranking them; a separate source
instead calls seed the best method despite rating overall propagation
difficulty as "moderate," citing germination taking 20-30 days but
sometimes over 4 months, and explicitly short seed viability requiring
prompt sowing after ripening [16]. Pests are relatively well-documented
and non-exotic for a tropical tree crop (scale insects, whiteflies,
aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, plus the cinnamon butterfly
specifically favoring young foliage as larval food) [16]; the main
disease concerns are stripe canker (caused by *Phytophthora cinnamomi*),
anthracnose, various leaf spots, rusts, and root rot in poorly-drained
soil [16].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical, perennial — a coppiced woody tree/
  shrub, not an annual crop. Repeated single-harvest cycles per coppice
  cut, roughly every 2 years once the stool is established. First
  harvest at 3-4 years after planting (time to first bearing, not a
  single-cycle length, since the stool is a multi-year perennial cut
  repeatedly thereafter).
- **Climate:** Tropical, with a real cross-source rainfall-range
  conflict kept visible: 2,000-2,500mm (one source) vs. 1,250-2,500mm
  (another); frost-sensitive.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — bark is always processed (peeled,
  rolled, dried) before sale or use.
- **Pollination:** Not required for fruit set, though at a lower rate
  (~4% insect-pollinated vs. ~8% hand-pollinated in one closed-
  environment trial). Insect-pollinated (bees/wasps, flies/hoverflies,
  beetles; ants used experimentally), largely outcrossing but not
  strictly self-incompatible (Finding 5) — the crop is grown for bark,
  not seed/fruit, so pollination's practical importance here is mainly
  for propagule/seed supply rather than the harvested product itself.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 168-250 kg/ha (subsequent-crop
  range, dried quills, consistent with national-average implied
  figures). Exceptional yield 632-979 kg/ha (high-density intercropping
  trial, flagged as a management outlier, not a typical ceiling). Poor/
  first-crop yield 50-67 kg/ha (3-4 years after planting). The
  subsequent-crop range is the most representative for ordinary
  smallholder practice; the high-density trial figure is an explicit
  management/planting-density outlier, not a general achievable
  ceiling. Bark oil at roughly 65-80% cinnamaldehyde by most sources
  (69.89% in one Sri Lankan GC-MS study), leaf oil the chemical inverse
  at 70-98% eugenol (81-84.5% by one study).
- **Water use:** No formal crop-coefficient curve was found. Drought
  tolerance is estimated low, based on the crop's own sourced rainfall
  requirement (well-distributed, 1,250-2,500mm) suggesting a crop that
  is not drought-tolerant by nature, though not independently confirmed
  by a dedicated drought-stress source. Waterlogging tolerance is low —
  root rot is a named disease concern specifically in poorly-drained
  soil.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by cuttings/seed/layering/division
  (disputed which is preferred); the harvested stool is established and
  then coppiced repeatedly on a roughly 2-year cycle once mature enough
  for first harvest (3-4 years). Softwood or semi-hardwood cuttings
  (10-15 cm, from non-flowering shoots, rooted with a rooting-hormone
  treatment per one commercial source in 45-60 days), true seed (short
  viability, sow promptly), or division of old rootstocks.
- **Soil:** pH optimal ~6.0, min 5.0 (field) or 4.5 (container-growing
  figure, lower-confidence), max 7.0 — a real conflict kept visible
  between field-agronomy figures (5.5-6.5, tolerating 5-7) and a
  houseplant-care source's lower container-growing range (4.5-5.5).
- **Stand life:** Yields improve over subsequent coppice cycles after
  the first harvest (3-4 years), then decline after 10-12 years by one
  source's figure — not independently cross-checked against a second
  source this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Stripe canker (*Phytophthora cinnamomi*),
  anthracnose, leaf spots, rusts, and root rot (poorly-drained soil).
  Scale insects, whiteflies, aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, plus the
  cinnamon butterfly specifically favoring young foliage as larval food.
- **Genome:** 2n=24 per a single standard-handbook citation, not
  independently verified against a primary cytogenetic study. No
  nuclear genome assembly for *C. verum* itself found; chloroplast
  genome 152,765 bp is sourced with reasonable precision.
- **Cultivars:** No named Ceylon cinnamon cultivars were sourced this
  pass — unlike some other spice crops this project has researched with
  well-documented cultivar lineages. Sri Lanka and the Seychelles are
  the two sourced production regions, but these are geographic origins,
  not named cultivars.
- **Harvest:** Single stage per coppice cycle — the coppiced shoots are
  cut once per roughly 2-year cycle at a single maturity point; leaves
  are a secondary, separately-timed harvest from the same stools rather
  than a maturity-stage choice on the same harvest.
- **Storage:** Quills and bark oil keep for years when properly dried
  (modeled at roughly 1,460 days for quills, 1,095 days for both oils);
  the raw peeled bark and coppice shoots themselves are highly
  perishable (2-3 days) and must be processed promptly. Dried to about
  14% or below over 4-7 days of shade-then-sun drying.

## Hazards

Coumarin content is dramatically lower in Ceylon cinnamon than in
cassia (roughly 0.004% vs. roughly 1% by one commonly repeated figure —
a difference of roughly two orders of magnitude) — this is the same
dose-dependent food-safety issue that led the EU and other regulators to
set coumarin limits, and is a key reason the two species are modeled as
separate crop entries. Stripe canker (*Phytophthora cinnamomi*),
anthracnose, leaf spots, rusts, and root rot (poorly-drained soil). The
cinnamon butterfly specifically favoring young foliage as larval food —
a named pest-specificity detail, not a human/animal food-safety hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** stool coppiced on a ~2-year cycle → young shoots peeled by
  hand (two longitudinal strips) → shade-dried → rolled on a board to
  form quills → dried further (shade then sun, 4-7 days, to ~14%
  moisture) → graded by diameter/count-per-kg → sold as quills, or
  broken "quillings" byproduct distilled into bark oil. Leaves
  separately harvested and distilled into leaf oil.

## Open research questions

- Stand-life decline figure (10-12 years) sourced from only one source,
  not independently cross-checked this pass.
- Root bark oil's camphor content is a genuine, unresolved chemotype-or-
  sampling conflict: one set of sources gives 60% camphor; a separate
  rootlet-specific study found only 1-8.4% camphor with cinnamaldehyde
  dominant instead.
- No sourced labor-hour figures for the hand-peeling/rolling/drying
  chain, despite peeling being specifically named as the most skilled
  and time-consuming step.
- No named Ceylon cinnamon cultivars sourced this pass — unlike some
  other spice crops this project has researched with well-documented
  cultivar lineages.
- The weather-response archetype used for this crop is a name of
  convenience, not yet formally defined in this project's own
  conventions.
- Chromosome count (2n=24) sourced from a single handbook entry, not an
  independently verified primary cytogenetic study.
- Propagation-method preference (seed vs. cuttings) genuinely disputed
  between sources.
- Root bark oil, fruit/flower oil fractions, and traditional-medicine
  bark use were all out of scope for this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Drought tolerance (low) and waterlogging tolerance (low) are estimated
from the crop's own sourced rainfall requirement and a named root-rot
disease concern in poorly-drained soil, respectively, rather than from a
dedicated drought-stress or waterlogging study.
