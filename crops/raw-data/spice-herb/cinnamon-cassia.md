# Cinnamon — Cassia (*Cinnamomum cassia*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/cinnamon-cassia.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-31

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Cinnamomum cassia* J. Presl (a major botanical
database treats *Cinnamomum aromaticum* Nees as the accepted name for
the same plant, with *C. cassia* as a widely-used synonym in floras
covering Vietnam and China) [1][2], family Lauraceae — "Chinese
cinnamon," native to southeastern China and Vietnam [1][2]. Ceylon
cinnamon (*Cinnamomum verum*), a separate crop this project has also
researched, is a direct sibling: same genus, same category, but kept as
a separate entry given a different species, markedly different flavor
and bark structure, and (Finding 4) a major coumarin food-safety-dose
difference.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — cassia's harvest-method sourcing is genuinely more
conflicted than Ceylon's, with three incompatible pictures of what
happens to the tree.** A Guangxi agronomic study states cassia "can be
barked after growing 8-10 years, but the optimal time is during 10-15
years" [3], and a separate 2023 study instead gives a notably younger
window, bark "often harvested from 5-8-year-old trees" [4]. Separately,
a popular account (on Sumatran cassia) describes branches being chopped
off and whole trees felled at harvest, with new trees regenerating from
the root stump for a few subsequent generations [5] — a fundamentally
different practice from Ceylon's repeated coppice-and-regrow cycle on a
surviving stool. A commercial supplier page instead describes the tree
surviving harvest, with bark beginning to be peeled at 8-10 years old
[6]. None of these accounts agree on whether the same tree is barked
repeatedly over its life (Ceylon's model), barked once and felled (the
Sumatra account), or something else; this is recorded as an open,
unresolved conflict rather than resolved by picking one source.

**Finding 2 — bark yield figures come only from Indian trial plots
growing cassia outside its native range, and are per-plant/per-
accession, not a general per-hectare figure.** An evaluation of ten
cassia accessions in Kerala found two high-yielding accessions reaching
roughly 750 kg dry bark/ha, with per-plant dry weights ranging 82.5-475
g across the ten accessions [7]. A separate trial of fifteen cassia
genotypes in Karnataka, assessed seven years after planting, found one
genotype the highest yielder at 1,083 g dry bark per plant [8]. Both
trials are Indian introduction/breeding-selection plots, not reports
from cassia's native Chinese/Vietnamese production regions, so neither
figure should be read as a typical commercial yield for the crop's main
production areas; no Guangxi- or Vietnam-specific per-hectare bark yield
was found this pass.

**Finding 3 — bark oil cinnamaldehyde content and total oil yield both
vary substantially by growing region within Vietnam, with no single
figure dominant.** A three-region Vietnamese GC-MS comparison found bark
essential-oil yields of 2.6% (Quang Ngai, the highest), 1.5% (Kon Tum),
and 1.3% (Yen Bai, the lowest) [9], while trans-cinnamaldehyde content
ran the opposite way in relative ranking, 96.07% (Kon Tum), 94.72% (Yen
Bai), and 94.06% (Quang Ngai) [9] — the region with the lowest oil yield
had among the highest cinnamaldehyde purity, a genuine yield/quality
tradeoff pattern across sourcing regions rather than a single consistent
"best" region. A separate general review gives a wider 65-80%
cinnamaldehyde range for cassia bark oil generally [10], and a different
bark-twig GC-MS study (location not specified) found 88.05% [11] — all
broadly consistent with cassia's bark oil running hotter in
cinnamaldehyde than Ceylon's own sourced figures, though the ranges
genuinely overlap rather than being cleanly separated.

**Finding 4 — coumarin content is cassia's defining food-safety fact
relative to Ceylon, but the sourced figures disagree by several orders
of magnitude depending on units, sample type, and measurement method.**
A WHO-monograph-citing review gives cassia bark up to about 1% coumarin
by mass, against Ceylon's roughly 0.004% trace level — a roughly
250-fold difference [12]. A Serbian retail-market study measured non-
Ceylon ("cassia-type") barks at 1.38-5.80 mg/g (roughly 0.14-0.58% by
mass), against Ceylon samples at only 0.08-0.15 mg/g [13]. An Indian
accession-comparison study instead found much lower absolute cassia bark
levels, 27.2-222.2 mg/kg across accessions (roughly 0.003-0.022%), with
cassia bark averaging about 111.4 mg/kg — the same study frames this as
"approximately 11 times higher" than *C. verum*, a far smaller
multiplier than the ~250-fold WHO figure [14]. A German market study of
retail powders found cassia powder containing on average up to 63 times
more coumarin than Ceylon powder, with large variation even within bark
from a single tree [15]. The same Indian accession study also found leaf
coumarin running significantly higher than bark coumarin across all
species tested, including cassia — a genuinely unexpected organ-level
finding [14]. This full spread (11x to 250x+, across four independently
sourced studies using different units, markets, and methods) is the
clearest illustration this project has found of why coumarin dose
figures resist a single authoritative number.

**Finding 5 — cassia has a product that Ceylon does not: dried immature
fruits sold as "cassia buds" (Flos Cinnamomi), with their own distinct
volatile chemistry.** Cassia buds are harvested as the plant's immature
fruit, resembling cloves in shape, with a scent and taste close to the
bark [16]. An older pharmacognosy source puts their volatile-oil content
at roughly 1.6%, dominated by cinnamic aldehyde [16]; a 2018 GC-MS study
identified 36 compounds in the buds' volatiles, with cinnamaldehyde the
most abundant at concentrations up to 40 mg/g sample — the same study's
authors note that little was previously known about the buds' volatile
profile before that work [17]. Buds are used as a spice in pickling,
marinades, and teas [16]. This is a genuinely distinct harvested organ
and product (immature fruit, not bark or leaf) with no Ceylon equivalent.

**Finding 6 — pollination biology is far less precisely characterized
for cassia than Ceylon's own detailed flower-timing account, and one
source directly conflicts with the genus-level dichogamy finding.** A
genus-level review states that *Cinnamomum*'s synchronous dichogamy
"makes controlled pollinations difficult," so most elite breeding
material to date derives from open-pollinated progeny of selected plants
rather than controlled crosses [18] — consistent with Ceylon's own
protogynous-dichogamy finding, though phrased at the genus rather than
species level. A separate cinnamon-genus study (species unspecified)
reports that protogynous dichogamy drives cross-pollination and
contributes to morphological variation in progeny [19]. Against this, a
commercial plant-vendor growing guide flatly states *C. cassia* is
"Self-Fertile: Yes" [20] — directly contradicting the dichogamy-driven
outcrossing picture from the more credible sources, and kept visible as
an unresolved, lower-confidence conflict rather than discarded.

**Finding 7 — propagation sourcing for cassia specifically favors
vegetative methods even more strongly than Ceylon's own disputed seed-
vs-cuttings picture.** A general cinnamon-cultivation source states that
cinnamon trees (including cassia) are usually propagated commercially by
cuttings or air-layering specifically because seed-grown trees show wide
genetic variability [21] — a clearer, single-direction recommendation
than Ceylon's own genuinely disputed seed-vs-cuttings sourcing. A
separate gardening source similarly reports seed germination as "slow
and inconsistent" compared to vegetative methods for cassia specifically
[22], and a Vietnamese agricultural program source notes that
developing a reliable cutting-propagation technique for *C. cassia* was
itself an active, not-yet-routine research objective in its own country
context [23] — suggesting vegetative propagation, while preferred in
principle, may not yet be fully standardized in all production regions
either.

**Finding 8 — commercial grading in cassia's main Chinese production
regions follows a national standard organized around two physical
product forms rather than Ceylon's diameter/count-based quill grading.**
A Chinese national standard, "Quality grades for products of cassia,"
drafted with input from the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region and
Guangdong Academies of Forestry Sciences, defines two principal
commercial forms: a flat, "plate-shaped" product made from bark stripped
from the trunk (with or without the outer skin retained), termed Bangui,
and a "roll-shaped" product (Guitong) peeled from the main trunk or
large branches, structurally closer to Ceylon's rolled quills [24]. The
standard also covers offcuts and twig material [24]. This document does
not have access to the standard's full grading-tier text (diameter/
thickness cutoffs), only its top-level product-form distinction — Ceylon's
own detailed diameter/count grading table should not be assumed to
transfer to cassia, since that table is specifically Sri Lankan and tied
to the hand-rolled quill form.

**Finding 9 — climate and genome data both show cassia tolerating a
notably wider range than the figures sourced for Ceylon, though possibly
reflecting different source types rather than a genuinely wider
biological tolerance.** Ecocrop-derived figures give cassia an annual
daytime temperature preference of 15-32°C but tolerance to 10-38°C, and
a mean annual rainfall preference of 1,100-1,500mm but tolerance to
900-3,000mm [25] — both ranges wider than the single-point rainfall
figures sourced for Ceylon. No soil pH figure was found for cassia
specifically this pass, unlike Ceylon's sourced figure. On genome size,
only the chloroplast genome was found (152,675 bp, closely matching
Ceylon's own chloroplast figure of 152,765 bp — unsurprising for
congeneric species) [26]; no nuclear genome size or chromosome count
specific to *C. cassia* was found this pass at all, a genuine data gap
deeper than Ceylon's own single-handbook-sourced figure. Search results
returning the unrelated legume genus *Cassia* (Fabaceae, sharing the
English common-name collision) rather than *Cinnamomum cassia* were
deliberately excluded from this finding.

**Finding 10 — pest and disease documentation is sparser and less
cassia-specific than Ceylon's own relatively well-documented pest/
disease list.** A dedicated pests-and-diseases book chapter explicitly
states that "very little authentic information is available on the
insect pests of cassia cinnamons," contrasting this with cinnamon (*C.
verum*), reported to be infested by over 70 insect-pest species,
especially in India and Sri Lanka [27]. The same source notes disease
reports for cassia are similarly sparse, though many fungi have been
recorded on dried, stored cinnamon bark generally [27]. A general
cinnamon-pathogen source (not cassia-specific) lists leaf spot and
dieback caused by *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* (controllable with 1%
Bordeaux mixture), the cinnamon butterfly (also named for Ceylon) and
mites [28] — these are recorded as genus-level findings applied to
cassia by analogy, not independently confirmed cassia-specific
observations.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical/subtropical, perennial woody tree;
  Ecocrop-derived range extends into the subtropics more clearly than
  Ceylon's tropical-only sourcing.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical — Ecocrop-derived: 15-32°C
  preferred, tolerating 10-38°C; 1,100-1,500mm rainfall preferred,
  tolerating 900-3,000mm (Finding 9) — wider-tolerance range than
  Ceylon's tropical-only sourcing.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — bark, buds, and twigs are always
  processed (stripped/peeled, dried, or distilled) before sale or use.
- **Pollination:** Not required for the bark/buds/leaf harvest — fruit
  set occurs without controlled pollination; most breeding material
  derives from open pollination of selected plants rather than hand
  crosses. Likely insect-pollinated (genus-level dichogamy-driven
  outcrossing; one conflicting vendor source instead claims full self-
  fertility) (Finding 6).
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield ~750 kg/ha dry bark (Indian trial,
  best two accessions, from a trial outside the crop's native range, not
  a native-region commercial average) (Finding 2). Only per-plant
  figures exist for the exceptional tier (1,083 g dry bark/plant, best
  genotype, from a trial seven years after planting). Bark oil yield
  itself varies by Vietnamese growing region: 2.6% (Quang Ngai) to 1.3%
  (Yen Bai), with cinnamaldehyde purity running in the opposite
  direction across the same three regions (94-96%) (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-style crop-coefficient curve was found.
  Drought tolerance is estimated low-to-medium: Ecocrop's wider
  rainfall-tolerance range (900-3,000mm) than Ceylon's sourced figures
  suggests somewhat greater drought tolerance than Ceylon's own low
  rating, but this is not independently confirmed by a dedicated
  drought-stress source for cassia, and the comparison is confounded by
  different source methodologies. Waterlogging tolerance was not sourced
  this pass.
- **Establishment:** Propagated predominantly by cuttings or air-
  layering in commercial practice, specifically to avoid the genetic
  variability of seed-grown trees (Finding 7). First bark harvest occurs
  much later than Ceylon's coppice-stool model (5-8 to 10-15 years by
  conflicting sources) and the underlying harvest method (repeated
  coppicing vs. one-time felling vs. partial bark-strip-and-heal) is
  itself unresolved (Finding 1).
- **Soil:** No pH figure was found for cassia specifically this pass — a
  genuine gap deeper than Ceylon's own sourced range.
- **Stand life:** Not independently sourced this pass for cassia
  specifically — entirely dependent on which harvest-method account
  (Finding 1) is correct: a repeatedly-barked standing tree (Ceylon-like
  stand life) versus a felled tree regenerating from the root stump for
  "a few generations" (a fundamentally different, multi-generation
  stand-life model) versus a bark-strip-and-heal cycle reported to take
  about three years per section in one Indian grower account.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Thinner and less cassia-specific than
  Ceylon's own sourced list. A dedicated pests-and-diseases source
  explicitly notes very little authentic information exists on cassia-
  specific insect pests. Leaf spot/dieback, the cinnamon butterfly, and
  mites are sourced generically for cinnamon and applied to cassia by
  genus-level analogy, not independently confirmed.
- **Genome:** Only the chloroplast genome was found (152,675 bp, closely
  matching Ceylon's own chloroplast figure); no nuclear genome size or
  chromosome count specific to *C. cassia* was found this pass at all
  (Finding 9).
- **Cultivars:** No confirmed named, released cassia cultivar was
  sourced this pass. One research program identified "four elite cassia
  types" from a germplasm survey of 26 trees at a private estate, and
  separately evaluated ten numbered accessions and fifteen numbered
  genotypes in formal yield trials [7][8] — but none of these sources
  confirmed a formal cultivar release with a public name, unlike named
  Ceylon-cinnamon releases found during this same search pass but for
  the other species. Recorded as numbered accessions/genotypes rather
  than named cultivars.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for bark/buds/leaf-twig material
  respectively — bark, buds, and leaf/twig material are each harvested
  from the plant as distinct, separately-timed organs, the same general
  pattern as Ceylon's bark/leaf split but with a third organ (buds)
  added (Finding 5). Harvest pattern is set conservatively given the
  unresolved harvest-method conflict (Finding 1).

## Hazards

- **Coumarin**: cassia's defining food-safety fact relative to Ceylon —
  sourced figures disagree by several orders of magnitude (11x to 250x+
  higher than Ceylon, depending on source, units, and method) (Finding
  4).
- **Leaf spot and dieback** (*Colletotrichum gloeosporioides*), the
  cinnamon butterfly, and mites — sourced generically for cinnamon,
  applied to cassia by genus-level analogy rather than independently
  confirmed (Finding 10).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (bark):** trunk/branch bark → stripped/peeled (method
  disputed, Finding 1) → dried → cassia bark (Bangui plate or Guitong
  roll forms, per Chinese national grading standard, Finding 8) or
  distilled into bark oil. **Chain (buds):** immature fruit → harvested
  → dried → cassia buds (spice, used in pickling, marinades, and teas,
  Finding 5). **Chain (leaf/twig):** leaf/twig material → distilled into
  leaf oil.

## Open research questions

- Cassia's actual harvest method is unresolved between three
  incompatible accounts: repeated coppicing of a surviving stool,
  one-time felling of the whole tree with multi-generation stump
  regrowth, or partial bark-stripping with a ~3-year healing cycle
  before the same tree is harvested again. This is the single most
  consequential open question, since it affects harvest pattern, growth-
  cycle length, stand life, and the entire labor-operations chain.
- No per-hectare bark yield figure from cassia's actual native
  production regions (China, Vietnam) was found — only Indian
  introduction/breeding trials outside the native range.
- Pollination self-fertility claim genuinely conflicts between sources
  — a commercial vendor's "Self-Fertile: Yes" claim directly contradicts
  the genus-level dichogamy-driven-outcrossing picture from more
  credible sources.
- A Chinese national grading standard's full grading-tier text
  (diameter/thickness cutoffs for the two product forms) was not opened
  this pass, only its top-level two-form product distinction.
- No soil pH figure of any kind was found for cassia specifically — a
  genuine gap deeper than Ceylon's own sourced range.
- No nuclear genome size or chromosome count specific to *Cinnamomum
  cassia* was found this pass, only the chloroplast genome.
- Search-result contamination risk: the English common-name/scientific-
  name collision between *Cinnamomum cassia* (this crop) and the
  unrelated legume genus *Cassia* (Fabaceae) produced several irrelevant
  search hits this pass.
- Pest and disease findings are sourced generically for "cinnamon"
  (likely meaning *C. verum*) and applied to cassia only by genus-level
  analogy, not independently confirmed for *C. cassia* specifically.
- Storage/shelf-life figures are borrowed from Ceylon by analogy, with
  no cassia-specific figure found.
- Stand life is entirely dependent on the harvest-method question above
  being resolved.
- No cassia-specific labor-hour figures were found for any operation,
  and the correct labor chain to research depends on the harvest-method
  conflict's resolution.
- No confirmed, formally released named cassia cultivar was found, only
  numbered accessions/genotypes from Indian breeding trials and an
  unreleased "four elite types" survey finding.
- No cassia-specific frost-sensitivity dose-response threshold was
  sourced, despite Ecocrop's wider temperature-tolerance figures
  suggesting possibly greater cold tolerance than Ceylon.
- Ceylon's sourced soil pathogen (*Phytophthora cinnamomi*, stripe
  canker) was not independently re-confirmed for cassia this pass and
  should not be assumed to transfer without a cassia-specific source,
  despite the shared genus.

## Estimates and derivations

Drought tolerance is estimated at low-to-medium by comparing cassia's
Ecocrop-derived rainfall tolerance range (900-3,000mm, preferred
1,100-1,500mm) [25] against Ceylon's own sourced rainfall figures
(2,000-2,500mm or 1,250-2,500mm, no tolerance range given); cassia's
sourced tolerance range extends to a notably lower rainfall floor than
any figure sourced for Ceylon, suggesting somewhat greater drought
tolerance, though this comparison is confounded since the Ceylon figures
are preferred-range figures from agronomic sources while the cassia
figures are an Ecocrop tolerance range — a methodologically different
kind of figure. Storage-life figures (1,460 days bark / 1,095 days oils)
are carried over by direct analogy to Ceylon's own sourced storage-
duration figures, since no cassia-specific shelf-life figure was found
this pass beyond the qualitative observation that fungi are recorded on
dried, stored cinnamon bark generally; cassia bark is reported as
structurally thicker/denser than Ceylon's thin many-layered quill, which
could plausibly extend or shorten shelf life in either direction — not
confirmed.
