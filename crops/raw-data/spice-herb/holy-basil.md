# Holy Basil / Tulsi (*Ocimum tenuiflorum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/holy-basil.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers *Ocimum tenuiflorum* L. (synonym *O. sanctum* L., the older
and still widely used name) — a major botanical database currently
accepts *O. tenuiflorum* as the valid name with *O. sanctum* as a
synonym [1]. "Krishna tulsi" (purple-leaved) and "Rama tulsi" (green-
leaved) are treated in this document as named subtypes/cultivars of this
one species, not separate species — this is supported by a 2015 genome
paper that specifically sequenced and compared transcriptomes of the
Krishna and Rama "subtypes" of *O. tenuiflorum*, treating them as
subtype-level variation within one sequenced species, not as two taxa
[2]. This document does not cover sweet basil (*Ocimum basilicum*,
covered separately in this project's own basil research) as a close
sibling in the sense of sharing fields/estimates — the two are genuinely
distinct species with different primary uses, different dominant
essential-oil chemistry, and (per this project's basil research) a
documented disease-resistance delta between them — but this document
does compare directly against that research throughout, since it is the
closest already-researched crop in this project by a wide margin.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — holy basil's taxonomic status is comparatively well-
resolved relative to some other species-identity questions this project
has encountered (e.g. black cardamom's own species-conflation risk), and
the Krishna/Rama "species-or-subtype" question is answerable from a
single strong source.** A major botanical database lists *Ocimum
tenuiflorum* L. as the currently accepted name, with *Ocimum sanctum* L.
(1767) as a synonym, alongside several further synonyms [1]. A 2015
genome-sequencing paper specifically generated a draft genome of the
"Krishna" subtype (612 Mb estimated genome size, ~89% of essential genes
captured in the assembly) and separately sequenced transcriptomes of
both the Krishna and Rama subtypes for comparison — treating them
throughout as subtype-level variation within the one sequenced species,
not as two separate taxa [2]. This document treats that paper's own
usage as the more authoritative resolution of this question, though
neither source directly states in so many words "Krishna and Rama are
not separate species" — this is an inference from how the genome
paper's own methodology treats them, not a taxonomic ruling quoted
verbatim.

**Finding 2 — holy basil's yield figures disagree by roughly an order
of magnitude across sources, a genuinely worse disagreement than this
project found for sweet basil's own yield-figure spread, and the
growing-condition guidance itself is comparatively thin for such a
widely cultivated crop.** One source reports herb yield of 200-250
quintals/ha (20,000-25,000 kg/ha) with oil yield 100-125 kg/ha [3]. A
second source instead gives average leaf yield of 9,000-14,000 kg/ha
with essential oil around 10-25 kg/ha — roughly half the herb yield and
a fifth of the oil yield of the first source [3]. A third source states
irrigated holy basil gives herbage yield up to 20,000 kg/ha and oil
yield up to 40 kg/ha — a third, again-different pairing [3]. This
document does not average these figures away. Growing conditions found
this pass are comparatively thin: ideal temperature 22-28°C with 75-80%
humidity, full sun, well-drained soil (explicitly not clayey or
waterlogged ground) [3], with one source's soil pH range (4.3-8.2)
considerably wider than sweet basil's own already-disputed range
[3][4] — not independently cross-checked against a second source this
pass.

**Finding 3 — holy basil's harvest timing and method are reasonably
well-sourced and agree closely across sources, in sharp contrast to
Finding 2's yield disagreement — and a sourced shade-vs-sun cultivation
tradeoff directly parallels sweet basil's own general full-sun
requirement while adding a genuinely new, chemistry-level finding.**
First harvest is reported at roughly 3 months after planting, with later
harvests following at a consistent 70-75-day interval across sources
[3] — notably more consistent than sweet basil's own disputed 60-95-day
first-harvest range. Harvest method calls for cutting at full bloom,
leaving at least 15cm of stubble above ground level to support
regrowth, with sunny-day cutting specifically preferred for better yield
and oil content [3]. A Kerala study separately found that cutting height
itself matters: harvesting at 20cm or 30cm from ground level gave
higher biomass and essential-oil yield than other heights tested [3]. A
shade trial found 50% shade superior to open/full-sun conditions for
fresh biomass and oil yield — but open-grown plants had higher eugenol
content specifically [3] — a genuine, sourced cultivation-practice
effect on essential-oil chemistry (not just yield quantity), a finding
type this project has not previously found for any spice/herb crop.
Reported pests/disorders include leaf rollers, powdery mildew, root rot,
and seedling blight [3] — not independently cross-referenced against
this project's own sweet basil downy-mildew/Fusarium-wilt findings this
pass, beyond the specific disease-resistance connection in Finding 6
below.

**Finding 4 — holy basil's essential-oil chemistry is, like sweet
basil's own chemotype finding, organized around named chemotypes rather
than simple cultivar-level taste variation, but the dominant compound
and its disputed proportions are genuinely different from sweet basil's
own linalool/methyl-chavicol split.** One general reference describes
the oil as mostly eugenol (~70%), with beta-elemene and beta-
caryophyllene as the next main constituents [5]. A study of Indian-grown
material instead found methyl eugenol dominant at 82.9%, with germacrene
D a distant second at 5.1%, and the authors attributed the difference
from separately-reported Pakistani and Australian material to chemotype
or edaphic (soil) factors rather than simple measurement error [5] — a
genuinely sourced explanation for cross-study disagreement, not merely
an unreconciled conflict. A dedicated chemotype study of seven distinct
chemotypes, each rich in either eugenol or methyleugenol, additionally
found one chemotype with reduced methyleugenol accumulation tied to a
specifically truncated gene — a genetic-mechanism-level explanation for
chemotype variation, directly paralleling sweet basil's own enzyme-
specificity finding, though via a different specific gene/mechanism
[5]. This document records the eugenol-vs-methyl-eugenol dominance
question as genuinely chemotype-dependent rather than picking one
figure.

**Finding 5 — holy basil's chromosome number is, like sweet basil's own
ploidy finding, genuinely disputed across the cytological literature,
but the genome size is comparatively well-anchored by a real sequenced
draft genome — directly paralleling the shape of sweet basil's own
genome finding, right down to which measurement type is more
reliable.** The chromosome number of *O. tenuiflorum* is most commonly
observed as 2n=36 per the 2015 genome paper, though the same paper notes
individuals with 2n=32, 2n=36, and 2n=76 have all been separately
recorded [2] — a within-species disagreement structurally similar to
sweet basil's own purple-leaf-type ploidy anomaly. More broadly, *Ocimum*
species as a genus are noted to show different base chromosome numbers
(x=8, 10, 12, or 16) [2] — directly paralleling this project's own
finding that different authors use different base-number conventions
for sweet basil. Genome size, by contrast, is comparatively well-
anchored: the 2015 draft genome of the Krishna subtype gives an
estimated genome size of 612 Mb [2] — a real sequenced figure, though
notably much smaller than sweet basil's own sequenced 2.13 Gbp reference
genome [2][6] (not an inconsistency to resolve, since the two are
different species with presumably different underlying genome sizes,
but a concrete point of quantitative difference worth recording). The
genome paper's authors specifically proposed that small amino-acid
changes at functional sites of metabolite-synthesis-pathway genes may
account for the herb's reported medicinal properties [2] — a genuine,
sourced genetic hypothesis for why this crop is medicinally notable, not
just a description that it is.

**Finding 6 — holy basil carries a specific, sourced disease-resistance
finding that connects directly back to sweet basil's own disease-
susceptibility finding, strengthening both findings independently rather
than merely cross-referencing by assertion.** This project's own sweet
basil research cited a 2015 downy-mildew resistance-screening study
finding that most *O. basilicum* (sweet basil) entries were highly
susceptible to *Peronospora belbahrii*, while most entries of several
wild relative species — including *O. tenuiflorum* specifically — were
highly resistant, with the study's authors concluding that interspecific
crossing with these wild relatives is a feasible breeding path for sweet
basil [7]. This document independently confirms that same citation
applies to *O. tenuiflorum* by name, directly supporting the species-
level distinctiveness between the two basil species (different disease
profile, not just different flavor and use). This pass did not find an
independent, holy-basil-specific disease-resistance-breeding program
analogous to sweet basil's own named resistant cultivars — holy basil's
own pest/disease list (Finding 3: leaf rollers, powdery mildew, root
rot, seedling blight) does not include downy mildew or Fusarium wilt by
name in the sources opened this pass, consistent with the resistance
finding above but not independently re-confirmed by a holy-basil-
specific disease survey.

**Finding 7 — holy basil's propagation reliability is explicitly
reported as a genuine, named production bottleneck across all three
available propagation routes, a notably more pessimistic framing than
sweet basil's own single disputed isolation-distance figure.** A 2025
review identifies low seed-germination uniformity, limited rooting
efficiency in stem cuttings, and high tissue-culture cost as the three
named bottlenecks, one per propagation route, without giving a direct
yield or cost comparison between them [8]. Seed germination specifically
depends on temperature, viability, and dormancy status, and gibberellic
acid (GA3) pre-treatment (0.1% solution, 24-hour soak, under 20-30°C
alternating temperatures) is reported by the same review to raise
germination above 90% — a specific, actionable dormancy-breaking
treatment this document did not find an equivalent for in sweet basil's
own propagation findings [8]. A separate seed-technology study
recommends testing holy basil germination specifically at 30±5°C,
evaluated 5-6 days after sowing [8]. This document did not find a
seeding-rate, isolation-distance, or pollination-biology figure for holy
basil this pass — a genuine gap relative to sweet basil's own sourced
(if disputed) insect-pollination/cross-pollination/isolation-distance
finding.

**Finding 8 — holy basil carries a rich, genuinely production-relevant
religious-use finding sourced in far more concrete, calendar-specific
detail than this project found ("none found") for sweet basil.** Hindus
revere the plant as a manifestation of the goddess Lakshmi (as Tulsi),
principal consort of Vishnu; daily worship includes offering a garland
of Tulsi leaves, and many households maintain a dedicated courtyard
structure (tulsi vrindavan) for a living plant [9]. Tulsi Vivah — a
ritual marriage between the Tulsi plant and a Saligram stone
representing Vishnu or his avatar Krishna (both framings used across
sources) — is performed between Prabodhini Ekadashi and Kartik Purnima,
marking the end of Chaturmas and the start of the Hindu wedding season
[9]. Dead plants' woody stems are reported to be made into prayer beads
(japa mala) [9]. Separately, and more concretely production-rule-
relevant, plucking/harvest restrictions are widely reported: several
sources list Sunday, Ekadashi tithi itself, Dwadashi, solar/lunar
eclipses, and the period after sunset as times when Tulsi leaves should
not be plucked, with devotees commonly picking leaves the day before
Ekadashi and storing them for that day's offering instead [10]. This
restriction is explicitly sect- and region-varying: one Gaudiya
Vaishnava forum source states the no-plucking rule applies only on
Dwadashi (the day after Ekadashi), while a separate commenter reports
that North India specifically has no Sunday restriction but does
observe the Dwadashi restriction [10] — this document records both
variants rather than picking one as authoritative. The reasons given for
the Sunday restriction specifically are explicitly folk/devotional
rather than scriptural in the sources opened this pass (one account
holds that the goddess Tulsi fasts for Vishnu on Sundays, so offering
her water would break her fast) [10] — labeled as such, not presented as
settled doctrine.

**Finding 9 — holy basil carries its own, independently sourced drug-
interaction safety profile distinct from sweet basil's (which had none
found), but the evidence base for both of holy basil's own flagged
interactions is explicitly weaker than some comparable precedents this
project has encountered for other crops' food/herb-and-warfarin
interactions.** Consumer drug-reference sources report that holy basil
may slow blood clotting, creating a theoretical bruising/bleeding-risk
concern with anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel,
enoxaparin, heparin, warfarin named specifically), rated "moderate" by
one reference with an explicit caveat that there isn't enough
information to know how large a concern it really is; the same source
recommends stopping holy basil at least 2 weeks before scheduled surgery
[11]. Separately, holy basil is reported to lower blood glucose,
creating a theoretical hypoglycemia risk when combined with insulin or
sulfonylurea-class diabetes medications; the best human evidence found
this pass is a single small controlled crossover study (40 patients)
showing improved fasting and postprandial glucose, not a large trial
[11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial (tropical/subtropical) — a genuine
  point of difference from sweet basil's own frost-tender true-annual
  habit. Described as an aromatic perennial shrub native to India
  (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again — repeated cutting every
  70-75 days after an initial ~3-month establishment (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, warm temperate — 22-28°C ideal,
  75-80% humidity, explicitly susceptible to frost/cold-temperature
  injury like sweet basil (Finding 2).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh leaf eaten in some traditional
  preparations and used fresh in ritual offering (Finding 3/8).
- **Pollination:** Not independently sourced this pass — a genuine gap
  relative to sweet basil's own sourced insect-pollination/cross-
  pollination finding.
- **Yield / composition:** Disputed across three sources by roughly an
  order of magnitude (Finding 2): herb 9,000-25,000 kg/ha fresh or
  20,000-25,000 kg/ha (quintal-based figure, basis unclear); oil 10-125
  kg/ha, three sources giving three different pairings, not reconciled.
  Cutting height (20cm vs. 30cm from ground) and shade level (50% shade
  vs. open) both independently affect yield and, for shade specifically,
  oil chemistry — a genuinely richer cultivation-practice-to-outcome
  finding than sweet basil's own yield data offered (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass. Waterlogging
  tolerance is low — explicitly unsuitable for clayey or waterlogged
  areas (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** First harvest ready ~3 months after planting
  (Finding 3); three named propagation routes each with their own
  reported bottleneck (Finding 7): direct seed (dormancy-breaking GA3
  pre-treatment available), stem cuttings (limited rooting efficiency
  reported), or tissue culture (high cost reported). Direct seed is
  described as "one of the simplest and most widely practised methods."
  GA3 0.1%, 24h soak, 20-30°C alternating temperatures raises
  germination above 90%; germination testing is recommended at 30±5°C,
  evaluated 5-6 days after sowing.
- **Soil:** pH 4.3-8.2 (one source) — notably wider than sweet basil's
  own already-disputed range, not independently cross-checked against a
  second source this pass.
- **Stand life:** Perennial shrub — a genuine structural contrast with
  sweet basil's own true-annual habit. No specific productive-lifespan
  or replanting-cycle figure was sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Leaf rollers, powdery mildew, root rot,
  seedling blight (Finding 3). A sourced disease-resistance finding
  (downy mildew, Finding 6) rather than a susceptibility — genuinely the
  opposite framing from most hazard entries this project has recorded.
- **Genome:** 612 Mb estimated (2015 draft genome, Krishna subtype) — a
  real sequenced figure, notably smaller than sweet basil's own
  sequenced 2.13 Gbp reference genome (different species, not an
  inconsistency) (Finding 5). Chromosome number genuinely disputed
  (2n=36 most common, but 2n=32/36/76 all separately recorded; genus-
  wide base numbers of x=8/10/12/16 reported), directly paralleling the
  shape of sweet basil's own ploidy dispute.
- **Cultivars:** 'Krishna' (purple-leaved) and 'Rama' (green-leaved) are
  the two named subtypes with the richest sourcing this pass —
  specifically, both had their transcriptomes independently sequenced
  and compared in the 2015 genome paper (Finding 1/5). This document
  treats them as subtype/cultivar-level variation within one species.
  No quantified trait-delta (yield, chemistry, disease-resistance)
  between Krishna and Rama specifically was sourced this pass beyond
  their both being sequenced.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — leaf/flowering-top harvest at full bloom,
  no distinguishable immature-vs-ripe harvest-stage choice sourced this
  pass. Single continuous window from an established perennial planting
  (contrast with sweet basil's own single-season annual window), no
  multi-flush/separate-cohort pattern sourced.

## Hazards

Leaf rollers, powdery mildew, root rot, seedling blight (agronomic
pests/diseases, Finding 3). A sourced disease-resistance finding (downy
mildew, Finding 6) rather than a susceptibility.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — two human-health drug-interaction concerns, both
weakly evidenced, are deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data: (a) an anticoagulant/antiplatelet bleeding-
risk concern, and (b) a glucose-lowering/hypoglycemia concern with
diabetes medications (Finding 9).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** herb grown out from establishment (~3 months) → repeat-cut
  at full bloom, every 70-75 days, leaving 15cm stubble → used fresh, or
  dried, or distilled into essential oil (chemistry varies by chemotype
  and by shade/sun growing conditions).

## Open research questions

- The Krishna/Rama "subtype vs. species" resolution is an inference
  from how a 2015 genome paper's methodology treats the two subtypes
  (sequencing both transcriptomes for comparison within one species),
  not a taxonomic ruling quoted verbatim from any source opened this
  pass. No quantified trait-delta (yield, chemistry, disease-resistance)
  between the two named subtypes was sourced beyond their both being
  sequenced.
- The one sourced soil-pH range (4.3-8.2) was not independently cross-
  checked against a second source this pass.
- Holy basil's own pest/disease list was not independently cross-
  referenced against sweet basil's own downy-mildew/Fusarium-wilt
  disorder list this pass, beyond the specific disease-resistance
  connection.
- No holy-basil-specific disease-resistance-breeding program (analogous
  to sweet basil's own named resistant cultivars) was found this pass —
  the resistance finding comes from a screening study using holy basil
  as a wild-relative breeding source for sweet basil, not from a holy-
  basil-focused breeding program in its own right.
- No seeding-rate, isolation-distance, or pollination-biology figure was
  found for holy basil this pass.
- No specific postharvest storage-days figure was sourced for fresh
  holy basil leaf.
- No bolting-trigger finding analogous to sweet basil's own multi-
  trigger profile was sourced — holy basil's perennial habit may make
  "bolting" a less applicable concept, but this is an inference, not
  independently sourced or ruled out.
- No specific productive-lifespan or replanting-cycle figure was sourced
  for this perennial crop.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any holy basil
  operation.
- Holy basil's controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not
  independently assessed this pass.
- Frost/cold-temperature susceptibility was noted but not quantified
  with a specific threshold the way sweet basil's own chilling-injury
  figures were.

## Estimates and derivations

A fresh-to-dried-leaf yield-chain fraction of 0.10-0.20 is estimated by
direct analogy to sweet basil's own fresh-to-dried-leaf yield chain,
reasoning that both crops are similarly tender leafy herbage with a
similar expected moisture-loss fraction on drying; no source this pass
gave a direct holy-basil-specific dry-recovery percentage.

A fresh-leaf-to-oil yield-chain fraction of 0.001-0.006 is derived from
reconciling three partially-independent sources that give three
different herb-yield/oil-yield pairings that do not agree: (a)
20,000-25,000 kg/ha herb (basis ambiguous between fresh and dried) with
100-125 kg/ha oil, implying ~0.004-0.006; (b) 9,000-14,000 kg/ha fresh
leaf with 10-25 kg/ha oil, implying ~0.001-0.003; (c) up to 20,000 kg/ha
(irrigated, fresh) with up to 40 kg/ha oil, implying ~0.002 at the upper
bound. This document widened the yield-chain range to span the lower
end of all three readings (0.001-0.006) rather than averaging them,
since the first source's own fresh-vs-dried basis could not be
confirmed and its implied fraction was markedly higher than the other
two.
