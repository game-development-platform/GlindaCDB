# Black Pepper (*Piper nigrum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/black-pepper.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Piper nigrum* L., family Piperaceae, the woody
climbing vine known as "the King of Spices" — a single species that
yields four distinct commercial peppercorn products (black, white,
green, red) depending purely on harvest maturity stage and post-harvest
processing, not on cultivar or sub-species [1][2][3]. This covers the
species as a whole, with the four product types modeled as separate
commodities sharing one plant.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one species, four commercial products, distinguished
entirely by harvest timing and post-harvest treatment.** Black pepper is
unripe green berries, sun-dried, the skin darkening and wrinkling in the
process [2][3]. White pepper is fully ripe berries with the outer skin
removed (usually by water-retting/soaking) before drying [2][3]. Green
pepper is unripe berries preserved without drying, by brining/pickling
or freeze-drying [2][3]. Red pepper is fully ripe berries specially
treated to retain their red color through drying — described by one
source as rare and genuinely difficult to source, the least common of
the four [2]. Sources genuinely disagree on one mechanical detail of the
black-pepper path: one source describes black pepper as "dried and
fermented in the sun," while another explicitly describes the berries
as dried quickly specifically to prevent fermentation [2][3] — this is a
real, unresolved process-detail conflict, kept visible rather than
silently picking one account.

**Finding 2 — yield figures vary by roughly an order of magnitude and
frequently mix green and dried weights without saying so.** Indian
extension figures give about 800-1,000 kg/ha of (presumably dried) black
pepper from a 7-8-year-old plantation [4]; a separate reference-style
figure gives 2 t/ha of sun-dried black pepper at low input, versus 8-9
t/ha of green pepper in the first harvest rising to 12-20 t/ha of green
pepper by the sixth or seventh harvest under higher inputs [4] — the
green-weight figures are roughly an order of magnitude above the dried-
weight figures, consistent with a large fresh-to-dry weight loss on
drying (Finding 1), but the two figure sets are not on the same basis
and should never be compared directly. A Malaysian estimate gives about
6 t/ha/yr under conventional practice and about 5 t/ha/yr organic, with
the fresh/dried basis unstated [4]. A Vietnam-focused commercial page
gives 2.6 t/ha, but is flagged as weakly sourced and treated cautiously
[4]. The two most directly comparable dried-weight figures — 800-1,000
kg/ha and 2 t/ha — are kept as the primary reference range; the green-
weight figures are recorded separately rather than averaged in.

**Finding 3 — propagation, timeline, and lifespan are comparatively
well-sourced and mutually consistent.** Commercial propagation is almost
exclusively by stem cuttings (20-60 cm long, with a node at the
cutting's base, taken from vigorous vines under 5 years old), not by
true seed, though seed propagation is biologically possible and
germinates in 20-40 days [5][6][7]. First flowering occurs at 18-24
months after planting; first harvest at roughly 2-4 years; full
production is reached after about 7 years; and the economic productive
life is commonly cited at 12-20 years [5][6]. These figures come from
multiple independent sources and do not conflict with each other, a
notable contrast with this document's yield figures (Finding 2).

**Finding 4 — pollination biology carries a real, named historical
disagreement, and the species is a tetraploid with a chromosome-scale
reference genome.** One source describes cultivated *P. nigrum* as
self-fertile, even partially self-fertile; a dedicated research article
states that self-fertilization "is undoubtedly the rule in the
cultivated species" but also cites two named prior studies that reported
the same species as cross-pollinated [8]. A plant-encyclopedia source
instead describes self-pollination mechanically, by gravity, as pollen
falls from upper flowers to lower flowers on the same spike [6][9] —
none of these three accounts are reconciled here. The chromosome count
itself is well-sourced and consistent: 2n=52, with a proposed basic
chromosome number of x=13 for the genus, interpreted by at least one
review as indicating a "balanced tetraploid" genome structure — though
that tetraploid label rests on a secondary review rather than the
primary chromosome-count papers, and is recorded with that caveat [10].
A 2019 chromosome-scale reference genome assembly gives approximately
761 Mb across 26 pseudochromosomes (45 scaffolds, N50 29.8 Mb), with
notably high heterozygosity (1.33%) and 59.54% repetitive sequence
content [10].

**Finding 5 — piperine content is well-sourced for the dried product but
varies sharply by plant organ, and the fruit's own peak timing is
specifically dated.** Dried peppercorns (both black and white) commonly
contain 2-7% piperine by one review's figure, with a narrower seed-
specific range of 2.13-5.80% given by the same source; leaf piperine, by
stark contrast, ranges far more widely at 0.12-20.86% and is explicitly
not comparable to the fruit figures [11][12]. The chromosome-scale
genome paper itself identifies the berry at 8 months after pollination
(MAP) as the organ/timepoint with the single highest piperine content
measured [10] — a specific, sourced developmental peak rather than a
vague "ripens over months" statement. A separate 2022 study found that
piperine is "naturally low or negligible" in untreated leaves
specifically, and that an artificial chemical priming treatment
measurably increased leaf piperine biosynthesis alongside improved
resistance to *Phytophthora capsici* — a leaf-level finding, not a
fruit-level one, recorded here only for completeness since it does not
change the modeled product's own chemistry [11].

**Finding 6 — living support trees (notably *Erythrina variegata*) are a
real, named companion-planting system worth documenting alongside this
crop.** Indian farmers commonly use *Erythrina variegata* as a living
support/trellis tree for climbing crops including betel, black pepper,
vanilla, and yam together — the same support species used across at
least four different climbing crops in real farming systems [13]. *E.
variegata* itself is reported as bird-pollinated, outcrossed, and
"sometimes genetically incompatible" by one source, while another
instead describes all species in the genus as self-compatible [13] —
the same kind of unreconciled pollination-biology conflict already seen
for the pepper vine itself in Finding 4, now appearing in its own
support species too. Support trees are typically planted at 2x2 to 2x3
m spacing [13]; *E. variegata* itself tolerates 800-1,500 mm annual
rainfall over a 5-6-month rainy season, with mean minimum/maximum
temperatures around 20°C/32°C [13] — figures that sit comfortably inside
pepper's own broader climate envelope (Finding 7), consistent with the
support tree and vine being grown together rather than needing separate
microclimates.

**Finding 7 — climate tolerance ranges carry a real, sourced discrepancy
between a database-style figure and a cultivation-guide figure, kept
visible rather than resolved.** One tropical-plants database gives *P.
nigrum*'s best annual daytime temperature range as 22-35°C, with a wider
tolerance of 10-40°C, and its preferred mean annual rainfall as
2,500-4,000 mm, tolerating 2,000-5,500 mm [6]. A separate cultivation
guide instead states the crop needs "abundant rainfall, ideally between
1,500 and 3,000 mm per year, well distributed over at least 7-8 months"
[5] — a lower and non-overlapping-at-the-low-end range from the database
figure. Both are recorded rather than collapsed to one number.

**Finding 8 — disease pressure is dominated by one well-characterized
oomycete, with a wild relative already identified as a resistance
source and breeding tool, not a harvested crop in its own right.**
*Phytophthora capsici*, causing "foot rot" or "quick wilt" disease, is
named as the major global production constraint for this crop [14].
Researchers have identified endophytic bacteria (including *Bacillus
megaterium* strains) that antagonize the pathogen as a biocontrol
avenue, and have studied the wild species *Piper colubrinum*
specifically for its resistance to *P. capsici* foot rot, with several
resistance traits reported [14]. *P. colubrinum* is a breeding/rootstock
resource here, not itself a wild-gathered or farmed food/spice product.
Piper yellow mottle virus (PYMoV), a badnavirus transmitted by at least
two named mealybug species, is a second well-characterized pathogen,
causing mottle disease — notably, some infections are entirely
symptomless, confirmed by PCR testing in multiple black-pepper varieties
and even in a wild *Piper* species, meaning visual inspection alone
would miss real infections [14]. The pollu beetle (*Longitarsus
nigripennis*, Chrysomelidae) and the top shoot borer are separately
named as the crop's major insect pests, distinct from the disease
pressures above [14].

**Finding 9 — named cultivars are richest and best-documented in India's
Kerala state, with a thinner and less authoritative picture for
Southeast Asian growing regions.** Karimunda is described as a
traditional land-race and "the most popular variety throughout Kerala"
[15]. Panniyur-1 is a named F1 hybrid; several further Panniyur-numbered
lines (including Panniyur-6, released 2000) are themselves selections
made from Karimunda, alongside two further Karimunda-derived selections
[15]. "IISR Malabar Excel" is a separate named hybrid specifically bred
for high-altitude growing and higher oleoresin content [15]. "Malabar"
pepper, by contrast, is used loosely as a regional type-group label
(pepper grown in Kerala's Malabar region) rather than a single cultivar
— an important distinction not to collapse into a cultivar name [15].
For Sarawak, Malaysia, one source references ten named cultivars
described by a 2020 study without listing them individually in the
snippet available this pass; for Indonesia/Malaysia more broadly, one
weaker source names three cultivars as popular varieties, but no
authoritative primary list of cultivars from that region was found this
pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical, perennial woody climbing vine, not
  an annual-crop season category.
- **Climate:** Tropical, with a real cross-source range conflict kept
  visible: 22-35°C/2,500-4,000mm rainfall (one database) vs.
  1,500-3,000mm rainfall over 7-8 months (one cultivation guide)
  (Finding 7).
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — always dried/preserved/processed
  into one of the four peppercorn products before use.
- **Pollination:** Likely not required — self-pollination is described
  as "the rule" in cultivated material, but this conflicts with at least
  two named prior studies cited by the same source describing the
  species as cross-pollinated (Finding 4); kept unresolved.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 800-1,000 kg/ha (Indian 7-8-year
  plantation, dried); exceptional yield up to 2,000 kg/ha (sun-dried, low
  input, upper figure). The 800-1,000 kg/ha dried figure from a mature
  7-8-year Indian plantation is the most representative single figure
  for ordinary smallholder practice; the green-weight figures (8-20
  t/ha) are not comparable without a sourced fresh-to-dry conversion
  ratio (Finding 2).
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-style crop-coefficient curve specific to
  *Piper nigrum* was found. Drought tolerance was not independently
  sourced, though the crop's own rainfall requirement (1,500-5,500mm
  across sources) suggests a crop that is not drought-tolerant by
  nature. Waterlogging tolerance was not sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Propagated by stem cuttings (dominant commercial
  method) planted against a support — traditionally a living tree such
  as *Erythrina variegata*, shared with betel, vanilla, and yam in the
  same farming systems (Finding 6); concrete posts or dead-wood standards
  are also used in some systems but were not independently sourced this
  pass.
- **Soil:** No soil pH figure was sourced this pass despite a dedicated
  search.
- **Stand life:** Economic productive life commonly cited at 12-20
  years; first harvest at 2-4 years; full production reached by about
  year 7 (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Phytophthora capsici* foot/quick-wilt rot,
  the dominant global production constraint (Finding 8). Piper yellow
  mottle virus (PYMoV), mealybug-transmitted, with a genuinely
  symptomless infection mode confirmed by PCR. Pollu beetle and top
  shoot borer, the major named insect pests.
- **Genome:** 2n=52 (tetraploid per a secondary review's interpretation,
  x=13 basic number); chromosome-scale assembly approximately 761 Mb
  across 26 pseudochromosomes, 1.33% heterozygosity, 59.54% repetitive
  content (Finding 4).
- **Cultivars:** **Karimunda** — traditional Kerala land-race, described
  as the most popular variety throughout Kerala. **Panniyur-1** — named
  F1 hybrid. **Panniyur-6** — a Karimunda selection released 2000 for
  cultivation across Kerala's pepper-growing tracts. **Subhakara,
  Sreekara** — further Karimunda-derived selections. **IISR Malabar
  Excel** — named hybrid bred for high-altitude growing and higher
  oleoresin content. No eating-quality/flavor cultivar deltas were
  sourced this pass — this crop's cultivar axis in the sources opened is
  almost entirely about yield, altitude suitability, and disease/
  oleoresin traits rather than sensory quality differences.
- **Harvest:** Two real, sourced harvest-maturity stages — immature
  green (feeding the black-pepper and green-pepper paths) and fully ripe
  red (feeding the white-pepper and red-pepper paths) (Finding 1).
  Spikes are cut individually as they reach the desired maturity stage;
  the vine itself bears across its 12-20-year productive life.

## Hazards

No human or animal food-safety hazard was sourced for the dried spice
product itself this pass — piperine at normal culinary doses is not
flagged as hazardous by any source opened.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** green/unripe berry → (a) sun-dried → black pepper, or (b)
  preserved without drying (brining/pickling or freeze-drying) → green
  pepper. Fully ripe berry → (c) outer skin removed (usually by water-
  retting/soaking), then dried → white pepper, or (d) specially treated
  to retain red color through drying → red pepper (rare, the least
  common of the four).

## Open research questions

- Fermentation-vs-quick-drying conflict for the black-pepper path,
  genuinely unresolved: one source calls the process "dried and
  fermented in the sun"; another explicitly says drying is done quickly
  to prevent fermentation.
- Pollination-biology conflict, a three-way source disagreement:
  "self-fertile," "self-fertilization is the rule" (while citing two
  named prior studies reporting cross-pollination in the same species),
  and "self-pollinated by gravity" are three distinct, not-fully-
  reconciled accounts.
- Yield figures span almost an order of magnitude and frequently mix
  green/dried weight bases without stating which.
- Soil pH was not sourced at all despite a dedicated query.
- No labor-hour figures were sourced, and harvest-labor/hand-picking
  technique specifically returned nothing from a dedicated search.
- No authoritative primary-source list of named Indonesian/Malaysian
  cultivars was found this pass.
- Support-structure alternatives to living trees (concrete posts,
  dead-wood standards) were not independently sourced.
- Living-support-tree companion-planting (*Erythrina variegata* and
  others) is recorded descriptively here, not as a structured mechanic —
  a genuinely interesting topic (shared trellis infrastructure across
  multiple climbing crops — betel, vanilla, yam, black pepper, all in
  the same real farming systems) that would benefit from its own
  dedicated cross-crop research pass.
- Piperine/oleoresin industrial extraction is a real downstream
  flavoring/nutraceutical-industry process, not covered here as a
  farm-level activity.

## Estimates and derivations

A green-to-dried-black-pepper yield conversion fraction of roughly
0.33-0.40 is an estimate: no single sourced fresh-to-dried conversion
ratio was found for this crop specifically this pass. The figure is a
general estimate based on typical weight-loss ranges for drying a
fleshy drupe/berry (broadly comparable in principle to similar spice-
drying ratios, though not independently cross-checked against a
*Piper*-specific source).
