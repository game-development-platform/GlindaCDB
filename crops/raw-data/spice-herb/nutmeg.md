# Nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*)

**Researched:** 2026-11-02

## Naming and scope

Like black pepper's own four-products-from-one-plant shape, nutmeg and
mace are two distinct commercial products from the same fruit of the
same tree — the kernel (nutmeg) and the bright-red, lace-like aril
surrounding it (mace) — not two different plants or two different
harvest stages of the same organ. This document treats them as two
commodities of one crop, following the same multi-product-from-one-fruit
precedent this project's own black pepper research established, rather
than cilantro's own two-organs-two-names shape.

No close existing sibling shares the genus or growth habit among this
project's Apiaceae/Brassicaceae spice/herb research (cilantro, cumin,
dill, the mustards) — nutmeg is the first true tropical tree crop this
project has researched since black pepper, clove, cinnamon, and vanilla.
This document treats black pepper's own multi-product-from-one-fruit
precedent as the most directly relevant catalogue-design analogy, not a
botanical one.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — nutmeg is a dioecious tree with separate male and female
individuals, confirmed with a specific chromosome count and a roughly
1:1 seedling sex ratio attributed to sex chromosomes — a genuinely
different reproductive-biology category from every crop this project has
researched in its recent spice/herb work, none of which required
separate male and female plants (as opposed to separate male and female
flower parts on the same plant).** An authoritative agricultural
organization's own botanical account reports a somatic chromosome count
of 2n=42, with a suggested base number of 7 for the genus [1]. A 2013
pollination study found nutmeg seedlings germinate in a roughly 1:1 sex
ratio, attributed to sex chromosomes, though the source itself notes it
is unclear whether this holds across all species within the genus
*Myristica* [1] — this document does not generalize the 1:1 finding
beyond *M. fragrans* itself, preserving the source's own hedge. Dioecy
is reported the main obstacle to commercial cultivation [1] — a uniquely
tree-architecture-level cultivation challenge not shared by any other
crop in this project's recent research, including the other tree crops
(black pepper, clove, cinnamon) researched earlier, none of which were
reported dioecious.

**Finding 2 — because sex cannot be reliably determined before flowering
(around year 6-8), and because seedling populations split roughly 1:1
male:female, vegetative propagation (grafting/budding onto specific
named rootstocks) is reported the only viable commercial-multiplication
route, with a sourced, specific skill/success-rate caveat and a named
list of rootstock species.** A 2025 review states that since seedlings
segregate into males and females at roughly 1:1, vegetative methods are
the only viable route for commercial multiplication, specifically to
avoid ending up with unproductive male trees at scale [2]. Budding and
grafting are both used; budding is reported highly skill-dependent with
a low success rate [2] — a specific, sourced propagation-difficulty
caveat, stronger than a generic "vegetative propagation works" claim.
Three species serve as rootstocks: *M. fragrans* itself, *M. beddomei*,
and *M. malabarica*, with self-rootstock (*M. fragrans* on *M. fragrans*)
reported to perform better than the other two [2] — a specific,
actionable rootstock-choice finding. A separate commercial source adds
that sex cannot be reliably determined before flowering, which occurs
around year 6-8 [2] — this document treats the commercial source's own
7-9-years-to-fruiting figure (Finding 3 below) as consistent with, not
contradicting, this 6-8-years-to-flowering figure, since fruiting
naturally follows flowering by some additional time.

**Finding 3 — nutmeg trees have an unusually long and sourced multi-
decade productivity curve, rising "considerably" until age 25 and
continuing to rise more slowly until reaching maximum capacity at 35-40
years — the longest sourced maturation/peak-productivity timeline of any
crop in this project's spice/herb research to date, including the
other tree crops already researched.** One source reports trees
typically begin fruiting 7-9 years after planting, with yield peaking
around 25 years [1] — already a notably long juvenile period. A separate
source gives a more granular, three-phase productivity curve: yield
increases considerably until the tree is about twenty-five, then
continues increasing more slowly until reaching its maximum capacity at
around thirty-five to forty years of age [3] — a specific, sourced,
three-phase (rapid rise, slow rise, plateau) productivity curve, a
genuinely more detailed finding than a simple single-number "years to
peak bearing" claim. The same agricultural organization separately notes
that male trees are usually removed from fields at an early age, roughly
4-15 years [1] — consistent with Finding 1's own dioecy finding: once a
male tree's unproductive sex is confirmed (by flowering, around year 6-8
per Finding 2), there is no further reason to retain it, explaining the
sourced 4-15-year removal window as the period during which sex becomes
confirmable and removal becomes worthwhile. Seedling-grown trees are
separately reported usually single-stemmed and taller than vegetatively
propagated trees, which are usually multi-stemmed [1] — a sourced
architectural difference between the two propagation routes, relevant
given Finding 2's own emphasis on vegetative propagation as the
commercial standard.

**Finding 4 — the crop's own two named commercial products, nutmeg
(kernel) and mace (aril), show genuinely different essential-oil content
ranges sourced from the same regional selection and a broader review,
with mace consistently richer in oil than the kernel across both
sources.** A Konkan-region (India) selection reports nut (kernel)
essential-oil content of 7.25%, against mace essential-oil content of
7.1% — nearly equal in this one selection — but the same source reports
a far larger gap in oleoresin content: 2.5% for the nut versus 13.8% for
mace, more than 5x higher [4]. A broader review instead gives wider,
non-overlapping ranges across unspecified samples: leaf 0.7-3.2%, mace
8.1-10.3%, seed (kernel) 0.3-12.5%, and kernel (a separately listed
figure) 6.2-7.6% [4] — this document does not merge the Konkan-selection
figures with the broader review's own ranges, since they come from
different sampling contexts and, in the review's case, seed/kernel
ranges that are wider than and only partially overlap the single-
selection figures. Major chemical constituents across the literature
include myristicin, elemicin, safrole, and trimyristin [4] — this
document did not locate a source directly quantifying trimyristin's own
share in mace versus nutmeg oil specifically, despite finding it
independently named as a major constituent.

**Finding 5 — nutmeg's own pollination mechanism is reported in direct,
unresolved conflict between an older source (insect/moth-pollinated) and
a more recent dedicated field study (wind-pollinated, with a specific
non-sticky-pollen finding supporting the wind-pollination conclusion).**
An older account states pollination in nutmeg is carried out by a moth
[5]. A more recent, dedicated field study instead found no crawling or
flying insects visiting nutmeg flowers at all during its observation
period, and on that basis presumed pollination is instead assisted by
wind; the same study separately reports the pollen itself is non-sticky,
a trait generally favoring wind pollination over insect pollination [5]
— a mechanistically consistent, two-part finding (absence of insect
visitors plus a pollen trait favoring wind) that this document treats as
the stronger of the two sources, though it does not discard the older
moth-pollination claim outright, since the dedicated field study's own
observation period and geographic scope were not independently verified
this pass. This is a genuinely different kind of pollination uncertainty
from any other crop in this project's research: cilantro's, cumin's, and
dill's own pollination findings disputed rates or dependency strength,
not the basic insect-versus-wind mechanism itself.

**Finding 6 — disease pressure is dominated by several named fungal
pathogens causing fruit-level damage (split, rot, drop) reported serious
in a majority of Indian nutmeg gardens, alongside separately documented
Indonesian nursery diseases and two named insect pests, one of them an
emerging defoliator with documented severe impact.** In Kerala, India,
immature-fruit split, fruit rot, and fruit drop are reported serious in
a majority of nutmeg gardens, with die-back and thread blight (caused by
*Marasmius equicrinus*) also occurring [6] — a genuinely severe,
majority-of-gardens disease-impact finding. Indonesian nursery surveys
separately identify dry fruit rot (*Stigmina myristicae*), leaf
anthracnose (*Gloeosporium* sp.), and necrotic spot (*Cylindrocladium*
sp.) [6] — this document records these as distinct named pathogens from
the Kerala findings, not assuming overlap, since the sources describe
different symptoms and different geographic/nursery-vs-garden contexts.
On pests: nine insect species have been recorded on the crop in India,
described as sap-feeding bugs, none of which are reported serious pests
[6] — a notably low-severity general pest finding, structurally similar
to dill's own low-pest-pressure finding. In contrast, a leaf beetle is
separately reported as a new, emerging pest in India, with massive
infestation causing total defoliation of trees [6] — a severe, specific,
newly-documented threat genuinely at odds with the otherwise low-
severity general pest picture, and a black scale insect is separately
named as a sap-feeding pest causing shoot wilting and drying under
severe infestation [6].

**Finding 7 — a major pet-safety organization's own direct, dedicated
article on nutmeg and related fall-spice toxicity confirms myristicin
as the toxic principle, with pet-specific secondary sources converging
on a qualitative symptom picture but genuinely disagreeing on the
quantitative dose threshold by more than 2x across three separate pet-
care sources.** That organization's own published article on fall-spice
hazards is confirmed as a direct, dedicated source (not merely a third-
party claim) addressing nutmeg specifically, stating that nutmeg
contains a chemical causing a wide variety of problems when large
amounts are ingested, and directing pet owners to its animal poison
control service [7] — a genuinely dedicated primary-source article,
though this document notes it is a general awareness article, not a
structured toxic-plant-database entry with the same field format used by
that organization's other plant pages in this project's research.
Multiple secondary pet-care sources converge on myristicin as the
specific toxic principle, with a shared symptom list (hallucinations,
disorientation, increased heart rate and blood pressure, dry mouth,
abdominal pain, possible seizures), one source adding a specific onset
window of 3-8 hours after ingestion [7]. However, the dose threshold
genuinely conflicts across sources: one gives roughly one grated
teaspoon as toxic for cats; a second gives 1-2 teaspoons of ground nutmeg
as toxic for an average dog; a third gives approximately 5 grams for
both species [7] — this document does not resolve this conflict or pick
a "representative" figure, since the underlying units (teaspoons vs.
grams, grated vs. ground) are not directly convertible without
additional assumptions this document does not make. Nutmeg essential oil
is separately flagged by one source as more dangerous than the whole
spice, containing a more concentrated form of myristicin [7] — a
concentration-based hazard-escalation finding paralleling the general
pattern already seen for several essential-oil-bearing crops this
project has researched, but here tied to a specific named compound
(myristicin) rather than a generic "oils are stronger" claim.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Tropical evergreen tree, long-lived, slow-
  maturing — the longest sourced maturation timeline in this project's
  spice/herb research to date (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per fruit, but effectively
  continuous across the year from a mature tree, as fruits ripen
  individually — not independently characterized with a formal
  continuous-harvest structure this pass, though the sourced "good tree
  yields ~2,000 fruits annually" framing implies a season-long, not
  single-event, harvest.
- **Climate:** USDA zones 10-12 per one commercial source, cannot
  tolerate temperatures below 2°C (36°F) — sourced from a single
  commercial source, not independently verified against an authoritative
  extension/university source this pass.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — both nutmeg (dried kernel) and mace
  (dried aril) are used as ground/whole spices, not eaten raw.
- **Pollination:** Required for fruit set — mechanism genuinely disputed
  between an older moth-pollination claim and a more recent wind-
  pollination field study (non-sticky pollen, no insect visitors
  observed) (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** Per-hectare: ~3,122 kg dry nut (with shell)
  and ~480 kg dry mace per hectare at 360 plants/ha per one source; a
  second source gives a notably lower figure of 0.5-1.2 t/ha with 250
  female trees/ha (implying ~1,250 kg nutmegs/ha at 5g per dry shelled
  seed) — this document does not reconcile the two per-hectare figures,
  since they assume different stocking densities and sex ratios (360
  plants/ha of unspecified sex mix vs. 250 female trees/ha specifically).
  Per-tree: a "good tree" yields ~2,000 fruits annually, with a sourced
  range from a few hundred up to ~10,000 fruits — an exceptionally wide
  (>20x) per-tree range, the widest sourced for any crop in this
  project's recent spice/herb research.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  tropical-evergreen climate requirement.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative (grafting/budding) is the only viable
  commercial route, specifically because of the dioecy/sex-ratio problem
  (Finding 2) — seed propagation remains biologically possible (the tree
  is seed-fertile) but is commercially avoided due to the resulting
  unpredictable 1:1 male:female seedling split. Budding is specifically
  reported highly skill-dependent with a low success rate. Named
  rootstocks: *M. fragrans* (self, reported best-performing), *M.
  beddomei*, *M. malabarica*.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Long-lived tropical evergreen tree; 7-9 years to first
  fruiting, yield rising considerably to age 25, then more slowly to a
  maximum at 35-40 years (Finding 3) — the longest sourced maturation/
  peak-productivity curve in this project's spice/herb research to date.
  Male trees typically removed at 4-15 years, once sex is confirmable
  and their unproductiveness established.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fruit-level fungal diseases (immature-fruit
  split, fruit rot, fruit drop) reported serious in a majority of Kerala
  nutmeg gardens; die-back and thread blight also occur. Separate
  Indonesian nursery diseases: dry fruit rot, leaf anthracnose, necrotic
  spot. Nine sap-feeding insect species recorded in India, none reported
  serious; a leaf beetle, an emerging pest, can cause total defoliation
  under massive infestation; a black scale causes shoot wilting under
  severe infestation (Finding 6).
- **Genome:** Chromosome number sourced: 2n=42, base number 7 for the
  genus (Finding 1). No nuclear genome-size (pg or Mb) figure was
  sourced this pass beyond the chromosome count itself.
- **Cultivars:** No specific named commercial cultivar with sourced
  trait deltas was found this pass beyond the general "Konkan-region
  selection" (India) used for the oil-content figures in Finding 4, and
  a separately-noted "yellow aril" nutmeg type reported with high
  essential-oil content in one source title, not independently
  characterized with figures this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage per fruit — nutmeg (kernel) and mace (aril)
  are harvested together from the same ripe fruit, not at separate
  maturity stages, distinguishing this from a true multi-stage harvest
  case (contrast black pepper's own four-products-by-maturity-and-
  processing shape, where timing genuinely differs by product).
  Effectively continuous across the year from a mature tree, as
  individual fruits ripen asynchronously.

## Hazards

Fruit-level fungal diseases (immature-fruit split, fruit rot, fruit
drop) reported serious in a majority of Kerala nutmeg gardens; die-back
and thread blight also occur. Separate Indonesian nursery diseases: dry
fruit rot, leaf anthracnose, necrotic spot. Nine sap-feeding insect
species recorded in India, none reported serious; a leaf beetle, an
emerging pest, can cause total defoliation under massive infestation;
black scale causes shoot wilting under severe infestation (Finding 6).

A major pet-safety organization's own dedicated article confirms
myristicin toxicity (hallucinations, disorientation, elevated heart
rate/blood pressure, dry mouth, abdominal pain, possible seizures), but
the dose threshold genuinely conflicts by more than 2x across three
pet-care sources, not resolved here. Nutmeg essential oil is specifically
flagged as more dangerous than the whole spice (more concentrated
myristicin) (Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fruit harvested as it ripens (effectively continuous
  across the year) → kernel (nutmeg) and aril (mace) separated and dried
  → both sold as ground/whole spices.
