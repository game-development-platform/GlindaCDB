# Peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/peppermint.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced

## Naming and scope

This covers *Mentha × piperita*, the menthol-dominant commercial hybrid
mint. It does not cover spearmint (*M. spicata*, covered separately
elsewhere in this project's own research) or the wider *Mentha* genus
(watermint, horsemint, and dozens of other species/cultivars), beyond
cornmint, which this project has also researched separately.

No close sibling existed in this project's spice/herb research prior to
this crop and cornmint — this is among the first mint-family crops this
project researched outside the rosemary/sage/thyme arc, but peppermint's
own cultivation shape (herbaceous, rhizomatous, essential-oil-focused)
is structurally closer to basil's own annual framing than to the woody-
subshrub Lamiaceae trio, so this document does not lean heavily on
either as an analogy base.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — peppermint's own identity as a hybrid is well-established,
but its exact ploidy and parent species are genuinely disputed across
sources at two different levels of resolution, now clarified by a 2024
genome-assembly paper that settles the most commonly cited figure
without fully resolving the older literature's own wide scatter.**
Peppermint is widely reported as a sterile hybrid, most commonly of
spearmint (*M. spicata*) and watermint (*M. aquatica*) [1][2]. A 2024
paper describing the first draft chromosome-level genome assembly for
the 'Mitcham' cultivar confirms peppermint as an allohexaploid,
specifically determined as a natural hybrid of *M. aquatica* and *M.
spicata* based on karyotype data, with chromosome numbers reported as
2n=6x=66 generally or 2n=6x=72 specifically for 'Mitcham' [3] — this is
the single most authoritative, most recent source opened this pass, and
this document adopts 2n=6x=72 ('Mitcham') as its own primary figure
accordingly. However, older cytogenetic literature (a chromosome-count
checklist) reports a much wider scatter across different studies/
accessions: 36, 48, 64, 68, 72, 84 [3] — not reconciled with the 2024
paper's own more authoritative count, and flagged as a case where newer,
more rigorous methodology (genome assembly + karyotype) likely supersedes
older counting-method variability without this document discarding the
older figures outright. A different source disputes even the parentage
itself: a widely used encyclopedia's own article reportedly names *M.
viridis* × *M. aquatica* as the parent cross, with *M. viridis* itself
possibly a hybrid [2] — not independently cross-checked against the 2024
genome paper's own more authoritative *M. aquatica* × *M. spicata*
framing, and this document treats the 2024 paper as the stronger source
without claiming the other account is simply wrong. A claim that
peppermint's ploidy can be artificially doubled (colchicine treatment)
to produce a fertile form was not independently re-sourced in this
document. No exact total genome-size figure (Mbp/Gbp) was retrieved from
the 2024 assembly paper's own abstract-level snippet this pass, though
the paper is confirmed to report a k-mer-based size estimate — a
genuinely different sourcing gap from basil's own well-anchored genome-
size figure.

**Finding 2 — field yield figures for both fresh herb and essential oil
scatter widely across international trials, spanning roughly an order of
magnitude for fresh herb and with inconsistent units (L/ha vs. kg/ha)
complicating oil-yield comparisons, with nitrogen fertilization and
harvest timing independently identified as major yield drivers.** A
Turkish trial found fresh herb yield 3,236-16,438 kg/ha across trial
years [4]; a separate organic-system study reported fresh herb yield
940-1,696 kg/da (roughly 9,400-16,960 kg/ha by the standard conversion)
[4]; a Ho Chi Minh City greenhouse study found 3,492 kg/ha fresh yield
under its own best density/two-harvest combination [4] — a roughly 5x
spread even among these three studies alone, before considering
essential-oil yield separately. Oil yield is reported in inconsistent
units across sources: a Turkish spring-planting trial reports 56.3 L/ha
[4] (a volume figure, not directly comparable to kg/ha without a density
conversion, which this document does not perform without a sourced
peppermint-oil-specific density figure); an Iranian density study
reports 91.31 kg/ha [4]; a nitrogen/green-manure study reports an
average of 91.65 kg/ha for its best treatment, alongside a 0.73% oil-
content figure [4]. Typical oil content as a share of dry matter is
reported at roughly 2-2.5% in one Turkish harvest-stage study, with a
separate review citing a broader 1-3.5% range [4]. A Mississippi trial
found the greatest oil yields specifically from a 240 kg/ha nitrogen
treatment harvested at bud formation [4] — a genuinely actionable
fertilization/harvest-timing combination finding, though not
independently cross-validated against the other cited trials' own
management practices.

**Finding 3 — propagation is reported as essentially exclusively
vegetative (rhizome/stem-cutting division), directly consistent with
peppermint's own sterile-hybrid identity from Finding 1, with named
commercial cultivars showing a sourced, measurable disease-resistance
delta rather than merely a flavor/appearance difference.** A Canadian
provincial extension guide states commercial peppermint and spearmint
cannot be grown from seed, and are most commonly propagated by division
or rhizome cuttings [5]; a separate source adds that rooting stem
cuttings is specifically the preferred method for commercial growers
[5]. Verticillium wilt (caused by the soil/plant-resident fungus
*Verticillium dahliae*) is reported as the most serious peppermint
disease, with named-cultivar resistance differing measurably: one named
cultivar is reported less susceptible than another [5] — a genuine,
sourced cultivar-level disease-resistance delta, paralleling (in kind,
though for a different pathogen) Florence fennel's own bolt-resistance
cultivar finding this project has researched. Extension guidance
recommends avoiding sites previously cropped to Solanaceous crops (e.g.
potatoes) and rotating instead to onions, maize, or soybeans specifically
to manage Verticillium risk [5]. Rust (two different *Puccinia menthae*
races reported attacking peppermint and spearmint) is separately named,
worsened by moist conditions and managed via resistant varieties and
airflow; one study found potassium application specifically useful
against a related chlorosis/"rust" presentation [5]. Leaf spot and
anthracnose are also reported as problems [5]. USDA hardiness-zone
figures are disputed across sources (3-8, 5-9, 3-7 all separately cited)
[5] — a genuine, unreconciled spread.

**Finding 4 — menthol crystallization, the traditional route from
essential oil to the separate, higher-value crystalline menthol
commodity, is a genuinely distinct, imperfect industrial process with a
specific, sourced yield ceiling, not a simple extraction step, and the
leftover "dementholized oil" byproduct is itself a separately-traded
commercial product.** Menthol is traditionally crystallized from
peppermint oil at roughly 0 to -10°C, but the process is reported to
leave roughly 50% of the oil's own menthol content behind in the liquid
residue — a genuinely low-yield process, meaning only high-menthol-
content oils are practical feedstock for crystallization at all [6].
The residual liquid, "dementholized oil," is reported (per an older,
1950s-era patent) to still contain roughly 50% menthol itself, and was
historically sold to Europe as a cheaper peppermint-oil substitute [6] —
a genuinely separate, lower-value commercial product from either raw
essential oil or crystallized menthol, not a waste byproduct. The same
source notes American/European peppermint oils are often below 50% free
menthol content themselves, making them unsuitable for crystallization-
based menthol production entirely — implying menthol-grade feedstock oil
is itself a genuinely different quality tier from ordinary commercial
peppermint oil, not a universal property of the crop. More recent
patents describe process refinements (gas-stream or reduced-pressure
methods to strip residual oil/impurities from the crystals; multistage
seeded crystallization at 0-5°C) [6], with at least one patent explicitly
describing the traditional crystallization route as industrially
unattractive due to its own long processing time and low space-time
yield [6] — this document does not resolve which specific process (if
any) represents current mainstream commercial practice.

**Finding 5 — peppermint (the fresh plant) is reported toxic to pets by
secondary sources citing a major pet-safety organization, and that
organization's own primary page was subsequently located and lists the
genus generically rather than the species by name.** Multiple secondary
sources claim a major pet-safety organization's own toxic-plant list
includes peppermint and "all mint plants," for dogs, cats, and horses
[7] — this organization's own primary listing was subsequently located;
it lists "Mentha sp." (common name "Garden Mint") generically, not
peppermint or spearmint by species name, as toxic to dogs, cats, and
horses, with essential oils named as the toxic principle and vomiting/
diarrhea as the signs after large ingestions [7b]. This resolves the
initial gap in locating the primary page, but not a deeper one: the
listing is genus-level, not species-specific, so it does not itself
distinguish peppermint's own menthol-driven mechanism from spearmint's
own (different) chemistry, and the secondary sources' own direct
contradiction about severity remains unresolved — at least one secondary
source states the opposite, that the organization's current toxic-plant
list does not flag peppermint as highly toxic, directly contradicting
other secondary sources' own claims [7]. The concentrated essential oil
(not the fresh plant) is consistently described across sources as the
higher-risk form, with cats specifically described as more vulnerable
than dogs due to a reported liver-enzyme deficiency affecting menthol
metabolism (the same general CNS-active-terpene liver-metabolism framing
this project has already established for rosemary's camphor, sage's
thujone, and thyme's thymol findings) [7]. Several specific numeric
claims circulating in the secondary-source set (exact case counts,
percentages) are explicitly flagged by this pass's own search synthesis
as unverifiable and likely fabricated, and are not reproduced in this
document [7].

[7b] A major pet-safety organization's own "Garden Mint" (*Mentha* sp.)
toxic-plant listing, accessed 2026-11-02. Supplied: genus-level toxicity
to dogs/cats/horses, essential oils as toxic principle, vomiting/
diarrhea signs. Search-snippet level.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, herbaceous, rhizomatous (spreads via
  underground rhizomes, can "quickly take over a garden bed" per one
  extension-style source) (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again — foliage/whole-plant harvest
  for both fresh use and oil distillation, repeated through the growing
  season — not independently re-verified against a dedicated multi-cut
  source this pass, carried by analogy to this project's general
  Lamiaceae-herb framing.
- **Climate:** USDA zone 3-9 depending on source (3-8, 5-9, 3-7 all
  separately cited, not reconciled) (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh leaves used in culinary/beverage
  contexts; the crop's own primary commercial value is the distilled
  essential oil and, further downstream, crystallized menthol.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable to this crop's own commercial product
  — sterile hybrid, no viable seed set, no pollination dependency
  (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** Fresh herb 3,236-16,960 kg/ha across several
  international trials (not reconciled to one figure); essential oil
  ~56-92 kg/ha (unit-inconsistent across sources, L/ha vs. kg/ha, not
  converted without a sourced density figure) (Finding 2). Oil content
  ~1-3.5% of dry matter, typically cited around 2-2.5%.
- **Water use:** No crop-coefficient figures sourced this pass;
  qualitative guidance only: 1-2 in/week depending on conditions, with
  overwatering specifically flagged as a disease risk and drip/pre-
  midday irrigation recommended to let foliage dry before evening
  (Finding 3). Drought tolerance/waterlogging tolerance not
  independently assigned a figure this pass — the sourced finding that
  matters most is qualitative: excess moisture/overwatering is a named
  disease-risk factor (Verticillium, rust), not a drought-tolerance
  figure per se.
- **Establishment:** Vegetative only — rhizome division or rooted stem
  cuttings; rooting stem cuttings specifically named as the preferred
  commercial method (Finding 3). Not seed-propagated commercially at all,
  directly a consequence of the crop's own sterile allohexaploid hybrid
  identity (Findings 1 and 3) — a genuine schema contrast with thyme's
  own three-route finding and even sage's two-route finding.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Nitrogen fertilization is a sourced major yield driver, with
  one Mississippi trial finding the greatest oil yields specifically
  from a 240 kg/ha N treatment harvested at bud formation (Finding 2). No
  soil pH optimum independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** Perennial, rhizomatous, spreads aggressively via
  underground rhizomes; field rotation away from peppermint (onions/
  maize/soybeans recommended, Solanaceous crops avoided) is specifically
  tied to Verticillium-wilt management, not a general stand-age limit
  (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Verticillium wilt (*Verticillium dahliae*),
  the most serious named disease, with a sourced cultivar-level
  resistance delta. Rust (two *Puccinia menthae* races), worsened by
  moisture, managed via resistant varieties/airflow; potassium
  application found useful against a related chlorosis presentation in
  one study. Leaf spot and anthracnose also reported (Finding 3).
- **Genome:** An allohexaploid, 2n=6x=72 ('Mitcham', per the 2024
  genome-assembly paper, the most authoritative source opened) or
  2n=6x=66 more generally; older cytogenetic literature reports a much
  wider, unreconciled scatter (36, 48, 64, 68, 72, 84) across different
  studies/accessions (Finding 1). No specific total genome-size (Mbp/
  Gbp) figure was retrieved from the 2024 paper's own abstract-level
  snippet this pass.
- **Cultivars:** Two named cultivars with a sourced Verticillium-wilt
  resistance delta (one less susceptible than the other) (Finding 3) —
  the clearest cultivar-level disease-resistance finding for this crop,
  directly paralleling (for a different pathogen) Florence fennel's own
  bolt-resistance cultivar finding. A third cultivar is separately named
  in a US plant-patent filing found during this pass but its own trait
  deltas were not independently characterized.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — foliage/whole-plant harvest for fresh use
  or oil distillation, with bud formation specifically identified as a
  favorable harvest-timing target for oil yield in one trial (Finding
  2), not independently characterized as a formal multi-stage harvest
  system this pass. Single window per planting season, repeated cuts
  through the season.
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced this pass; the crop's own
  primary long-term-value product is not a dried-leaf form the way
  rosemary/sage/thyme's own products are, but the distilled essential oil
  and, further downstream, crystallized menthol — genuinely different
  storage-relevant commodities from this project's other Lamiaceae
  herbs.

## Hazards

Verticillium wilt (*Verticillium dahliae*), the most serious named
disease, with a sourced cultivar-level resistance delta. Rust (two
*Puccinia menthae* races), worsened by moisture, managed via resistant
varieties/airflow; potassium application found useful against a related
chlorosis presentation in one study. Leaf spot and anthracnose also
reported (Finding 3).

Pet toxicity is reported by secondary sources citing a major pet-safety
organization; that organization's own primary page lists the genus
("Mentha sp.") generically, not peppermint by species name, as toxic to
dogs, cats, and horses via essential oils, with vomiting/diarrhea after
large ingestions. The secondary-source set itself contains a direct,
unreconciled contradiction over how toxic peppermint specifically is
(Finding 5).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** foliage/whole plant harvested cut-and-come-again (bud
  formation favorable for oil yield) → distilled into essential oil →
  optionally crystallized into menthol (a low-yield, imperfect process
  leaving roughly 50% of the oil's menthol in a separately-traded
  "dementholized oil" byproduct).

## Open research questions

- Peppermint's own parent-species identity is disputed at two levels:
  the 2024 genome-assembly paper's own *M. aquatica* × *M. spicata*
  framing (adopted as this document's primary account) was not
  independently cross-checked against a widely used encyclopedia's own
  reported *M. viridis* × *M. aquatica* account.
- A claimed colchicine-ploidy-doubling-to-fertility route was not
  independently re-sourced in this document.
- No specific total genome-size (Mbp/Gbp) figure was retrieved from the
  2024 genome-assembly paper's own abstract-level snippet this pass —
  the paper is confirmed to report a k-mer-based estimate, but the value
  itself was not captured.
- Oil-yield figures are reported in inconsistent units (L/ha vs. kg/ha)
  across sources, not converted to a common unit without a sourced
  peppermint-oil-specific density figure.
- USDA hardiness-zone figures are disputed across sources (3-8, 5-9,
  3-7), not reconciled.
- No specific menthol-content threshold distinguishing "crystallization-
  grade" from ordinary commercial peppermint oil was independently
  sourced beyond the qualitative "often below 50% free menthol" framing
  for American/European oils.
- This document does not resolve which specific menthol-crystallization
  process (if any) represents current mainstream commercial practice —
  several competing/successive patents were found, not reconciled to a
  single "state of the art" account.
- A major pet-safety organization's own primary toxic-plant-list page
  for peppermint specifically (as opposed to genus-level) was not
  located — the organization's own genus-level listing was found
  instead. Secondary sources directly contradict each other on how
  toxic peppermint specifically is.
- Grow-days figures were not independently sourced this pass.
- kcalPerKg/proteinGPerKg were not independently sourced this pass.
- Water-use figures (crop coefficients) were not independently sourced
  this pass beyond qualitative overwatering-as-disease-risk guidance.
- Drought/waterlogging-tolerance figures were not independently assigned
  this pass.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any operation this
  pass.
- Soil pH optimum was not independently sourced this pass.
- Storage-life figures (fresh herb, dried leaf if applicable) were not
  independently sourced this pass — this crop's own primary value chain
  (oil/menthol) has no clean dried-leaf-storage analog.
- A third cultivar (named in a US plant-patent filing) was not
  independently characterized beyond its name.
- Religious/ritual use was not searched with the same depth as other
  crops this pass — flagged as genuinely unresearched, not confirmed-
  absent.
- Light requirement was not independently sourced this pass.
- Controlled-environment/off-world tractability was not independently
  assessed this pass — the crop's own aggressive rhizomatous spreading
  habit may make containment, not size or propagation reliability, the
  genuinely limiting factor for shared/communal growing systems.
