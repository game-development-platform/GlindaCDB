**Researched:** 2026-11-03

## Naming and scope

"Licorice" is used for multiple species in the genus *Glycyrrhiza*, most
notably *G. uralensis* (Chinese/Russian licorice), which is used
interchangeably with *G. glabra* in Chinese and Kampo medicine per at
least one source [1], without this document finding a confirmed single
glycyrrhizin-content figure distinguishing the two at the search-snippet
level. This document covers only *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (the species most
associated with the Western/European confectionery and flavoring trade);
*G. uralensis* is not researched here. No close existing sibling in this
project's own spice/herb research shares the genus or family (Fabaceae)
— the closest structural comparison is ginger's/turmeric's own rhizome-
harvest shape, used here only as an analogous harvest-organ comparison
(both are underground-organ crops), not a botanical analogy, since
licorice is a legume shrub, not a ginger-family herb.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the harvested organ is a woody perennial shrub's
underground root/stolon system, with a notably wide, multiply-sourced
harvest-timing range (2-5 years) across five independent cultivation
guides, converging loosely around 3-4 years as the most commonly cited
window.** A land-grant university extension sheet recommends harvesting
roots in the second or third year [2]. An authoritative plant database
reports commercial growers dig up whole plants after three to five years
[2]. An Indian agricultural extension guide states high yields come from
plants 3 or 4 years old [2]. A separate biodiversity reference puts
harvest at two to three years after planting [2]. A horticultural
society suggests waiting at least three years [2]. This document does
not collapse these five figures into a single number, since they
describe somewhat different things (root-only harvest vs. whole-plant
digging, "recommended" vs. "high-yield" framings), but notes the
genuinely tighter clustering (loosely 2-5 years, most commonly 3-4) than
many other crops' own far more divergent or entirely absent maturity
findings. Harvest is generally reported in autumn, though the land-grant
extension source separately also lists spring as viable [2] — a two-
season harvest-timing flexibility not found reported for most other
spice/herb crops this project has researched.

**Finding 2 — propagation is reported primarily vegetative (stolon
cuttings), with a specific, sourced reason given for this preference
(poor seed set and germination specifically in India), while a separate
source recommends seed propagation as a routine alternative method.**
An Indian agricultural extension guide states growers usually use stolon
cuttings of about 10-15 cm, and separately states that seed set and
germination are poor specifically in India [2] — a genuinely regionally
-scoped claim, not a universal species-level statement about seed
viability. A horticultural society instead recommends seed sowing in a
coldframe, or dividing roots, in early spring [2] — this document treats
this as consistent with a geography-dependent propagation preference
(vegetative preferred where seed set is poor, seed viable elsewhere),
rather than adopting either source's own implicit framing as a universal
species-wide rule. This document does not independently confirm whether
seed set is similarly poor outside India, or whether the UK-sourced
recommendation reflects UK-grown stock with better seed set.

**Finding 3 — chromosome number is cleanly, convergently sourced as
diploid (2n=16, x=8) across multiple independent sources, including a
dedicated 2015 karyological study of five Turkish *Glycyrrhiza* taxa
that found the same count (2n=16) in four of the five taxa tested (with
the fifth, *G. asymmetrica*, differing at 2n=14), though no absolute
genome-size (Mb or pg) figure was located for *G. glabra* itself despite
a dedicated search, with only a relative comparison located instead.** A
2015 peer-reviewed karyological study of Turkish *Glycyrrhiza* taxa found
2n=16 in *G. echinata*, *G. glabra*, *G. flavescens* subsp. *flavescens*
and *G. flavescens* subsp. *antalyensis*, with 2n=14 found in the fifth
taxon tested, *G. asymmetrica* [3] — a genuinely convergent finding
across most of the genus, with one confirmed exception at a different
base count. A separate 2008 Iranian study reports the presence of
2n=2x=16 in the species it examined, consistent with the Turkish study's
own finding [3]. An Indian chromosome-database genus datasheet separately
gives a basic number of x=8 with diploid ploidy for the genus generally,
without a dedicated *G. glabra*-specific entry [3] — this document
treats this as consistent with, but not independently confirming beyond,
the two species-level studies already cited. No absolute genome-size
figure (in Mb or pg) was located for *G. glabra* itself; the only
relevant result is a Turkish master's thesis using flow cytometry on
three Turkish *Glycyrrhiza* species, which found *G. flavescens* has
larger DNA content than both *G. glabra* and *G. echinata*, without
stating an absolute value for any of the three species at the search-
snippet level, and the same source separately notes that reports on
*Glycyrrhiza* genome sizes generally are limited [3] — this document
does not adopt the relative comparison as a substitute for an absolute
genome-size figure.

**Finding 4 — root rot/wilt caused by Fusarium species is confirmed
across three independent, geographically separate studies (Iran, Japan,
China), with the Chinese study giving a specific, severe, annually-
recurring mortality figure (~30% of plants die from this disease in
Ningxia every year), and the Japanese study identifying a different
causal Fusarium species (*F. vanettenii*) for a newly-named disease
("base and stolon rot") distinct from the other two studies' own *F.
oxysporum*/*F. solani* findings.** Researchers in Yazd province, Iran,
reported wilting and plant death in field-grown licorice, reproducing
the disease by inoculating wounded roots of 4-week-old *G. glabra*
plants with an *F. oxysporum* spore suspension, with the same paper
separately noting *F. oxysporum* had previously been reported as a root-
rot agent of the related species *G. uralensis* in China [4] — this
document treats the Iranian finding as specific to *G. glabra*, while
noting the cross-species precedent explicitly, rather than conflating
the two. A 2023 Japanese study identified a different causal organism,
*Fusarium vanettenii*, as the cause of stolon rot in cultivated *G.
glabra*, proposing the name "base and stolon rot" for this newly-
described disease, with inoculation experiments reproducing base rot and
blight symptoms on healthy seedlings [4] — a genuinely distinct causal-
species finding from the Iranian study's own *F. oxysporum*. A separate
Chinese study reports approximately 30% of plants die from root rot
every year in Ningxia, with *F. solani* and *F. oxysporum* the two most
frequently isolated fungi, confirmed by inoculation experiments
reproducing field symptoms [4] — this 30%-annual-mortality figure is the
single most severe, specifically quantified disease-mortality finding
sourced for this crop. No aphid or other named insect-pest finding was
located for this crop despite a dedicated search.

**Finding 5 — glycyrrhizin, the species' own primary active/flavor
compound, is confirmed to cause pseudoaldosteronism (also termed
pseudohyperaldosteronism) in humans via a specific, well-characterized
molecular mechanism (inhibition of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type
2, 11βHSD2), with a 2023 systematic review of 104 case reports giving a
detailed clinical picture, and glycyrrhizin content itself reported to
vary widely (2-25%) depending on the plant.** Glycyrrhizin metabolites
inhibit 11βHSD2, the enzyme that normally decomposes cortisol into
inactive cortisone in the distal nephron; when this enzyme is inhibited,
cortisol instead inappropriately activates mineralocorticoid receptors,
producing an aldosterone-like effect despite normal or low actual
aldosterone levels — hence "pseudo"-aldosteronism [5] — a specific,
named, well-characterized molecular mechanism. A 2023 systematic review
of 104 case reports found hypertension and electrolyte imbalance (mainly
hypokalemia, i.e. low blood potassium) to be the prevalent clinical
features, with severe cases additionally able to involve cardiac
arrhythmias, muscle paralysis, rhabdomyolysis (muscle tissue breakdown),
and coma [5] — a genuinely severe potential symptom spectrum at the high
end, though this document notes the review's own finding that neither
female sex nor old age appeared to be risk factors for this specific
toxicity. A separate, more recent (December 2025) case series reports
that toxicity can occur even at low exposure levels in some individuals
[5] — this document treats this as evidence of meaningful inter-
individual variability in susceptibility, not merely a simple dose-
response relationship, though no specific mechanism for this individual
variability was located. Glycyrrhizin content itself is separately
reported to range from 2% to 25% depending on the plant [5] — an
extremely wide compound-concentration range (over 10-fold), though this
document does not find a sourced explanation (cultivar, growing region,
harvest age) for what drives this specific range. This document does not
adopt a specific, searched-but-unverified numeric dose threshold (0.1-0.2
g glycyrrhizic acid/kg body weight) found in one dog-focused pet-care
source, since that source was explicitly judged low quality (a demo-
domain page with garbled formatting) at the point of discovery.

**Finding 6 — no authoritative pet-safety-organization entry specifically
for licorice/*Glycyrrhiza* was located despite a direct, site-restricted
search returning only unrelated plant pages, while secondary pet-focused
sources instead describe a dog-specific toxicity picture broadly
consistent with, though less mechanistically detailed than, this
document's own human-toxicity finding in Finding 5.** A direct, site-
restricted search of a major pet-safety organization's site for licorice
or *Glycyrrhiza* returned only unrelated plant-database pages (privet,
laurel, angelica tree, and several generic alphabetical listing pages)
[6] — this document treats this as a genuine absence, not merely an
unopened search hit. Several pet-care blog sources instead describe
glycyrrhizin as potentially toxic to dogs in large quantities, with one
source specifically describing large doses as able to cause high blood
pressure, lethargy, and abnormal heart rhythms through potassium loss [6]
— this document notes this pet-focused mechanism description (potassium
loss driving abnormal heart rhythms) is broadly consistent with, though a
simplified version of, the mechanistically detailed hypokalemia/
pseudoaldosteronism picture already sourced from the peer-reviewed human
-toxicity literature in Finding 5, rather than treating the two toxicity
pictures as unrelated or contradictory findings. No result addressed
cats specifically for this crop despite a dedicated search. One pet-care
source separately reports that in most cases, dogs recover completely
from licorice poisoning, though rare lasting kidney or heart damage was
noted [6] — this document records this prognosis claim as sourced from a
pet-care blog, not a veterinary toxicology reference, and does not treat
it with the same confidence as the peer-reviewed human-toxicity
literature in Finding 5.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Woody perennial shrub, harvested for its root/
  stolon system — loosely 2-5 years to harvest age, most commonly cited
  3-4 years, across five independently sourced cultivation guides
  (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — root/stolon system dug up once
  the plant reaches harvest age; not independently confirmed as a
  repeat-harvest crop this pass.
- **Climate:** USDA zones 6-11 per one source; a horticultural society
  gives a UK-specific hardiness rating not directly reconciled with the
  USDA figure this pass. A dry, sunny climate with 50-100 cm annual
  rainfall, irrigation available, is separately reported as the
  preferred growing environment by an Indian agricultural extension
  source.
- **Food status:** Not raw edible — the dried root is processed
  (chewed, extracted, or used as a flavoring/sweetening agent); not
  typically eaten raw as a vegetable.
- **Pollination:** Not independently sourced this pass — no pollination
  -mechanism finding was located despite the dedicated searches run this
  pass.
- **Yield / composition:** ~7 t/ha dry root at one named Indian
  location; 2.5-5.0 t/ha at a second named Indian location, per the same
  Indian extension source — a specific, named-location yield comparison
  within one country, though this document does not claim these two
  figures are directly comparable without accounting for possible
  differences in soil, cultivar, or plant age between the two locations.
  A separate land-grant university's own field-trial dry-weight-per-acre
  figures were noted as lower, but not extracted with full precision
  this pass.
- **Water use:** 50-100 cm annual rainfall with irrigation available is
  the preferred growing environment per one source; no specific crop-
  coefficient figure sourced this pass.
- **Establishment:** Primarily vegetative (stolon cuttings, 10-15 cm) in
  regions with poor seed set; seed sowing or root division viable
  elsewhere per a horticultural society (Finding 2).
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general dry/
  sunny/irrigated climate preference.
- **Stand life:** Harvest age loosely 2-5 years, most commonly cited 3-4
  years, across five independent sources; harvest reported in autumn,
  with one source also listing spring as viable (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fusarium root/stolon rot confirmed across
  three independent, geographically separate studies (Iran, Japan,
  China), with different causal species identified in different studies
  (*F. oxysporum* in Iran; *F. vanettenii* in a newly-named Japanese
  disease; *F. solani*/*F. oxysporum* in China, with a specific ~30%-
  annual-mortality figure in one region) (Finding 4). No aphid or other
  named insect pest located despite a dedicated search.
- **Genome:** Not found for *G. glabra* itself as an absolute value
  despite a dedicated search; only a relative comparison was located (a
  related Turkish species found to have more DNA content than *G.
  glabra* in a flow-cytometry thesis, without a stated absolute value
  for either). Chromosome number cleanly and consistently sourced:
  diploid, 2n=16 (x=8), convergent across multiple independent studies
  covering most tested *Glycyrrhiza* taxa, with one confirmed exception
  (*G. asymmetrica*, 2n=14) (Finding 3).
- **Cultivars:** No specific named commercial cultivar with sourced
  trait deltas was found this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — root/stolon system dug up once at harvest
  age; no distinct multi-stage maturity-timing split sourced this pass.
  Single window — no multi-flush or multiple-harvest-per-year finding
  sourced this pass, consistent with this crop being a root/stolon
  harvest rather than a repeat fruit/leaf harvest.
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced this pass.

## Hazards

Fusarium root/stolon rot confirmed across three independent,
geographically separate studies (Iran, Japan, China), with different
causal species identified in different studies and a specific ~30%-
annual-mortality figure in one Chinese region (Finding 4). No aphid or
other named insect pest located despite a dedicated search.

Glycyrrhizin confirmed to cause pseudoaldosteronism in humans via a
specific, well-characterized molecular mechanism (11βHSD2 inhibition),
with a detailed clinical picture from a 2023 systematic review of 104
case reports (hypertension, hypokalemia, and at the severe end
arrhythmias, muscle paralysis, rhabdomyolysis, coma); neither female sex
nor old age found to be risk factors; toxicity reported possible even at
low exposure in some individuals (Finding 5).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the glycyrrhizin/pseudoaldosteronism mechanism is a
genuine human-health hazard with a strong clinical-literature basis,
deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

No authoritative pet-safety-organization entry was located for this
species despite a direct search; secondary pet-care sources describe a
dog-toxicity picture broadly consistent with, though less detailed than,
the human mechanism; no cat-specific finding located (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** root/stolon system dug up at harvest age (2-5 years,
  commonly 3-4) → dried → chewed, extracted, or used as a flavoring/
  sweetening agent (not typically eaten raw).
