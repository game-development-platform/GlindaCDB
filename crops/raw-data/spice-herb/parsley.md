# Parsley (*Petroselinum crispum*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/parsley.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

*Petroselinum crispum* (Mill.) Fuss, family Apiaceae (the carrot/fennel/
dill family, not the mint family like rosemary/sage/thyme). This
document covers the two common leaf forms — curly-leaf and flat-leaf
("Italian") — grown and harvested for foliage. It does not cover Hamburg
/root parsley (*P. crispum* var. *tuberosum*), a genuinely distinct
root-harvested form of the same species, which this project treats as a
candidate separate crop entry rather than a cultivar of this one, not
researched here.

This project's own fennel research (the other Apiaceae herb this project
has researched; fennel is parsley's real botanical sibling) and its own
lavender research (for current trait-block structure) were consulted
while researching this crop.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — leaf-parsley yield figures vary enormously by basis and
country, with peer-reviewed Polish field trials reporting far higher
numbers than a looser commercial estimate.** Polish field trials
(2001-2003) across five cultivars reported total yields of 67.84-96.96
t/ha and marketable yields of 53.57-74.49 t/ha [1]; a later Polish trial
reported even higher figures in some years, up to 166.52 t/ha in 2015
[1]; a separate Polish trial reported leaf yields averaging 5.5-35.7 t/ha
[1] — all roughly an order of magnitude or more above a looser
commercial estimate. Root (Hamburg) parsley is reported separately and
much lower: Czech trials gave marketable root yields of 18.8-21.1 t/ha,
with a national mean of 9.9-11.8 t/ha [1]. A commercial Kenyan farming
guide cites 8,000-12,000 kg/acre/year (roughly 20,000-30,000 kg/ha),
treated here as a non-peer-reviewed estimate [1]. None of these sources
use a consistent basis (total vs. marketable, fresh vs. dry weight,
per-cut vs. annual total), so this document does not average them; the
wide range is recorded as a genuine open item.

**Finding 2 — parsley's seed is both famously short-lived and poor-
germinating even when fresh, a doubly confirmed and well-sourced
trait.** Seed-saving guidance lists parsley as insect-pollinated, giving
a 1-mile isolation distance, 30 recommended plants, and a seed life of
about 5 years, while separately warning that parsley seed heads shatter
easily [2]. A dedicated floral-biology study found the species is
protandrous (anthers mature before the stigma within each flower) and
that cross-pollination plays a significant role in seed production [2]
— directly explaining the real, sourced insect-pollination requirement
and isolation-distance figure. F1 hybrid parsley is reported as
commercially desirable over existing open-pollinated crossbred races; a
US patent describes a cytoplasmic-male-sterile (CMS) route to producing
F1 seed, and separately notes self-incompatibility (the Brassica-style
route) as an alternative hybridization mechanism [2] — though the
patent's own framing implies only open-pollinated types are presently
grown commercially, so this document does not assert an active
commercial F1 hybrid-production system.

**Finding 3 — parsley's real chromosome number and genome size remain
genuinely unresolved by a directly opened primary source this pass,
despite one promising lead.** Cultivated parsley is commonly reported as
diploid with 2n=22 from general botanical knowledge, but no source
opened this pass independently confirmed this figure. A 2024 parsley
genome-sequencing paper reports a draft contig assembly of 1.57 Gb
representing 80.9% of the estimated genome, implying a total genome size
around 1.9 Gb, with the assembly itself highly repeat-rich (81%
repetitive DNA) [3] — this document records the implied ~1.9 Gb figure
as derived (see Estimates and derivations), not as a directly sourced
genome-size-database C-value, since no such database entry for
*Petroselinum crispum* was found this pass despite a dedicated search.
Interestingly, *Petroselinum crispum* itself appears in the literature
as a reference flow-cytometry standard used to measure other species'
genome sizes [3] — a role reversal from this project's usual genome-size
findings, where the crop being researched is the one needing a standard,
not providing one.

**Finding 4 — parsley's biennial vernalization-gates-seed-not-yield
pattern is confirmed, with a specific, if non-peer-reviewed, numeric
bolting trigger.** One extension-style gardening source gives a specific
bolting trigger — flowering begins when soil temperatures consistently
exceed 75°F (24°C) for 10 or more consecutive days — alongside the
general day-length (photoperiod) cue also cited for the biennial
transition [4]; this document flags this specific figure as unverified
against a primary research source. A related Apiaceae comparison point:
carrot biennial cultivars are reported to require 11-12 weeks at 5°C to
flower [4] — not parsley data, but a useful family-level anchor given
parsley's own vernalization requirement was not independently quantified
in degree-days or weeks-at-temperature this pass. Commercial hydroponic
growers separately report bolting above sustained temperatures of about
28°C [8] — a second, independent high-temperature bolting trigger
broadly consistent with the 75°F/24°C figure above, though the two
figures do not fully reconcile and are not averaged here.

**Finding 5 — parsley's phototoxicity hazard is real, sourced, and
dose-dependent, with a separate non-phototoxic toxicity pathway at much
higher doses — a genuinely two-hazard profile.** A fragrance-industry
supplier safety sheet gives parsley leaf essential oil a typical
bergapten (5-MOP) content of 20 ppm, against a 15 ppm cumulative cap
recommended for consumer products combining several phototoxic oils [5]
— the same furanocoumarin mechanism this project has found for parsnip,
a close Apiaceae relative. Separately, apiole (found concentratedly in
parsley seed oil) is linked to blood dyscrasias, kidney and liver
toxicity at doses above roughly 10g by one source, while a different
source frames the oral danger around much larger (200g) doses of the
oil — the two sources disagree by an order of magnitude on the
threshold, not reconciled here [5]. Myristicin, also present, is linked
to giddiness and hallucination at high doses [5]. A major pet-safety
organization separately lists parsley as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses
via the same furanocoumarin-photosensitization mechanism documented for
parsnip, noting large amounts are needed [5].

**Finding 6 — parsley's disease complex is dominated by a seed-borne
fungal leaf spot and a soil-borne crown rot, a different profile shape
from both basil species' own foliar-disease and lavender's own
drainage-driven-root-rot findings.** Septoria leaf spot, caused by
*Septoria petroselini*, is seed-borne and can also persist in plant
debris and on volunteer plants, with resistant cultivars (e.g.
'Paramount') recommended [6]. Crown/root rot, caused by soil-dwelling
fungi and bacteria, is reported to begin at a few spots on the crown at
the soil line before spreading through the whole crown into a malodorous
mush, with crop rotation (avoiding replanting parsley in the same spot)
as the recommended cultural control [6] — a genuinely different root-rot
mechanism and trigger from lavender's own water-management-driven
Phytophthora/Pythium complex, though both ultimately recommend similar
cultural (non-fungicide) controls. Aster yellows (a phytoplasma disease)
affects related Apiaceae (carrot) via leafhopper vectors, not aphids as
initially assumed [6] — recorded here as a corrected initial assumption,
though no source directly confirmed aster yellows affects parsley itself
specifically.

**Finding 7 — parsley is a real, established commercial hydroponic
crop, with sourced EC/pH/temperature ranges and a genuinely parsley-
specific finding on ethylene sensitivity.** An Australian commercial
grower reports producing roughly 400 bunches/week split between Italian
and English (curly) varieties, describing hydroponics as cheaper and
easier to manage than field growing [8]; flat-leaf cultivars such as
'Giant of Italy' and 'Titan' are reported as commercially preferred for
stronger flavor and faster post-cut regrowth [8]. Sourced hydroponic
ranges: EC roughly 1.0-2.0 mS/cm, pH 5.5-6.5, temperature 10-24°C, with
bolting reported above sustained temperatures of about 28°C [8] (see
Finding 4). A postharvest guide specifically notes parsley is highly
sensitive to ethylene, which causes yellowing, and warns against leaving
bunches soaking in water for long [8] — a genuinely new, sourced
postharvest-handling finding. Separately, a plant-factory thesis notes
parsley seed can take 14-28 days to germinate under controlled
conditions, with seed priming improving speed and uniformity [8] —
directly consistent with, and quantifying, folklore-documented "3-4
weeks" germination-lag claims.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Cool-season — biennial, grown as an annual/
  overwintering leaf crop. Hamburg/root parsley is the same species but a
  genuinely distinct harvested-organ form, treated as a separate
  candidate crop entry, not this crop's cultivar.
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again — repeated foliage cutting
  through the season, consistent with every commercial hydroponic/field
  source opened this pass (Finding 7).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate, temperate.
- **Food status:** Raw edible.
- **Pollination:** Not required for the leaf harvest (harvested before
  flowering); required for seed production — insect-pollinated,
  protandrous florets, cross-pollination significant for seed set
  (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Not reconciled this pass — sources disagree by
  more than an order of magnitude and use inconsistent bases (total vs.
  marketable, fresh vs. dry, per-cut vs. annual): Polish field trials
  53,570-96,960 kg/ha (marketable, annual total); a 2015 trial peak of
  166,520 kg/ha; Czech Hamburg-root-parsley marketable 18,800-21,100
  kg/ha (a different harvested organ, not this crop's leaf product)
  (Finding 1). This document does not average these or assert a single
  figure.
- **Water use:** Drought tolerance and waterlogging tolerance not
  independently sourced this pass; no crop-coefficient figures sourced.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed; germination slow (14-28 days under
  controlled plant-factory conditions) and erratic, improved by seed
  priming (Finding 7).
- **Seed crop:** The seed crop requires the full second-year biennial
  bolt; the leaf-harvest crop is typically never allowed to reach this
  stage (Finding 4). A real cold/photoperiod requirement exists for
  seed production but no specific temperature-and-duration figure was
  independently sourced for parsley itself this pass; the carrot analogy
  (11-12 weeks at 5°C) is recorded as a family-level reference point
  only. A seed-saving guide recommends at least 30 plants, with an
  isolation distance of roughly 1 mile (1,609 m) for home-garden-level
  purity. Pollination is insect-pollinated, protandrous; self-
  incompatibility is described in patent literature as an alternative
  hybridization route but not confirmed as the naturally-occurring
  compatibility system. Seed heads shatter easily. Leaf harvest and
  seed-crop harvest are mutually exclusive; a plant allowed to bolt for
  seed is past useful leaf production.
- **Labor:** Not independently sourced this pass for any specific
  operation.
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Septoria leaf spot (*Septoria petroselini*,
  seed-borne, persists in debris/volunteers; resistant cultivar
  'Paramount' available) and crown/root rot (soil-borne fungi and
  bacteria, crown-line onset spreading into the whole crown; crop
  rotation the recommended control) (Finding 6).
- **Genome:** Genuinely unresolved to a directly sourced genome-size-
  database C-value this pass; a 2024 sequencing paper implies ~1.9 Gb
  (derived, see Estimates and derivations) from an 80.9%-complete 1.57
  Gb assembly [3]; no independent confirmation of the commonly-cited
  2n=22 chromosome number was found this pass either.
- **Cultivars:** Three broad, well-known market types rather than many
  named cultivars with sourced trait deltas: curly-leaf (*P. crispum*
  var. *crispum*) — milder flavor, more decorative garnish use, curled
  tightly-ruffled leaf; flat-leaf/Italian (*P. crispum* var.
  *neapolitanum*) — stronger flavor, preferred by commercial growers and
  cooks, flat broader leaf, faster post-cut regrowth than curly-leaf
  [8]; and two named flat-leaf cultivars ('Giant of Italy', 'Titan'),
  widely grown commercially [8], with no further individually sourced
  trait delta beyond the flat-leaf group description. No individually
  sourced per-cultivar chill/bloom/yield delta table was found this pass
  beyond this market-type split.
- **Harvest:** Single stage — leaves are cut at usable size through the
  season; no distinguishable immature-vs-mature harvest-stage choice
  sourced this pass. Single window — repeated cutting through one
  growing season from a single planting; no separate-cohort/flush
  pattern sourced.
- **Storage:** Not independently sourced to a specific day count this
  pass, but the regime itself is sourced: a postharvest review reports
  parsley is not chilling-sensitive and should be kept as cold as
  possible without freezing, though yellowing was separately reported in
  parsley held at 5°C (41°F), pointing to ordinary quality loss (not
  chilling injury) at that temperature [7]. Modified atmosphere
  packaging can extend storage life if temperature swings and
  condensation are avoided [7]. Hamburg (root) parsley roots — a
  different harvested organ, not this crop's product — can be stored at
  0°C for several months [7].

## Hazards

Phototoxicity — leaf essential oil carries bergapten (5-MOP) at ~20 ppm,
above the 15 ppm leave-on-product cap used for other phototoxic oils.
Separately, apiole/myristicin toxicity at much higher, seed-oil-
concentration doses (sources disagree by an order of magnitude on the
threshold, 10g vs. 200g of oil). A major pet-safety organization lists
furanocoumarin photosensitization in dogs, cats, and horses via the same
mechanism (Finding 5).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — German monograph material lists pregnancy and
inflammatory kidney disease as contraindications for parsley/parsley
seed products, and notes rare reported allergy — not independently
verified against a primary source this pass, deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** leaves cut cut-and-come-again through the season → used
  fresh or dried. Seed oil (apiole) extraction is a minor, unmodeled use
  with no yield figures sourced.

## Open research questions

- Hamburg/root parsley (*P. crispum* var. *tuberosum*) is a genuinely
  distinct root-harvested form of the same species, sold under its own
  name, with a different primary product (a parsnip/celery-like root)
  and reportedly less tender/flavorful leaves than dedicated leaf
  cultivars (sources disagree on this point) — this project treats it as
  a candidate separate crop entry, not researched here.
- Yield figures are not reconciled: Polish field-trial leaf yields
  (53,570-166,520 kg/ha marketable/total, several cultivars and years)
  vs. a non-peer-reviewed Kenyan commercial guide (~20,000-30,000 kg/ha)
  vs. Hamburg-root figures (18,800-21,100 kg/ha, a different organ).
  Sources also disagree on basis (total vs. marketable, fresh vs. dry,
  per-cut vs. annual). Not averaged; flagged as the weakest reconciled
  figure in this document.
- Seed-crop data gap: no seed-yield-per-hectare, seed-yield-per-plant,
  seed-multiplication-ratio, days-to-seed-from-sowing, or specific
  vernalization temperature/duration figure was sourced for parsley
  itself this pass — the single largest data gap in this document.
- Genome size and chromosome number remain genuinely unresolved to a
  primary source: the commonly-cited 2n=22 chromosome number was not
  independently confirmed this pass; the ~1.9 Gb genome-size figure is
  derived from a single sequencing paper's own completeness percentage,
  not a directly published genome-size-database C-value (none found
  despite a dedicated search).
- Bolting-trigger figures from two independent sources are not fully
  reconciled: a non-peer-reviewed extension source's 75°F (24°C)-for-
  10-days soil-temperature trigger, and a separate commercial-
  hydroponic source's ~28°C sustained-temperature trigger.
- No labor-hour or labor-day figure was sourced for any parsley
  operation this pass.
- No individually named cultivar with a sourced trait-delta beyond the
  curly/flat-leaf market-type split and two named flat-leaf cultivars
  was found this pass.
- Religious/ritual use was not specifically, separately searched for
  this pass — recorded as "none found" on the basis of the general
  research conducted, not a dedicated targeted search.
- Light requirement (daily light integral/hours-of-sun) was not
  independently quantified this pass.
- Aster-yellows susceptibility in parsley itself (as opposed to related
  carrot) was not independently confirmed by a source specific to
  parsley this pass — flagged as a corrected initial search assumption
  (aphid vs. leafhopper vector), not a resolved finding.
- German-monograph contraindications (pregnancy, inflammatory kidney
  disease) and rare allergy reports were not independently verified
  against a primary source this pass.
- Hybrid-seed question not confirmed as commercially active — CMS and
  self-incompatibility routes to F1 parsley seed are described in
  patent literature as technically available, but the same source
  frames currently-grown parsley as open-pollinated, not F1 hybrid.

## Estimates and derivations

A genome size of approximately 1.9 Gb is derived: a 2024 parsley genome
paper reports a draft contig assembly of 1.57 Gb representing 80.9% of
the estimated total genome [3]. Method: 1.57 Gb ÷ 0.809 ≈ 1.94 Gb. This
is a single-source implied estimate, not independently cross-checked
against a second assembly or a genome-size-database C-value (none
found); treat as approximate.
