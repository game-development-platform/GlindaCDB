# Dill (*Anethum graveolens*)

**Category:** Spice/Herb · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/spice-herb/dill.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-02

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

Like cilantro/coriander, "dill weed" (fresh/dried leaf) and "dill seed"
are the same plant and the same crop entry, not two separate crops — but
unlike cilantro, this document finds the two organs' own essential-oil
chemistry is genuinely different, not merely a difference in which part
is eaten (see Finding 4).

This project's cilantro and cumin research are the closest siblings —
all three are Apiaceae seed/leaf dual-product crops. Dill's own leaf-
vs-seed timing split (40-45 days to leaf, 85-105 days to seed, per one
named cultivar) is structurally similar to cilantro's own dual-harvest
shape, but dill's own chemistry split between the two organs is a new
finding not present in either sibling crop's research.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — chromosome number is cleanly sourced as diploid (2n=22,
x=11), consistent across cultivated and wild accessions, but genome size
(in picograms or megabases) could not be sourced at all despite a
dedicated search — a genuinely different, and in one respect worse,
sourcing outcome than this project found for either cilantro or cumin,
both of which at least had some genome-size figure (however disputed).**
The best-supported chromosome count is 2n=22, with a base number of
x=11, reported consistently for both cultivated and wild accessions by a
plant-genetics database entry attributing the count to a 2020 source
[1]. An older chromosome-count database record separately gives 11 (from
a 1976 source), which this document interprets as the haploid count
(n=11), not a conflicting ploidy claim, consistent with the 2n=22 figure
rather than contradicting it [1]. No nuclear genome-size figure (pg or
Mb) was found for dill specifically despite a dedicated search — the
only genome-size-adjacent figure located was a related Apiaceae crop's
own chloroplast genome size, which this document does not substitute for
a nuclear genome-size estimate, since chloroplast and nuclear genome
sizes are entirely different quantities [1]. This is a genuine, flagged
sourcing gap, not an estimate.

**Finding 2 — dill is reported strongly protandrous and genuinely
pollinator-dependent, with a sourced caged-exclusion experiment (not
merely a descriptive claim) showing very poor seed set without insect
access — a stronger, more direct pollination-dependency finding than
this project found for either cilantro or cumin.** Dill is described as
a cross-pollinated crop with small yellow flowers, strongly protandrous,
with pollinators including both flies and bees [2]. Critically, one
source reports that caged plants excluded from insect visitors set very
few seeds [2] — a controlled-exclusion finding, a stronger form of
evidence for pollinator dependency than this project's cilantro research
(a general "allogamous" description) or cumin research (a self-
pollination-rate claim), neither of which cites a comparable experiment.
No explicit statement on self-compatibility (as opposed to self-
pollination rate) was found this pass, so this document does not claim
dill is self-incompatible, only that it is reported to perform poorly
without pollinator access.

**Finding 3 — pest and disease pressure is reported strikingly low
relative to other spice/herb Apiaceae crops this project has
researched, with sources actively disagreeing only on how low, not on
the general direction.** One extension source describes dill as
"usually disease-free," occasionally bothered by aphids [3]; a second
describes it as "generally disease-free" [3]; a third, describing
pollinator-attracting qualities, separately notes it draws "medium
numbers of lygus bugs and small numbers of leafhoppers, leaf beetles and
aphids" [3] — these sources broadly agree dill is a low-pressure crop,
with only the third source naming a specific pest complex at all, and
even that description frames the insects in the context of beneficial-
insect/pollinator attraction, not crop damage. This is a genuine,
striking contrast with this project's cumin research (severe Fusarium
wilt, 35-80% yield loss) or its cilantro research (stem gall explicitly
named "the most important disease") — dill is the first Apiaceae seed-
spice crop this project has researched with no comparably severe,
quantified disease-loss figure, a genuine agronomic-ease finding, not
merely an absence of research.

**Finding 4 — dill weed (leaf) and dill seed carry genuinely different
dominant essential-oil chemistry, a real organ-level chemistry split not
found in either cilantro or cumin, both of which treat leaf and seed as
sharing (or not separately characterizing) their aroma chemistry.** An
extension source states plainly that carvone is the chief essential-oil
component of dill seed, responsible for its characteristic odor, while
the odor of dill weed (leaf) is instead due primarily to alpha-
phellandrene and dill ether [4] — two entirely different named
compounds dominating two different organs of the same plant. This
genuinely echoes this project's cumin research (an irrigation-driven
dominant-compound-switching finding) in structure (a named compound
identity determining the product's own character) but differs in
mechanism: cumin's switch is driven by water management at one
developmental stage of the same organ (the seed), while dill's split is
a standing, organ-level difference between two different organs (leaf
vs. seed) of the same plant, present regardless of water management as
far as this document's sources indicate. No percentage figures for
carvone, alpha-phellandrene, or dill ether were found this pass.

**Finding 5 — named cultivars show a sourced, large (roughly 2x) spread
in days-to-leaf-harvest timing and an explicit, named bolting-resistance
trait distinguishing cultivars, with heat and drought specifically
identified as the bolting trigger, the same general trigger this
project found for cilantro but without cilantro's own quantified
genotype range.** One cultivar is reported at roughly 40-45 days to leaf
harvest and 85-105 days to seed harvest from one supplier [5]; a second,
dwarf cultivar is described as the slowest-bolting dwarf dill available,
with leaves ready in 40-50 days from a different source [5] — broadly
consistent with the first cultivar's own leaf timing despite the
different growth habit. A third cultivar is separately named as the
most widely grown cultivar for both dill weed and dill seed production
[5] — a dual-purpose-cultivar finding not paralleled by any single named
cultivar in this project's cilantro or cumin research (both of which
named yield-optimized seed cultivars specifically, not explicitly
dual-purpose ones). An extension source states that hot weather and dry
spells cause bolting, and that repeated cutting back of foliage can
delay bloom [5] — a sourced, actionable cultivation lever (cutting
delays bolting) not found in either sibling crop's research. The first
cultivar is separately described by its own supplier as "somewhat"
drought and heat tolerant [5] — a hedged, seller-sourced claim, flagged
as weaker than the extension-source findings above.

**Finding 6 — dill's own authoritative pet-safety listing is the
cleanest, most directly located primary-source toxicity finding this
project has found for any crop in its spice/herb research, confirmed
non-toxic with a narrowly scoped, specific caveat.** An authoritative
pet-safety reference's own listing, located directly via a targeted
search, lists dill as non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses [6]. The only
toxic principle that reference's own page names is "essential oils,"
with the only clinical sign listed being dermatitis from prolonged
contact [6] — a narrowly scoped, topical-contact-only caveat, not an
ingestion hazard, genuinely different in kind from this project's
cilantro research (a fully clean non-toxic finding with no caveat at
all) or its cumin research (an unresolved toxicity gap). Several
secondary sources repeat that reference's own classification faithfully,
though one lower-quality source overstates it, describing the
reference as treating dill essential oils specifically as "likely toxic"
[6] — this document treats that characterization as an overstatement of
the reference's own actual wording (which names only prolonged-contact
dermatitis, not a "likely toxic" classification), and does not adopt the
stronger secondary framing.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual, herbaceous — true annual, same general
  shape as cilantro and cumin.
- **Climate:** No USDA-equivalent hardiness-zone figure was found this
  pass.
- **Food status:** Raw edible — fresh leaf eaten raw/as garnish/pickling
  flavoring; dried seed used whole or ground as a spice.
- **Pollination:** Not required for leaf harvest; required for seed
  harvest — strongly protandrous, cross-pollinated, with a sourced
  caged-exclusion experiment showing very poor seed set without insect
  access (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Seed yield figures scatter widely by row
  spacing, nitrogen rate, and population: a Sicilian trial found the
  narrowest row spacing (30 cm) gave over 2 t/ha; a Punjab study
  reported a range from over 2 t/ha down to under 0.8 t/ha depending on
  plant population; an Indian trial found 50x10 cm spacing gave the
  highest yield at 1,504 kg/ha, with 75 kg N/ha the best nitrogen rate
  tested [7]. No leaf (dill weed)-specific yield-per-hectare figure was
  independently sourced this pass — a Danish freeze-drying study found
  total yield rose with seed rate but the leaf share of that yield fell
  as seed rate rose, and the highest fresh-herb yield came from the
  closest plant spacing tested (10 cm within rows, 15 cm between rows)
  [7] — a genuinely actionable, sourced leaf-vs-seed-yield tradeoff by
  planting density, paralleling this project's cilantro research (a
  product-dependent seed-rate finding) but expressed here as a density/
  yield-share tradeoff rather than a simple seed-rate difference.
- **Water use:** Not independently sourced this pass beyond the general
  "hot weather and dry spells cause bolting" finding (Finding 5) — no
  specific crop coefficient or irrigation-regime figure sourced.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, the sole propagation route found
  this pass — no vegetative propagation route was sourced or expected
  for this true annual. Seed rate recommended at 10 kg/ha for maximum
  seed yield in one Punjab study, sown mid-October in 30 cm rows with 90
  kg N/ha [7] — broadly similar in magnitude to this project's cilantro
  seed-crop rate (10-25 kg/ha) and cumin rate (12-15 kg/ha).
- **Soil:** Not independently sourced this pass.
- **Stand life:** True annual — dual-harvest tension between leaf and
  seed products, same general shape as cilantro, with a sourced
  density-based yield-share tradeoff (closer spacing favors leaf share;
  seed rate increases favor total yield but reduce leaf share) [7].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Strikingly low pest/disease pressure
  relative to other Apiaceae crops this project has researched —
  described as "usually" or "generally" disease-free by two extension
  sources, with aphids the only consistently named pest, and a third
  source separately naming a low-intensity pest complex (lygus bugs,
  leafhoppers, leaf beetles, aphids) in the context of pollinator/
  beneficial-insect attraction rather than crop damage (Finding 3).
- **Genome:** No genome-size figure was found despite a dedicated
  search — a genuine sourcing gap. Chromosome number well-converged:
  2n=22 (x=11), consistent across cultivated and wild accessions
  (Finding 1).
- **Cultivars:** One cultivar (40-45 days to leaf, 85-105 days to seed;
  "somewhat" drought/heat tolerant per its own seller). A second, dwarf
  cultivar explicitly marketed as the slowest-bolting dwarf dill (40-50
  days to leaf). A third cultivar named as the most widely grown
  cultivar for both dill weed and dill seed production — a genuinely
  dual-purpose-named cultivar. Two further cultivars named by a seed
  catalogue but not independently characterized with sourced trait
  deltas this pass.
- **Harvest:** Two distinct products from the same plant at genuinely
  different developmental stages — leaf (pre-bolting, repeated cutting,
  dominated by alpha-phellandrene/dill-ether chemistry) and seed (post-
  flowering/fruiting, single harvest, dominated by carvone chemistry)
  (Finding 4) — a formal dual-harvest-stage system with a genuine organ-
  level chemistry split, not just a timing split, the first such finding
  this project has made in its spice/herb Apiaceae research. Single
  window per planting (one bolting/flowering event per annual cycle).

## Hazards

Strikingly low pest/disease pressure relative to other Apiaceae crops
this project has researched — described as "usually" or "generally"
disease-free, with aphids the only consistently named pest (Finding 3).
An authoritative pet-safety reference's own direct listing confirms the
plant non-toxic to dogs/cats/horses, with the sole, narrowly scoped
caveat being essential-oil-contact dermatitis from prolonged topical
contact, not an ingestion hazard (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (leaf):** fresh leaf → repeat-cut harvested pre-bolting → used
  fresh/raw, or as pickling flavoring. **Chain (seed):** plant allowed to
  bolt/flower/fruit → single harvest post-flowering → dried → used whole
  or ground as a spice.
