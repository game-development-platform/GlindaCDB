# Queensland Arrowroot / Edible Canna (*Canna edulis*)

## Naming and scope

*Canna edulis* (achira, purple arrowroot, among many other names),
family Cannaceae, order Zingiberales — the same order as true
arrowroot (a different family this project has also researched) but a
different family, sharing only the general starchy-rhizome habit
[2][3]. **Naming caution:** most botanical sources now fold this
cultivated crop into the broader species *Canna indica*, and general
references on "Canna indica" commonly describe this exact crop [3][5].
This entry treats it as one cultivated starch crop with one economic
organ (the rhizome) — not the many ornamental canna varieties grown
purely for flowers.

This crop shares the general starchy-rhizome extraction pattern with
arrowroot, a related crop this project has also researched, and shares
wet-rhizome water-use characteristics with taro, another related root
crop this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the largest starch granules of any plant, which defines
the entire processing route.** This crop's starch granules measure
roughly 30-100 µm — about three times the size of potato starch
granules, and visible to the naked eye. This means the washed starch
settles quickly, and its high amylose content (25-30%) sets into clear,
firm gels [6]. North Vietnam specifically exploits this property to
make transparent "glass" noodles that reportedly "totally replaced
expensive mung-bean starch" in that industry [6]. The rhizome-to-starch
extraction route itself (wash, grate, sieve, settle, dry) closely
mirrors the process used for arrowroot.

**Finding 2 — a real industrial-scale crop, with unusually strong
yield data for a starch rhizome.** North Vietnam grows an estimated
20,000-30,000 ha of this crop; one specific northern province alone
reported 475 ha and 25,000 t of production in 2022 (implying roughly
53.6 t/ha), with documented field trials reaching 30.4-38.4 t/ha [6]. A
classic reference work on Andean root and tuber crops gives a broader
Andean yield range of 13-85 t/ha, averaging 22-50 t/ha [2]. Historic
Queensland production, by contrast, ran only about 11-22 t/ha,
illustrating a dramatic yield gap between traditional smallholder
production and modern industrial cultivation. The crop takes roughly
6 months minimum in warm climates, 8-10 months for the larger
documented yields, and 10-12 months under Vietnamese growing
conditions [1][2][6].

**Finding 3 — rhizome composition figures conflict significantly
across sources, unresolved.** One general reference gives fresh
rhizome at 73% water and 24% starch [3]; the Andean reference work
gives roughly 25% starch fresh and 75-80% of dry matter, with higher
starch in sterile triploid types [2]; Vietnamese industry data give
22-25% dry matter and only 12-16% actually extractable starch on a wet
basis [6]; another growing-guide source claims up to roughly 80%
starch on a dry basis plus up to 14% sugars [1]. Food-composition
summaries separately put the cooked rhizome at roughly 123-130 kcal,
66-67 g water, 0.9-1.4 g protein, and about 31 g carbohydrate per 100
g [7]. The Andean reference work also separately describes the tuber
as "quite nutritious, containing at least 10% protein" — a claim the
same source does not reconcile with its own, much lower 1-3% dry-matter
protein figure elsewhere, almost certainly reflecting a shoot-versus-
rhizome or dry-versus-fresh basis mix-up within that one source.
Commercial starch recovery (roughly 12-16% of fresh weight) is the
figure actually used for extraction-chain modeling here [6].

**Finding 4 — the complete glass-noodle production chain.** After the
washed starch settles out, steam turns the wet starch and water
mixture into thin gel sheets, which are stretched and semi-dried on
bamboo frames, folded, cut into noodles, and dried down to roughly
18-21% residual moisture [4]. Village-scale equipment consists of
wooden drum graters and simple sedimentation tanks; the leftover sieve
residue is a documented dietary-fiber byproduct, separately
characterized in both Colombian and Chinese sources [4].

**Finding 5 — a genuinely multi-organ crop.** Rhizomes are baked (in
Peru, traditionally for up to roughly 12 hours in an earth oven) or
boiled, and can technically be eaten raw, though raw rhizome is
described as "a fibrous, watery potato" [1][2]. Young shoots are
cooked as a green vegetable [2]. The plant's large leaves serve as food
wrappers or plates for steamed dishes, and as livestock fodder [2][5].
Immature seeds are cooked into a specific traditional fried dish, while
hard mature seeds are used for beads and rattle fillings rather than
eaten [2][5]. As animal feed, both rhizomes (fresh dry matter around
24%, roughly 3.7% protein on a dry-matter basis, with high organic-
matter digestibility) and leaves (lower dry matter but notably higher
protein content on a dry-matter basis) are fed to cattle and pigs,
notably in at least one documented tropical feeding system, typically
cut 4-8 months after planting [5].

**Finding 6 — a systemic virus problem affecting the clonal planting
stock.** Rhizome infection by several named viruses is systemic, with
"no cure except meristem culture" — once a rhizome line is infected,
the virus travels with every piece of planting stock propagated from
it [1]. Because propagation is almost entirely by rhizome piece,
edible plantings must be kept isolated from ornamental canna
varieties to avoid cross-contamination and virus spread. This crop
joins several other clonally propagated root crops this project has
researched in sharing this systemic-virus vulnerability. The crop is
also affected by a specific rust fungus and two other fungal diseases,
while slugs tunnel exposed rhizomes and voles (described as the worst
pest) eat both rhizomes and buds [1].

**Finding 7 — a documented weed and volunteer-escape risk.** This
crop is declared invasive or weed-prone in several countries and
regions, and "can escape from cultivation and become a weed" [2]. This
places it among a small group of crops this project has researched
that share this volunteer/invasive persistence risk.

**Finding 8 — true seed is not the commercial propagation route but
has extraordinary longevity.** Seeds have a very hard coat requiring
scarification (chipping or filing, plus a 24-48 hour soak) before they
will germinate; they keep viable for 10+ years at 10°C, and one
source repeats an unverified claim of 550-year-old seed successfully
germinating. Seed-raised plants can flower in their first year, but
seed is used only for breeding and for maintaining certain fertile
diploid lines that reproduce true from seed — the actual commercial
method is entirely clonal [1][2].

**Finding 9 — ploidy drives both fertility and starch content.** Most
commercial cultivars are sterile triploids, which carry a higher
starch fraction than fertile diploid types; some fertile diploids are
self-compatible and reproduce true from seed, while others are
male-sterile and require a separate pollinator plant [1]. No nuclear
genome-size measurement has been published for this species; only its
chloroplast genome has been sequenced, the first complete one published
for its family [9]. Fewer than 50 named varieties exist, many nearly
identical, differing mostly in skin color [1].

**Finding 10 — no photoperiod dependence for tuberization, frost-
killed foliage but freeze-recovering rhizomes, and a very wide
elevation tolerance.** Rhizome formation and flowering are photoperiod-
neutral once the plant reaches sufficient size — a genuine contrast
with several short-day-triggered tuber crops this project has
researched. Above-ground foliage dies at around -2°C (with young
growth already harmed at 0°C), but the plant regrows from the rhizome
as long as the soil freezes only an inch or two deep; it is perennial
in warmer climate zones and can still be grown as an annual-harvest
crop in somewhat cooler zones given an adequate growing season.
Optimum growing temperature is roughly 20-30°C, with a tolerated range
of roughly 9-32°C; growth is very slow below 10°C and damaged by
sustained temperatures above 32°C. The crop is documented growing from
sea level up to roughly 2,600-2,900 m elevation [1][2]. It is
susceptible to wind lodging above roughly 40 mph, with one source
suggesting a height cap around 2 m specifically to reduce this damage
[1].

**Finding 11 — harvest timing and holding behavior.** Eating quality
peaks at the first harvest; rhizomes become progressively more fibrous
if left in the ground for multiple years, so unlike some other root
crops, there is no quality benefit to extended in-ground storage. A
standing clump can, however, be partially harvested by slicing
straight down a short distance from the base and pulling out the
severed rhizome ends while the rest of the plant keeps growing [1].
Fresh postharvest storage is quite short — only about 3-4 weeks at
roughly 7°C — and no real preservation method beyond prompt starch
processing is documented: canning is unsuitable, and drying the fresh
rhizome directly "gives poor results." In mild climates, the
recommended practice is simply to leave the crop in the ground and
dig it as needed [1].

**Finding 12 — a documented religious/festival food identity.** In a
specific Peruvian valley, the rhizome was traditionally eaten at a
major Catholic feast day, with harvest timed to the week before the
observance [10]. In Colombia, flour made from this crop's rhizome is
the base ingredient of a well-known regional gluten-free, clay-oven-
baked biscuit, with one specific department being the largest producer
[11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season perennial, modeled here as an
  annual dig crop; planted once soil has warmed, with best growth at
  20-30°C (Finding 10).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of the whole rhizome mass per
  cycle; a standing clump can also be partially harvested while
  continuing to grow, a documented harvest nuance (Finding 11).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, warm-temperate, and (for an
  annual-harvest cycle) temperate zones, given an adequate frost-free
  growing season (Finding 10).
- **Food status:** Technically raw-edible but of poor quality raw
  ("a fibrous, watery potato"); normally baked or boiled (Finding 5).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the rhizome crop, which is
  propagated and harvested clonally before flowering becomes relevant;
  the seed-breeding pathway involves mixed self-compatible and
  male-sterile diploid lines (Finding 8, 9).
- **Yield / composition:** A dramatic range exists between traditional
  smallholder yields (roughly 11-50 t/ha) and modern industrial
  Vietnamese production (consistently 30-55 t/ha, with documented
  trial yields up to 38.4 t/ha and a provincial average of 53.6 t/ha)
  (Finding 2).
- **Water use:** A preferred rainfall range of roughly 1,200-1,700 mm
  is documented, with a much wider tolerated range; drought slows
  growth sharply and toughens the rhizome, while waterlogged soil
  increases disease pressure and lowers eating quality (Finding 3, 11;
  see also Estimates below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from rhizome tips or
  large rhizome pieces with buds; true seed germinates poorly and
  slowly and is used only for breeding (Finding 8; establishment detail
  below).
- **Labor:** No sourced canna-specific labor-hour budget exists; labor
  estimates used here are scaled up by analogy from a sourced arrowroot
  labor budget, reflecting this crop's larger, deeper rhizome mass.
- **Soil:** Tolerates a very wide pH range, from neutral down to
  strongly acidic soils (as low as roughly pH 4.5); prefers deep, rich,
  well-drained soil but tolerates heavy soils; benefits from high
  nitrogen early in growth, withheld later to favor larger rhizomes
  (Finding 10; soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** A perennial clump-forming plant, grown here as an
  annual dig crop; rhizomes can be left in the ground for multiple
  years but gain no quality benefit from doing so, only toughening
  over time (Finding 11).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Several systemic, incurable (except by
  meristem culture) viruses affect the clonal planting stock; a
  specific rust fungus and two other fungal diseases are documented;
  slugs and voles (the worst pest) damage rhizomes and buds (Finding
  6).
- **Cultivars:** Fewer than 50 named varieties exist, spanning ploidy
  level, skin color, and regional end-use (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single annual dig of the whole rhizome mass, or
  repeated partial harvest from a standing clump that continues
  growing (Finding 11).
- **Storage:** Short fresh storage only — roughly 3-4 weeks at about
  7°C; no effective longer-term preservation method is documented
  beyond prompt processing into starch; in mild climates the crop is
  typically left in the ground and dug as needed instead of stored
  (Finding 11).

## Hazards

A specific rust fungus and two other fungal diseases are documented
pests of this crop; slugs tunnel exposed rhizomes, and voles (described
as the worst pest) eat both rhizomes and buds. Several systemic viruses
affect the clonal planting stock, with no cure except meristem culture
(Finding 6).

No significant human-health or livestock hazard was found for this
crop's rhizome — it is rated as a generally benign starchy food with
no known special toxicity, in contrast to the pungent mustard-family
root crops this project has also researched.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** rhizome tip or piece planted → grown roughly 6-12 months
  depending on climate → dug (as a single annual harvest, or
  repeatedly from a standing clump) → washed, peeled → eaten baked or
  boiled, or (raw, lower quality) eaten fresh. Separately: washed
  rhizome → grated → sieved → starch settled out and dried → used
  directly as starch, or milled into flour. Starch and water → steamed
  into gel sheets → stretched and semi-dried on bamboo frames → folded,
  cut, and dried into glass noodles. Young shoots are cooked as a
  green vegetable; leaves are used as food wrappers/plates and as
  livestock fodder; immature seeds are cooked into a traditional fried
  dish.
- Not covered by this research in detail: the sieve-residue dietary-
  fiber byproduct's specific industrial uses; hard mature seed
  processing into beads or rattle fillings as a craft practice; the
  specific breeding methods used to maintain fertile, seed-true diploid
  lines.

In a specific Peruvian valley, this crop's rhizome was traditionally
eaten at Corpus Christi, with harvest timed to the week before the
observance. In Colombia, flour from this crop is the base of a
well-known regional gluten-free biscuit.

## Open research questions

- Rhizome starch and nutrition figures conflict substantially across
  sources — one source's own internal figures for protein content (a
  "quite nutritious, at least 10% protein" claim versus a separately
  stated 1-3% dry-matter figure) do not reconcile with each other,
  almost certainly reflecting a basis mix-up (shoot vs. rhizome, or
  dry vs. fresh) within that one source — not resolved here.
- The documented elevation ceiling for this crop differs slightly
  across sources (roughly 2,600 m vs. 2,900 m) — not a major
  discrepancy, but not resolved.
- An unverified claim of 550-year-old seed successfully germinating
  is repeated in one source without independent verification; this
  entry treats it as hedged, not established fact.
- No sourced labor-hour budget exists specifically for this crop; the
  labor estimates used here are scaled by analogy from a sourced
  arrowroot labor budget and should be replaced by a dedicated study
  if one becomes available.
- No sourced planting-material (rhizome set) weight was found; the
  seeding-rate and multiplication-ratio figures used here are derived
  from an assumed set weight combined with documented spacing figures.
- No nuclear genome-size measurement has been published for this
  species; only its chloroplast genome has been sequenced.
- Whether the wild/naturalized form of this broader species should be
  treated as a separate wild-gathered food source is a marginal,
  unresolved question — the cultivated starch type modeled in this
  entry is a distinct clonal crop from any wild-gathered use.
- Soil nutrient-uptake figures used here are estimated by analogy to
  arrowroot and taro, since no dedicated removal-rate study for this
  crop was found.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric content (roughly 1,250 kcal/kg) and protein (roughly 10 g/kg)
are derived from food-composition summaries giving roughly 123-130
kcal and 0.9-1.4 g protein per 100 g, taking the midpoint; the broader
plausible range is roughly 1,000-1,400 kcal/kg and 9-14 g/kg protein,
given the composition conflicts noted above (a separately documented
"at least 10% protein" dry-basis claim is not used, since it does not
reconcile with the same source's own other figures).

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, seasonal water-use
estimate) are estimated by analogy to taro, a related wet-rhizome crop
this project has also researched, since no standard crop-water-use
reference includes a dedicated row for this crop; this crop likely
tolerates somewhat drier soil than paddy-grown taro, so the mid-season
water-use figure used here may run slightly high. A drought-buffer
estimate of about 4 days before yield penalty begins, and an associated
daily yield-penalty rate, are estimated by the same analogy.

Planting-density and seed-rate figures (roughly 2,500 kg/ha of rhizome
sets, implying a seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of about 10 on a
mass basis) are derived from documented spacing figures across sources,
combined with an assumed, unsourced set weight of roughly 100-130 g
per piece.

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are scaled up by analogy
from a sourced arrowroot labor budget (reflecting a documented total
labor requirement for that crop), adjusted upward specifically for
harvest labor to reflect this crop's larger, deeper rhizome mass
requiring more hand-digging effort.

Soil nutrient-uptake figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of rhizome) are estimated by analogy to arrowroot and taro,
since no dedicated nutrient-removal study for this crop was found;
this crop's larger rhizome and documented "high nitrogen early, then
withhold" management practice could plausibly raise its actual
nitrogen demand above the analogous figures used here.
