## Naming and scope

Garden beet, red beet, beetroot: *Beta vulgaris* L. subsp. *vulgaris*,
Conditiva Group (including golden and white-fleshed types), family
Amaranthaceae. This entry covers the root beet grown primarily for its
swollen storage root and, secondarily, for its leaves (beet greens).
A separate leaf-only beet crop, grown only for its foliage, is a
closely related but separately scoped sibling entry this project has
also researched (Swiss chard). Red, golden, white, zoned, and
cylindrical types differ in color and shape but share the same growing
practice, so they are treated here as cultivars rather than separate
entries.

This is the same species as sugar beet, Swiss chard, and a fodder-type
cultivar group this project has not yet researched. This entry is
grounded against this project's own prior research into sugar beet
(the same species, researched first within this species group) and
into Swiss chard (the same species, researched after this entry).

Shared background across all members of this species: biennial seed
biology (vernalization, overwintered-root and seed-to-seed seed-
production methods, wind pollination, strict isolation from every
other cultivar group of the same species), a multigerm seed-cluster
structure alongside a monogerm variant, cytoplasmic-male-sterility-
based hybrid seed, leaf oxalate and nitrate content, and genome size.
This entry's own distinguishing features are a comparatively short
50-80-day root cycle, a lower salt-tolerance threshold than sugar beet,
physical (rather than sugar-value) storage decay, a red pigment
(betalain) as the defining quality trait, and a distinct food-
processing chain.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a 50-80-day root that stores for months, in sharp
contrast to sugar beet's much longer sugar-crop cycle.** Round
cultivars mature in roughly 60-70 days and cylindrical cultivars in
roughly 70-80 days in one regional trial; a separate regional cultivar
recommendation list gives a shorter range, and a standard international
crop-water-use reference gives roughly 70-90 days for Mediterranean and
arid-region sowings [1][2][4]. Fresh-market roots are typically
harvested at roughly 3.8-7.6 cm diameter; bunching beets (sold with
tops attached) are pulled somewhat smaller, and greens can be cut even
earlier [2][5][8]. Topped roots keep roughly four to six months at
near-freezing temperature and very high humidity, with processing
stock keeping even longer; a cold root cellar keeps late-autumn beets
roughly three to five months, while bunched beets with tops attached
last only 10-14 days [2][3][5]. Unlike sugar beet, whose stored roots
lose sugar value continuously, this crop's storage loss is purely
physical: roots shrivel in low humidity, decay within about a week if
kept too warm, and sprout or re-root if storage is too warm [3].

**Finding 2 — color is the defining quality trait, and warm weather
degrades it (a phenomenon called zoning).** The red pigment is a
water-soluble betalain-class compound (not the anthocyanin pigment
found in many other red vegetables), so washing, cutting, and water
contact during processing are kept brief to limit pigment loss [3][8].
Best commercial color and quality develop at roughly 10-18°C; rapid
growth in warm weather gives lighter color and visible light-colored
rings within the root, called zoning [2]. Cool nights favor pigment
development, and several specific cultivars are documented resisting
zoning even in warm regional summers; the light zones mostly disappear
on cooking [2][4][5]. Sugar content is also highest under cool
temperatures with good sunlight [5].

**Finding 3 — multigerm seed clusters require thinning, with monogerm
and hybrid alternatives available.** A seed unit of this crop is
actually a dried fruit containing one to three true seeds, so several
seedlings typically emerge together from a single planted unit;
thinning must happen while seedlings are still small, since later
thinning damages the plants left behind [2][5]. Seed suppliers quote a
"sprout count" per seed unit to set precision-planting rates, and
processing-grade seed is sized to a documented seed-count-per-pound
range [2][4]. Single-seed (monogerm) cultivars have existed since
around 1980, and current monogerm hybrids remain available [2][5]. A
majority of cultivars recommended in one regional guide are F1
hybrids, made using cytoplasmic male sterility — the same hybrid-
breeding system documented for sugar beet [2][10][14].

**Finding 4 — moderately salt-tolerant as a mature root, but sensitive
as a seedling.** A standard international reference lists this crop's
storage root at a salinity threshold of roughly 4.0 dS/m with a yield-
loss slope of about 9% per additional dS/m, categorized as moderately
tolerant — a notably lower threshold than sugar beet's considerably
higher documented threshold. Both crops are documented as sensitive
during germination and emergence, with salinity recommended not to
exceed roughly 3 dS/m at that stage [7].

**Finding 5 — boron deficiency causes a specific internal and external
root disorder.** This crop has a documented high boron requirement.
Boron deficiency causes black spots inside roots and larger black dry
rot patches on the root surface; this disorder is most likely on
alkaline, high-calcium soils and is worsened by dry conditions [2].
Seed growers broadcast boron before planting specifically to guard
against a related canker disorder in the seed crop [6].

**Finding 6 — seed production uses two wind-pollinated methods, both
requiring strict isolation from every other member of the species.**
An overwintered-root (steckling) method sows roots by early summer,
lifts and tops them in late autumn, stores them over winter in crates
at a specific cool temperature or in insulated field pits, discards
off-type roots the following spring, and replants in early spring [6].
A seed-to-seed method instead sows as late as midsummer and
overwinters the plants directly in the field; seed growers are
documented objecting to this method's poor winter hardiness and the
lost opportunity to discard off-type roots before they flower [6].
Harvest timing is judged by stalk-base browning and mid-stalk seed
reaching the hard-dough stage; stalks are cut, cured in windrows, and
then threshed with reduced machine settings to avoid damaging the seed
coat, since later cutting loses seed to shattering and bird feeding
[6]. Documented seed yields average several hundred pounds per acre,
with well-managed fields reaching considerably more [6]. This crop may
reach full vernalization (cold-requirement) saturation in as little as
four weeks at a specific cool temperature, according to one source
[13].

**Finding 7 — a crop with specific religious roles in Judaism.** This
crop is one of several symbolic foods eaten on the Jewish New Year,
drawn from a rabbinic list in the Talmud; the relevant Hebrew word is
read as a pun on "removed," tied to a prayer asking that misfortune be
removed [19]. At the Passover meal, a named Talmudic sage is recorded
naming beets and rice as two cooked dishes eaten that night, and a
separate sage is recorded specifically seeking them out; some
Ashkenazi Passover seders still serve a cooked condiment made from this
root combined with grated horseradish (a crop this project has already
researched), and beet-based borscht and a fermented beet beverage are
both documented as traditional Passover preparations [18][19].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial grown as a root
  annual; grown in fall, winter, and spring in some warmer regions, with
  a specific sowing window for Mediterranean and arid regions (Finding
  1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per planting — pulled or
  topped and dug once, though bunching beets within a stand can be
  pulled selectively over time.
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate, temperate, and Mediterranean (autumn and
  winter) zones; subtropical zones in a cool season; semi-arid and arid
  zones under irrigation with a specific early-spring sowing window.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (roots grated raw; young leaves in
  salads).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production, since the root
  crop is harvested before flowering; the seed crop is wind-pollinated.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields vary
  considerably between fresh-market and processing production, roughly
  8,000-56,000 kg/ha depending on market and growing conditions
  (Finding 1; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** A documented rooting depth of roughly 0.6-1.0 m and a
  fairly generous allowable soil-moisture depletion before irrigation
  is needed; poorly aerated or flooded soil favors damping-off and
  root rot (water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded at a documented depth and spacing,
  thinned to a final in-row spacing; processing stands are precision-
  planted at a denser spacing to skip hand-thinning (establishment
  detail below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour survey was found for this crop;
  labor figures for several operations are carried over from sugar
  beet (the same species, same seed-cluster structure, similar row
  culture), while harvesting and planting labor are separately
  estimated.
- **Soil:** Deep, friable, well-drained mucks, sands, sandy loams, and
  silt loams; a documented moderate pH optimum, tolerant of neutral and
  some alkaline soils, though alkaline high-calcium soils raise the
  risk of boron-deficiency disorders (soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** Biennial in natural life cycle, harvested as an
  annual for its root; the seed crop requires a full second season.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Several fungal leaf-spot diseases,
  damping-off pathogens, a soil fungus causing pocket rot and related
  root disorders, aphids, leafminers, flea beetles, beet armyworm, and
  webworms are documented, along with a viral disease restricted to
  certain seed-growing regions (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named red, golden, white, zoned, and
  cylindrical open-pollinated and F1 hybrid cultivars, including
  monogerm hybrids that eliminate hand-thinning (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** Baby/bunching, mature, and greens harvest stages, with
  a documented harvest window of several weeks for a single planting
  (harvest-stage detail below).
- **Storage:** A long, cold, very humid storage regime for topped
  roots (four to six months, with processing stock keeping longer),
  in sharp contrast to a much shorter bunched-with-tops storage life
  (Finding 1).

Water detail: documented total seasonal water-use figures for one
specific growing region fall in the range of roughly 300-450 mm,
depending on whether seasonal evapotranspiration or net irrigation is
measured; sandy soils may need irrigation every three to four days at
peak water demand in that region.

Establishment detail: fresh-market stands are typically direct-seeded
at a shallow depth in rows with a documented row spacing, thinned to a
specific final in-row spacing; processing stands use narrower row
spacing and are precision-planted to a final stand without hand-
thinning; a specific wider bed-and-row layout is documented for baby-
beet production.

Soil detail: fertilizer guidance documents a substantial nitrogen
requirement alongside phosphorus, potassium, and a notably high boron
requirement, with the latter specifically guarding against the
boron-deficiency root disorder described in Finding 5; crop rotation
of two to three years between beet-family plantings is recommended
against leaf-spot disease buildup.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include an early-20th-century
globe-shaped deep-red open-pollinated type with a strain resistant to
zoning; an early, fast-maturing flat-globe red type grown for both
greens and bunching; a 19th-century flat red heirloom with visible
purplish zoning in warm weather; a mid-20th-century round red type
used mainly for processing; a cylindrical red type valued for uniform
slicing; an Italian heirloom with alternating red-and-white internal
rings that fade on cooking; a gold-fleshed type that does not bleed red
color; a white-fleshed type; a red-leaved type grown for its leaves as
well as its root; an F1 hybrid noted for leaf-spot tolerance and zoning
resistance; a monogerm F1 hybrid requiring no thinning; and a late-
maturing long-rooted heirloom noted for good storage life.

Harvest-stage detail: a baby/bunching stage (roots pulled with tops
attached at a small size, bunched for sale); a mature stage (topped
and dug at full size, the primary stage for storage, processing, and
most culinary and ceremonial uses); and a greens stage (young leaves
cut for salad use, available somewhat earlier than root maturity).

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's roots, juice, and leaves contain
nitrate, a plant compound of dose-dependent concern especially for
infants under roughly six months of age, since nitrate can convert to
nitrite (particularly in cut or cooked vegetables stored warm) and
cause a blood-oxygen-carrying disorder in young infants; deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 7 context; general nutrition sourcing).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's leaves contain oxalate, at levels
documented somewhat higher than the closely related leaf-only beet
sibling crop's leaves; deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's red pigment can harmlessly color
urine and stool in some people after consumption, a benign pigment
effect rather than a true hazard, but worth flagging so it is not
mistaken for a bleeding symptom; deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — livestock fed this crop's leaves or roots face
oxalate and nitrate/nitrite exposure (worse in leaves after a dry
spell) and a risk of rumen acidosis from excess root feeding; deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

Several fungal leaf-spot diseases, damping-off pathogens (affecting
roots and crowns in cool, wet soils), aphids, leafminers, flea beetles,
beet armyworm, and webworms are documented pests and diseases; a viral
disease historically restricted seed production in one specific region,
and certain true bugs are documented pests of the seed crop
specifically. Storage diseases include a bacterial soft rot of bunched
leaves and stems, a black rot affecting the root tip, and a gray mold
that grows slowly even at near-freezing storage temperatures.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → sown (multigerm, thinned, or monogerm direct to a
  stand) → baby/bunched beets with tops, mature roots, or greens.
  Mature roots → topped and sorted (greens and culls as by-products) →
  stored cold and very humid. Roots → cooked and peeled → pickled in
  sweetened vinegar (canning itself is not covered by this research).
  Raw roots → lacto-fermented in a dilute salt brine for several days
  → fermented beets and a fermented beet beverage. Raw roots → pressed
  → juice and press cake; juice → filtered and evaporated → a natural
  red food colorant. Cooked roots combined with grated horseradish and
  vinegar → a traditional red condiment. Seed crop: overwintered roots
  (sown by early summer, stored over winter, replanted) or a seed-to-
  seed method (sown by midsummer) → seed stalks → cut and cured in
  windrows → threshed → cleaned seed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen beets;
  borscht and other cooked dishes; historical use of this crop's juice
  as a wine colorant; use of this crop's pigment to color pickled
  turnips; dried beet chips and powder as food products; exact shelf
  lives of pickled, fermented, juiced, and colorant products
  (placeholders only, not independently sourced).

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 16,000 kg/ha, good 40,000 kg/ha, exceptional
56,000 kg/ha, poor 8,000 kg/ha) are derived from a documented regional
fresh-market average yield figure (worldAvg), a documented regional
processing-market average yield figure (good), a documented regional
processing "good" yield figure (exceptional), and roughly half the
fresh-market average for the poor tier, since no dedicated poor-tier
figure was found; a separate documented historical processing-yield
range from a different region, used only as a rough sanity check, was
not used directly. These tiers mix fresh-market and processing
production, which have substantially different typical yields, so a
model using fresh-market beets specifically should read roughly
16,000/22,000 kg/ha rather than the higher processing-market figures.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a standard international
reference work's documented stage lengths and crop-coefficient values
for a Mediterranean sowing window, with the initial-stage coefficient
taken as a general root-and-tuber-group value since this crop's own
specific coefficient cell could not be read from the source. A days-
without-water tolerance of roughly 10 days is derived from standard
soil-water-holding assumptions combined with this crop's documented
rooting depth and moisture-depletion allowance, though one specific
regional sandy-soil context documents a considerably shorter tolerance
of only a few days at peak water demand.

A seeding rate of roughly 10 kg/ha is derived by averaging documented
fresh-market seeding-rate figures from two sources; processing stands
use a documented, somewhat higher seeding rate. Seed mass (roughly 18
mg per seed unit) and a seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly
110 are derived from documented seed-count-per-unit-weight and seed-
yield figures.

Seed viability and germination-rate figures are carried over by close
analogy from sugar beet's own committed values (themselves originally
derived by analogy from an unrelated root crop), since both share the
same species and seed-unit structure; pelleted or primed seed is
documented keeping only about a year, considerably less than untreated
seed.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 360-450 days is derived separately for
the two documented seed-production methods, based on documented sowing
dates and an assumed harvest timing for whichever method is used.
Seed-crop plant population (roughly 20,500-35,900 plants/ha) is
derived from documented seed-crop row and in-row spacing figures.

Labor-hour figures for land preparation, weeding (including thinning),
post-harvest handling, and seed-saving are carried over by analogy
from sugar beet's own historically measured figures, reflecting the
same species, same multigerm seed-unit structure, and similar row
culture, though this crop is often grown on smaller plots by hand and
its shorter cycle may need less hoeing, while its denser fresh-market
stands may need more thinning labor per hectare than sugar beet.
Planting labor at the smallest production tier is derived from a
documented smallholder cost-model sowing-labor figure from a different
world region; harvesting labor at all tiers is estimated from sugar
beet's documented pulling-and-topping labor figure, adjusted upward to
account for additional bunching, grading, and washing steps specific
to this crop.

A harvest window of roughly 30 days per planting is estimated from a
documented processing-harvest date range and size-grade targets,
combined with a separate trial's finding that marketable yield rises
with additional days left in the field before harvest.

Soil pH optimum (roughly 6.4) is derived as the midpoint of a
documented best-practice pH range; the documented upper pH tolerance is
carried over from an earlier, unsourced internal estimate rather than
independently resourced.

Salinity yield-loss points (50% loss at roughly 9.6 dS/m, 100% loss at
roughly 15.1 dS/m) are derived by extrapolating the documented salinity
threshold and yield-loss slope from Finding 4.

Fresh-market plant population (roughly 300,000 plants/ha) is derived
from documented fresh-market row and in-row spacing figures.

Edible yield per unit area per day is derived separately for fresh-
market (roughly 25 g/m²/day) and processing (roughly 54 g/m²/day)
production, by dividing each market's yield tier by its respective
typical days-to-harvest.

Storage life (roughly 150 days cool and humid) is derived as the
middle of a documented four-to-six-month range for topped roots at
near-freezing temperature and very high humidity; this is a cold-humid
storage regime rather than a cool-dry one, with a shorter root-cellar-
only range and a longer processing-stock range documented at the two
extremes.

Product shelf lives for pickled, fermented, and juiced products and
for the extracted red colorant are rough, unsourced placeholders
carried over from similar product categories, pending dedicated food-
science shelf-life studies.
