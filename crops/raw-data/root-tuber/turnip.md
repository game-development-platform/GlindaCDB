# Turnip (*Brassica rapa* subsp. *rapa*)

## Naming and scope

*Brassica rapa* L. subsp. *rapa*, family Brassicaceae (a Rapifera
cultivar grouping in standard agricultural salinity references). This
entry covers the table turnip specifically: greens receive their own
harvest stage, and greens-only cultivars are treated as a cultivar
group within this entry rather than a separate crop; Asian salad-
turnip cultivars (kabu types) are likewise treated as a cultivar group
here. A forage/stubble turnip cultivar, grown mainly for grazing
livestock, is a closely related but not-yet-researched sibling entry
this project expects to cover separately within the same family.

This is the same species as several leafy Asian brassica crops (bok
choy, napa cabbage, mizuna, tatsoi, komatsuna, choy sum, and rapini),
all of which are separate entries this project has not yet covered,
and as turnip rape and field mustard, two oilseed/forage types not
covered here. Rutabaga, a related but genetically distinct species
(*Brassica napus*), is a separate sibling entry this project has
already researched.

This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research into
carrot (a biennial root crop with comparable harvest stages and a
humid cold-storage profile).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — three products from one quick-growing crop, with very
different shelf lives.** Baby turnips are pulled at roughly radish
size around 30 days and sold bunched with their greens attached; roots
are smoothest in texture when harvested small. A specific widely sold
F1 Japanese salad-turnip cultivar takes roughly 38 days [4][14].
Full-size roots take roughly 40-50 days [4]. Greens come either from
dedicated greens-only cultivars (whose roots are woody or inedible) or
from the tops of ordinary root-type cultivars [5]. Storage life varies
dramatically by product: topped roots keep roughly four to five months
at near-freezing temperature and very high humidity, or up to six
months at a slightly warmer range and somewhat lower humidity for one
specific widely grown fall storage cultivar; roots sold with their
greens still attached keep only 10-14 days at near-freezing
temperature; greens alone keep 10-14 days with ice or protective
packaging [3][4].

**Finding 2 — the root is salt-sensitive, but the leaves are salt-
tolerant.** A standard international water-quality-for-agriculture
reference documents two separate salinity curves for the same plant,
depending on which organ is measured: the storage root has a
documented salinity threshold of roughly 0.9 dS/m with a 9% yield-loss
slope per additional dS/m, categorized as moderately sensitive, while
the leafy top has a documented threshold of roughly 3.3 dS/m with a
4.3% slope, categorized as moderately tolerant [2].

**Finding 3 — water-use parameters are only partly documented.** A
standard international crop-evapotranspiration reference work
documents a mid-season crop-coefficient value of roughly 1.10 and a
late-season value of roughly 0.95 for this crop (grouped together with
rutabaga), along with a plant height and an initial-stage coefficient
taken as a general root-and-tuber-group value; documented rooting
depth runs roughly 0.5-1.0 m with a fairly generous allowable soil-
moisture-depletion fraction. However, this same reference work has no
documented stage-length row for this crop specifically, so the total
seasonal water figure in this entry is estimated rather than directly
sourced [1].

**Finding 4 — detailed cold-storage rules and disorders.** Decay
accelerates sharply at a specific moderate-cool temperature and above.
The documented freezing point is a specific mild sub-zero temperature;
frozen roots turn soft and flabby, developing tan or gray internal
tissue and a fermented odor. Hydrocooling with a temperature difference
greater than roughly 10°C between the root and the cooling water
causes cracking. Waxing is not recommended before long-term storage.
Peeled turnips keep roughly two to three weeks in perforated film.
Sprouting is best held back by strict near-freezing storage
temperature. Boron deficiency causes brown spots, cracks, and a water-
soaked appearance in the root, while overly mature roots develop
separate growth cracks [3].

**Finding 5 — the seed crop must be isolated from every other member
of the same species.** Most turnip-type plants of this species are
self-incompatible and insect-pollinated, so they readily cross with
Chinese cabbage, bok choy, and other cultivated forms of the same
species [6]. Documented isolation-distance guidance ranges from
roughly 1 km (reducible to roughly 500 m behind a windbreak hedge) to
roughly 0.8-1.6 km, with a documented minimum population of at least
100 plants recommended to maintain genetic diversity [6]. This is a
biennial seed crop: sown in midsummer in temperate climates, it
flowers and sets seed the following year [6]. One specific leafy
turnip type showed a facultative (rather than obligate) vernalization
requirement in a New Zealand study [6]. Documented seed yields range
from roughly 450-560 kg/ha using honey-bee pollination in an older
regional report on vegetable-type turnip, up to roughly 1,150-1,400
kg/ha in a more recent trial of forage-type turnip [7]. F1 hybrid seed
production in this crop uses the self-incompatibility system: a patent
document describes self-incompatibility-based hybrid seed production
in turnips and other brassica crops without requiring hand bud
pollination, and the widely sold F1 salad-turnip cultivar mentioned in
Finding 1 uses this hybrid system [14].

**Finding 6 — history and culture.** A historical four-course crop
rotation (clover, wheat, turnips, barley) — credited in popular history
to a particular 18th-century agricultural reformer, though farmers are
documented having grown turnips in rotation decades before that
individual's involvement — used this crop as winter livestock fodder in
place of a fallow year, allowing considerably more animals to be kept
through the winter season [12]. In Japan, a traditional seven-herb rice
porridge eaten on a specific early-January date (Jinjitsu, part of a
broader seasonal observance) includes this crop's greens, known under
a specific traditional name for the dish's turnip component [11].

**Finding 7 — a related, non-cultivated form of this same species has
flown seed-to-seed in space.** A rapid-cycling line of this species
(bred specifically for fast generation time in research and education
contexts, not a turnip cultivar) completed a full life cycle aboard
the Mir space station in 1997, documented as the first seed-to-seed
plant growth cycle completed in space; the resulting seed was smaller,
and the source documents that seed quality in this genus is generally
compromised by microgravity development [10]. A separate, earlier
spaceflight trial of the same rapid-cycling line aboard a 1995 space
shuttle mission documented normal growth in an automated growth-
chamber unit [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial grown as a root
  annual; tolerates moderate field frost.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per growth stage (each stage
  is lifted or cut once; repeat leaf-picking yield was not researched
  in detail).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (baby and salad-type cultivars
  especially).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  insect-pollinated and self-incompatible.
- **Yield / composition:** A documented regional good-yield figure of
  roughly 29,000 kg/ha comes from Japanese salad-turnip production
  statistics in a specific past year; no world yield figure exists
  specifically for this crop, since international agricultural
  statistics generally group it together with carrots (Finding 1; see
  also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** A documented mid- and late-season crop-coefficient
  curve and rooting-depth range exist, but total seasonal water-use
  figures are estimated rather than directly sourced, since no
  documented stage-length figures exist for this crop (Finding 3;
  water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded at a documented shallow depth in
  rows with a documented spacing, thinned to a specific closer spacing
  for small roots or wider spacing for large roots (establishment
  detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures were found; labor figures
  carried in internal planning material are unsourced estimates.
- **Soil:** A documented moderate pH optimum, with a boron requirement
  specifically to avoid the root disorder described in Finding 4 (soil
  detail below).
- **Stand life:** Biennial in natural life cycle, grown as a root
  annual.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Flea beetles and root maggots are
  documented pests in the field (mitigated with row covers at sowing);
  several storage-rot diseases are documented in stored roots (hazard
  detail below).
- **Cultivars:** A range of named western root, Japanese salad, and
  greens-only cultivars, including F1 hybrids and open-pollinated types
  (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Baby, mature, and greens harvest stages, each with a
  distinct typical harvest timing and storage life (harvest-stage
  detail below).
- **Storage:** A long, cold, very humid storage regime for topped
  mature roots (roughly four to five months), in sharp contrast to a
  much shorter shelf life for roots sold with greens attached or for
  greens alone (Finding 1).

Water detail: documented soil-moisture-depletion tolerance suggests a
days-without-water tolerance of roughly 9 days before a yield penalty
sets in, though this figure depends on an assumed rooting depth within
the documented range.

Establishment detail: direct-seeded at a shallow depth in bands within
rows at a documented row spacing; thinned to a closer in-row spacing
for small roots or a wider spacing for large roots.

Soil detail: a documented optimal pH range of roughly 6.0-7.5 applies;
boron deficiency specifically causes the brown-spot and cracking
disorder described in Finding 4.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a widely grown dual-
purpose western root cultivar (roots and greens) noted for good fall
storage life of four to six months; the F1 Japanese salad-turnip
cultivar described in Finding 1, eaten raw; a Japanese white-globe
(kabu) cultivar with smooth roots and mild edible greens, used in a
specific traditional Japanese pickle; a greens-only open-pollinated
cultivar with no useful swollen root (woody), traditionally autumn-sown
for winter greens in parts of the southern United States; a greens-only
F1 hybrid grown exclusively for its tops, with inedible roots; and a
western yellow-rooted cultivar carried over from older internal
planning material without independent sourcing in this research pass.

Harvest-stage detail: a baby stage (roughly 30 days, sold bunched with
greens); a mature stage (roughly 40-60 days, the primary stage for
storage, pickling, and seed production); and a greens stage (leaf
harvest specific to greens-type cultivars).

## Hazards

Glucosinolates are general to brassica crops broadly and were not
researched as a crop-specific hazard for this entry.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — tops and culls fed to livestock fall under the
general brassica grazing-disorder category (nitrate accumulation, a
specific sulfur-compound-related anemia, goiter-inducing compounds,
and photosensitization); deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data, and expected to be covered alongside
the forage-turnip sibling entry this project has not yet researched.

Flea beetles and root maggots are documented field pests, typically
mitigated with row covers at sowing; several storage-rot diseases
(a fungal root rot, bacterial soft rot, black rot, downy mildew, gray
mold, and a soil-fungus rot) are documented affecting stored roots.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → baby turnips with greens (roughly 30 days). Seed
  → mature roots → topped and graded (greens and culls removed as by-
  products) → cold storage. Topped roots → a specific Japanese salted-
  and-pressed turnip pickle, or a salt-fermented turnip pickle similar
  to sauerkraut. Greens-type seed → greens. Seed crop: roots selected
  and overwintered, or field-overwintered directly → seed stalks →
  threshed and cleaned seed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen
  turnips and greens (no sourced data found); a Lebanese pink pickled-
  turnip preparation and a Persian pickled-vegetable preparation
  (neither independently researched); forage/grazing use (expected to
  be covered by the not-yet-researched forage-turnip sibling entry);
  waxing and sprout-inhibitor treatments in commercial handling.

A Japanese New Year observance (a folk festival associated with Shinto
tradition) includes this crop's root and greens as a festival food in
a traditional seven-herb rice porridge eaten on a specific early-
January date. A Jain dietary tradition avoids root vegetables
generally, consistent with notes this project has recorded for other
root crops.

## Open research questions

- Documented seed-crop isolation-distance figures conflict somewhat
  across sources (a roughly 1 km figure, reducible with a windbreak
  hedge, versus a roughly 0.8-1.6 km figure from a different source).
- Documented seed-yield figures differ dramatically between vegetable-
  type and forage-type turnip seed production (roughly 450-560 kg/ha
  versus roughly 1,150-1,400 kg/ha).
- Documented storage relative-humidity figures conflict somewhat
  between two sources (a 95-99% figure versus a 90-95% figure for one
  specific cultivar).
- Documented seeding-rate figures conflict considerably between two
  sources (a considerably higher commercial seeding-rate figure versus
  a much lower general crop-database figure).
- The historical origin date of the Japanese seven-herb-porridge
  tradition described in Finding 6 is documented inconsistently across
  sources (one tracing it to an earlier historical period, another to
  a considerably later one).
- Weakest areas overall: seasonal water-use stage lengths (no
  documented reference-table row exists for this crop, so these are
  estimated); world/regional yield figures beyond the one specific
  Japanese statistic used here (otherwise unsourced, carried over from
  older internal planning material); labor-hour figures (unsourced,
  carried over from older internal planning material); and genome-size
  figure (a widely cited figure for this species that could not be
  independently confirmed in this research pass).
- Bolting triggers specific to root-type cultivars (as opposed to the
  seed crop generally) were not researched.
- Waterlogging tolerance and detailed soil nutrient-uptake figures
  were not researched.
- Detailed hybrid-seed parent-line information was not researched.
- Whether a wild form of this species has historically been gathered
  as food (as opposed to cultivated) was checked and no supporting
  source was found.
- The specific folk custom of carved turnip lanterns (a historical
  precursor to a modern carved-gourd holiday tradition) was not
  researched in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Calorie and protein content (roughly 280 kcal/kg, 9 g protein/kg) are
derived from a documented detailed composition breakdown (carbohydrate,
fiber, protein, and fat percentages) in a commodity storage manual,
converted to standard calorie equivalents; this derived figure falls
within the same general range as an older, separately carried internal
planning figure.

The good-yield tier (roughly 29,000 kg/ha) is derived directly from a
specific Japanese regional agricultural-statistics figure for salad-
turnip production in a past year, dividing total production by total
harvested area; this replaces an older, unsourced internal planning
figure.

Water-use stage lengths and the resulting seasonal water-use total are
estimated, since no standard reference-table row documents stage
lengths for this crop specifically; the estimate assumes a total
season length consistent with the documented full-size maturity
timeline plus an establishment period. A days-without-water tolerance
of roughly 9 days is derived from standard soil-water-holding
assumptions combined with this crop's documented rooting depth and
moisture-depletion allowance.

A seeding rate of roughly 6.4 kg/ha is derived by converting a
documented commercial per-acre seeding-rate figure to metric units; a
separate general crop-database source documents a considerably lower
seeding-rate range, not used here directly. A seed-to-yield
multiplication ratio of roughly 80 is derived by dividing the midpoint
of the documented vegetable-type seed-crop yield range by this derived
seeding rate; using the alternative, considerably lower general crop-
database seeding rate instead would produce a much higher ratio.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 300-330 days is derived from the
documented midsummer sowing date through an assumed early-summer seed
harvest the following year.

F2 (second-generation) behavior of saved hybrid seed is derived from
the self-incompatibility-based hybrid-breeding system described in
Finding 5: saved seed from a self-incompatibility-based F1 hybrid is
expected to segregate for both general uniformity and the underlying
self-incompatibility genetics.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 48 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing the good-yield tier by a typical full-size maturity timeline.

Genome size (roughly 0.50 pg, 1C) is derived by converting a widely
cited genome-assembly size in megabases for this species to picograms
using a standard conversion factor; this widely cited size figure
could not be independently confirmed within the search-snippet-level
sources consulted for this entry.
