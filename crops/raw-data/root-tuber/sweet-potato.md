## Naming and scope

*Ipomoea batatas* (L.) Lam., morning-glory family (Convolvulaceae): a
perennial vine grown as an annual for its storage roots. This entry
covers one species as a single entry: orange, cream, white, and
purple-fleshed types (including a well-known Japanese purple-fleshed
type) share the same cultivation and biology and differ mainly in
flesh color, texture, and sweetness, so they are treated as cultivar
notes rather than separate entries. A common naming confusion is
worth flagging: in North American usage, "yam" very often refers to
this crop, while true yam (a different genus entirely) is a separate
crop this project has also researched as its own entry.

This crop's closest wild relative is a diploid wild morning-glory
species; the crop itself arose from a cross between a tetraploid and
a diploid relative followed by a further genome duplication, and
notably carries naturally occurring bacterially derived DNA, making it
a naturally transgenic crop. Cultivated sweet potato is hexaploid.
Native to tropical South America and domesticated in Central or South
America, the oldest dated archaeological remains come from coastal
Peru, dated to roughly 8000 BC; the crop later spread across the
Pacific by vine cuttings carried by Polynesian voyagers, reaching East
Asia by the late 16th and 17th centuries. This entry is grounded
against this project's own prior research into both potato and
cassava, two repo-format sibling root crops.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the harvested root must be cured hot and humid, then
stored warm-cool and humid, but never cold.** Freshly dug roots are
first surface-dried for a couple of hours, then cured at roughly
29-32°C and 90-95% relative humidity for 5-14 days; one detailed North
Carolina regional practice instead documents a somewhat milder curing
condition over a shorter period [1][5]. For long-term storage, roots
are kept at roughly 12.8-15.6°C and 85-95% relative humidity for three
to ten months or longer depending on cultivar, with one source
documenting storage life extending up to 13 months for some cultivars
[1][4]. Storage above the upper end of this temperature range causes
roots to sprout and turn pithy, while storage below the lower end
causes a serious chilling-injury disorder: several weeks at a
moderately cool temperature produce the same damage as just a day or
two at a much colder temperature, and critically, this chilling injury
shows no visible sign until the root is later warmed, appearing then
as sunken surface areas, internal rot and darkening, off-flavors, or a
condition called "hard core" — hardened, seemingly undercooked spots
discovered only once the root is cooked [4]. Cured roots lost a
documented 7-16% of their weight over a 50-week storage trial at a
specific temperature and humidity, with one specific cultivar storing
particularly well for 38 weeks [4]. The root's freezing point is
documented at roughly -1.1°C. Critically, once roots have been washed,
re-graded, treated with fungicide, and lightly waxed for retail sale,
they behave essentially like uncured roots and keep only two to three
weeks even under otherwise good storage conditions [4]. For canning or
freezing specifically, roots may instead be held uncured at a
specifically chilled (but not cold-injurious) temperature for up to
ten days, since uncured roots actually give a firmer finished
processed product [4].

**Finding 2 — this crop has notably deep roots and is especially
drought-sensitive at one specific early growth stage.** A standard
international crop-water-use reference documents a substantially
larger effective rooting depth and soil-moisture-depletion allowance
for this crop than for either potato or cassava, meaning considerably
more water is held in reserve in the root zone before irrigation
becomes necessary [2]. Documented optimal annual rainfall runs roughly
750-1,000 mm, with at least 500 mm needed during the growing season
itself; the crop is specifically documented as sensitive to drought
during tuber (storage root) initiation, occurring roughly 50-60 days
after planting, and does not tolerate waterlogging at any growth
stage [1].

**Finding 3 — propagation uses slips grown from bedded roots, or
direct vine cuttings.** This crop is propagated clonally, either by
stem or root cuttings or, more commonly in temperate production, by
"slips" — shoots that sprout from stored roots deliberately bedded for
this purpose; true botanical seed is used only in breeding programs
[1]. In documented temperate practice, medium-sized seed roots are
bedded and slips are pulled from them several times over a season; the
resulting slips are transplanted into raised rows at a specific
spacing and planting depth over a defined spring-to-summer planting
window [5]. A documented US crop-insurance planting standard specifies
a particular row and in-row spacing with an assumed establishment
rate. Using clean, disease-free vine cuttings rather than root-derived
slips is documented as a way to avoid certain root-borne diseases; any
seed roots showing decay or internal corking (a viral symptom) should
never be used as planting stock [4].

**Finding 4 — the leaves provide a genuine second harvest.** Young
leaves and vine tips are eaten as cooked greens, documented rich in
beta-carotene and vitamin C, with an oxalate content roughly half that
of spinach; older, more mature vines are used as livestock fodder [1].
Several regional leaf-harvest trials found differing optimal
management: cutting every 20 days gave the best combined leaf-and-stem
dry-matter yield in one Southeast Asian trial; a single defoliation
event had little effect on final root yield in a separate East
African study, but repeated defoliations significantly reduced it; and
a West African trial found two moderate-intensity leaf harvests
balanced forage yield against root yield reasonably well [9]. This
establishes a genuine trade-off between harvesting leaves for food or
fodder and preserving full root yield.

**Finding 5 — climate, soil, and yield.** This crop has no frost
tolerance and grows best at a mean temperature around 24°C; roots
mature anywhere from two to nine months depending on cultivar [1].
Soil should be well-drained, light to medium in texture, at a fairly
wide pH range; notably, this crop is documented as very sensitive to
aluminum toxicity and requires lime amendment on acidic, aluminum-rich
soils — the opposite situation from cassava, which tolerates very high
soil aluminum saturation [1]. Recent global production figures put
world yield at roughly 11.9-12 t/ha across many tens of millions of
tonnes of total production, with China alone documented producing
roughly half of total world output in a recent year; a detailed US
grading standard documents specific target root diameter and weight
ranges for top commercial grades [1][3][4].

**Finding 6 — storage diseases, processing uses, and controlled-
environment research.** Documented storage diseases include a
bitter-tasting black rot, a fast-spreading Rhizopus soft rot
(encouraged by wet field soil at harvest), an internal corking viral
disease that worsens over four to six months of storage and is
especially bad at warmer storage temperatures, and several further
named rot diseases, one of which is reduced by a specific lower soil
pH [4]. A specific weevil pest has no adequate documented control
method, and infested roots should not be placed into long-term storage
at all [4]. Separately, roots infected by a specific fungal pathogen
produce a compound that converts into a toxin causing fatal lung
disease in cattle that eat the moldy roots [6]. Processing uses
include starch extraction for a well-known Korean noodle product,
fermentation into Japanese and Korean distilled spirits, and canned,
frozen, glazed, or pureed products, baby food, and dehydrated flakes —
made either from cured roots (better flavor) or uncured roots (firmer
texture), depending on the specific product [1][4]. Controlled-
environment research has grown this crop hydroponically in a nutrient-
film-technique system at a historically black land-grant university,
yielding substantial storage-root mass per plant; separate space-
agency-funded research documented edible-biomass yield figures per
unit area per day over multi-month growing cycles in enclosed growing
systems [8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season, frost-tender perennial vine
  grown as an annual for its storage root; needs a genuinely frost-
  free season (Finding 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Roots are dug once per planting (a single
  harvest); leaves and vine tips can additionally be picked repeatedly
  throughout the growing season as a secondary cut-and-come-again
  product, at some cost to final root yield (Finding 4).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, and warm-temperate zones,
  requiring a genuinely frost-free growing season.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible in practice; eaten cooked.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; true botanical
  seed is produced only for breeding purposes.
- **Yield / composition:** Recent world average yield runs roughly
  11.9-12 t/ha; documented commercial yield tiers span a considerably
  wider range depending on production intensity (Finding 5; see also
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Notably deep-rooted with a large soil-moisture
  buffer compared to related root crops; most sensitive to drought at
  tuber initiation, roughly 50-60 days after planting, and does not
  tolerate waterlogging at all (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** Propagated clonally from slips raised in bedded
  seed roots, or from direct vine cuttings, transplanted into raised
  rows or mounds (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour survey was found for this crop in
  this research pass.
- **Soil:** Well-drained, light-to-medium-textured soil at a fairly
  wide pH range; very sensitive to aluminum toxicity and needing lime
  amendment on acidic soils (Finding 5).
- **Stand life:** An annual crop made from a naturally perennial vine.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A weevil pest with no adequate control
  method, plus several named storage rots and a viral internal-corking
  disease, are documented concerns (Finding 6; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** A wide range of named orange-, cream-, white-, and
  purple-fleshed cultivars across multiple world regions, plus
  dedicated leaf-type cultivars and research clones used in
  controlled-environment studies (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Roots dug once, typically 90-150 days from planting for
  temperate-adapted cultivars, before frost; leaves picked repeatedly
  every 15-30 days if taken as a secondary product (harvest-stage
  detail below).
- **Storage:** Cured warm and humid immediately after digging, then
  stored at a narrow warm-cool, humid temperature band; colder storage
  causes a serious, delayed chilling-injury disorder (Finding 1).

Establishment detail: slips are transplanted into raised rows after
the last frost risk has passed, at a documented planting depth and
spacing; in tropical regions, direct vine cuttings are more commonly
used instead of root-derived slips.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a major orange-fleshed
US-bred cultivar now dominant in several export markets; a further
orange-fleshed cultivar documented storing particularly well for an
extended period; a well-known Japanese purple-fleshed cultivar
associated with a traditional roasted-sweet-potato preparation; a
Caribbean cream-fleshed type documented less sweet and moist than
orange types; a red-skinned New Zealand Māori cultivar (kūmara) that
is the most common red cultivar grown there; and two specific
cultivars used in documented NASA-funded and university hydroponic
research, alongside dedicated purple-leaf types considered
nutritionally preferable specifically for leaf harvest.

Harvest-stage detail: a root stage (90-150 days, harvested before
frost, requiring curing with essentially no delay after digging) and a
leaf stage (picked every 15-30 days as a repeat-cut secondary product).

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — roots infected by a specific fungal pathogen and
left moldy produce a compound that converts into a toxin causing fatal
lung disease in cattle that consume them; deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 6).

The leaves contain oxalate at a level roughly half that of spinach, not
considered unusual. A weevil pest with no adequate documented control
method, several named storage rots (including a bitter-tasting black
rot and a fast-spreading soft rot worsened by wet field soil), and a
viral internal-corking disease are documented pest and disease
concerns.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** cured seed roots → bedded → slips pulled repeatedly →
  transplanted. Alternatively: vine cuttings → planted directly. Roots
  dug → cured immediately → stored at a narrow warm-cool, humid
  temperature → washed, graded, and lightly waxed for retail sale
  (shelf life then drops sharply) → market; sound culls go to
  livestock feed. Roots → starch → noodle production, or fermented via
  a koji-style process into distilled spirits. Leaves and vine tips →
  cooked greens; mature vines → livestock fodder and further cuttings.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned, frozen, pureed,
  and baby-food products, and dehydrated flake production, in
  industrial detail; the specific fermentation starter culture used in
  spirit production; detailed weevil and disease control programs.

A Māori tradition in New Zealand treats cultivation of this crop (as
kūmara) as sacred, under the patronage of a specific deity of
cultivated food; documented ceremonial practice includes a separate
ceremonial planting plot whose produce is set aside as an offering, a
blessing and offering (traditionally a bird) at the main planting, a
ritually restricted status placed on the growing gardens by a
ritual specialist and maintained until harvest, carved stone figures
representing the deity placed in the fields, and a ceremony lifting
the restriction at harvest followed by a first-fruits offering and a
feast; planting is traditionally timed to sunrise facing east. A
tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, consistent with
notes this project has recorded for other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Documented effective rooting depth conflicts between a standard
  international reference (roughly 1.0-1.5 m) and a notably shallower
  figure from elsewhere; this affects the derived days-without-water
  tolerance substantially, and is not resolved here.
- Documented curing conditions differ moderately between a general
  encyclopedia source and a specific North Carolina regional practice
  source (temperature and duration both differ somewhat).
- Documented optimal long-term storage temperature differs somewhat
  between a detailed industry storage manual and at least one other
  source.
- Documented soil pH minimum differs slightly between sources.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour figures (no dedicated source found
  in this research pass); yield tiers above and below the world
  average (not independently re-sourced); the leaf-harvest yield
  profile (no fresh-weight-per-hectare figure was found, only relative
  management guidance); soil salinity tolerance (no reference-table row
  was recovered); and genome size (not found for this hexaploid
  species in this research pass).
- Photoperiod response and flowering biology were not researched in
  detail, since they are not relevant to root production.
- A traditional Polynesian underground storage-pit method for this
  crop was not researched in detail.
- Vitamin-A biofortification breeding programs for orange-fleshed
  cultivars were not independently researched in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a standard international
reference work's documented crop-coefficient values and two regional
stage-length breakdowns (one Mediterranean, one tropical rainy-season),
with the initial-stage coefficient taken as a general estimate since
this crop's own specific coefficient cell could not be recovered from
the source. A days-without-water tolerance is derived from standard
soil-water-holding assumptions combined with this crop's documented
rooting depth and moisture-depletion allowance; this figure is highly
sensitive to which of two conflicting rooting-depth figures is used,
yielding a considerably shorter or longer tolerance depending on which
is chosen.

Yield tiers are set with the world-average tier matching recent global
production statistics directly; the good, exceptional, and poor tiers
were not independently re-sourced in this research pass and are
carried forward as working estimates.

Leaf storage life (roughly 2 days) is estimated by analogy with other
leafy greens, which wilt quickly without refrigeration.

Edible yield per unit area per day in field conditions (roughly 9.5
g/m²/day) is derived by dividing the world-average yield figure by a
representative tropical growing-season length; this is notably lower
than documented edible-yield figures from hydroponic controlled-
environment research, which achieved considerably higher output per
unit area per day over a multi-month growing cycle.
