# Chinese Water Chestnut (*Eleocharis dulcis*)

## Naming and scope

*Eleocharis dulcis* (Burm.f.) Trin. ex Henschel, family Cyperaceae
(the sedge family): the Chinese water chestnut, also known under
several regional names including a Cantonese name meaning "horse's
hoof" and a Mandarin name. Several historical synonyms exist for this
species.

A critical naming distinction: "water chestnut" also commonly names
the water caltrop (a floating aquatic nut in an entirely different
plant family), a separate crop this project has already researched as
its own entry. A specific flour eaten during Hindu religious fasting
days is made from that other plant, not from this crop. This entry
covers one species with one product; a fresh-eating cultivar group and
a starch-production cultivar group are treated as cultivar groups
within this single entry rather than separate crops.

This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research into
taro (a flooded-field corm crop with a comparably chilling-sensitive
storage profile).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an obligate flooded-paddy crop with a specific water
calendar the farm must run.** The field must stay flooded for at least
six months [2]. The usual documented sequence is: plant; flood for 24
hours to settle the soil, then drain; reflood when the tops reach
roughly 20-30 cm tall and hold at roughly 10-12.5 cm of standing water
(a US source documents roughly 10-15 cm) until harvest; then drain the
field roughly three to four weeks before harvest (about 30 days in one
documented Florida practice) [2][4][5]. A documented 1957 US trial
found at least 10 cm of standing water was needed for maximum yield.
Documented Chinese practice instead uses shallower standing water
(roughly 3-5 cm after transplanting), deliberately drying the field
once or twice mid-season to stop unproductive tillering, and keeping
the soil moist rather than deeply flooded while the corms swell —
these two documented water-depth practices conflict and both are kept
in this entry rather than resolved. Walking on the field is avoided
once the underground stems have spread, to prevent injury. Turbid
water is documented shading out young shoots badly enough that one
Texas pond with very turbid, acidic water lost most of its stand
within a month. No standard international crop-evapotranspiration
reference work documents this crop; rice is the closest tabulated
flooded-crop analogue [1].

**Finding 2 — corm formation is gated by short days and cool nights.**
Corm formation is strongly suppressed by long days and promoted below
a critical day length of roughly 12 hours; above-ground growth itself
is documented unaffected across a wide range of day lengths [3][7][13].
Under long days, the underground stems grow sideways and produce
daughter plants; under short days, they instead turn downward and form
a corm at the tip, angled into the mud on short underground runners
[2][3]. Flower spikelets appear when the above-water stems reach
roughly 15 cm above the water surface, and corms form after flowering
[2]. Growth is best at roughly 22-30°C; corms sprout only above roughly
14°C soil temperature [2][3][7]. In one documented high-elevation
Yunnan growing region, a day length of roughly 11.5 hours combined
with nighttime temperatures below roughly 18°C in late summer brought
the crop to premature maturity; countermeasures (shading for a few
hours daily from a specific growth stage) delayed maturity by roughly
10 days and raised yield by more than 15% [7].

**Finding 3 — two cultivar groups: a fresh-eating type and a starch
type.** The fresh-eating type produces a large, brown-skinned, sweet,
juicy corm eaten raw like an apple or sliced into dishes, grown on
sandy soil, and typically drained and harvested after the first
killing frost or dug from dry ground at the end of winter. The starch
type produces a smaller, black-skinned, drier corm grown on mucky clay
soil, mostly ground into starch, and harvested in autumn by hand from
wet, muddy paddy soil [4]. Two modern Chinese regional cultivars are
documented: a fresh-type cultivar maturing in roughly 150 days with
a yield over roughly 45,000 kg/ha, and a starch-type cultivar with
over 30% starch content [7]. A 2026 genome-comparison study found the
fresh-type cultivar has less total starch and amylopectin but more
amylose and sucrose than the starch-type cultivar, and that in both
types starch content rises during corm growth and falls again at
maturity [14]. Wild forms produce corms up to roughly 1 cm across and
almost black in color, while cultivated corms reach up to roughly 4 cm
across [2].

**Finding 4 — corms keep safely in the soil, and leaving them there
changes the product.** Corms "do not deteriorate in the soil" unless
frost is severe, so harvest timing can be deliberately delayed [2][8].
Fresh-type corms left in the drained paddy over winter convert starch
into sugar and become sweeter; however, later digging produces sweeter
corms with a correspondingly shorter shelf life. Fresh-market corms are
typically dug a little early for best appearance, while starch-type
corms are dug a few days later [7]. The harvest cue is the above-water
stems turning brown, or being killed by frost, roughly 7-9 months after
transplanting [2]. This gives two distinct harvest stages: a default
mature stage and a separate winter-held stage.

**Finding 5 — storage must be cold and humid, never warm, never
frozen.** Cold storage can hold corms up to roughly six months at
roughly -1 to 4°C in moisture-proof but not airtight containers [2].
Full quality is documented lasting roughly four to six weeks at
roughly 0-4°C and 85-90% relative humidity [7], and a university thesis
found no rot after 86-159 days at 4°C [13], with 4°C specifically
identified as the best storage temperature in one source. Corms sprout
at roughly 14°C or above, and last only about two weeks at 25°C before
sprouting and rotting [2][3][7]. Below roughly -1°C, the flesh turns
glassy and water-soaked [7]. A documented extended-storage trial held
corms submerged for eight months at 0-5°C in a dilute sodium
hypochlorite solution and found them staying sound, with roughly 20%
still able to sprout afterward [12]. Freshly peeled corms turn yellow
and brown within days due to specific flavonoid compounds; a trial
storing peeled corms at 10°C and 85-90% relative humidity in sealed
bags for five days found measurable color loss (lightness) from day
two onward [9]. Corms are typically sold unpeeled specifically because
disease-causing organisms sit on the peel [9]; the leathery skin is
roughly 0.5-1 mm thick [7].

**Finding 6 — documented yields vary widely by source.** By country,
documented ranges are roughly 20,000-40,000 kg/ha in China, roughly
28,000 kg/ha in the US, and roughly 25,000-38,000 kg/ha in Thailand
[2]; one modern Chinese regional cultivar is documented giving over
45,000 kg/ha [7]; and Australian growers in one documented region
expected roughly 30,000 kg/ha [8]. Measured smaller-scale trials
include two 1978 Texas pond trials at roughly 13,200-13,600 kg/ha
under low soil fertility and acidic conditions [4], a catfish-pool raft
trial at roughly 3,180 kg/ha [4], and a North Queensland backyard-bed
trial at roughly 0.5 kg per surviving plant [8]. A documented Chinese
historical range gives roughly 17,250-34,500 kg/ha [4]. One general
encyclopedia source gives considerably higher US yield figures (roughly
47,000-85,000 kg/ha) that sit far above every other source consulted
and are not used for this entry's yield tiers. A documented seed-corm
rate conflict exists: Chinese direct-planting practice uses roughly
3,000-4,500 kg of seed corms per hectare (about a tenth of total
output), while a wider transplanting spacing uses only roughly 450
kg/ha by derivation [4][7].

**Finding 7 — a raw-eaten aquatic crop with a documented parasite
hazard.** Corms are commonly eaten raw [2][3], but where paddies are
fertilized with pig manure or human waste, the corm skin can carry
infective larval cysts of a specific giant intestinal fluke parasite
[10][11]. Documented transmission routes include eating raw corms,
drinking untreated water, or peeling corms with the teeth. The
documented control is to peel the corm, then boil it for one to two
minutes. Pigs are the parasite's reservoir host, and documented Chinese
agricultural advice recommends feeding pigs ensiled rather than fresh
aquatic greens from these paddies to break the transmission cycle
[11].

**Finding 8 — established plants tolerate salt, but sprouting corms do
not.** This crop's wild habitat includes salt, brackish, and fresh
swamps [2]. In a documented Vietnamese hydroponic trial, established-
plant growth was not measurably reduced up to roughly 20 parts per
thousand sodium chloride (roughly 38 dS/m) [17]. However, a separate
Philippine graduate thesis found that sodium chloride suppressed corm
germination even at low concentrations and reduced plant height, stem
count, and dry weight [17]. No standard international salinity-
threshold reference-table entry was found for this crop.

**Finding 9 — products and culture.** Fresh corms are eaten raw or
cooked, staying notably crisp after cooking or canning because a
specific plant compound (ferulic acid) cross-links the cell walls
[2][3]. Traditional Chinese starch production crushes the corms,
washes the pulp through a bamboo basket on filter cloth while stirring
for roughly 15 minutes, lets the starch settle for roughly five hours,
then sun-dries it [2]. Other documented products include dried chips
(a traditional Indonesian and Philippine snack) and a specific water
chestnut cake eaten as a dim sum item and a Lunar New Year food [19].
The above-water stems are used as cattle feed, mulch, and packing
material, and are woven into mats and skirts in parts of Southeast
Asia and Papua New Guinea, typically cut after frost [2][4]. The corm
is documented containing a specific antibiotic compound active against
two common bacterial species [2]. This crop is grown in rotation or
alongside rice, a closely related aquatic corm crop this project has
already researched (arrowhead), lotus, and sweet flag in China [4].
US production history includes an introduction in 1934 to a USDA
garden in Georgia, substantial 1977 US imports (mostly canned from
Taiwan), and the development of mechanical peelers and modified potato
diggers in 1976 that made domestic US growing feasible only above a
specific break-even import price [4]. In Aboriginal Australia, Yolngu
women traditionally gathered the corms together in the dry season on
dried-out floodplains as a staple food before wheat flour became
available, while two other Aboriginal groups traditionally roast the
corms and use the stems for bedding and shelter [18].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season perennial grown as an annual
  from corms; needs at least roughly 220 frost-free days; corms sprout
  only above roughly 14°C soil temperature.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per planting, with two distinct
  harvest stages (a default mature stage and a winter-held stage)
  reflecting the in-ground holding practice described in Finding 4.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, and warm-temperate zones, from
  sea level up to roughly 1,350 m elevation, documented as far north as
  40°N latitude in China.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, though peeling and (in fluke-endemic
  areas) a brief boil are recommended (Finding 7).
- **Pollination:** Not required for corm production, since corms form
  only after flowering has already occurred and the crop is propagated
  clonally.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields vary
  considerably by country and management, roughly 5,000-45,000 kg/ha
  (Finding 6; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-water-use reference
  work documents this crop directly; water-use parameters are
  estimated by analogy to paddy rice, the closest tabulated flooded-
  crop analogue (Finding 1; water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from seed corms, either
  through a nursery-and-transplant method or direct planting, depending
  on climate (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** A documented hand-harvest rate of roughly 200-300 kg per
  worker-day is the only directly sourced labor figure; all other
  labor-tier figures are unsourced estimates.
- **Soil:** A documented optimal pH range of roughly 6.9-7.3, with lime
  recommended on acidic soils; rich clay or muck suits the starch
  cultivar group, while sandy loam suits the fresh-eating cultivar
  group (soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** A perennial grown as an annual crop from corms; not a
  multi-year standing crop.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Several fungal wilt and stem-blight
  diseases, a rust disease in warm climates, rice-shared diseases in
  Thailand, a billbug, two nematode species, grasshoppers,
  planthoppers, leafhoppers, stem borers, rats at harvest, waterfowl,
  and mole crickets after draining are all documented (hazard detail
  below).
- **Cultivars:** A traditional fresh-eating cultivar group, a
  traditional starch cultivar group, a heritage US introduction, two
  modern Chinese regional cultivars, a blight-tolerant cultivar, a
  Japanese cultivated form, and a regional Chinese named type
  (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A mature stage (roughly 180-270 days from planting) and
  a winter-held stage (corms left in the drained paddy over winter to
  sweeten before digging) (Finding 4).
- **Storage:** A cold, humid storage regime (roughly -1 to 4°C) keeping
  corms sound for several months, with sprouting above roughly 14°C and
  freezing injury below roughly -1°C (Finding 5).

Water detail: this crop needs standing water kept at roughly 10-15 cm
(with a documented shallower Chinese alternative of 3-5 cm with
deliberate mid-season drying) from the time shoots are a hand's height
tall until roughly three to four weeks before harvest, when the field
is drained so the corms can be dug.

Establishment detail: established either through a nursery bed (corms
spaced a few centimeters apart, lightly shaded, then transplanted at
roughly 15-20 cm height with several stems) where growing seasons are
short, or by direct planting of seed corms at roughly 10-12.5 cm depth
in warm areas with a long season. Sprouting begins roughly three weeks
after planting under good conditions, with daughter-plant production
beginning around six to eight weeks.

Soil detail: documented fertilizer practice centers on a specific
nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium combination (with one documented US
optimal rate), applied alongside a traditional Chinese manure-based
system in parts of Asia using cow and hog manure, human waste, peanut
cake, plant ash, and lime.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include the traditional fresh-
eating type (large, brown-skinned, sweet corms on sandy soil); the
traditional starch type (small, black-skinned, drier corms on mucky
clay, milled for starch); a heritage US-introduced fresh type
documented in a Texas pond trial; a modern Chinese fresh-type regional
cultivar with lower total starch and higher sucrose and amylose
content than its starch-type counterpart; a modern Chinese starch-type
regional cultivar with over 30% starch content; a blight-tolerant
cultivar with documented lower blight incidence than local varieties;
a Japanese cultivated form; and a regional Chinese named type.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the corm skin and above-water stems of this crop,
when grown in water contaminated with pig manure or human waste, can
carry infective cysts of a specific giant intestinal fluke parasite,
transmissible through eating raw corms, drinking untreated growing
water, or peeling corms with the teeth. Peeling and boiling for one to
two minutes is the documented mitigation; ensiling rather than feeding
fresh aquatic greens to pigs (the parasite's reservoir host) is a
further documented preventive measure. Deferred until this project has
a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 7).

Several fungal wilt and stem-blight diseases (one specifically
triggered by acidic soil), a rust disease in warm climates, rice-
shared diseases in parts of Southeast Asia, a billbug, two documented
nematode species, grasshoppers, planthoppers, leafhoppers, stem
borers, rats at harvest time, waterfowl, and mole crickets after
draining are all documented pests and diseases; a documented two-to-
three-year rotation with rice reduces blight incidence by roughly 40%.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed corms (shade-dried, then soaked) → nursery bed →
  transplants → flooded paddy → corms and above-water stems.
  Alternatively: seed corms → direct planting at roughly 10-12.5 cm
  depth → corms. Corms → held in the drained paddy over winter →
  sweeter corms with a shorter shelf life. Starch-type corms →
  crushed → washed through a bamboo basket → starch milk → settled for
  roughly five hours → sun-dried starch. Corms → washed, shade-dried,
  brushed → cold storage. Corms → peeled → fresh-peeled corms (short
  shelf life measured in days). Corms → sliced and dried into chips.
  Above-water stems → livestock feed, mulch, matting material; ensiled
  for pig feed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen water
  chestnuts (no sourced data found); mechanical harvesting and peeling
  equipment in detail; specific cooked dishes beyond the water chestnut
  cake already described; tissue-culture propagation in detail.

A Chinese Lunar New Year folk tradition treats a cake made from this
crop's starch and corms as a festival food, with the crop's Cantonese
name punning on phrases meaning "rising high" and general success.
A Jain dietary tradition avoids root vegetables generally, including
underground storage organs like this crop's corm, consistent with
notes this project has recorded for other root crops. A specific flour
eaten during Hindu fasting days is made from the unrelated water
caltrop, not from this crop.

## Open research questions

- Documented season length conflicts considerably between a general
  tropical-crop reference (roughly 7-9 months) and a more recent
  Chinese cultivation review (roughly 120-150 days); the shorter figure
  may reflect a specific southern Chinese autumn crop planted after an
  early rice crop into shortening days.
- Documented standing-water depth conflicts between a roughly 10-15 cm
  figure from several Western sources and a roughly 3-5 cm figure with
  deliberate mid-season drying from a Chinese cultivation review.
- Documented seed-corm rate and planting-density figures conflict
  considerably between a derived Western-practice figure and a much
  higher Chinese direct-planting figure.
- Documented yield figures conflict considerably: one general
  encyclopedia source's US yield figures sit far above every other
  source consulted and are not used in this entry's yield tiers.
- Documented composition figures (calories and water content) conflict
  moderately between two sources.
- Documented nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium uptake ratios conflict
  between two sources.
- Documented chromosome-number figures conflict considerably across
  three sources (one roughly 64, one roughly 212, and one describing
  111 pseudochromosomes after a whole-genome duplication).
- Documented salt-tolerance findings conflict by growth stage:
  established plants tolerate high salinity in one trial, while
  sprouting corms are suppressed even at low salt concentrations in a
  separate study.
- An older general tropical-crop reference states that no breeding
  programs exist for this crop, while a more recent Chinese
  cultivation review documents an active regional breeding program
  with named modern cultivars — these appear to reflect different time
  periods rather than a genuine conflict.
- This crop's photosynthetic pathway (C3, C4, or an intermediate form)
  was not confirmed in any source; the broader genus is documented
  containing all three types, with at least one species even switching
  pathway type between land and underwater growth.
- Weakest areas overall: water-use parameters (no dedicated study
  found, estimated entirely by analogy to rice); most labor-hour
  figures beyond the one sourced harvest rate (unsourced estimates);
  seed-corm mass and seed rate (estimated); harvest-window length
  (estimated); and photosynthetic pathway (unconfirmed placeholder).

## Estimates and derivations

World-average yield (roughly 20,000 kg/ha) is estimated from the lower
end of several documented commercial-yield ranges across multiple
countries, since no world agricultural statistic exists for this crop
specifically and smaller-scale or lower-input production is documented
yielding less. The poor-yield tier (roughly 5,000 kg/ha) is derived
from a documented North Queensland backyard-bed trial figure, cross-
checked against a documented catfish-pool raft yield figure in a
similar range.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water-use slots) are estimated entirely by analogy to
paddy rice, the closest tabulated emergent flooded crop, since no
standard reference-table row exists for this crop; this crop's above-
water stems are leafless and the field is drained three to four weeks
before harvest, so the late-season crop coefficient is set at a value
between rice's own late-season range. Estimated stage lengths stretch
rice's documented tropical stage-length pattern to match a documented
210-day Texas trial length. These estimated figures represent crop
water use only; paddy percolation and field-preparation water (which
can be substantial in flooded rice systems) are not included. A soil-
moisture-depletion allowance and a very short days-without-water
tolerance of roughly 1 day are both estimated by analogy to rice,
consistent with documented Chinese practice of deliberately drying the
field for short periods mid-season without apparent harm.

A seed-corm rate of roughly 450 kg/ha and a seed-to-yield
multiplication ratio of roughly 44 are derived from a documented wide
planting spacing combined with an assumed individual seed-corm weight;
Chinese direct-planting practice at a considerably higher seed rate
implies a much lower multiplication ratio of roughly 10, consistent
with that practice's own documentation that seed corms represent more
than a tenth of total output.

Harvest labor at the smallest production tier (roughly 830 h/ha) is
derived from the one directly sourced hand-harvest throughput rate,
applied across a range of documented yield levels and adjusted
slightly upward to account for a documented harvest-damage re-sorting
step; all other labor-tier figures and all non-harvest labor operations
are unsourced estimates.

Soil nutrient-uptake figures per tonne of fresh corms are derived from
a documented Chinese nutrient-removal range, converted from oxide units
where necessary; a separate documented nutrient-uptake ratio from a
different source implies relatively more phosphorus and potassium than
this derivation, an unresolved conflict.

Soil pH optimum (roughly 7.1) is estimated as the midpoint of a
documented optimal range; the minimum pH figure reflects a documented
stem-blight threshold on acidic soil, while the maximum pH figure is
not independently sourced.

Storage life (roughly 120 days cool and humid) is derived as a middle
figure between documented "full quality" storage duration (several
weeks) and documented "sound stock" storage duration (up to roughly
six months), appropriate for a general farm cold-storage context.

Nursery establishment period (roughly 40 days) is derived from a
documented Texas planting-to-transplant-size timeline.

Enclosed-space figures (minimum bed depth, edible yield per unit area
per day) are derived from documented root-zone depth combined with
standing-water depth, and from dividing documented yield figures by
documented season lengths.

Genome size (roughly 0.49 pg, 1C) is derived by converting a documented
genome-assembly size in megabases to picograms using a standard
conversion factor, assuming the assembly approximates a haploid genome
size, since no flow-cytometry-based genome-size figure was found.

A dry-matter ratio of roughly 4:1 is derived from documented water-
content figures in two separate composition sources.

A starch-yield-chain ratio of roughly 0.05-0.15 kg of starch per kg of
fresh pulp is estimated from documented starch-content figures in
peeled corms combined with an assumed, unsourced traditional-washing
recovery efficiency.
