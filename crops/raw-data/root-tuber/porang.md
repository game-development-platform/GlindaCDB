# Porang (*Amorphophallus muelleri*)

## Naming and scope

*Amorphophallus muelleri* Blume, arum family (Araceae), universally
called porang in Indonesian agricultural and trade usage, also known
by several regional Indonesian names [1][13]. Indonesian-language
sources frequently use two closely related scientific names
interchangeably for this plant, so figures cited under either name are
treated here as describing the same crop [1][13]. It grows wild from
the Andaman Islands through Myanmar and northern Thailand to Sumatra,
Java, Flores, and Timor [13].

This species is not the same as the Chinese cool-highland gum crop of
the same genus, which this project has already researched as its own
entry (a related species grown the same general way, for the same gum
product, but botanically and climatically distinct), nor is it the
same as a separate, starchy food-corm species of the same genus this
project has also already researched (grown for food starch rather than
industrial gum). This entry is grounded against both of those already-
migrated entries — the first for format precedent and the shared gum-
crop production model, the second for a shared catalog lineage and a
shared calcium-oxalate hazard.

This species is distinctive enough among root and tuber crops to merit
its own entry: a multi-year lift-and-replant cycle whose length
depends on which type of planting material was used to start it
(small bulbil, cut tuber piece, or true seed), a forest-understorey
shade crop requiring no structure to build, a gum product like its
Chinese relative's but from a genetically distinct and far less
thoroughly researched population, and an acridity combining calcium
oxalate with a reported but unidentified alkaloid.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a gum crop much like its better-known Chinese relative,
but whose field cycle depends on which propagule you start with rather
than running on a fixed year count.** A detailed regional plant-
resources reference, read directly for this entry, gives sourced
harvest timing that both refines and partly contradicts older,
less-reliable trade material: harvest comes about 2.5 years after
planting from small tubers, or about 1.5 years from bulbils [1]. Older
trade material's claim that reaching a large, trade-size tuber from a
bulbil takes about three years (because the plant lies dormant roughly
six months of every dry season) appears to describe the traditional,
multi-season route to a large trade tuber, rather than the earliest
possible harvest at a smaller tuber size; both figures are kept here,
flagged as likely measuring different endpoints rather than
genuinely conflicting. Separately, a 2024 Indonesian university field
trial testing different starting materials against varying soil-
organic-carbon levels found that plants started from bulbils gave the
highest eventual yield, followed by tubers grown from leaf cuttings,
and then true seed-origin plants coming in last, with a specific
higher soil-organic-carbon level producing the strongest growth and
yield of any level tested; the exact numeric yields were not available
in the summary consulted [2].

**Finding 2 — propagation has three distinct routes, with
conflicting size and spacing figures across sources.** Indonesian
trade and extension material documents small bulbils running roughly
170-175 to the kilogram (very roughly 5.7-5.9 g each) and larger tuber
pieces running roughly 20-30 to the kilogram (33-50 g each), with
field practice generally favoring bulbils over 2.5 cm in diameter; this
conflicts with an older trade figure giving considerably heavier
bulbils, a discrepancy not resolved here [8][13]. A detailed regional
plant-resources reference gives more formal propagation figures: seed
dormancy is broken by soaking in running water for six days; roughly
50,000 bulbils or roughly 1.8 million true seeds (at around 60%
germination) are used per hectare; planting distances run about 10 cm
for true seed, 35-70 cm for bulbils, and 35-90 cm for tuber pieces; and
growers producing planting stock specifically keep back roughly 25%
of their harvest as seed stock for the next cycle — the same
retention figure documented for the related starchy food-corm species
this project has also researched [1][16]. A popular Indonesian grower
handbook instead recommends a considerably wider spacing, and a
university thesis recommends a tighter spacing for a shorter harvest
target — spacing guidance is clearly cultivar- and goal-dependent and
not standardized across sources [9]. True (sexual) seed does exist and
is occasionally used, though farmers overwhelmingly plant bulbils or
tubers; this revises an older claim that seed is "almost never used"
into "rare, but a real and documented planting option" with its own
sourced dormancy-break method and planting rate [1][2].

**Finding 3 — the species' chromosome makeup is more varied than a
simple "always triploid" description suggests.** Multiple sources
describe this species as triploid, with the extra chromosome set
offered as an explanation for its characteristic asexual (apomictic)
seed production, bulbil formation, and low pollen output [1][11]. A
dedicated cytology study, however, found the species is not uniformly
triploid in the wild: a wild Myanmar population showed four distinct
chromosome counts, and a separate root-tip chromosome-counting study
confirmed triploidy specifically in three cultivated parent plants and
their apomictic progeny, arguing against a sexual "triploid bridge"
origin for those particular individuals [10][11]. A further laboratory
study working from tissue culture reports a diploid base chromosome
count for this species, with artificially chemical-induced triploid
and tetraploid plant lines, several of them a genetic mosaic of more
than one ploidy level [12]. So while cultivated clonal stock is
typically triploid and reproduces asexually, the species as a whole
spans a wider range of natural chromosome variation in the wild than a
flat "always triploid" description would suggest. No complete nuclear
genome assembly was found for this species, only a small chloroplast
genome sequence [11].

**Finding 4 — shade is documented as actively promoting tuber
production, not merely tolerated.** A detailed regional plant-
resources reference states directly that 50-60% shade promotes tuber
production, a sourced, yield-relevant positive response rather than
simply a tolerance [1]. An earlier multi-year phenology study
similarly found the plant responding positively to shading, good
drainage, and heavy organic-matter application [4]. The crop is
accordingly grown as a forest-understorey crop beneath existing tree
canopy — teak, a fast-growing nitrogen-fixing tree used in
agroforestry, rosewood, and mahogany — requiring no shade structure of
its own, often on state-forest or community-forest land [13]. One
regional case study of growing this crop beneath a nitrogen-fixing
shade tree describes short-term income from the understorey crop
combined with long-term income from the timber, while flagging
planting-stock quality shortages and volatile farm-gate prices as
ongoing constraints [6]. One district-level study found average income
from this crop substantially and statistically significantly higher
than from sugarcane grown on comparable land [6].

**Finding 5 — yield remains poorly pinned down overall, but now has
two directly sourced anchor points.** A detailed regional plant-
resources reference states that a three-year-old crop planted at a
specific wide spacing may yield roughly 50 t of fresh tubers per
hectare [1]. A 2020 clone-comparison trial testing ten distinct
genotypes under 45% shade netting found individual corm weights per
plant ranging from under 1 kg up to more than 2.5 kg depending on
genotype, and recommended three specific genotypes as candidates for
new commercial clones, each estimated at field productivity above
30 t/ha pending further farm-scale trials [3]. An unconfirmed, lower-
quality secondary claim describes a gap between potential yield
(roughly 40 t/ha) and typical farmer-level productivity (only roughly
6-10 t/ha); this figure could not be traced to a reliable source and
is recorded here only as a hedge, not used to set any yield figure.
Traditional, low-input yields are documented considerably lower still,
at roughly 2-4 t/ha [13]. A national-statistics-derived figure
(converting total reported dry-tuber production and harvested area
through a documented drying ratio) implies a national average closer
to 9-10 t of fresh tuber per hectare [13].

**Finding 6 — calcium oxalate, now joined by a reported but
unidentified plant alkaloid, both handled through processing.** A
detailed regional plant-resources reference states that the tubers
contain calcium oxalate crystals and alkaloids, making them acrid,
especially in wild plants — the alkaloid claim is new relative to
older trade material, which documented only the calcium-oxalate
hazard; no specific alkaloid compound has been named or measured in
any source consulted here. Processing to remove these compounds uses
industrial wet extraction and purification (saltwater and ethanol
washing, reaching roughly 93% purity in one documented process) rather
than the simple household boiling used for the related starchy food-
corm species; a dilute sodium metabisulfite dip is used to whiten the
finished chips [13]. A detailed regional plant-resources reference adds
a sharp processing deadline: the characteristic gum in grated or
sliced tuber breaks down within 24 hours if left moist, so slices must
be dried quickly after cutting [1].

**Finding 7 — pests and diseases are now documented by name rather
than as a bare mention.** Genus-wide documented threats include a
fungal foot rot, a separate fungal dry rot, a white leaf disease, a
bacterial leaf blight, and two distinct mosaic viruses, plus a leaf
beetle, a tuber weevil, scale insects, caterpillars, and nematodes
[1]. Species-specific Indonesian research adds further detail: a
molecular survey of wilted and rotted plants from two growing regions
identified several specific fungal pathogens as causal agents,
including the same fungal foot-rot organism and a separate fungal wilt
pathogen; a biocontrol trial found a beneficial fungal genus effective
against the wilt disease in tests; storage tuber rot was traced to two
specific named bacteria; and a specific mealybug species was found
infesting stored tubers, with its sap-feeding causing desiccation and
shriveling [5].

**Finding 8 — dormancy can be broken by controlled light exposure, in
addition to chemical growth regulators.** Beyond an unconfirmed claim
that growth regulators can force early shoot emergence, a university
study on small tubers found that one month of controlled light
exposure broke dormancy outright, with 16 hours of light per day
giving the strongest bud growth and the largest tuber weight gain
(more than doubling, and then some) of any treatment tested — a second,
independently sourced dormancy-breaking technique working through
photoperiod manipulation rather than chemistry [7]. Separately,
growing containers that are too small for the nursery stage trigger
early dormancy and produce smaller daughter corms [7].

**Finding 9 — fertilizer trials conflict by soil type and give no
single clean per-hectare recommendation.** On mineral soil
intercropped with oil palm, a specific combined urea, phosphate, and
potash rate per plant gave the best growth among tested rates, with a
statistically significant effect on bulbil count specifically but not
on plant height, stem diameter, leaf count, leaf greenness, or tuber
weight [9]. On peat soil, one trial found a specific NPK rate the most
efficient among a wide range tested, while a separate, shorter trial
found a combined dolomite-lime and NPK treatment sufficient for
growth [9]. These per-plant and per-hectare figures cannot be
reconciled into one clean recommendation without a settled planting
density, which itself remains unresolved across sources (Finding 2).

**Finding 10 — a first officially released Indonesian cultivar, and an
early bulbil-size effect on vigor.** A 2021 conference paper documents
the first officially released cultivar of this crop in Indonesia, with
a separate university study finding that heavier bulbils (around 5 g)
produced significantly larger plants — greater height, crown size, and
stem diameter — than a range of lighter bulbil weights tested over a
three-month period [14].

**Finding 11 — export trade exists but is thin, intermittent, and
increasingly regulated.** The only dated shipment figure found
documents a roughly 110-tonne consignment of dried chips shipped to
China in mid-2022, described as the first shipment after a roughly
two-year closure of the Chinese market; China has since required
registration of porang-producing land before accepting imports again
[13]. A 2023 processing facility on Lombok was documented buying
farmers' fresh tubers at a fixed price, sized to process several
hundred tonnes of tubers per month toward an annual target of several
hundred tonnes of finished gum flour for both domestic and export
markets [13]. No 2023 or 2024 Indonesian export tonnage to China was
found in this research pass.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A tropical, warm-season forest-understorey
  corm crop; shoots emerge with the rains and the plant lies dormant
  through the dry season, with a multi-year lift-and-replant cycle
  whose length depends on the starting planting material (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single dig at dormancy; the multi-year size
  gate (small early tuber versus large trade-size tuber) is a
  cultivation-path choice rather than a separate harvest pattern
  (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Tropical lowland and lower-montane zones up to roughly
  700-900 m, frost-intolerant (climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Never eaten raw — acrid with calcium oxalate and a
  reported, unidentified alkaloid (Finding 6).
- **Pollination:** Not relevant to production — the crop is grown and
  lifted clonally, well before flowering size, and true seed (when
  used at all) is a minor alternative planting-stock route rather than
  a yield driver (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** A gum crop, not a starch crop; documented
  yields span a very wide range, from roughly 2-4 t/ha under
  traditional low-input management up to roughly 50 t/ha in a
  dedicated research trial (Finding 5; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Needs steady rain from shoot emergence through tuber
  bulking, in loose, shaded, well-drained soil; never tolerates
  swampy or clay ground, and must stay dry while dormant or the tuber
  rots (water-use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively, overwhelmingly from
  bulbils or cut tuber pieces, planted into loose, shaded, well-
  drained soil under existing tree canopy; true seed is a documented
  but minor alternative (Finding 2).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist specifically for this
  crop; figures here are estimated by analogy to its two already-
  researched relatives (labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Well-drained, humus-rich, deep sandy loam at pH roughly
  6-7.5; clay soil is unsuitable and hampers tuber development
  (Finding 9; soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as an annual-to-multi-year lift-and-replant
  crop depending on starting planting material and target tuber size
  (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal foot rot, a separate fungal wilt
  disease, several further fungal pathogens, bacterial storage rot,
  and a storage mealybug are all documented by name; genus-wide
  threats include further fungal diseases, two mosaic viruses, and
  several insect pests (Finding 7; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** One officially released Indonesian cultivar, several
  promising station-trial genotypes under development, and otherwise
  undifferentiated regional landraces (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Hand-dug at dormancy, under existing tree canopy with
  no structure to remove first (Finding 1, 4).
- **Storage:** Dormant tubers must be kept dry and well-ventilated or
  they rot; sliced tuber must be dried within about a day of cutting
  or its gum content degrades; bulbils keep roughly six months in open
  air, not in sealed plastic (Finding 2, 6).

Climate detail: grown up to roughly 700-900 m elevation, with an
optimum air temperature of roughly 25-35°C and an optimum soil
temperature of roughly 22-30°C; entirely frost-intolerant, consistent
with its fully tropical native range.

Water-use detail: no official crop-coefficient curve exists for this
species; one is estimated here by analogy to its already-researched
Chinese relative, with stage lengths stretched considerably to reflect
this crop's longer, multi-season-per-dig cycle, using the shortest
sourced single-dig interval (the roughly 1.5-year bulbil route) as the
basis for a single growing cycle. The crop has low drought tolerance
by the same analogy, and is documented never tolerating waterlogged
or clay soil at all.

Labor detail: no sourced labor-hour figures exist specifically for
this crop; figures are estimated by analogy to its two already-
researched relatives, with harvest (hand-digging at dormancy) as the
largest single component, and a new, unmodelled dormancy-breaking
operation (either a light-exposure treatment or a growth-regulator
soak) added as a distinct, lightly estimated labor step.

Soil detail: fertilizer response differs substantially by soil type —
mineral soil under partial tree-crop shade responds mainly in bulbil
count to a specific combined fertilizer rate per plant, while peat
soil responds to differing bulk NPK rates across separate trials — and
no single reconciled per-hectare recommendation currently exists
across this conflicting trial evidence.

Cultivar detail: one cultivar has been officially released by the
Indonesian government as a clonal selection; several further station-
trial genotypes have shown notably larger average corm weight than
others under identical trial conditions and have been recommended as
candidate new commercial clones pending further farm-scale testing;
otherwise, farm-saved regional landraces remain largely
undifferentiated.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw tubers contain needle-shaped calcium oxalate
crystals and a reported but unidentified plant alkaloid, making them
acrid and irritating, worst in wild plants; the crop is always
processed (by industrial wet extraction and purification, not simple
household boiling) before any food or industrial use. This hazard is
shared with other crops in the same plant family that this project
has also researched. Deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data (Finding 6).

Field and storage disease pressure includes a fungal foot rot, a
separate fungal wilt disease, further fungal pathogens causing wilting
and rot, two bacterial species causing storage tuber rot, and a
mealybug species that infests and shrivels stored tubers through sap-
feeding; genus-wide pressures documented elsewhere include a further
fungal dry rot, a white leaf disease, a bacterial leaf blight, two
distinct mosaic viruses, and several additional insect pests
(Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** bulbils or tuber pieces → planted under existing tree
  canopy → dug at dormancy as a small early tuber (from bulbils, the
  faster route) or a large trade-size tuber (from tubers, or carried
  over additional seasons). Fresh tubers → washed and sliced → dried
  quickly (within about a day, before the gum content degrades) →
  dried chips. Dried chips → milled and purified (saltwater and
  ethanol washing) → refined gum flour, whitened with a dilute
  sulfite dip. Gum flour → the same gelled food product made from the
  related Chinese species' flour. Harvested tubers → roughly a
  quarter retained as the next cycle's planting stock.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** the specific unnamed
  alkaloid's chemistry; current (2023-2024) export volumes; the exact
  commercial breeding or release process behind the newly released
  cultivar; large-scale mechanized harvest, if any exists.

No specific religious or ritual production practice was found for
this crop in the sources consulted, in contrast to some of its
relatives.

## Open research questions

- Documented harvest timing from a detailed regional reference
  (roughly 1.5 years from bulbils, roughly 2.5 years from small
  tubers) appears to measure a different endpoint than older trade
  material's "traditional three years to a large trade-size tuber,"
  but no source directly reconciles the two — recorded as a probable
  difference in measurement endpoint, not resolved here.
- Planting density and fertilizer recommendations remain unresolved
  across at least four sources, with spacing recommendations ranging
  from a tight 30×30 cm up to roughly 90 cm depending on propagule
  type and source, and fertilizer recommendations ranging from a small
  per-plant dose up to a thousand kilograms per hectare depending on
  soil type.
- An unconfirmed secondary claim describing a large gap between
  potential yield and typical farmer-level productivity could not be
  traced to a reliable source and is recorded only as a hedge.
- The specific alkaloid compound reported alongside calcium oxalate in
  raw tubers has not been named, measured, or evaluated for toxicity
  in any source consulted.
- The species' chromosome-count variation (spanning diploid, triploid,
  and tetraploid forms across wild populations and laboratory-induced
  lines) is a genuinely unusual trait among crops this project has
  documented, worth flagging for any future cross-crop ploidy
  analysis.
- Documented bulbil weight figures conflict by roughly a factor of
  three to four between two source types (older trade material's
  heavier figure versus a detailed thesis-sourced lighter figure),
  plausibly reflecting different cultivars, maturities, or counting
  conventions (whole versus graded bulbils) — not resolved here.
- No current (2023-2024) Indonesian export tonnage to China was found;
  only a single dated 2022 shipment and a 2023 processing-facility
  capacity figure were located.
- Weakest areas overall: no fresh-tuber composition data specific to
  this species was found (calorie and protein figures here rely
  entirely on analogy to its Chinese relative); labor-hour figures are
  likewise purely analogical; and several key secondary sources (a
  planting-material field trial, a genotype-comparison trial, and a
  pests-and-diseases journal article) were read only as search-
  extracted abstracts, not as full original documents.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 9,000 kg/ha, good 30,000 kg/ha, exceptional
50,000 kg/ha, poor 3,000 kg/ha) are derived from a national-statistics-
implied average (converting total reported dry production and
harvested area through a documented drying ratio) for the world-
average tier, a research-station elite-clone trial's productivity
recommendation for the good tier, a detailed regional reference's
documented spaced research-trial yield for the exceptional tier, and
documented traditional low-input yield for the poor tier; all figures
describe fresh tuber weight per multi-year crop cycle, not an
annualized rate.

Energy and protein content of the fresh tuber (roughly 350 kcal and
15 g protein per kilogram) are estimated entirely by analogy to the
crop's already-researched Chinese relative, since no fresh-tuber
composition source specific to this species was found in this
research pass.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, root
depth, and a soil-moisture depletion allowance) are estimated by
analogy to the same Chinese relative crop, with stage lengths
stretched considerably longer than that single-season crop's cycle to
reflect this crop's longer, multi-season-per-dig growing pattern,
using the shortest sourced single-dig interval (the roughly 1.5-year
bulbil route) as the basis for one complete growing cycle.

A planting-material multiplication figure of roughly 10 is estimated
from a documented bulbil-count-per-plant progression across
successive growing years, representing a bulbil-count multiplier
rather than a tuber-mass enlargement ratio, since no sourced
enlargement-ratio figure (as exists for the related Chinese crop)
was found for this species.

A seed-reserve fraction of roughly 0.25 is directly sourced from a
documented practice among planting-stock growers of retaining about a
quarter of the harvest as the next cycle's planting material — the
same figure documented for a related starchy food-corm species this
project has also researched.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh tuber) and soil rooting depth are estimated entirely by
analogy to the crop's already-researched Chinese relative, since no
species-specific figures were found; optimal soil pH (roughly
6.0-7.5), by contrast, is directly sourced from a detailed regional
plant-resources reference and is narrower than the wider analogy-based
range that would otherwise have been used.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 5-14 g/m²/day as fresh
tuber) is derived by dividing representative yield figures by the
crop's documented multi-year cycle length in days, rather than by a
single growing season, to reflect the true land-occupation time of
this multi-year crop.
