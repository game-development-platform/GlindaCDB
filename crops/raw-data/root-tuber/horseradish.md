## Naming and scope

Cultivated *Armoracia rusticana*, family Brassicaceae. A hardy
perennial growing to 1.5 m, native to southeastern Europe and western
Asia, grown for its large white tapered root. This entry treats it as
a single species with a single product (the root); its two main
cultivar groups and several named selections are documented as
cultivar variations, not treated as separate crops, since they differ
mainly in leaf shape, eating quality, and disease resistance rather
than in anything else this project models.

This crop is grounded against radish and turnip, two related mustard-
family root crops this project has also researched (used here for
water-use analogy), and against maca, another related mustard-family
root crop this project has also researched (used for pungency-
chemistry analogy); none of the three has yet been migrated into this
collection, so they are referenced generally rather than by file.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — propagated only from root cuttings, which are
themselves a byproduct of the crop.** Planting material ("sets") are
pencil-thick pieces of side root, roughly 20-35 cm long, cut with a
square top and a slanted bottom so the orientation is always known,
then planted at a 45-degree angle with all tops facing one way, at a
shallow depth [2][3][4]. Typical planting density is roughly
21,500-24,000 sets per hectare, in rows 75-90 cm apart with 38-60 cm
spacing within the row [2][3]. These sets are literally a byproduct:
side roots stripped off during a specific field operation and again
at harvest are bundled and overwintered in moist sand in a pit or
cold cellar until spring planting [2][3][4]. Cultivated horseradish
rarely sets viable seed; tissue culture is the only non-vegetative
propagation route, and is considerably more expensive [2][4]. Dividing
a root crown (quartering a root so each piece carries both leaf and
root tissue) is a documented garden-scale alternative [4].

**Finding 2 — a named field operation ("lifting") that forces a
smoother, more marketable root.** Side roots are deliberately removed
twice per season — first when the largest leaves reach about 20-25 cm
long, and again roughly six weeks later — specifically to force "a
comparatively smooth root free from side roots." During this
operation, the crown is lifted, only the best single sprout is kept,
small side roots are rubbed off, and the main root is reset into the
ground [3][4]. The side roots removed during this operation become
next year's planting sets [3][4].

**Finding 3 — a perennial grown as an annual dig crop, invasive if
left undisturbed, and requiring a cold winter.** The plant is hardy
across a wide range of cold climate zones, and is grown less
successfully as an annual in warmer zones [1]. It requires cold winter
temperatures for proper dormancy [1][5]. If left undisturbed, it
"spreads via underground shoots and can become invasive"; roots left
in the ground go woody over time, and small leftover root fragments
readily sprout as volunteer weeds — harvesting equipment is
deliberately set deep specifically to recover as much root as possible
and limit this problem [1][2][4]. This places horseradish among a
small group of crops this project has researched that share this
volunteer/invasive persistence risk. The growing season runs roughly
one year: planted in early spring, dug in autumn after frost kills the
foliage, or sometimes left until the following spring [2][3].

**Finding 4 — pungency is created only by physical damage, fades
quickly, and irritates handlers.** The intact root is nearly odorless.
Crushing or grating the root allows an enzyme to break down a specific
glucosinolate compound into allyl isothiocyanate (AITC), the "mustard
oil" responsible for the characteristic heat [1]. AITC is unstable and
degrades over a period of days (faster when warm), which is why grated
horseradish and prepared sauces lose their bite unless vinegar is added
to the mixture — the acid halts the breakdown reaction and locks in the
flavor [1]. The vapor released during grating is a lachrymator and a
skin/airway irritant; the distilled essential oil (essentially pure
AITC) is a genuinely hazardous material, not a food product in its own
right.

**Finding 5 — long storage as a whole root, much shorter storage once
processed.** Commercial storage uses roughly -1 to 0°C, 90-95%
relative humidity, for 10-12 months, kept in the dark (light causes
the root to green) [2]; perforated bags help hold humidity, and roots
dug while still actively growing store noticeably worse than fully
cold-conditioned ones [2]. Straw-lined pits or trenches are a
documented farm-scale alternative to mechanical cold storage [3].
Whole roots keep only about 3 months at home storage scale [4];
prepared (grated, vinegar-fixed) horseradish keeps about 3 months
refrigerated before it darkens and loses potency [1].

**Finding 6 — a serious soil-borne and stock-borne disease complex,
with a required rotation practice.** Root rots are described as the
most serious problem, worsened by overwatering; a specific white-rust
disease, a wilt disease, and a mosaic virus also occur, and because
the planting stock is root cuttings taken directly from the crop,
disease and virus travel with the sets from one planting to the next
[2][4][18]. Documented pests include a cabbage-feeding caterpillar, a
specific leaf beetle, and a horseradish-specific flea beetle [1][18].
One of the two main cultivar groups carries notably better disease
resistance than the other, higher-quality group; one specific named
selection is documented as resistant to both the white-rust disease
and the mosaic virus [2][4][13]. Recommended rotation practice is to
avoid planting the same land with this crop more than once every 3-4
years [18].

**Finding 7 — modest, well-documented yield.** One national
agricultural bulletin gives a yield range from "less than 2,000 to
more than 9,000 pounds of marketable roots to the acre" (roughly
2.2 to 10+ t/ha); good growing conditions are documented at roughly
7.8-9.0 t/ha [2][3]. A home-garden-scale figure gives a considerably
lower per-area yield [4]. European production totals roughly 30,000
tonnes per year, with one country the largest single producer at
roughly 12,000 tonnes; major production centers in the United States
include three specific named localities [1].

**Finding 8 — a condiment with deep cultural and religious
significance, plus a biotech byproduct and an essential oil.** The
main commercial forms are prepared horseradish (grated root preserved
in vinegar, either plain white or mixed with beetroot into a red
variant used in Ashkenazi Jewish cooking), various cream- or fruit-
based horseradish sauces, a dried powder commonly rehydrated and sold
as a substitute for genuine wasabi, and a distilled volatile mustard
oil [1]. **Religious use:** grated horseradish or the beetroot-mixed
red variant is widely used by Ashkenazi Jews as the bitter herb at the
Passover seder, with documented use traced back to at least the 12th
century; in parts of Christian Central and Eastern Europe, it is eaten
at Eastertide as a symbolic reference to the Passion [1][11]. There is
genuine rabbinic debate on this practice: many religious authorities
actually prefer a leafy green like lettuce or endive for this ritual
role, and the identification of horseradish with the bitter herb named
in older religious texts is itself disputed, since horseradish is
sharp rather than bitter and was likely not present in the region
during the relevant historical period [11]. **Biotech use:** a specific
enzyme extracted from this plant is a widely used laboratory reagent
in immunoassays and histology — an industrial extraction process
outside the scope of this entry. **Geographic protection:** a specific
Bavarian horseradish product and a separate Spreewald regional product
both hold EU protected-geographical-indication status, the former
requiring 100% regional raw material processed and bottled within the
same region, on a documented tradition of more than 500 years [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A hardy herbaceous perennial, grown as an
  annual dig crop; planted in early spring as soon as soil is
  workable, with a cold autumn improving root size and flavor
  (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — the whole root dug once per
  season, modeled here as an annual crop despite the plant's perennial
  habit (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate and temperate zones, hardy across a wide
  cold-climate range; grown less successfully as an annual in warmer
  zones (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible — grated fresh raw is the normal
  condiment form, strongly pungent (Finding 4, 8).
- **Pollination:** Not required — the crop is clonal and cultivated
  plants rarely set viable seed (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** A documented range from roughly 2.2 to
  10+ t/ha, with good growing conditions reliably producing roughly
  7.8-9.0 t/ha (Finding 7; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard crop-water-use reference includes a
  dedicated figure for this crop; water-use parameters used here are
  estimated by analogy to radish and turnip, two related mustard-
  family root crops, adjusted upward for this crop's much larger
  canopy. A dry spell during late-summer root bulking costs yield and
  makes the root woody; overwatering makes roots soft, overly strong-
  flavored, and prone to root rot (water-use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from root-cutting sets
  planted at a 45-degree angle, or from crown divisions as a garden-
  scale alternative; true seed propagation is essentially unavailable
  commercially (Finding 1).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for any operation on
  this crop; labor estimates used here are rough analogies based on
  the operations named in a national agricultural bulletin and
  extension guides (deep land preparation, angled set planting,
  twice-seasonal lifting, deep-plough harvest, and post-harvest
  trimming/washing/bundling).
- **Soil:** Deep loam or sandy loam with good organic matter and no
  hardpan is wanted; preferred pH is roughly 6.0-7.5, with lime
  amendment recommended toward the lower end of that range; this crop
  is notably boron- and sulfur-hungry, consistent with its sulfur-
  containing pungency chemistry (Finding 6; soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as an annual dig crop despite the plant's
  genuinely perennial, spreading habit; if left undug, it persists and
  spreads via underground shoots and becomes invasive (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Root rots are the most serious problem; a
  specific white-rust disease, a wilt disease, and a mosaic virus
  travel with the clonal planting stock; documented pests include a
  cabbage-feeding caterpillar, a specific leaf beetle, and a
  horseradish-specific flea beetle (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** Two main cultivar groups exist, trading off eating
  quality against disease resistance, plus several named regional
  selections (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single dig, typically by deep plough followed by
  hand lifting, in autumn after frost kills the foliage, or sometimes
  deferred to the following spring (Finding 3, 7).
- **Storage:** Long commercial cold storage of 10-12 months at
  roughly -1 to 0°C and 90-95% relative humidity, kept dark to prevent
  greening; home storage of whole roots is much shorter, around 3
  months; prepared (grated, vinegar-fixed) horseradish also keeps
  about 3 months refrigerated (Finding 5).

## Hazards

Root rots are the most serious disease problem, worsened by
overwatering; a specific white-rust disease, a wilt disease, and a
mosaic virus all travel with the clonal planting stock, since the
planting material is cut directly from the crop itself. Documented
pests include a cabbage-feeding caterpillar, a specific leaf beetle,
and a horseradish-specific flea beetle (Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — cutting or grating this crop's root releases
allyl isothiocyanate, a genuine lachrymator and skin/airway irritant;
the distilled essential oil is a hazardous material in its own right,
not a food product. This is a real handling hazard for anyone
processing the root at scale, deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 4).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's foliage, culls, and spent processing
mash contain the same glucosinolate/mustard-oil compounds found
throughout the mustard family, which are documented as unsafe to feed
to livestock in quantity. Deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data (Finding 8 cross-reference).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** side roots (removed during the twice-seasonal lifting
  operation and at harvest) → bundled and overwintered in moist sand
  → next season's planting stock. Separately: roots → trimmed (side
  roots and culls removed) → washed → cold-stored for 10-12 months, or
  sold fresh. Washed root → grated → mixed with vinegar → prepared
  horseradish (plain white, or mixed with beetroot into a red variant).
  Washed root → dried → milled → powder, commonly sold as a wasabi
  substitute. Grated root → steam-distilled → volatile mustard oil
  plus a spent-mash byproduct.
- Not covered by this research: the specific laboratory-enzyme
  extraction process used to produce the biotech reagent noted above;
  multi-ingredient kitchen products such as cream- or fruit-based
  horseradish sauces (these combine this crop with other ingredients
  entirely outside this entry's scope); canned or frozen prepared
  horseradish.

Grated horseradish is widely used by Ashkenazi Jews as the bitter herb
at the Passover seder, with documented use traced to at least the
12th century, though its identification with the specific bitter herb
named in older religious texts is itself disputed among religious
authorities. In parts of Christian Central and Eastern Europe, it is
eaten at Eastertide as a symbolic reference to the Passion.

## Open research questions

- The growing-season length conflicts somewhat across sources
  (roughly 200-240 days for an autumn dig, versus "about one year" if
  the dig is deferred to the following spring) — both figures are
  treated as valid depending on timing, not a true conflict.
- No clean government composition entry for the raw root was found;
  only a "prepared" (vinegar-diluted) form is tabulated, meaning the
  raw root's actual caloric and protein content is likely
  underestimated by any figure derived from the prepared product.
- Storage-life figures differ sharply between commercial (10-12
  months) and home-scale (about 3 months) practice — likely reflecting
  genuinely different storage conditions (precise cold/humidity
  control vs. a basic cellar or refrigerator) rather than a true
  conflict.
- Preferred soil pH range differs slightly across sources (6.0-6.5 in
  one source vs. 6.0-7.5 in another) — not a major discrepancy.
- A genome-size figure from one source's data table was described as
  garbled in its original layout and is flagged here as unverified.
- No sourced labor-hour figures exist for any operation on this crop;
  all labor estimates used here are rough analogies based on named
  operations, not direct measurement.
- No standard crop-water-use reference includes a dedicated figure
  for this crop; water-use parameters are estimated by analogy to
  related mustard-family root crops.
- Nutrient-removal figures (as opposed to the much higher documented
  fertilizer application rates) are derived indirectly from
  composition data rather than from a direct tissue-removal study.
- The yield tier used for "exceptional" production in this entry is
  an estimate above the highest sourced yield ceiling, not itself
  directly sourced.
- A set-weight figure used to derive seeding rate and multiplication
  ratio is an estimate based on typical set dimensions, not a direct
  grower-reported measurement.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric content (roughly 480 kcal/kg) and protein (roughly 12 g/kg)
are derived from a government composition entry for the *prepared*
(vinegar-diluted) form of this crop, scaled to a per-kilogram basis;
the raw root is almost certainly higher in both calories and protein,
likely in the range of 600-720 kcal/kg, since no clean raw-root
composition entry was found. As a condiment eaten in small quantities,
this figure is of limited practical importance to how the crop is
actually consumed.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 8,000 kg/ha, good 10,000 kg/ha, exceptional
12,000 kg/ha, poor 2,200 kg/ha) are derived from a sourced good-
conditions range (roughly 7.8-9.0 t/ha) and a sourced overall range
(roughly 2.2 to 10+ t/ha); the exceptional tier is an estimate placed
above the highest documented figure, since no source gives a specific
ceiling above roughly 10 t/ha.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water-use index, drought-buffer days) are estimated by analogy to
radish and turnip, two related mustard-family root crops with standard
crop-water-use reference figures, adjusted upward to reflect this
crop's much larger, denser canopy (up to 1.5 m tall with leaves to 1 m)
and longer growing season.

Seeding-rate and multiplication-ratio figures (roughly 1,000 kg/ha of
planting-material sets, implying a multiplication ratio of about 8 on
a mass basis) are derived from documented planting density combined
with an estimated individual set weight based on typical set
dimensions, since no grower-reported set-weight figure was found.

Pack-out fraction (roughly 0.6-0.8) and the fresh-to-dried conversion
ratio (roughly 3.3-4.5:1) are derived from documented trimming losses
(side roots, crown, and culls removed before grading) and the root's
documented water content.

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are rough analogies based
on the operations explicitly named in a national agricultural bulletin
and extension guides, scaled similarly to other root crops this
project has researched; no direct labor-hour measurement exists for
this crop.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh root) are derived indirectly from the root's
composition data, distinct from the much higher documented fertilizer
*application* rates, which reflect agronomic practice rather than
actual nutrient removal by the harvested crop.

Genome size (roughly 0.75 pg, 1C) is derived from a documented
tetraploid chromosome count combined with a genome-sequencing paper's
reported assembly size, though the source table's original layout was
described as garbled, so this figure is flagged as needing independent
verification.
