## Naming and scope

Cultivated parsnip, *Pastinaca sativa* L. subsp. *sativa*, carrot
family (Apiaceae), diploid with 22 chromosomes [15]. Wild parsnip (a
different subspecies of the same plant) is a weedy field-edge relative
richer in the skin-burning compounds described below [13]. This entry
covers the cultivated root crop as a single entry; it is grounded
against a closely related biennial root crop of the same plant family,
already migrated into this collection, for formatting precedent and
for shared traits (humid cold storage, ethylene-driven bitterness).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — cold makes parsnips sweet, whether in the field or in
storage.** Early-harvested roots held at 0-1.1°C reach, within two
weeks, the same sweetness and quality that later-harvested roots
develop only after two full months of autumn field frosts [3]. In a
long-term cold-storage study, root starch content (measured at
4.5-5.4% at harvest) fell to below a tenth of its starting level
within the first month of storage, with essentially complete
conversion to sugar by the end of the second month [7]. Roots can also
simply be left in the ground through winter and dug as needed, so long
as digging finishes before spring regrowth begins [9]. The practical
upshot is that cold acts here as a genuine quality upgrade (sweetening
through starch-to-sugar conversion) rather than a damage trigger, with
the field and a cold store driving the same process at different
rates.

**Finding 2 — storage wants near-freezing temperatures in nearly
saturated air.** A detailed storage manual recommends 0-1.1°C at
98-100% relative humidity for four to six months, with roots cooled
promptly to 5°C or below by hydrocooling or package icing; a separate
extension source gives a somewhat different 0-4°C and 90-95% relative
humidity range for two to six months [3][4]. Parsnips dry out easily
in storage — ventilated plastic liners help retain moisture, though
waxing does not [3]. Slight freezing causes no harm, but hard, sustained
freezing does; the root's highest freezing point is documented at
about -0.9°C [3]. As with a related root crop this project has also
researched, ethylene exposure from nearby ripening fruit (apples,
pears, peaches) raises phenolic compounds and makes stored roots
bitter [3]. Surface skin color shifts from white to a dull yellowish-
brown in storage after abrasion, an enzymatic browning reaction that
low temperature and high humidity both slow down [3].

**Finding 3 — a long, slow-growing crop grown from notably short-
lived seed.** Documented maturity ranges from roughly 95 to 130 days
depending on source and cultivar [5]. Seed emerges within three to
four weeks in cool soil around 10°C, with an ideal germination
temperature closer to 20°C, and emergence is notably poor in very warm
soil [5]. Seed viability is short — about one year under favorable
storage conditions, with this crop grouped alongside two close
relatives (carrot and onion) as "generally useless after one to two
years' storage under average commercial conditions" by one classic
agricultural-science reference [10].

**Finding 4 — the plant's sap can burn skin in sunlight.** This crop
contains several documented plant defense compounds (furanocoumarins)
in its leaf and stem sap [13]. Sap contact on skin followed by
sunlight exposure causes a burn-like reaction with redness and
blistering, and the affected skin can remain unusually light-sensitive
for up to two years afterward; cultivated parsnip carries measurably
lower levels of these compounds than its wild relative [13]. Documented
livestock cases include severe skin photosensitization in goats and a
horse from direct skin contact, and a separate case series of nine
horses that developed photosensitization and eye injury after eating
hay heavily contaminated with the wild relative [13][14].

**Finding 5 — water use and salinity sensitivity.** An international
reference crop-coefficient table gives this crop a Kc curve of
roughly 0.5 (initial), 1.05 (mid-season), and 0.95 (late), a plant
height around 0.4 m, a rooting depth of 0.5-1.0 m, and a soil-moisture
depletion allowance of roughly 0.40, though it provides no documented
stage lengths [1]. A separate water-quality reference rates this crop
as salinity-sensitive, though on an estimated basis with no specific
threshold or slope value established [2].

**Finding 6 — the seed crop is a distinct, biennial undertaking.**
This crop is biennial, setting true seed only in its second year [6].
Seed-crop plants are spaced roughly 60-90 cm apart, with at least
15-20 plants kept together to maintain genetic diversity [6]. The
flowers are insect-pollinated and protandrous — a flower's male parts
mature before its female parts, so a single flower cannot self-
pollinate, though different flower clusters on the very same plant
can still cross with each other [6]. Documented isolation-distance
guidance for seed purity ranges from roughly 300 m between varieties
(reducible to 100 m behind a hedge) up to a full mile among dedicated
seed-saving growers, and this crop is documented crossing readily with
its wild relative [6]. The primary (first, central) flower cluster on
a plant gives the best seed quality, with later, smaller clusters
giving progressively poorer seed [6]. At least one F1 hybrid cultivar
has been commercially sold since the early 1980s, though the specific
breeding method used to produce hybrid seed in this species was not
found in the sources used here [11].

**Finding 7 — history and named cultivars.** Before cane sugar
reached Europe in quantity, this root served as an everyday natural
sweetener, and it is still made into a traditional rustic wine in some
regions [8]. One well-known heirloom cultivar was selected from wild
parsnip by a 19th-century English agricultural researcher starting in
1847, with its commercial release dated variously to around 1860 or
1869 depending on source; a second, older heirloom cultivar is dated
to either just before 1830 or to the 1850s in different sources, and
some sources treat the two heirlooms as effectively the same cultivar
under different names — a conflict not resolved here [8]. A fungal
canker disease is a major cultivar-selection driver: several named
modern cultivars are sold specifically for canker resistance, while
one professional crop-research center reports that resistance has
proven difficult to breed for reliably; hilling soil up over the
root's exposed shoulders is documented to reduce canker incidence
directly [11]. One major European growing region is documented
farming roughly 4,000 hectares of this crop, with a typical yield
figure of roughly 50 t/ha and a documented range of 40-60 t/ha [12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial root crop grown as
  an annual; frost-hardy roots can overwinter directly in the ground
  (Finding 1, 7).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest, either as an autumn lift
  after the first frosts or dug progressively through winter directly
  from the field (Finding 1; harvest-stage detail below).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, though usually cooked.
- **Pollination:** Not required for the root crop; the seed crop is
  insect-pollinated and protandrous, meaning individual flowers cannot
  self-pollinate (Finding 6).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields run roughly
  40-60 t/ha in one major growing region, with no broader worldwide
  yield statistic available (see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Needs even moisture while the long roots fill out
  over a long growing season (water-use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded; germination is notably slow,
  taking three to four weeks even in favorably cool soil (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures were found for any
  operation.
- **Soil:** Deep, stone-free soil is recommended to grow a straight,
  unblemished root, though this specific guidance was not independently
  sourced; the crop is rated salinity-sensitive on an estimated basis
  (Finding 5).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as an annual despite being genuinely
  biennial; roots can be left in the ground over winter without
  triggering second-year regrowth if dug before spring (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal canker disease is the dominant
  disease concern and the main driver of modern cultivar selection,
  managed partly through hilling soil over the root's shoulders and
  through resistant cultivar choice; various storage rots (bacterial
  soft rot, grey mold, a separate watery soft rot, and a bacterial
  black rot) also occur (Finding 7; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Historical open-pollinated heirlooms alongside modern
  canker-resistant cultivars, including at least one F1 hybrid
  (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Hand- or machine-lifted after the first autumn frosts,
  or dug progressively through winter from roots left standing in the
  field (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Near-freezing and very high humidity (roughly
  0-1.1°C at 98-100% relative humidity) for four to six months, with
  prompt cooling after harvest essential to quality (Finding 2).

Harvest-stage detail: an autumn-harvested stage (after the first
frosts, suited to cold storage, winemaking, or seed-crop
continuation) and a separate overwintered stage (roots dug as needed
through the winter directly from the field, before spring regrowth
begins) are both documented production paths for this crop.

Water-use detail: no documented stage-length breakdown exists for
this crop's international reference crop-coefficient curve; stage
lengths used here are estimated from the crop's overall documented
maturity range. The crop's long growing season means the period of
meaningful water sensitivity is correspondingly long.

Cultivar detail: historical open-pollinated heirlooms include two
long-standing English cultivars with disputed exact release dates
(one selected directly from a wild relative in the mid-19th century),
plus a documented regional half-long-root cultivar type; modern
cultivars include several bred or marketed specifically for canker
resistance, and at least one F1 hybrid cultivar first released in the
early 1980s.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the plant's leaf and stem sap contains several
documented defense compounds (furanocoumarins) that cause a burn-like
skin reaction when sap-contaminated skin is then exposed to sunlight,
with affected skin remaining unusually light-sensitive for up to two
years; cultivated parsnip carries measurably lower levels of these
compounds than its wild relative, but handling the crop's foliage
still warrants gloves and covered skin. Documented livestock cases
include severe skin photosensitization in goats and a horse from
direct contact, and photosensitization with eye injury in horses that
ate contaminated hay. Deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data (Finding 4).

A fungal canker disease is the dominant disease concern, managed
through hilling soil over the root's shoulders, long crop rotations,
well-drained fields, and resistant cultivar choice; bacterial soft
rot, grey mold, a watery soft rot, and a bacterial black rot are also
documented storage diseases (Finding 2, 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → autumn-harvested roots → topped, culled, and
  cooled → cold-conditioned for about two weeks → stored four to six
  months. Alternatively: seed → roots left standing in the ground →
  dug progressively through winter. Cooled roots plus sugar →
  traditional rustic wine. Seed crop: selected roots replanted or left
  in place over winter → second-year flowering umbels → threshed and
  cleaned seed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** frozen product; the
  specific breeding method used to produce F1 hybrid seed in this
  species; the historical use of this root as an everyday sweetener
  before cane sugar became widely available.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables and other underground
storage organs generally, a note this project has also recorded for
other root crops. No other religious or ritual use was found in the
sources consulted.

## Open research questions

- Recommended cold-storage relative humidity and resulting storage
  life conflict somewhat between two sources (98-100% relative
  humidity for four to six months versus 90-95% for two to six
  months) — not resolved here.
- Documented maturity range differs somewhat across seed-catalogue
  sources (roughly 95-125 days versus 110-130 days).
- Recommended seed-isolation distance for maintaining variety purity
  varies by an order of magnitude between general seed-saving guidance
  (roughly 300 m) and dedicated seed-saver practice (up to a mile).
- The exact release date of one heirloom cultivar, and whether it is
  genuinely distinct from a second, similarly dated heirloom
  cultivar, is disputed across sources and not resolved here.
- Documented lists of canker-resistant cultivars differ somewhat
  across sources, and one professional crop-research center reports
  that breeding reliable resistance has proven difficult in practice.
- Weakest areas overall: no documented seed-crop yield or vernalization
  requirement was found; labor hours, nutrient-removal figures, and
  waterlogging tolerance are all unsourced; and the specific breeding
  method behind this crop's F1 hybrid seed was not found in any source
  consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

Energy and protein content of the fresh root (roughly 750 kcal and
12 g protein per kilogram) are derived from a detailed storage
manual's documented composition figures (carbohydrate, fiber, protein,
and fat content), applying standard calorie conversion factors; the
resulting range brackets an older, unsourced legacy figure closely
enough to retain it as the working value.

Water-use stage lengths and a total seasonal crop-water-use figure
are estimated from the crop's overall documented maturity range, since
no official stage-length breakdown exists for this crop's
international reference crop-coefficient curve; a days-without-water
tolerance of roughly 8 days is derived from standard soil-water-
holding assumptions combined with this crop's documented rooting depth
and moisture-depletion allowance.

A seed-to-first-harvest timeline of roughly 400-450 days is derived by
combining this crop's documented root-growing-season length with its
biennial habit, which requires overwintering before flowering and
seed-setting in a second growing season.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 42 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing a representative commercial yield figure by an estimated
growing-season length.

Genome size (roughly 1.74 picograms, 1C) is derived from a genomic-
library metadata record's stated genome-size estimate for a specific
named cultivar, using a standard genome-size conversion factor; this
figure comes from library-preparation metadata rather than a
completed sequenced genome assembly.
