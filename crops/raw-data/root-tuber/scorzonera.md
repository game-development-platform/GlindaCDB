# Scorzonera / Black Salsify (*Scorzonera hispanica*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-24

## Naming and scope

*Scorzonera hispanica* L., daisy family (Asteraceae): black salsify,
Spanish salsify, viper's grass, or serpent root. Some modern botanical
references file this species under a newer genus name following a
taxonomic split of the older genus, though trade, extension, and
research literature overwhelmingly still use the older genus name
used in this entry's title. The cultivated root belongs to one named
botanical variety; a second named variety exists but is not
cultivated. The species has 14 chromosomes. This entry covers one
species with one main product, distinguished from the related salsify
(its own separate entry in this project) by genus, root color, a
distinctive latex, perennial habit, bolting behavior, and an entire
industrial canning chain — differences substantial enough that no
scoping or splitting question was needed. This entry is grounded
against this project's own prior research into salsify, its closest
relative crop in the same plant tribe, sharing similar uses and even
some of the same agricultural research programs.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a detailed Dutch production manual turns a previously
thin research base into one of this project's best-documented root
crops.** A comprehensive national cultivation manual, drawing on
Belgian and Dutch field trials from the 1970s and 1980s, supplies
detailed trial data on sowing date, planting density, row spacing,
tillage method, irrigation, fertilizer, cultivar comparison, seed
grading, harvest timing, storage color quality, and labor hours — all
areas where prior research on this crop had little to no sourced data
[3].

**Finding 2 — this is fundamentally an industrial crop: most of the
harvest is canned, and the canning process itself sets the harvest
date.** Historical European production data document a substantial
cultivation area concentrated in a small number of northwestern
European countries, with the great majority of one major producing
country's crop exported, mostly to a neighboring country's canning
industry [3]. Today the crop is grown mainly in Belgium, France, and
the Netherlands, then Germany [2]. The entire harvest timing is driven
by a specific processing constraint: roots containing more than
roughly 7% inulin by fresh weight crystallize when sterilized during
canning, turning into an unappealing, shapeless, tasteless mass with
cloudy liquid [3]. Inulin content falls steadily along an S-shaped
curve from early October to early December, usually dropping below
the 7% processing threshold by mid-November and always by early
December, regardless of whether roots remain in the field or sit in
cold storage, with inulin breaking down faster at low temperature [3].
Canned roots must also be white in color; color quality holds well
from September through December, then declines after February, with
color deterioration happening faster in simple outdoor clamp storage
than in proper mechanical cold storage [3]. The overall recommendation
from the source manual is to harvest for best quality and yield from
mid-November to mid-February [3]. At the processing factory, roots are
cut to a standard length and peeled using steam or a chemical
treatment, so straight, round, smooth roots within a specific diameter
range are most suitable for processing [3].

**Finding 3 — sowing date is the single biggest lever on yield, and
late April is consistently best.** Detailed Belgian field trials
comparing sowing dates from mid-April through mid-June found end-of-
April sowing consistently gave both the highest total yield and the
lowest rate of premature flowering (bolting), while later sowings in
May and June produced steadily declining yields [3]. Yield continues
rising from an early-autumn harvest through a late-December harvest,
holds roughly steady through winter, and gains only a little more
with a spring harvest, while the root's dry-matter content falls
substantially from autumn to spring [3]. One trial using an early-
ripening cultivar found maximum yield was already reached by early
October [3]. Later sowings are documented getting more powdery mildew
disease, while earlier sowings get more white rust disease; sowing too
early, especially combined with an early cold-water irrigation, is
documented triggering premature first-year flowering [2][3].

**Finding 4 — documented yield figures span a wide range across
national averages, research trials, and grower guidance.** Historical
national average yields for several major European producing
countries in the 1980s ranged roughly 14-28 t/ha depending on country
and year [3]. A detailed Dutch grower guide describes 20-25 t/ha as
normal under good conditions and 25-30 t/ha as "very good," with a
regional yield range as wide as 7.5-28 t/ha depending on weed control
and irrigation [3]. Density trials found yield leveling off at roughly
33 t/ha above a certain planting density, with the single best-
documented plots reaching over 40 t/ha [3]. Separate Polish field
trials using the same commercial cultivar found total yields around
19.5-20.1 t/ha, with a marketable fraction of roughly 63% under
Polish grading standards, and found that both a preceding cover crop
and ridge planting (rather than flat planting) measurably raised yield
[4][9].

**Finding 5 — irrigation produces a clearly measured yield response,
using crop-coefficient figures borrowed from other crops.** Because no
dedicated water-use data exists for this crop specifically, the
detailed Dutch manual borrows crop-coefficient figures from carrot and
witloof chicory for irrigation planning [3]. Recommended irrigation
timing runs from early July to early September, triggered when a
specific soil-moisture-depletion threshold is reached, applying no
more than roughly 25-30 mm per irrigation event [3]. One specific
field trial found a clear, nearly linear yield response to irrigation
amount, with total natural rainfall of 173 mm producing substantially
lower yield than the same season with significant added irrigation —
working out to a documented yield gain of roughly 47 kg of root per
additional millimeter of irrigation water applied; separate German
trials found irrigation raising marketable yield by roughly 11% and
reducing crude fiber content [3]. Irrigating too early in the season
with cold water is documented raising the rate of premature bolting
[3]. Home-garden guidance separately emphasizes not letting the soil
dry out during germination [7].

**Finding 6 — soil, tillage, and rotation requirements are specific.**
This crop needs light sand or very light loam soil with a moderate
organic-matter content, a dark topsoil at least 50 cm deep, and
groundwater below the effective root zone; on clay soil, roots come
out short, rough, and prone to breaking, while loamy sand produces
better roots [3]. Deep rotary tillage gave the best yield in trials,
and low ground-pressure tires are recommended to avoid soil
compaction [3]. Recommended crop rotation allows this crop only once
every six years, ideally following cereals or maize, specifically
avoiding sugar beet (which shares a soil-borne fungal disease strain)
and potato (which shares a damaging nematode species); fresh or green
manure is documented causing forked roots, and any volunteer plants
from a previous crop must be controlled [3]. Separate Polish trials
instead found that cover crops plowed into the soil in spring raised
yield, an apparent conflict with the Dutch forking warning that may
be explained by differences in timing between the two practices [4].

**Finding 7 — fertilizer needs are low nitrogen, high potassium, and
specifically chloride-free.** Documented nitrogen application runs
roughly 75-100 kg N/ha, generally split into multiple applications,
with no nitrogen applied after mid-summer to avoid a quality penalty
[3]. Phosphorus removal runs roughly 60-70 kg P2O5/ha per year, a
notably high phosphorus demand [3]. Potassium uptake runs roughly
100-200 kg K2O/ha; growers are specifically advised to use chloride-
free potash fertilizer, because chloride is documented spoiling canned
product color [3]. Magnesium uptake runs a modest amount, and root
calcium content is documented improving firmness after canning [3].

**Finding 8 — weed control takes up more than half of total labor.**
A detailed 1990-91 Dutch cost budget for a mechanized contract crop
totals roughly 60 hours per hectare using the farm's own equipment,
not counting contracted sowing, harvesting, or irrigation labor; more
than half of that total is weed control, split between hand-weeding
and mechanical hoeing [3]. The high weeding burden is explained by
this crop's notably slow canopy closure combined with the difficulty
of selectively controlling related composite-family weeds [3]. Older,
fully manual harvest methods involved plowing soil away from the root
row and hand-pulling, or using a specialized horse- or tractor-drawn
implement requiring a crew of several workers to lift a single
hectare over many hours; modern mechanized harvesters run by a single
operator can lift a substantial fraction of a hectare per day [3].

**Finding 9 — unlike salsify, bolted roots of this crop remain edible
rather than becoming woody.** Premature flowering (bolting) is
triggered by a cold spell followed by long days, and can happen in the
first year after unusually early sowing; Belgian cultivar trials
recorded bolting rates under 3% across tested cultivars [3]. Critically,
bolted roots of this crop "stay soft" and remain edible, in clear
contrast to the related salsify, where bolted roots go woody and
inedible [3]. This crop is fundamentally a perennial grown
commercially as an annual; roots left in the ground are documented
keeping their culinary quality into a second year, and a plant can
flower successfully for two to three consecutive years, a trait
breeders exploit for certain cross-breeding techniques [2][3][6].
Pollination is protandrous (male parts mature before female parts
within the same flower), with cross-pollination between flowers of
the same flower head being the normal pattern, though some self-
pollination also occurs; insects visit for nectar [3].

**Finding 10 — the seed is large, short-lived, and emerges slowly and
unevenly.** Seed achenes are documented at roughly 12-17 mm long, with
a typical thousand-seed weight of roughly 12-14 g [3]. Laboratory
germination tests on fresh seed lots run 82-92%, but field emergence
is documented considerably lower, at only 44-51%, with field emergence
itself taking anywhere from 4-5 days under ideal conditions to 18-21
days under typical conditions [3]. Documented seed storage life
conflicts across sources, ranging from a recommendation to simply buy
fresh seed each year, to a guarantee of one to two years, to a claim of
at least two years under proper storage [2][3][16]. Documented seeding
rates cluster around 12-18 kg/ha depending on source and target
planting density [2][3][4][9][10][11]. A dedicated seed-crop
management protocol from one national genetic-resources collection
describes roots lifted in autumn, stored cold, and replanted the
following spring in isolated plots, with first seed harvest the
following summer [8]. No seed yield per hectare was found for this
crop in any source consulted.

**Finding 11 — composition is inulin-rich, with a substantial,
unreconciled conflict in reported inulin values across sources.** Four
different sources report inulin content for this crop ranging from
roughly 16.5% to over 74% depending on whether the figure is expressed
as a percentage of fresh weight or of dry weight, and depending on
harvest date, since inulin content falls steadily through the storage
season described in Finding 2; this entry records all four figures
without choosing among them, since sampling date and measurement
method likely explain much of the apparent conflict [4][7][9][13].
Documented nutrition figures per 100 g raw root also conflict somewhat
between a Scandinavian food-composition database and the Dutch
cultivation manual's own figures, particularly for protein content,
where the two source types differ by roughly a factor of two-and-a-
half [3][12]. This crop is documented having among the highest
carbohydrate content of any vegetable, second only to sweet corn, by
one source's reckoning [3]. Nitrate content is documented as low,
falling further from early autumn to spring [3].

**Finding 12 — a substantial, well-documented list of pests and
diseases.** A root-knot nematode species is documented as the biggest
threat on sandy soils, forking the taproot above a specific soil
population density threshold; a non-host rotation crop reduces the
nematode population by roughly 80% [3]. A second nematode species
causes rough, warty root skin and is worse in warm years; a third
causes short, stubby roots [3]. Several fungal diseases are documented,
including seedling damping-off in cold spring weather (controlled with
a seed-dressing treatment), a leaf-spot disease, powdery mildew
(affecting nearly every crop in dry, warm weather, requiring routine
fungicide spraying), a root-tip-death disease favoring high soil pH, a
disease causing plant losses at the junction of leaf and root (closely
related to a sugar-beet disease), and white rust disease favoring
cool, wet weather [3][10]. A detailed Polish study of fungi found on
diseased roots after harvest documented the relative frequency of
several specific fungal species, and found that biostimulant and
chitosan seed treatments improved seedling emergence and health
[10][11]. Insect pests include leatherjackets and wireworms (worst
following grassland), and a carrot-family root fly; bacterial soft rot
is also documented; mice and rabbits are documented eating roots left
in the field over winter, controlled in one source by a specific
electric-fence configuration [2][3].

**Finding 13 — storage secures year-round supply, even though the
roots themselves are hardy enough to need no storage for quality.**
Because the roots are winter-hardy in the ground, cold storage is not
actually needed to preserve root quality; it exists instead to secure
a continuous supply when the field is frozen, snow-covered, or
waterlogged and cannot be harvested [3]. Documented mechanical cold
storage runs at near-freezing temperature with minimal water loss,
using either humidified cooling or a controlled, modest temperature
difference during dry cooling, with storage containers wetted and
wrapped in perforated plastic [3]. A separate industry storage-manual
source documents the related black-salsify-adjacent crop keeping up to
six months under a specific controlled low-oxygen, elevated-carbon-
dioxide atmosphere at a slightly sub-freezing temperature [5]. A
successfully tested outdoor ventilated-clamp storage method is
documented in detail, including specific dimensions, insulating straw
rate, and plastic-sheet covering, running at a temperature only
slightly above outside air and costing a documented modest amount per
kilogram for materials and labor [3].

**Finding 14 — uses beyond the simple boiled root.** The root is
typically boiled for a period and then peeled, or peeled first and
held in acidulated water to prevent browning; its sticky natural latex
turns black on exposure to air [2][16]. Young roots are sometimes
eaten raw in salads, though one extension source specifically notes
that boiling is needed to remove a bitter taste [2][16]. Other edible
parts include the chewed latex (used historically as a kind of gum),
young leaves eaten raw in salads, young spring shoots, raw flower
buds, flowering shoots prepared like asparagus, and flower petals used
in salads [2][6][7]. The roasted root is documented used as a coffee
substitute [6]. A crystallized candy made from this root is a
traditional specialty of a specific Portuguese town, recognized by an
international food-heritage catalog [2]. The plant has a folk history
as a traditional antidote to snakebite (the source of some of its
common names in several languages) and as a general tonic [2][7].
Historically, the crop was documented at a Middle Eastern market in
the late 16th century, cultivated in Italy and France by 1660 and in
England by 1683, with large-scale Belgian cultivation following later;
Dutch cultivation dates to around 1600, where it eventually displaced
the related salsify as the dominant crop of this type [2][3]. The
species is native to southern Europe and the Near East; wild
populations are documented as rare and officially protected in at
least one European country [2][11].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season perennial taproot crop grown
  commercially as an annual; sown in late April, with harvest running
  from autumn through the following spring (Finding 3, 9).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single planting supports up to three distinct
  harvest windows — an early fresh harvest in early autumn, a main
  winter harvest (fresh or for canning) from mid-November to mid-
  February, and a spring harvest of overwintered roots — with the
  choice between them driven mainly by market and processing
  requirements rather than biology (Finding 2; harvest-stage detail
  below).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate, temperate, and Mediterranean zones,
  consistent with a native range in southern Europe and the Near East.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible by some accounts (young roots in
  salads), though at least one extension source recommends boiling to
  remove a bitter taste.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  insect-visited and protandrous, with cross-pollination between
  flowers of the same head being the usual pattern (Finding 9).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial and trial yields span
  roughly 7,500-43,000 kg/ha depending on region, cultivar, and
  practice (Finding 4; see also Estimates below). The root's
  carbohydrate reserve is stored mainly as inulin, at a documented but
  widely varying percentage depending on harvest date (Finding 11).
- **Water use:** Strongly responsive to irrigation during a specific
  mid-summer window, using crop-coefficient figures borrowed from
  other crops since none exist for this crop specifically (Finding 5).
- **Establishment:** Precision direct-seeded in deeply tilled, stone-
  free sandy soil (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** Weed control is documented taking up more than half of
  total labor on a mechanized crop, a notably high share driven by
  slow canopy closure (Finding 8).
- **Soil:** Light sand or very light loam with moderate organic
  matter and deep topsoil; performs poorly on clay (Finding 6).
- **Stand life:** Perennial in natural life cycle, grown commercially
  as an annual, though roots can be left a second year without losing
  culinary quality.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Two documented root-knot nematode species,
  several fungal diseases (including powdery mildew and white rust),
  and rodent damage to roots left in the field over winter are
  documented concerns (Finding 12; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Several named Belgian, Dutch, and German open-
  pollinated cultivars spanning high-inulin/low-dry-matter processing
  types, early-ripening types, and disease-resistant types (cultivar
  detail below).
- **Harvest:** A long harvest window from early autumn through the
  following spring, with the main commercial harvest timed around a
  specific inulin-content threshold required for canning (Finding 2).
- **Storage:** Roots are winter-hardy in the ground and do not
  strictly require storage for quality, but are commonly stored cold
  or in an outdoor ventilated clamp to secure continuous supply
  (Finding 13).

Establishment detail: precision-drilled roughly 1-2.5 cm deep
depending on soil moisture, in rows spaced either closely for flat-bed
production or more widely for ridge production; deep rotary tillage
before sowing gives the best root quality; soil is kept moist through
germination.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a widely used Belgian-
Dutch industrial-processing type serving as the standard comparison
cultivar in both Belgian and Polish trials; a Dutch industrial type
documented as the best performer in trials, with the fewest hollow-
root defects, earliest ripening, and highest inulin content; a
Belgian type documented with the lowest inulin content, making it
suited to early-season canning; an older German open-pollinated
standard cultivar; two further Belgian high-inulin, low-dry-matter
types; a Belgian type bred specifically for powdery mildew resistance;
and two German short-rooted types with a distinctively thick neck.

Harvest-stage detail: an early stage (September-October, fresh market
only, crystallizes if canned due to high inulin); a winter stage (mid-
November to mid-February, the main commercial harvest, suitable for
both fresh sale and canning once inulin content has fallen below the
processing threshold); and a spring stage (March-April, slightly
higher total yield but notably lower dry-matter content).

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this root's inulin content (ranging widely by
harvest date, as described in Finding 11) is a fructan fiber that can
cause dose-dependent digestive discomfort (flatulence); deferred until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

The root's natural latex is a cosmetic nuisance, staining hands and
turning black on exposure to air, but is not documented as a health
hazard. No toxicity to people or animals is recorded in any source
consulted, and nitrate content is documented as low. Two documented
root-knot nematode species, several fungal diseases (seedling damping-
off, a leaf-spot disease, powdery mildew, a root-tip-death disease,
a disease shared with sugar beet, and white rust), bacterial soft rot,
and rodent damage to roots left in the field over winter are
documented pest and disease concerns.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → late-April precision sowing on deeply tilled
  sandy soil → winter roots (mid-November to mid-February) → topped,
  sorted, and graded → sold fresh or sent to canning. Alternatively:
  the same crop lifted early (September-October) for fresh market
  only, or left in the field and dug the following spring. Graded
  roots → cold storage, or an outdoor ventilated clamp. Graded roots →
  roasted as a coffee substitute, or candied in a traditional regional
  confection. Roots lifted in autumn → stored cold → replanted in
  spring → seed heads → threshed → cleaned seed. Young leaves, spring
  shoots, flower buds, and petals → salads.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned, jarred, and
  frozen product in industrial detail, beyond the canning constraints
  recorded in Finding 2; the root's latex as chewing gum; harvest
  machinery in mechanical detail; soil fumigation and wind-erosion
  binder products.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, consistent
with notes this project has recorded for other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Inulin content is reported wildly differently across four sources
  consulted (roughly 16.5% to over 74%, depending on whether measured
  as a fraction of fresh or dry weight and on harvest date); not
  reconciled into a single figure here.
- Protein content conflicts sharply between a Scandinavian food-
  composition database and the Dutch cultivation manual's own figures,
  by roughly a factor of two-and-a-half.
- Documented edible-portion percentage (after trimming/peeling)
  conflicts moderately between sources.
- Documented seed storage life conflicts across three sources, ranging
  from "buy fresh seed each year" to "guaranteed one to two years" to
  "at least two years under proper storage."
- Documented soil pH guidance varies somewhat across sources.
- Whether this crop is safely raw-edible is disputed: one source
  describes young roots eaten raw in salads, while another
  specifically recommends boiling to remove bitterness.
- A seed-crop management source describes this as a strictly biennial
  crop for seed purposes, in some tension with other sources
  describing it as a true multi-year perennial capable of flowering
  well in two to three successive years.
- A documented conflict exists on green-manure practice: the Dutch
  manual warns that fresh or green manure forks roots, while separate
  Polish trials found that a cover crop plowed in during spring
  actually raised yield — possibly explained by a difference in
  timing between the two practices, not resolved here.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour figures outside the one sourced
  Dutch budget tier; the seed-viability curve by year; one specific
  water-use crop-coefficient figure for an autumn period, estimated
  rather than sourced; and nutrient-removal figures, given the protein
  conflict noted above.
- No seed yield per hectare was found for this crop in any source
  consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 17,000 kg/ha, good 25,000 kg/ha, exceptional
35,000 kg/ha, poor 7,500 kg/ha) are derived from the middle of a
documented historical multi-country European yield range, weighted
toward the two countries that historically held most of the
cultivated area, cross-checked against a general encyclopedia source's
own documented range.

A fresh-to-dry weight ratio of roughly 4.1:1 is derived from one
trial's documented dry-matter percentage at autumn harvest, though
this ratio shifts considerably through the storage season as dry
matter content falls (documented dropping to a notably higher ratio
by spring).

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from the Dutch cultivation
manual's documented crop-coefficient figures — themselves borrowed
from carrot and witloof chicory, since no dedicated figures exist for
this crop — combined with an estimated stage-length breakdown and an
estimated autumn crop-coefficient value for the period when leaves die
back and inulin content falls. A days-without-water tolerance of
roughly 5 days (varying by soil type) is derived from the manual's own
documented soil-moisture-holding figures and irrigation-trigger
threshold.

Seed viability and germination-rate figures are estimated by combining
the documented range of seed-life claims across sources with the
documented fresh-seed laboratory germination rate.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh root) are derived from documented protein and mineral-
content figures, cross-checked against the cultivation manual's own
documented whole-crop nutrient-uptake figures; the nitrogen figure in
particular is sensitive to the unresolved protein-content conflict
noted above.

Storage life (roughly 90 days cool and dry) is derived from the
overlap between documented Dutch color-retention data (reliable
quality for roughly three months in mechanical cold storage) and a
separate source's documented six-month figure under a specialized
controlled atmosphere, which is treated as outside the ordinary
storage case.

Labor-hour tiers are derived from the one sourced, detailed 1990-91
Dutch mechanized-crop labor budget for an intermediate production
tier, with less-mechanized tiers estimated upward — weeding hours
raised further, since the sourced budget shows weed control is over
half of total labor even under full mechanization, and harvest hours
raised substantially for the least-mechanized tier to reflect fully
manual lifting of a deep, fragile root. A harvest window of roughly 90
days is derived from the manual's own recommended mid-November-to-
mid-February harvest period.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 410-450 days from sowing to seed
harvest is derived from a documented late-April sowing date, an
autumn root-lifting and cold-storage period, a spring replanting date,
and a documented early-summer seed-harvest window the following year.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 10 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing a representative total yield figure by a representative full
growing-season length.

Growing-season length (150-220 days) is derived from a late-April
sowing date through an early-autumn minimum harvest and a mid-
November-to-early-December main harvest; a spring-dug harvest option
is excluded from this range since the crop is effectively dormant
over the intervening winter.

Soil pH guidance (optimum roughly 6.0, minimum 5.3, maximum 7.0) is
estimated from the lower bound documented for naturally sandy Dutch
growing soils, a middle value from two further sources, and an upper
bound from a general plant-reference database's description of the
species tolerating mildly alkaline soil.

A seeding rate of roughly 11-16 kg/ha is derived from a documented
seed mass combined with a target viable-seed planting density and an
assumed germination rate, cross-checked against directly documented
seeding rates from multiple sources.
