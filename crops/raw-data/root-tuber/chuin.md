# Chuin (*Pachyrhizus tuberosus*, Chuin cultivar group)

**Category:** Root/Tuber · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/root-tuber/chuin.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-26

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

**Data quality note:** this entry is moderate. One full grower's manual
from a Peruvian Amazon research institute covers site selection,
calendar, spacing, deflowering, harvest, storage, farming systems, and
a detailed cost budget per hectare [1]. Composition data comes from a
thesis review, a West African trial of unstated cultivar-group
identity, and a dedicated starch study. There are no production
statistics, and the grower's manual's own dry-matter figures conflict
with every other source found.

## Naming and scope

The Chuin cultivar group of *Pachyrhizus tuberosus*, family Fabaceae,
2n = 22 [2][12]. This is a floodplain crop of the Peruvian Amazon whose
dry, starchy root is cooked like cassava — unlike the raw-eaten Ashipa
and jicama types within the broader yam-bean species group [1][2][3].
Several local varieties are named by color and use, including a
cooking type and a distinct raw-eaten type. The crop is currently
limited to a small number of communities along specific Amazonian
rivers and is reported as declining, due to cultural shift away from
traditional foods, exclusion from formal markets, and seed scarcity —
earlier and longer floods in recent years are also reported as
disrupting the plant's ability to complete its seed cycle [1].

This crop shares general yam-bean biology — the seed rotenone hazard
and broader *Pachyrhizus* biology — with the Ashipa type and jicama,
two related crops this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a flood-recession staple that substitutes for cassava.**
The crop is grown on Amazonian floodplain land at modest elevation,
where seasonal white-water river flooding (lasting roughly 4-6 months
depending on source) deposits nutrient-rich sediment; it is sown on
several distinct flood-recession land types as the water falls, and
also on higher, non-flooded land with more intensive preparation [1][2].
Documented yields reach up to roughly 70 t/ha of roots, with the crop
specifically noted as having the highest dry matter and protein content
of any type within the broader yam-bean species group, and functioning
as a real alternative staple to cassava and plantain in its growing
area [1]. A separate starch-research source corroborates riverine
yields of around 70 t/ha over a 4-6 month cycle [6].

**Finding 2 — a climbing vine producing one large root per plant.**
The plant is a climbing vine reaching several meters, with distinctive
trifoliate leaves and flowers in blue, violet, or white [1]. Unlike the
multituberous Ashipa type, this cultivar group typically produces just
one root per plant, which can grow quite large (up to about 30 cm long
and 8-25 cm across), in a globose, elongated, or irregular shape, with
flesh color varying by variety (white, yellow, or white with purple)
[1]. The group is characterized overall by a climbing growth habit,
shallowly three-lobed leaflets, a single root, and notably high dry
matter compared to its relatives [4].

**Finding 3 — the growing calendar is set by the river, not the
season.** On floodplain land, vegetation is deliberately cut before the
flood rises so it traps sediment as the water recedes; sowing happens
as the water falls (typically late spring in the local calendar), often
intercropped with cowpea, with roots needing to be lifted before the
next flood arrives [1]. On higher, non-flooded land, slash-and-burn
clearing and ridged or mounded beds are used instead, with a different
sowing window; sowing too late on this higher land risks the crop
hitting a seasonal dry period, while sowing too early risks seed and
root rot from a different seasonal rainy period — the crop specifically
does not tolerate excess soil moisture [1]. A documented full
production cycle for one local community runs from seed sowing, through
root harvest a few months later, to replanting of a specific "cotito"
seed-propagation structure (described in Finding 5), with final seed
harvest several months after that [1]. Sowing is done with a long
dibble stick, placing several seeds per hole at a shallow depth, with
specific spacing recommendations varying by whether the crop is grown
as a monocrop, intercropped, or as a dedicated seed crop [1].

**Finding 4 — deflowering roughly doubles the root yield.** Without
deflowering, root yield falls by about half [1]. Standard practice is
three deflowering passes timed relative to the onset of flowering
(roughly 70-75 days after sowing, then two further passes at specific
intervals after that), done by hand or with scissors; some plants are
deliberately left undeflowered specifically to produce seed [1]. Plants
grown specifically for seed are staked using a simple crossed-stake
support structure. Weeding frequency varies by land type — none needed
on the most frequently flooded land, a modest schedule on intermediate
flood-recession land, and a more intensive schedule on higher,
non-flooded land [1]. The crop requires no nitrogen fertilizer, since
it nodulates with nitrogen-fixing soil bacteria and is specifically
valued as a cover crop, soil improver, and weed suppressor capable of
growing on marginal soils [1].

**Finding 5 — harvest and storage practices are well documented.**
Roots reach full maturity around 6 months after sowing but can be
harvested from about 4 months; maturity signs include cracking, rising
soil above the root and leaf drying [1]. Mature roots can be left in
the ground for about a month after reaching maturity without spoiling.
Once lifted, roots keep for about a month in a cool, dark place, during
which their sugar content actually increases, though leaving soil on
the root slows drying and extends usable life slightly; roots spoil
after about a month regardless [1]. Seed is harvested once pods are
fully dry, with a locally observed traditional practice of timing pod
harvest to a specific lunar phase as protection against a seed-eating
beetle pest; seed is then sun-dried briefly over several days and
stored either in airtight dark glass with camphor and ash (keeping up
to 2 years, best used within 1) or using a traditional smoke-curing
method over a hearth, which repels insects and keeps seed viable for
about 6 months [1]. A distinctive propagation structure called a
"cotito" — the root's top portion together with its stem collar — can
be replanted after the root harvest; this structure produces more seed
but no further root, effectively extending a single planting's useful
life by several more months purely for seed production [1][4].

**Finding 6 — composition is dry, starchy, and protein-rich, though
one key source conflicts sharply with every other.** Multiple
independent sources agree this cultivar group has notably high dry
matter for a yam bean, in the roughly 26-32% range, suitable for
flour-making and generally processed like cassava [3][4][5]. However,
the grower's manual itself prints dry-matter figures for two named
varieties that are far too low to be consistent with this
characterization or with every other source — these figures may
actually represent a different measure (such as protein content)
rather than dry matter, and are not adopted here [1]. Protein content
is reported around 10% of dry matter, higher than cassava, sweet
potato, or potato [1][3]. Carbohydrate is mainly starch (amylopectin),
with one West African trial measuring total starch around 50-55% of
dry matter [1][3]. A dedicated starch-characterization study found easy
extraction with rapid sedimentation, a moderate extraction yield, with
the highest yield specifically in a purple-fleshed raw-eaten variety,
along with specific amylose content, gelatinization temperature, and
low starch retrogradation [6].

**Finding 7 — uses span boiled roots, a fermented beverage, toasted
meal, chips, flour, and starch.** Roots are boiled directly; boiled and
mashed into a traditional fermented beverage; grated and toasted into a
cassava-style toasted meal product; fried into chips; or processed into
flour and starch used in traditional sweets and porridges [1]. Leaves
are used as forage and in traditional skin-ailment poultices [1]. Seeds
have documented insecticidal use (their rotenoid content controls
several insect pests) and are also used traditionally as a human
purgative and, more concerningly, as drops of crushed-seed juice given
to children for certain respiratory/viral illnesses — a traditional
practice involving a genuinely toxic plant part given directly to
children. Seed oil, once rotenoids and saponins are removed, is
reported to be of good quality, comparable to peanut or cottonseed oil
[1].

**Finding 8 — documented intercropping systems and a detailed cost
budget.** The crop is commonly intercropped with cowpea, rice, cassava,
or maize, using specific row arrangements and relative timing (the
yam-bean crop sown once the companion crop reaches a certain height);
it is also grown in small home-garden patches in full sun [1]. A
detailed per-hectare cost budget from the grower's manual documents
labor requirements across sowing, weeding, deflowering, plant
protection, and harvest, along with seed quantity, pesticide input,
and packaging material [1]. No diseases are recorded for this crop; a
beetle pest affects young leaves (controlled with traditional plant-
based insecticide preparations) and nematodes occur occasionally [1]. A
distinctive wilting disorder occurs under high heat combined with low
rainfall, or following a sudden cold spell followed by heat; the
documented remedy is stripping a quarter of the oldest leaves from
affected plants [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An annual vine grown for its single large
  storage root; sowing timing is tied to river flood-recession on
  floodplain land, or to the regional dry/wet season calendar on
  higher land (Finding 1, 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of one root per plant, from 4-6
  months after sowing; a separate seed-crop path exists via either
  staked, undeflowered plants or a distinctive replanted root-top
  structure ("cotito") that produces seed but no further root (Finding
  4, 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical rainforest and floodplain (várzea) zones,
  specifically tied to a seasonal flood-recession cycle on the lowest-
  lying land types (Finding 1, 3).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible for at least one named variety; the more
  common cooking-type variety is not eaten raw; seeds are toxic and
  not food (Finding 6, 7).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production, since plants are
  deliberately deflowered; seed-crop plants are mainly self-pollinating
  (Finding 4).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable — documented yields range
  from roughly 5,000 kg/ha (undeflowered or flood-damaged crops) up to
  a documented maximum around 70,000 kg/ha, with deflowering
  specifically roughly doubling yield compared to undeflowered plants
  (Finding 1, 4).
- **Water use:** Grows primarily on residual flood moisture on
  floodplain land, or seasonal rainfall on higher land; does not
  tolerate excess soil moisture, and a flood reaching the field before
  harvest can destroy the crop entirely (Finding 1, 3).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded by dibble stick at shallow depth,
  with spacing varying by whether the crop is a monocrop, intercrop, or
  dedicated seed crop; the distinctive "cotito" propagation structure
  is planted separately, after a root harvest, purely for seed
  production (Finding 3, 5).
- **Labor:** A detailed cost budget documents labor across sowing,
  weeding, deflowering (a significant, repeated hand-labor task), plant
  protection, and harvest; harvest labor is measured per unit of plants
  processed per workday (Finding 8; see also Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Alluvial sandy-silt soils at specific floodplain positions
  are typical; higher land requires more intensive soil preparation
  (ridging/mounding plus manure); the crop requires no nitrogen
  fertilizer due to natural nitrogen fixation and tolerates marginal
  soils generally (Finding 3, 4).
- **Stand life:** Grown as a single-season annual for root production;
  extended to roughly 9 months total when the seed-only "cotito"
  replanting phase is used after the root harvest (Finding 5).
- **Disease/pest profile:** No diseases are recorded; a beetle pest
  affects young leaves, nematodes occur occasionally, and a specific
  stored-seed beetle pest is a significant seed-storage concern
  (Finding 5, 8).
- **Cultivars:** Several named local varieties exist, distinguished by
  root color, whether eaten raw or cooked, and flesh color; no named
  commercial cultivars exist in trade (Finding 6; cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** Single-stage root harvest at 4-6 months, done by hand
  with a machete or dibble stick, one root per plant, with harvest
  labor measured per standard workday in the documented cost budget
  (Finding 5, 8).
- **Storage:** Mature roots can wait roughly a month in the ground
  after reaching maturity, and keep roughly another month once lifted
  if stored cool and dark (during which sugar content actually
  increases); seed, by contrast, can be stored for up to 2 years using
  an airtight camphor-and-ash method, or about 6 months using a
  traditional smoke-curing method (Finding 5).

## Hazards

A beetle pest affects young leaves (controlled with traditional plant-
based insecticide preparations), nematodes occur occasionally, and a
specific stored-seed beetle pest is a significant seed-storage concern;
no diseases are recorded for this crop (Finding 5, 8). A distinctive
heat-and-drought-triggered wilting disorder is remedied by stripping a
quarter of the plant's oldest leaves (Finding 8).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed, pods, and foliage of this crop contain
rotenone and related rotenoid compounds, along with a related toxic
compound; only the root is eaten. Despite this known toxicity, the
seed has documented traditional uses as a human purgative and,
concerningly, as drops of crushed-seed juice given directly to
children for certain illnesses. This is a genuine hazard to humans,
livestock, and fish if seed or seed waste is ingested or enters feed or
water, deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed sown by flood-recession, upland, or intercrop method
  → deflowered → roots, a replantable "cotito" structure, and tops
  harvested. Alternatively, staked seed-crop plants → dry pods →
  threshed or smoke-cured → seed; or a replanted cotito → dry pods only
  (no root). Harvested roots are boiled directly, boiled and mashed
  into a fermented beverage, grated and toasted into a cassava-style
  meal, sliced and fried into chips, or processed (grated, washed,
  settled) into starch used for traditional sweets and porridges. Seed
  is toasted and ground into an insecticide or purgative, or (proposed)
  pressed for oil. Tops are used as forage and ground cover.
- Not covered by this research: canned, pickled, or frozen product (no
  sources found); traditional leaf poultices in detail; the specific
  plant-based insecticide preparations used against pests; the leaf-
  stripping heat-stress remedy as a formal practice beyond what is
  noted above.

## Open research questions

- The grower's manual's own printed dry-matter figures for two named
  varieties are far too low to be consistent either with the same
  manual's general characterization of this crop or with every other
  composition source found — these figures may represent a different
  measurement (such as protein content) rather than dry matter, but
  this was not confirmed, and the figures are not adopted in this
  entry.
- A documented nitrogen-fixation figure for a related crop within the
  same manual appears to use the wrong units (almost certainly meant
  as a much smaller figure than printed), but this was not independently
  confirmed.
- A related crop within the same broader species group is documented
  as unable to form roots under warm night temperatures around 30°C
  combined with daytime heat, yet this crop is grown in an area with a
  similar mean temperature — whether this crop is simply more heat-
  tolerant, or whether field night temperatures are cooler than the
  regional mean suggests, is not resolved.
- The seasonal flood duration for this crop's growing area is given
  slightly differently across sources (roughly 4-5 months in one source
  vs. 4-6 months in another).
- No reliable yield statistics exist for this crop at any larger scale
  than the documented grower's manual and research-trial figures; the
  world-average, good, and poor-yield estimates used here are
  extrapolated from a fairly narrow evidence base (see Estimates
  below).
- Seed yield per hectare specifically was not documented in the primary
  grower's manual and is estimated here from a different crop's trial
  data and an assumed seed-crop planting density.
- This crop's distinctive "cotito" seed-propagation phase (replanting a
  root top after harvest to get seed with no further root) is a
  genuinely unusual propagation pattern not seen in most other root
  crops this project has researched, and is not quantified beyond the
  qualitative description available.
- A reported decline in cultivation, attributed partly to earlier and
  longer river floods disrupting the plant's ability to complete its
  seed cycle, is a documented concern but not quantified.
- Labor-hour estimates at mechanization levels above the documented,
  entirely hand-labor-based grower's-manual budget are built by
  analogy to a related crop rather than from any direct source.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 15,000 kg/ha is estimated, with good yield at
30,000 kg/ha and poor yield at 5,000 kg/ha: a documented maximum figure
of roughly 70,000 kg/ha implies an unusually large individual root size
at standard planting density; a more typical root size at the same
density implies a world-average figure closer to 15,000 kg/ha, broadly
consistent with a West African trial's pruned-yield mean for a related
crop within the same species. Plausible range: 10,000-25,000 kg/ha. Farm
yield records from the crop's actual growing region would replace this
estimate.

Caloric and protein content are derived from composition figures: dry
matter around 30% (consistent with the better-supported sources, not
the conflicting grower's-manual figures noted above), with starch and
sugar together making up roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of dry
matter, giving a derived energy density of roughly 1,000 kcal/kg
(plausible range 800-1,150 kcal/kg) and protein around 30 g/kg fresh
weight.

A dried-slice yield fraction of roughly 0.24-0.32 is derived from
documented dry-matter content, less an allowance for peel loss, with
slices dried to about 10% residual moisture.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived by analogy with jicama, a related crop
this project has researched, itself drawing on a sweet-potato-type
water-use pattern; stage lengths are estimated from the documented
6-month maturity timeline and the roughly 70-75 day onset of flowering,
giving a seasonal water-use index of about 175 (days-equivalent).

A drought-tolerance rating of "medium," a buffer of about 10 days
before yield penalty begins, and a corresponding penalty rate are
estimated: the crop grows primarily on residual flood moisture, but
documented difficulty with late upland sowing meeting a seasonal dry
period suggests a real, if moderate, drought sensitivity once that
stored moisture is exhausted.

Seed-related figures (a seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly
65, a seed-reserve fraction of about 0.015, and an estimated seed yield
around 1,000 kg/ha) are derived from a documented seeding rate combined
with an assumed seed-crop planting density and a comparable seed-yield
figure from a related crop's West African trial, since this crop's own
grower's manual does not report seed yield directly. Days from sowing
to seed harvest (roughly 180-270 days) are derived from the documented
local full-cycle calendar.

Labor-hour estimates at mechanization levels beyond the documented,
fully hand-labor-based grower's-manual cost budget are built by analogy
to jicama, a related crop this project has researched, with the
distinctive deflowering operation held constant across all tiers since
it is inherently a hand-labor task regardless of overall mechanization
level.

Nutrient removal per tonne of fresh root (roughly 4.8 kg nitrogen, 0.3
kg phosphorus, and 1.4 kg potassium) is derived from the estimated
protein content converted to nitrogen, with phosphorus and potassium
figures drawn by analogy from related crops and scaled upward somewhat
to reflect this crop's notably drier, starchier root.

Genome size (roughly 0.54 pg, 1C) is derived from a published genome-
size measurement for the broader species (not specifically this
cultivar group), converted using the standard base-pairs-per-picogram
conversion factor.
