# Arrowhead (*Sagittaria trifolia*, cultivated)

**Researched:** 2026-10-26

**Data quality note:** this entry is moderate. Good sources exist for

Missing: a measured yield trial outside these sources, a dedicated

## Naming and scope

Cultivated *Sagittaria trifolia*, family Alismataceae [2][34]. Known as
Chinese *cígū* and Japanese *kuwai* (the specific Japanese cultivar
group is sometimes listed under a separate botanical variety name),
and in English as arrowhead, Chinese arrowhead, or swamp potato
[2][10][14]. Note on naming: *S. sagittifolia* (European arrowhead) is
a separate, mostly wild species, but its name is often used
interchangeably for the Chinese crop in some regulatory and scientific
literature, including a US noxious-weed listing and some composition/
heavy-metal studies [16][21] — readers should check which species a
given source actually means. Wapato (*S. latifolia*), a wild-gathered
North American species with its own rich historical harvest and trade
tradition, and the true European arrowhead, are both outside the scope
of this entry as distinct, largely non-cultivated species.

This crop shares general paddy-cultivation and harvest characteristics
(water-jet digging, flooded-field growing, similar production
calendars) with two other paddy-grown root crops this project has
researched — a corm crop and a rhizome crop, both grown in standing
water in similar regions — and this entry draws on that research by
analogy in places.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a cooked-only corm whose sprout is part of the
product.** Multiple sources agree the corm should be cooked, not eaten
raw, describing raw corms as poisonous, though the responsible agent is
not confidently identified [2][11][22]. One source states some raw
tubers are edible but bitter, a milder framing than "poisonous" [22].
Japanese cooks peel the corm, soak it, and parboil it in rice-washing
water or with rice bran specifically to remove bitterness [10][32]; the
bitterness itself is attributed to oxalic acid by one source and to
polyphenols by another — not reconciled [10]. In Japan the corm is
specifically a New Year festival food because its visible sprout is
a symbol of good fortune (a pun on a phrase meaning "a sprout/fortune
emerges"); harvesting, washing, and peeling are all done carefully to
keep the sprout whole, and commercial corms are graded by size and
class, with rotten or floating corms rejected from shipment [7][8][10][
14][32].

**Finding 2 — a paddy crop with a stage-by-stage water calendar.**
Chinese practice calls for about 3 cm of water at sprouting and after
transplanting (to warm the soil), 8-15 cm during vigorous growth
(sources disagree on the exact range), and back down to about 3 cm as
corms form [5][6]. Japanese practice is similar: about 5 cm after
planting and in autumn, 6-9 cm in summer, with the field never allowed
to dry out [10]. A Taiwanese method deliberately drains the field for a
period to slow leaf growth and encourage stolon (runner) formation
[5]. In wild, uncultivated stands, plants grow across a wide water-
depth range, and water deficiency has been observed to accelerate
tuber formation at the expense of top growth [16]. During an
exceptionally hot growing season in one well-documented Japanese
growing region, growers had to actively manage water volumes to
prevent the standing paddy water itself from overheating, which
delayed growth and reduced early shipments [17]. Harvest itself uses a
water jet and large volumes of water, significant enough that the
local irrigation authority specifically guarantees water rights for
the harvest period [8][17]. No standard crop-water-use reference
includes a dedicated row for this species; rice is used as the closest
analogue [27].

**Finding 3 — short days and cool, bright autumns fill the corms;
frost ends the season.** Corms sprout at around 13-15°C [6][10]. Leaf
growth is best at 20-30°C, producing a new leaf roughly every 5 days
under favorable conditions [6][10]. Stolon (runner) formation begins
after a certain number of mature leaves have formed — sources give
somewhat different leaf counts for this threshold, not reconciled
[6][10]. This is a short-day plant: long days favor leaf growth, while
short days favor corm filling, with corm formation specifically favored
by moderate temperatures (10-20°C), a large day-night temperature
swing, ample sunshine, and shallow standing water [6][8]. In one
well-documented Japanese growing region, the vegetative phase runs
roughly July-August, the reproductive phase late August to
mid-November, and corm swelling early September to mid-November, with
corm formation to harvest-readiness taking about 30 days once it
starts [5][6][7][10]. Frost kills the leaves and stops corm swelling,
directly triggering harvest; the specific climate requirements named in
a geographic-indication registration are no early frost after corm
swelling begins, plus a large day-night temperature range [8]. Dormant
plants can survive temperatures as low as about −10°C, but young
growth is badly damaged even at 0°C [11].

**Finding 4 — Japanese and Chinese growing systems differ in planting
material and season.** In the well-documented Japanese system, seed
corms are refrigerated from harvest until about a week before planting,
then soaked for several days until sprouts appear; the field is plowed
in spring and puddled roughly two weeks before planting (typically late
June to early July), with corms hand-planted a few centimeters deep at
a specific row and in-row spacing; market preference for corm size
varies by region, which directly drives planting-density choices; and
each plant typically produces around 30 corms [7][9][10]. In the
Chinese system, mother plants are deliberately selected for short,
dense stolons and a specific target corm count; seed corms are either
pre-sprouted and set whole, or only a cut top bud is used; young plants
are raised in a nursery bed before being transplanted at a specific
leaf-count stage; spring crops are planted in late spring, with a
separate autumn-crop tradition in at least one southern growing region
following an early rice crop — late planting generally gives smaller
corms [4][5][6][8]. Days to harvest vary substantially by cultivar,
from roughly 110-120 days for some southern Chinese types up to about
180 days for a specific named cultivar [4]; one broader reference gives
6-7 months to first harvest generally [2]. Field operations common to
both systems include repeated leaf stripping to control canopy density
and leaf area, periodic removal of old stolons, and a specific
root-pruning technique (cutting a ring of roots around the plant at a
set distance and depth in late autumn) intended to even out corm size
[5][6][10][12]. At least one well-documented Japanese growing region
nets entire fields against waterfowl from planting through mid-summer,
with the netting staked against storms [9][10].

**Finding 5 — yields: roughly 11-20 t/ha in China, about 11-12 t/ha in
Japan from much smaller individual corms.** Chinese cultivar yield
ranges run roughly 11-20 t/ha depending on cultivar, with up to about
22.5 t/ha cited as a high target and 11-15 t/ha as a more typical
average [4][6][11]. Japanese prefecture-level production statistics
for one recent year, divided by harvested area, work out to roughly
11-12 t/ha in the two largest-producing prefectures and notably lower
in a smaller producing region [18]. One well-documented growing region
saw a substantial contraction in both grower count and harvested area
over roughly a decade [8][17]. Individual corm size differs enormously
by type: small Japanese kuwai corms run only a few grams each, while
Chinese cultivars range from roughly 15 to 75 g per corm [2][4][14].
Nitrogen fertilization has been shown to raise both corm number and
total fresh weight per plant, while lowering dry matter content per
individual corm [35].

**Finding 6 — storage: dormant corms keep cold and humid, in a store,
a pit, or the field.** Corms go naturally dormant at harvest and
require low temperature and high humidity to keep well, remaining
viable for several months at 1-3°C [3]. Several traditional storage
methods are documented: simple field storage (tops removed, drainage
trenches cut, soil heaped against frost, with corms dug for market as
needed) is described as the simplest and best method; clay-pit storage
(corms layered with mud under a soil cap, opened in early spring to
remove emerging sprouts); wet sand storage outdoors with periodic
spraying; and storage under flowing water with a straw covering,
switched to dry straw below freezing to prevent ice damage [3][5].
Seed corms specifically are kept refrigerated or pit-stored from
harvest until the following planting season, roughly 6-7 months
[9][10]. Commercial transport is done cool, in lined, moistened
containers [3][17]. The crop is generally sold from autumn through
early spring [10][14][16].

**Finding 7 — a paddy weed and, in some jurisdictions, a regulated
species.** The wild form of this same species is a common weed of rice
paddies, and herbicide-resistant biotypes (resistant to a major class of
paddy herbicides) have spread via their corms across multiple growing
regions in East Asia [11][19][34]. The related European species is
documented as a principal weed of rice cultivation in several countries
and a nuisance in irrigation infrastructure in many more, and has been
formally listed as a federal noxious weed in the United States since
1981, meaning the edible corms can only be legally imported there under
specific consumption-only permits during a defined seasonal window; it
is also prohibited in New Zealand [16]. Practically, this means corms
left in the field readily sprout as volunteers in the following rice
crop — a persistence and regulatory consideration distinct from most
other root crops in this research.

**Finding 8 — health notes.** Raw corms are reported as poisonous by
at least one authoritative agricultural reference, though the
responsible toxic agent is not confidently identified [2][22]. Peeling,
soaking, and boiling are consistently described as the safe preparation
route [10][32]. A traditional pregnancy caution exists in multiple
popular and herbal sources, claiming the corm "may induce premature
birth," and the crop appears on at least one popular list of foods
Chinese traditional dietary guidance advises pregnant women to avoid,
citing a traditional "cold, blood-moving" property classification —
both claims are weak, traditional, and unquantified rather than
clinically established [11][16][26]. Separately, one study (using the
European-species name, which as noted above is sometimes applied to
this crop) found that this plant accumulates cadmium, copper, and lead
from polluted water and sediment, concentrating them in the edible
corm [21] — a general aquatic-vegetable heavy-metal concern. No source
specifically names this crop as a host for the intestinal fluke
parasite that affects several other aquatic vegetables, so that
specific concern is not extended to this entry [25]. A visually similar
wild plant (arrow arum) is poisonous unless properly processed and is
a documented foraging hazard distinct from this cultivated crop [22].

**Finding 9 — products and uses.** Corms are commonly simmered
(including as part of a traditional Japanese New Year meal assortment),
fried, used in hotpots, or braised; they can also be roasted like a
potato, or dried and ground into flour for gruel or bread [10][11][14].
Young leaves are eaten cooked, and above-ground plant parts are used as
pig fodder, with whole tubers also fed to cattle and pigs in some
countries [2][11][16]. Thin fried corm slices are a traditional
Lunar New Year snack in Cantonese culinary tradition, and a similar
chip product exists in Japan [13][14]. At least one heritage Japanese
cultivar is distilled into a named spirits product, though this use is
not explored further here [14]. A high-starch Chinese cultivar exists
specifically as a processing/starch type, and starch properties (granule
size, crystallinity, pasting behavior) have been shown to differ
meaningfully among cultivars in at least one comparative study [3][12].
Composition figures vary by source: one standard food-composition
database gives raw corm at roughly 99 kcal, 5.3 g protein, and 72.5 g
water per 100 g, with notable potassium and phosphorus content; a
separate Japanese source gives a substantially higher energy figure
(around 125 kcal per 100 g, described as the highest of any vegetable)
with different potassium, phosphorus, and zinc figures — the two
composition sources are not reconciled [1][10][11]. Traditional
medicinal uses recorded include use as a tonic and laxative in one
regional tradition, and as an antiscorbutic, diuretic, and galactofuge
(milk-suppressing agent) in another [2][11].

**Finding 10 — history and culture.** The crop is recorded in Chinese
literature as far back as the 5th century CE [14]. In Japan, the crop
was probably present by antiquity, served as a documented famine crop
during an 18th-century famine, and its New Year symbolic role led to
its wholesale trade being specifically regulated by a historical
ruling authority; one heritage cultivar was historically offered to the
Imperial Court until the late 19th century and nearly disappeared after
a 20th-century war, when cultivation was restricted and shrank along
with urbanizing paddies [10][14]. One well-documented modern Japanese
growing region traces its cultivation back to around the turn of the
20th century, grew substantially after a mid-20th-century boom in New
Year food culture, formed a shippers' cooperative in the 1960s, and
maintains local cultural customs including a dedicated folk dance and
school-run demonstration paddies [8][15].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season aquatic/paddy crop grown
  vegetatively from corms; a perennial grown as an annual, planted in
  late spring to early summer and harvested after the first killing
  frost in autumn/early winter (Finding 2, 3, 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest after frost kills the leaves,
  though field-held corms (left in the flooded, drained field and dug
  to order through the selling season) function as a distinct holding
  stage rather than a separate harvest (Finding 3, 6).
- **Climate:** Subtropical to warm-temperate paddy regions; requires
  no early frost during the corm-swelling period and benefits from a
  large day-night temperature swing during that period (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible; cooking (typically peeling, soaking,
  and boiling or parboiling) is required both for safety and to remove
  bitterness (Finding 1, 8).
- **Pollination:** Not required — this is a clonal, vegetatively
  propagated crop; cultivated types set little seed, and seed
  propagation functions mainly as a weed-persistence route rather than
  a planting method (Finding 7).
- **Yield / composition:** Roughly 11-20 t/ha in China depending on
  cultivar, and roughly 11-12 t/ha in Japan from much smaller
  individual corms; nitrogen fertilization raises corm count and total
  weight per plant but lowers dry matter per corm (Finding 5).
- **Water use:** An obligate flooded-paddy crop with a stage-specific
  water-depth calendar — shallow at sprouting and during corm
  formation, deeper during vigorous vegetative growth; the field must
  never be allowed to dry out, and harvest itself requires a large,
  dedicated water allocation for water-jet digging (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** Propagated from seed corms (sprouted and
  hand-planted directly in the Japanese system) or via nursery-raised
  transplants from pre-sprouted corms or cut top buds (the Chinese
  system) (Finding 4).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour study was found for this crop;
  labor-intensive hand operations specifically documented include
  hand-planting in deep paddy mud, repeated leaf stripping, root
  pruning, and careful two-stage washing and grading at harvest
  (Finding 4, 9; see also Estimates and derivations below).
- **Soil:** Fertile, fine-grained gley paddy soils or clay are
  preferred; sandy or volcanic-ash soils give lower yields; the crop is
  described as a heavy feeder under intensive Chinese cultivation
  guidance (Finding 4; composition-derived nutrient removal below).
- **Stand life:** Grown as an annual from replanted corms each season,
  though the species itself is a perennial (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal red-blight disease serious enough
  that affected fields are not replanted, a leaf blight disease, aphids
  later in the season, waterfowl grazing after planting (addressed by
  netting in at least one region), a leaf smut disease favored by hot,
  humid weather, and a bacterial rot affecting stored seed corms are
  all documented (Finding 4, 6; see also Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** A large number of named Chinese and Japanese
  cultivars exist, spanning corm sizes from a few grams to roughly 75 g,
  cycle lengths from about 110 to 180 days, and distinct uses (fresh
  eating, storage, starch processing); essentially all are clonal, with
  no commercial hybrid seed trade (Finding 4, 5, 9).
- **Harvest:** Dug after frost kills the top growth, either by water-
  jet (large-scale, uses significant water) or by hand in 2-3 passes;
  corms with an intact sprout are specifically graded higher for the
  Japanese New Year market (Finding 1, 3, 7).
- **Storage:** Dormant corms keep for several months at 1-3°C and high
  humidity; traditional alternatives include field storage, clay-pit
  storage, wet-sand storage, and flowing-water storage, all aimed at
  preventing freezing, drying, or premature sprouting (Finding 6).

## Hazards

A fungal red-blight disease is severe enough that affected fields are
specifically not replanted; a separate leaf blight disease, aphid
infestation later in the season, and a leaf smut disease favored by
hot, humid weather are also documented, along with a bacterial rot of
stored seed corms sorted out by hand (Finding 4, 6). The wild form of
this species is a significant, herbicide-resistant weed of rice paddies
in multiple regions, and the related European species carries formal
noxious-weed/import-restriction status in at least two countries,
meaning volunteer corms and uncontrolled spread are a real agronomic
and regulatory concern (Finding 7).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw corms are reported as poisonous by at least
one authoritative source, with the responsible toxic agent not
confidently identified (described alternately as oxalic acid or
polyphenols by different sources); cooking (peeling, soaking, boiling)
is the established safe preparation route. A separate, traditional,
unquantified pregnancy caution ("may induce premature birth") is
recorded in multiple herbal and popular sources but is not clinically
established. Both are deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data (Finding 1, 8).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — at least one study (using a species name that is
sometimes applied interchangeably to this crop, so the identification
should be treated with some caution) found this plant accumulates
cadmium, copper, and lead from polluted growing water and sediment,
concentrating them in the edible corm. This is a general aquatic-
vegetable heavy-metal uptake concern, deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 8).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** mother-plant selection → seed corms refrigerated or
  pit-stored → soaked to sprout → hand-planted in puddled paddy mud →
  corms plus stripped leaves; or seed corms pre-sprouted → nursery →
  transplanted at a specific leaf stage → paddy; or a cut top bud used
  for the nursery while the corm body itself is eaten. Harvested corms
  are washed (typically twice), graded, and either cold-stored, pit-
  stored with spring sprout removal, or left in the drained field and
  dug to order. Stored corms are also peeled, sliced, and dried into
  flour; crushed and washed into starch; or thinly sliced and fried
  into chips. Leaves and peel are used as livestock feed.
- Not covered by this research: canned or frozen product (no sources
  found); a named heritage-cultivar distilled spirits product;
  detailed kitchen debittering methods; water-jet harvesting equipment
  in technical detail; bird-netting construction.

A Japanese New Year tradition treats the whole corm, with its sprout
intact, as a specific festival food symbolizing good fortune; a
historical Imperial Court offering tradition existed for one heritage
cultivar until the late 19th century. A separate Cantonese Lunar New
Year tradition uses fried corm slices as a festival snack, with
benevolence symbolism tied to the crop's Chinese name, served as part
of a tea ceremony where younger family members honor elders.

## Open research questions

- The identity of the raw-corm toxic/bitter agent is disputed: oxalic
  acid vs. polyphenols, across different sources — not reconciled.
- Composition figures conflict substantially between a standard food-
  composition database (lower energy, higher water content) and a
  separate Japanese source (notably higher energy, different mineral
  values) — not reconciled.
- Whether raw corms are properly described as "poisonous" or merely
  "edible but bitter" is disputed between sources.
- The leaf-count threshold at which stolon formation begins is given
  differently by Chinese and Japanese sources — possibly reflecting
  genuinely different cultivar groups rather than a true conflict, not
  confirmed.
- Water depth during the vigorous-growth stage is given as two
  somewhat different ranges within even a single source's citations.
- No dedicated labor-hour study exists for this crop in either growing
  system; labor estimates here are built entirely by analogy to related
  flooded-field root crops and should be treated as rough.
- A specific water-use/crop-coefficient study for this crop does not
  exist; estimates here are built entirely by analogy to paddy rice.
- Starch-extraction yield for processing-type cultivars is estimated
  rather than directly measured.
- Genome size data comes only from search-snippet-level sources and
  includes an apparent internal inconsistency (a very large genome size
  described in the same source as "medium-sized") that was not
  resolved.
- A specific weight for commercial seed corms, and a precise seed
  planting rate, were not found in a single authoritative source and
  are estimated from spacing and typical corm-size figures.
- This crop's documented harvest-quality standard (an intact sprout as
  a graded quality trait) is a genuinely unusual trait-of-interest among
  the root crops this project has researched, worth noting for any
  simulation or game design drawing on this material.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 12,000 kg/ha is derived from the lower end of
several overlapping ranges: Japanese prefecture-level harvest-divided-
by-area figures (roughly 11,000-12,500 kg/ha in the two largest
producing regions), a general reference range of 12,000-15,000 kg/ha,
and Chinese average-yield figures converted to a per-hectare basis
(roughly 11,000-15,000 kg/ha). Good yield is estimated at 18,000 kg/ha
(the upper-middle of named Chinese cultivar yields); exceptional at
22,500 kg/ha (a commonly cited high target in Chinese cultivation
guidance); poor at 6,500 kg/ha (the lowest Japanese prefecture-level
figure found). Plausible range: 10,000-15,000 kg/ha for the world
average. Direct national production statistics broken out for this
specific crop would replace this estimate.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived by analogy to paddy rice, since no
dedicated reference exists for this crop; a roughly 150-day season from
early-summer planting to late-autumn harvest is split into four growth
stages, with the late-stage coefficient lowered somewhat to reflect
this crop's practice of keeping water shallow specifically during corm
swelling. This produces a seasonal water-use index in a plausible range
of roughly 450-1,000 mm of crop evapotranspiration — not including
puddling, percolation, or the substantial extra water used for
water-jet harvesting. A dedicated water-balance study for this crop
would replace this estimate.

Seed-corm requirements and planting rates are derived from documented
spacing figures in both growing systems (giving roughly 45,000-85,000
planting positions per hectare in the Japanese system and roughly
60,000 in the Chinese system) combined with an estimated typical seed-
corm weight, yielding roughly 600 kg/ha of seed corms in the Japanese
system (a multiplication ratio of about 20) and roughly 2,000 kg/ha of
whole seed corms or 250 kg/ha of cut top buds in the Chinese system (a
multiplication ratio of roughly 7.5 for whole corms). A cross-check
using documented corms-per-plant and plant-density figures against a
typical small corm weight produces a figure reasonably close to the
statistically derived Japanese yield, lending some support to these
estimates.

Labor-hour estimates are built by analogy to two related flooded-field
root crops this project has researched, since no dedicated labor
budget exists for this crop. Harvest labor in particular is estimated
as unusually high relative to total yield weight, because individual
corms are small and numerous — a rough cross-check using a documented
hand-harvest rate for a similar wild aquatic tuber, scaled to this
crop's much smaller corm size, suggests harvest labor for the small-
corm Japanese crop likely sits well above typical root-crop harvest
labor figures. A dedicated cost-and-labor survey from either growing
region would replace this rough estimate; plausible range is roughly
2,000-6,000 hours per hectare for the most labor-intensive management
tier.

Nutrient removal per tonne of fresh corms is derived from the lower
composition figure cited above: roughly 8.5 kg nitrogen, 1.7 kg
phosphorus, and 9.2 kg potassium per tonne of corms removed (not
counting leaves and stripped foliage, so whole-crop nutrient uptake is
meaningfully higher). A high-yield Chinese fertilization program applies
substantially more nitrogen per hectare than simple corm-removal
arithmetic would suggest is strictly necessary, consistent with this
being managed as a heavy-feeding crop.

A dried-slice yield fraction of roughly 0.25-0.35 and a starch-
extraction yield fraction of roughly 0.09-0.15 are both derived from
composition figures and analogy to extraction efficiencies documented
for other starchy aquatic root crops, pending a direct measured
extraction study specific to this crop.
