# *Amorphophallus variabilis* (Cumpleng)

## Naming and scope

*Amorphophallus variabilis* Blume, family Araceae, with a chromosome
count of 2n = 26, confirmed by a directly consulted primary genetics
source. Known under several local Indonesian names, including one
Javanese name shared confusingly with the related gum crop covered in
a sibling entry (so it is not a safe search term by itself when
researching this species alone). Cited sources conflict on this
species' accepted synonyms and even on its native range: one tertiary
plant-database source gives a broader native range and three additional
synonym names, while a primary genetics paper and a standard tropical-
Africa/Asia plant-resources reference both restrict the wild native
range to three specific Indonesian islands and accept only one
historical synonym. This conflict is not resolved here (see Open
research questions).

This entry is explicitly not the related gum/flour crop in the same
genus, nor the starchy aroid corm crop in the same genus, both of which
this project has already researched as their own entries. This entry
is grounded against this project's own prior research into that gum
crop (the closest precedent in format, sharing genus, processing
chain, and shade response) and into that starchy corm crop (sharing a
catalogue family tree and the same aroid calcium-oxalate hazard).

This species has its own distinguishing traits: a smaller, lower-value
tuber than either of its two better-known genus siblings; confirmed
absence of the aerial secondary tubers that most clearly distinguish
it from its gum-crop sibling in the field; an unresolved conflict over
whether its mannan content is lower than or roughly equal to that
sibling's; and it is the only entry in this project's research so far
within this genus that remains mostly wild-gathered rather than
farmed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — confirmed by a directly consulted primary source: no
aerial secondary tubers; this species reproduces by cormels and
seed.** A peer-reviewed 2012 genetic-diversity study states directly
that this species has no aerial secondary tubers on the leaf-stalk
branch structure, unlike its gum-crop sibling, and that it reproduces
using cormels (small tubers, fragments with an apical bud, or seasonal
offsets) and true seed [1].

**Finding 2 — a direct, unresolved conflict on mannan content.** A
standard tropical-Africa/Asia plant-resources reference's genus-level
account states that this species has lower mannan content than its
gum-crop sibling, making it "less commercially attractive" [4].
However, the same primary genetics paper cited in Finding 1 states, as
background, that this species has been reported as a traditional food
in Java because its corm contains glucomannan at roughly 50% of fresh
mass — similar to its gum-crop sibling — citing an earlier primary
study [1]. These two sourced claims directly contradict each other and
are not reconciled in this entry. Measured flour-glucomannan figures
from a separate detailed processing study (Finding 4, roughly
12-22% of flour, not fresh tuber) are consistent with either framing
once the fresh-tuber-versus-flour measurement basis is accounted for,
so they do not resolve the conflict either way.

**Finding 3 — yield remains only a theoretical ceiling; no field trial
was found.** The same plant-resources reference states that "at 70×70
cm a spacing a yield of 30 t/ha is theoretically possible" — this
figure assumes every single plant reaches its documented maximum tuber
weight of roughly 1.5 kg, not a measured outcome [4]. No measured
yield trial, on-farm or experimental-station, was found in an
extensive search. The species is broadly documented as "rarely
cultivated... although its glucomannan-rich tubers have strong
potential for functional food and health products" [2], while at
least one documented case of farmer-level growing does exist (a
specific Central Java village supplying tubers for a detailed 2018
processing study) — so "rarely cultivated" and "some thin, patchy
farmer-level growing occurs" both hold at once.

**Finding 4 — a detailed salt-soak processing study, with exact
measured figures.** A 2018 pharmacy-journal study used 1.5 kg of fresh
tuber from a specific Central Java farming village; tubers were
peeled, cut into thin chips, pre-soaked in warm water for three hours,
then soaked in salt-brine solutions at four different concentrations
(0%, 5%, 10%, and 15%) for 60 minutes, then sun-dried, milled, and
sieved into flour [6]. Measured results showed that as salt
concentration increased from 0% to 15%: dry chip weight rose from
roughly 290 g to 333 g (from the 1.5 kg fresh starting material);
flour weight rose from roughly 153 g to 208 g; flour-yield percentage
("rendemen") fell from roughly 22.7% to 19.8% over the same rows (an
internal inconsistency in the source's own tables, not reconciled);
moisture content rose from roughly 7.2% to 9.8%; measured glucomannan
content in the flour rose from roughly 12.2% to 21.5%; and oxalate
reduction rose dramatically from roughly 10.4% (no salt) to 65.1% (5%
salt) to 80.8% (10% salt) to 91.6% (15% salt). An accepted calcium-
oxalate safety limit cited in the same study is met specifically by
the 15%-salt flour. The same study separately cites a comparable
salt-soak trial on this species' gum-crop sibling reaching only about
40% oxalate-reduction efficiency at a lower salt concentration, and a
trial on a specific taro cultivar reaching roughly 97% reduction,
attributed to taro's lower starting oxalate level [6].

**Finding 5 — a correlation study ties tuber size to glucomannan
content, a potential breeding marker.** A 2001 agricultural-science
study examined 36 wild accessions collected from three regions of
Central Java, grown in a randomized trial design. According to the
study's published abstract (the full text could not be extracted from
the available document in this research pass): plant height, leaf-
stalk diameter, and tuber diameter were all positively correlated with
tuber weight; tuber diameter and weight were in turn positively
correlated with glucomannan content; glucomannan content was
positively correlated with starch content; and starch content was
positively correlated with calcium-oxalate content. A path-coefficient
analysis concluded that tuber weight is a practical, measurable marker
for selecting plants with higher glucomannan content [7]. If this
correlation chain holds generally, it suggests that breeding for
bigger, starchier tubers may also mean breeding for more acrid
(higher-oxalate) tubers — an open question, not modeled in this entry.

**Finding 6 — starch-granule density and tissue anatomy.** A 2025
anatomical study of six mature wild plants from a specific Central
Java forest found starch-granule density of roughly 8,400 granules per
square millimeter in tuber tissue, against roughly 25 granules per
square millimeter in leaf stalks and roughly 71 in leaf blades — the
tuber is overwhelmingly the plant's storage organ. Needle-shaped and
cluster-shaped calcium-oxalate crystals dominate the tuber tissue,
which the study's authors link to stress tolerance; tannin-bearing
cavities are more abundant in leaf stalks, while oxalate-crystal-
bearing cells are more frequent in tubers [2].

**Finding 7 — conservation concern, genetic diversity, and a
historical wartime propagule-mobilization anecdote.** A companion 2022
study frames this species as "a wild plant that lives in forests" in
Central Java, whose general neglect and limited local community
knowledge of it "possibly causes its extinction and may have an impact
on the ecological balance" — no formal international or national legal
protection status was found for this species in any source consulted
[3]. Separately, the primary genetics paper cited in Finding 1 reports
substantial genetic diversity among 78 wild accessions collected from
28 sites across West and Central Java, identifying five genetic
clusters that do not track geography and only partially match an
earlier four-group, seven-subgroup morphological classification system
from a 2004 study [1]. The paper proposes bird- and rat-assisted seed
dispersal (the fruit is bright red-orange with sweet pulp) alongside
human-assisted dispersal — farmers reportedly uproot and discard the
plant because of its unpleasant flowering odor and snake-patterned leaf
stalk, inadvertently redistributing it to new sites. The same paper
records a striking historical detail: during a 1940s wartime
occupation of Java, local government authorities reportedly ordered
farmers to collect corms of this genus for wartime logistics purposes,
with corms transported from eastern to western Java; the paper's
authors suggest this historical propagule movement may have
contributed to the species' present-day genetic structure, though they
note no confirming scientific study exists [1].

**Finding 8 — pollination is specifically by sap beetles, with some
documented inbreeding.** The primary genetics paper names the
pollinator group directly as a specific family of sap beetles
(Nitidulidae), attracted during flowering by the plant's unpleasant
odor, and notes that some degree of inbreeding may still occur within
wild populations [1]. This does not affect vegetative cultivation,
since the crop is propagated clonally in practice, but refines the
pollination detail for the minor sexual-seed reproduction route.

**Finding 9 — documented agroforestry-compatible habitat.** The
primary genetics paper states this species is shade-loving and is
"mainly found at the edge of teak forests, bamboo stands, coffee
plantations, cemeteries, and along river banks," noting potential for
agroforestry integration [1] — a directly sourced, species-specific
site list, rather than a general genus-level inference.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season, rainy-season tuber crop,
  dormant during the dry season (consistent with the rest of this
  genus); optimum air temperature roughly 25-35°C, optimum soil
  temperature roughly 22-30°C.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest, dug at dormancy; no sourced
  cycle length was found for this species specifically, though its
  much smaller maximum tuber weight compared to its gum-crop sibling
  suggests a shorter growing cycle, without a specific sourced figure.
- **Climate:** Tropical, found wild up to roughly 700-900 m elevation
  on specific Indonesian islands; frost kills planting material.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — the raw tuber is acrid from
  calcium-oxalate crystals and reportedly also contains alkaloids
  documented at the genus level, not specifically confirmed or
  quantified for this species (hazard detail below).
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production, since this is a
  vegetatively propagated crop; the minor sexual-seed reproduction
  route requires sap-beetle pollination (Finding 8).
- **Yield / composition:** No measured field yield exists; a
  theoretical spacing-derived ceiling of roughly 30,000 kg/ha exists,
  alongside considerably lower working estimates for ordinary field
  conditions (Finding 3; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-water-use reference
  work documents this genus; water-use parameters are estimated by
  analogy to this species' two better-documented genus siblings (water
  detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from cormels or tuber
  fragments planted into moist, shaded, well-drained, humus-rich soil;
  no cultivar- or propagule-type yield data exists for this species
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for this species;
  labor figures are estimated by analogy to its two better-documented
  genus siblings.
- **Soil:** A documented pH range of roughly 6.0-7.5, well-drained,
  humus-rich, deep sandy loam; clay soil is unsuitable (soil detail
  below).
- **Stand life:** A single lift-and-replant annual-style cycle; no
  multi-year standing-crop data exists for this species, unlike its
  gum-crop sibling.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No species-specific soil-borne pathogens or
  storage diseases were found in this research pass, in contrast to
  its two better-documented genus siblings.
- **Cultivars:** No named variety, clone, or breeding line was found
  for this species; documented genetic diversity exists only at the
  wild-population level (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single harvest stage; the tuber is dug at dormancy.
- **Storage:** Dormant tubers reportedly keep for months in the ground
  without loss; planting material should be kept dry, dark, and cool.

Water detail: no standard international crop-water-use reference work
documents this genus at all; a crop-coefficient curve, rooting depth,
and seasonal water totals are all estimated by analogy to this
species' two better-documented genus siblings, since no species-
specific water study was found. This species needs steady rain and
dappled shade while its single leaf grows, in moist, humus-rich, well-
drained soil; clay and swampy ground are unsuitable.

Establishment detail: a documented planting spacing of roughly 70×70
cm is the only sourced spacing figure, and it is specifically the basis
for the theoretical yield ceiling in Finding 3 rather than a general
field recommendation. Dormant tubers may remain in the ground for
months without loss; planting material should be kept dry, dark, and
cool (roughly 10°C); frost or temperatures over 40°C can kill it. No
species-specific dormancy-break study was found, in contrast to its
gum-crop sibling's documented photoperiod and growth-regulator
dormancy-management techniques.

Soil detail: documented pH optimum of roughly 6.0-7.5 applies at the
genus level; shade of roughly 50-60% is documented promoting tuber
production at the genus level, and this species is specifically found
at the edges of teak forests, bamboo stands, coffee plantations,
cemeteries, and river banks (Finding 9).

Cultivar detail: no named cultivar, clone, or breeding line exists for
this species. Documented wild-population genetic diversity includes
five AFLP-marker-based genetic clusters and a separate four-group,
seven-subgroup morphological classification system, imperfectly
matching each other (Finding 7); these describe wild population
structure rather than any selected or released cultivar.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the raw tuber contains calcium-oxalate crystal
structures (a physical and chemical irritant) and reportedly also
contains alkaloids, documented only at the genus level and not
specifically confirmed or quantified for this species. The toxic
effect is worst in wild plants, as is generally true across this
genus. Traditional mitigation is repeated washing and thorough boiling
or baking; fully drying the tuber is also reported to reduce oxalate
content; a salt-brine pre-soak is a documented industrial-style
alternative, cutting oxalate content by roughly 65-92% depending on
salt concentration (Finding 4). Residual risk is low after proper
processing. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

A traditional use of this species' leaves as a smoked tobacco
substitute is documented in one source only and is noted as an
unconfirmed traditional use rather than a production hazard.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** cormel or tuber fragment → planted into moist, shaded,
  well-drained soil → single leaf growth through the rainy season →
  tuber dug at dormancy. Fresh tuber → peeled, sliced into thin chips
  → pre-soaked in warm water, then in salt brine at a specific
  concentration and duration → sun-dried → milled and sieved → flour
  (the salt-soak step is simultaneously the primary documented
  detoxification method, per Finding 4). Alternatively: raw tuber →
  thoroughly boiled, baked, or fully dried as a traditional
  detoxification and food-preparation method.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** industrial starch
  extraction into a konjac-style gel product (documented only at
  general-knowledge confidence, not specifically confirmed for this
  species); detailed cultivation methods beyond the general
  establishment guidance already described; any named cultivar or
  breeding program, since none exists for this species.

No crop-specific religious or ritual production rule was found for
this species in this research pass.

## Open research questions

- A direct, unresolved conflict exists on whether this species' fresh-
  tuber glucomannan (mannan) content is genuinely lower than its gum-
  crop sibling's, or roughly similar to it (Finding 2).
- This species' accepted native range and synonym list conflict
  between sources: one tertiary plant-database source gives a broader
  range and three additional synonym names not accepted by a primary
  genetics paper or a standard regional plant-resources reference.
- No measured field or experimental-station yield trial exists for
  this species; the only documented yield figure is a theoretical
  spacing-derived ceiling, not a measured outcome (Finding 3).
- The detailed 2018 salt-soak processing study's own tables contain an
  internal inconsistency: chip and flour weight both rise with
  increasing salt concentration, while the flour-yield percentage
  figure falls over the same rows; this is not reconciled by the
  source itself (Finding 4).
- The 2001 correlation study linking tuber size, glucomannan, starch,
  and oxalate content could only be read at the published-abstract
  level in this research pass, since the available document appeared
  to be a scanned image with no extractable text; if its correlation
  chain holds generally, breeding for larger tubers may also mean
  breeding for higher oxalate content, which is not modeled here
  (Finding 5).
- Formal conservation or legal protection status for this species was
  not found despite two independent academic papers raising
  conservation concerns.
- Weakest areas overall: growing-cycle length (no species-specific
  source found); calorie and protein content (estimated by analogy
  between this species' two genus siblings, since no fresh-tuber
  composition source was found); water-use parameters, labor-hour
  figures, and soil nutrient-uptake figures (all estimated by analogy
  to genus siblings, with no species-specific source found); and most
  yield tiers below the theoretical ceiling (estimated by analogy,
  scaled down from a genus sibling's figures).
- Whether this species has been tested in any space-agency or
  controlled-environment life-support research was checked and no
  such trial was found.
- No nuclear genome-size figure or chloroplast genome record exists for
  this species, in contrast to a genus sibling's documented chloroplast
  genome.

## Estimates and derivations

Growing-cycle length (roughly 365-730 days) is a rough estimate with
no direct source: no documented cycle length exists for this species
specifically, though this species' much smaller maximum tuber weight
compared to its gum-crop sibling (and that sibling's own shortest
documented cycle length) suggests a shorter cycle than that sibling's,
without a specific sourced number to anchor it.

Calorie and protein content (roughly 700 kcal/kg, 15 g protein/kg) are
estimated by analogy, falling between a starchy aroid corm sibling's
documented composition and a lower estimated figure for the gum-crop
sibling, reflecting this species' dense starch-granule structure
(Finding 6) against its disputed and uncertain mannan fraction (Finding
2); no fresh-tuber composition source was found for this species
directly.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 8,000 kg/ha, good 15,000 kg/ha, exceptional
30,000 kg/ha, poor 3,000 kg/ha) are set with the exceptional tier
matching the sourced theoretical spacing-derived ceiling described in
Finding 3 directly; all other tiers are estimated by analogy to the
gum-crop sibling's own derived and traditional yield figures, scaled
down to reflect this species' considerably smaller individual tuber
size. No measured yield trial exists for any tier besides the
theoretical ceiling. Processing pack-out fractions (chips roughly
19-22% of fresh weight, flour roughly 10-14% of fresh weight) are taken
directly from the detailed 2018 processing study's measured table
(Finding 4).

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, rooting depth, and
seasonal water-use slots) are all estimated by analogy to this
species' two better-documented genus siblings, since no standard
international crop-water-use reference work documents this genus and
no species-specific water study was found.

A planting-material seeding rate of roughly 2,000 kg/ha is derived
from the documented planting spacing combined with an assumed typical
individual seed-tuber weight, since no directly sourced planting-
material weight figure was found for this species. A seed-to-yield
multiplication ratio of roughly 5 is estimated conservatively, well
below the theoretical ratio implied by the spacing-derived yield
ceiling, to reflect realistic field conditions rather than the
theoretical maximum.

Labor-hour figures across all production tiers, soil nutrient-uptake
figures, effective rooting depth, storage duration, and harvest-window
length are all estimated by analogy to this species' two better-
documented genus siblings, since no species-specific sourced figures
exist for any of these parameters.
