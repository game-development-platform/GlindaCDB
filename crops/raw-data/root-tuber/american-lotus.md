# American Lotus (*Nelumbo lutea*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-26

**Data quality note:** this entry is thin. No one farms this plant
commercially for food, so every yield, labor, and water-use value here
is an estimate or borrowed by analogy from the sacred lotus (*Nelumbo
nucifera*), a closely related crop this project has also researched.

## Naming and scope

*Nelumbo lutea* (synonyms *N. pentapetala*, *Nelumbium luteum*). It is
the only lotus native to the Americas and one of just two species in
the genus [1][2]. Common names include water chinquapin, yonkapin,
yockernut, pondnut, duck acorn, alligator button, and rattlenut; in
Cajun French it is *graine à voler*; it carries distinct names in
several Indigenous languages as well [2][3][5][13]. Range: Maine and
southern Ontario to Wisconsin, south to Florida and Texas; also Central
America, the West Indies, and northern South America [1].

This species hybridizes freely with the sacred lotus in the ornamental
water-garden trade, and a meaningful fraction of Chinese ornamental
lotus cultivars carry American lotus ancestry [2][9]. Genus-level traits
— thermogenic flowers, the self-cleaning "lotus effect" leaf surface,
and exceptionally long seed viability — are shared with the sacred
lotus and are only briefly noted here; fuller treatment is in this
project's own research on that related crop [10].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a pond plant, not a paddy crop.** It prefers 0.6-2.1 m of
water, establishing in shallow water and growing out into deep water
[1]; the best planting site is a shallow pond about 90 cm deep with
20-30 cm of soil [2]. This is a sharp contrast with sacred-lotus paddy
cultivation, which is kept at only a few centimeters to half a meter of
water depending on whether it is grown for root or seed [10]. It grows
best on acid soil (about pH 4.5) but tolerates a wide range of pH,
soils, and depths, and needs full sun and room to spread [2]. It is
very aggressive: six plants set 3 m apart can cover 0.4 ha in one
growing season, and a patch can spread about 14 m in each direction per
summer [2][5]. Anglers who plant it in private ponds report it will
"utterly dominate the pond," making fishing hard or impossible, and
large colonies can block small boats [1][5].

**Finding 2 — storage tubers, formed in the fall.** The cylindrical,
spongy rhizomes form thick tubers in the fall [1]; the tubers are
banana-shaped, up to nearly 30 cm long [4][13]. Eating quality is sweet,
like a baked sweet potato [5][6][14]; young roots are eaten raw [6]. A
cut tuber shows a wagon-wheel pattern of air channels, similar to
Asian-style lotus root [13]. Traditional harvesting involved people
finding the tubers with their toes in the mud, working them loose, and
lifting them with a hooked stick; by the mid-1800s tubers had become
scarce in at least one documented region due to harvest pressure
[4][15]. Documented uses include cooking fresh, drying for winter
storage, cooking with hominy/meat/beans, and grinding dried roots into
flour [4][6][12]. One account describes soaking tubers in lye to loosen
the skin before peeling and boiling [12]. For nursery purposes, dormant
tubers are lifted after leaves die back and kept in moist sphagnum peat
in a cool, dark, frost-free room [1].

**Finding 3 — seeds: a prized green snack and hard, long-keeping
mature seed.** Flowering runs June to September; flowers are 7.6-20 cm
across, each opening in the morning and closing in the afternoon for
3-4 days total per one source, or 2 days per others — not reconciled
[1][6][13]. Pollination is insect-mediated [5]. Pods are about 10-15 cm
across, held about 0.9 m above the water, with about 20 seeds each
[4][5][6][13]. Seeds are round, hard, and brown, about 1 cm across per
most sources, though one source gives "up to 4 cm" — likely a different
measurement or an error, not reconciled [1][2][13]. Seed density
estimates range up to 85,000 seeds per acre by one count, or about
160,000 per acre by a different flowers-times-seeds-per-pod calculation
— these two approaches are not reconciled [5][6][13]. The prized green
stage runs from around July to August-September: pods are bent over and
snapped off by hand from a boat, then the seeds are popped out and
eaten raw (tasting like peanuts) or boiled in seasoned salt water and
shelled [2][5][6][7]. Poorly filled seeds are empty, and thin seeds
taste bitter [5][13]. At the mature stage, ripe seeds come loose and
rattle, and the pod nods over and drops its seeds into the water — so
harvest must happen before this point [5][6]. Traditional processing
of mature seed documented across several Indigenous groups includes
soaking and parching in hot sand, roasting and grinding into a sweet
meal, cooking with corn, and boiling into soups [2][4]. The green
embryo inside a mature seed is too bitter to eat [13]. Composition
figures from a foraging-reference source (not a peer-reviewed
laboratory source) give dried seed at about 350 kcal, 17-18 g protein,
63-68 g carbohydrate (mostly starch), 1.9-2.5 g fat, and about 13%
water per 100 g [13].

**Finding 4 — leaves, shoots, and flowers are also used.** Young,
unfurling leaves are cooked like spinach; large leaves are used to
wrap food for baking [2][6]. Young stalks are peeled and cooked,
reportedly tasting somewhat like beets; dried, strung shoot ends were a
documented winter-storage method in at least two Indigenous traditions
[4][6][13]. Flower stamens are dried for a fragrant tea, and dried
flowers are used in cooking [6][13]. Dried pods are sold for floral
arrangements [1][2].

**Finding 5 — propagation.** From seed: scarify the hard seed coat;
sinkers are viable, floaters are not; change the water daily;
germination takes 4-5 days above 27°C [1][2]. Seedlings are potted at
about 2.5-5 cm of water and rarely bloom until the following season per
most sources, though one source reports about six years from seed to
first bloom — not reconciled [1][2][5]. From rhizomes/tubers: use
dormant pieces with at least one bud (ideally two segments sealed by
intact nodes), planted before dormancy breaks at a 15° angle with the
tip 5 cm deep; first leaves appear in about 3 weeks; water at 10-16°C
is ideal [1][2]. Planting time is May-June in temperate zones, or
March-October in warmer zones [2]. Spacing for restoration plantings is
1-1.5 m [2]. Container culture uses large (57-70 L) round containers
filled a quarter to half deep with silt or clay loam, fed periodically
with a balanced fertilizer, with iron supplementation if leaves yellow
between the veins [2].

**Finding 6 — hazards, pests, and legal status.** The American lotus
borer (a moth species) has been documented wiping out a regional
population's seed crop at peak production; spider mites and the
waterlily aphid also attack the plant [2]. The species is formally
listed as endangered, threatened, or extirpated in several US states,
which restricts collecting in those jurisdictions [1]. It is a
recognized aggressive colonist of ponds and lakes [1][2][5]. One
historical source notes the seeds act as a laxative [4]. No specific
toxin is reported for this species; the genus-level heavy-metal uptake
concern that applies to aquatic plants generally also applies here
[10].

**Finding 7 — cultural and religious importance.** The plant holds
status as a sacred food among the Osage people, symbolizing the wish
for health and long life, especially for children; it has been served
at ceremonial dinners and was the subject of a 2025 discussion on
making it "a more regular food source" as part of a tribal food-
sovereignty effort [3]. Several other Indigenous nations across North
America are documented as having eaten the plant and, in multiple
accounts, treated it as a sacred plant with mystical significance [1].
In Louisiana, Cajun and other local families continue to gather and eat
the green seeds as a traditional snack [5][6][7]. Selection for food
traits is essentially absent — one named natural color variant exists,
along with several ornamental hybrids with the sacred lotus that are
not reported for food use [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season perennial grown in permanent
  water; planted May-June in temperate zones (March-October in warmer
  zones); flowers June-September; pods harvested July-October; tubers
  dug after leaf fall (Finding 1, 3, 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Perennial bearing — pods are picked repeatedly
  through the summer at either a green or mature stage, while tubers
  are dug yearly from only part of the stand, allowing it to regrow
  (Finding 2, 3).
- **Climate:** Temperate, subtropical, and tropical zones; the species
  is notably more cold-hardy than the sacred lotus, with rhizomes
  surviving under ice in frozen ponds (Finding 6; cross-reference to
  the sacred lotus comparison in Finding 1).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — green seeds and young tubers are eaten
  raw; mature seed and tubers are typically cooked, roasted, or dried
  (Finding 2, 3).
- **Pollination:** Required for seed production; insect-pollinated
  (Finding 3). Root/tuber harvest does not depend on pollination.
- **Yield / composition:** No reliable field yield data exists for
  either seed or tuber, since the crop is not commercially farmed;
  estimates are built from seed-density counts and from the sacred
  lotus's paddy-root yield scaled down for partial, hand-dug harvest
  (see Estimates and derivations below).
- **Water use:** Requires permanent standing water; does not tolerate
  drought at all, but tolerates extreme waterlogging, since it is a
  true aquatic plant rather than a paddy crop grown in shallow,
  managed flood (Finding 1).
- **Establishment:** From dormant tubers or rhizome pieces with at
  least one bud (preferred method), or from scarified seed; spacing for
  restoration plantings is 1-1.5 m (Finding 5).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist; harvesting both green
  pods (by boat) and tubers (by hand, toe-and-hook digging) are
  reported as slow, labor-intensive traditional practices (Finding 2,
  3).
- **Soil:** Best on acid soil around pH 4.5, but tolerant of a wide
  range; soft silt or clay loam, 20-30 cm deep, with some organic
  matter (Finding 1, 5).
- **Stand life:** A long-lived, indefinitely spreading perennial; once
  established, a stand persists and expands for decades (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** The American lotus borer (a seed-crop
  pest), spider mites, and the waterlily aphid are the named pests
  (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** Essentially no food-oriented selection exists; one
  named natural color variant and several ornamental interspecific
  hybrids with the sacred lotus, none reported for food use (Finding
  7).
- **Harvest:** Pods are harvested repeatedly through the season at
  either a green (preferred for raw/boiled eating) or mature (hard
  seed) stage; tubers are dug from only part of the stand each year,
  typically after the leaves die back in fall (Finding 2, 3).
- **Storage:** Mature seed is exceptionally long-keeping (documented
  viability of decades to centuries under the right conditions); fresh
  tubers are comparatively short-lived and were traditionally dried for
  winter storage or kept in cool, damp moss (Finding 2, 3).

## Hazards

The American lotus borer can destroy a seed crop at peak production;
spider mites and the waterlily aphid also attack the plant (Finding
6). The species is an aggressive colonizer that can take over ponds
and lakes, interfering with fishing and boating (Finding 1, 6). It is
formally listed as endangered, threatened, or extirpated in several US
states, meaning wild collection may be legally restricted depending on
location (Finding 6). One historical source notes a laxative effect
from the seeds (Finding 6). No specific plant toxin is documented for
this species; the general heavy-metal-uptake caution that applies to
aquatic plants broadly also applies here, deferred to this project's
own general aquatic-plant hazard material rather than treated as a
distinct hazard of this species specifically.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** tubers → eaten fresh, used as planting stock, or
  lye-peeled and dried into flour; green pods → raw or boiled green
  seeds; mature pods → hard seed (plus dried pods for floral use) →
  parched and ground into meal; young leaves used as cooked greens;
  shoots eaten fresh or strung and dried for storage.
- Dried pods are also sold commercially for floral arrangements
  (Finding 4).
- Not covered by this research: cooked preparations of green seed
  beyond basic boiling; stamen tea and dried-flower culinary
  preparations in detail; a historical root-poultice use mentioned only
  in passing; wild-stand foraging practice beyond what is already
  described in the findings above.

## Open research questions

- Seed size is disputed: about 1 cm across per most sources vs. "up to
  4 cm" per one source — possibly a different measurement basis or an
  error, not reconciled.
- Flower lifespan is disputed: 3-4 days per one source vs. 2 days per
  others.
- Time from seed to first bloom is disputed: the following season per
  most sources vs. about six years per one source.
- Seed density per unit area is disputed: a direct count of about
  85,000 seeds per acre vs. a flowers-times-seeds-per-pod calculation
  giving about 160,000 per acre.
- Seed composition figures come from only a single non-peer-reviewed
  foraging reference, with an internal conflict against a separately
  cited flour-protein figure — a dedicated proximate analysis would be
  valuable.
- Tuber yield, seed yield, and labor requirements are all unsourced
  estimates, since no commercial food production of this species
  exists anywhere — a documented stand-harvest count would be the most
  valuable missing data point.
- The pattern of partial-stand tuber digging (harvesting roughly a
  fifth of a stand per year, allowing regrowth) is documented
  qualitatively but not quantified.
- The traditional belief that a harvester's height determined the size
  of tuber they could reach is a culturally interesting detail, not an
  agronomic one, and was not pursued further.
- Genome size and photoperiod sensitivity were not found in available
  sources.
- A university archaeobotanical study measuring elemental composition
  of carbonized tubers exists but was not opened directly, and might
  refine the composition estimates used here.

## Estimates and derivations

Seed yield is derived from seed weight and seed-density figures. A
round seed about 1 cm across approximates a sphere of about 0.52 cm³;
at a density of roughly 1.15 g/cm³ (a hard, starchy seed that sinks),
that gives about 0.6 g per seed (plausible range 0.4-0.9 g). Seed
density estimates range from about 210,000 to 395,000 per hectare
across the two disputed counting methods noted above. Multiplying gives
roughly 126-237 kg/ha of dried seed from wild stands. A world-average
yield of 180 kg/ha is adopted as the midpoint of that range; good yield
is estimated at 300 kg/ha (a well-fertilized, borer-free stand);
exceptional at 450 kg/ha (by analogy with a comparable species' low-end
first-year figure); poor at 60 kg/ha (reflecting borer damage). Time
from flowering to hard seed is estimated at 30-45 days, and the green-
to-dried seed ratio at about 2.5:1, both by analogy with a closely
related crop this project has researched.

Tuber nutritional composition is estimated by analogy with raw sacred-
lotus root (about 740 kcal and 26 g protein per kg), adjusted upward
slightly on the assumption that this species' tubers are drier (since
they were traditionally dried for winter) and possibly richer; a
plausible range of 600-1,200 kcal/kg is used pending a dedicated
proximate analysis.

Tuber yield is estimated at about 3,000 kg/ha per year: sacred-lotus
paddy cultivation gives roughly 12,900-16,000 kg/ha when a whole field
is dug annually; since this species grows in deep water and is dug by
hand over only about a fifth of the stand area per year (allowing
regrowth), a comparable full-stand tuber density of about 15,000 kg/ha
scaled to a fifth gives roughly 3,000 kg/ha. Plausible range:
1,000-8,000 kg/ha.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived by analogy with sacred-lotus paddy
cultivation, treating this species as an open-water crop with a
comparable leaf canopy; season length is estimated at about 160 days
(planting in late spring through autumn leaf fall), shorter in more
northern latitudes.

A planting rate of roughly 1,000 kg/ha of tuber pieces is estimated
from typical restoration spacing (about 6,400 planting pieces per
hectare at roughly 150 g per two-node piece), though a much slower,
lower-input establishment (as few as a handful of plants per hectare)
is also documented as viable for a naturally spreading stand.

Labor estimates for pod picking, tuber digging, and seed processing are
built from approximate handling rates (pods per hour from a boat, kg of
tubers per hour of toe-and-hook digging, seeds shelled per hour) with
no direct source, giving a rough total of 400-1,500 hours per hectare
per season at a traditional, low-mechanization management level.

Shelf-life estimates: dried seed about 2 years (seeds are documented to
remain viable for decades, but food-quality shelf life is assumed
shorter); fresh tubers about 2 weeks and dried tubers about a year,
based on their traditional use as a winter store.
