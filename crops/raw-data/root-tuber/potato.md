## Naming and scope

*Solanum tuberosum* L., nightshade family (Solanaceae), Chilotanum
Group — the lowland, long-day-adapted potato that became the world's
dominant potato crop [5]. Cultivated potato is autotetraploid, with 48
chromosomes [15]. International statistics generally treat two
cultivated subspecies as nearly all of world production; under a more
recent taxonomic framework these correspond to the long-day Chilotanum
Group covered here and a separate short-day Andigenum Group, which
this project has already researched as its own entry [1][5].

This entry treats the modern long-day potato as a single crop, folding
several market-type groupings (russet/processing, waxy/salad,
yellow-fleshed all-purpose, fingerling, and colored-flesh types) in as
cultivar groups rather than separate crop entries, since they share
the same underlying biology and differ mainly in end use and storage
regime. A further related crop — a small group of frost-resistant
high-Andean potato species grown at extreme altitude and traditionally
freeze-dried — is a distinct, not-yet-researched entry, separate from
the crop covered here.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the same tuber ends up in one of three storage regimes,
chosen by end use.** After harvest, tubers are first cured at roughly
10-15.6°C and about 95% relative humidity, with good aeration, for 10-
14 days; the corky wound tissue that seals cuts and bruises begins
forming within 24-48 hours, but healing is too slow below about
7.2°C or above 35°C [3]. After curing, tubers are held in the dark at
a temperature set entirely by their intended end use: seed tubers at
roughly 3.3°C, where dormancy persists indefinitely (0°C instead
causes a specific chilling-injury browning); table stock at roughly
4.4-7.2°C with a sprout inhibitor, keeping five to ten months, with one
well-known processing cultivar keeping ten months or more at the
warmer end of that range; and processing stock (for fries, flakes, or
chips) at a somewhat warmer 7.2-12.8°C depending on the specific
product, since storage below roughly 7.2-10°C converts starch to sugar
and darkens fried products [3]. A short warm "reconditioning" period
can partially reverse this sugar buildup before frying, though it is
documented as sometimes less than fully effective, and the resulting
sugars are also linked to acrylamide formation during frying [3][6].
Sprouting begins four to six weeks after harvest at room temperature,
and most cultivars keep only about three months at a warmer 10°C
before a sprout inhibitor becomes necessary; controlled-atmosphere
storage adds at most two to three more weeks of life, and the tuber's
highest freezing point is documented at about -1.1°C [3].

**Finding 2 — water needs are stage-dependent: sensitive early in
tuber development, safely economized late.** Seasonal water
requirement runs roughly 350-650 mm, with fresh-tuber water
productivity of roughly 4-11 kg of tuber per cubic meter of water used
[1]. Available soil water should never be depleted below roughly
50-70% remaining (that is, no more than 30-50% depletion); the most
damaging deficits occur during stolon formation, tuber initiation, and
shortly afterward, while shorter, moderate deficits earlier in the
vegetative stage are largely recovered after rewatering, and the
senescence stage is the least sensitive of all — cutting irrigation
there saves several applications and can actually raise tuber dry
matter [1]. Quality effects of water stress are specific and severe: a
deficit early in tuberization produces elongated, spindle-shaped
tubers (worse in naturally long-shaped cultivars), a deficit followed
by irrigation causes cracking or an internal disorder called black
heart, and a deficit after tuber initiation can cut the marketable
tuber share from roughly 90% down to 50-70% [1]. Roughly 70% of water
uptake comes from the top 0.30 m of soil and nearly all of it from the
top 0.40-0.60 m, even though roots can occasionally reach well over
1.8 m deep [1].

**Finding 3 — tuber formation is governed by both temperature and
day length.** Frost or cold stress sets in at 1°C and below, and heat
stress at 35°C and above [1]. Optimum soil temperature for tuber
growth is roughly 15-18°C, with an optimum air temperature of
25-32°C by day and a notably cooler 12-18°C at night; as temperatures
rise above these optima, vegetative (leaf and stem) growth increases
while tuber initiation and growth are actively suppressed [1]. The
long-day group covered in this entry tuberizes most readily under
14-17 hour days but adapts somewhat to shorter days; under short days,
some cultivars tuberize earlier, flower less, mature earlier, and
ultimately yield less than they would under their preferred day
length [1].

**Finding 4 — yields and the scale of the world crop.** A recent
global production estimate puts total output at roughly 375 million
tonnes from roughly 17.8 million hectares, a world average yield of
roughly 21 t/ha [4]. An earlier international assessment found Asia
and Europe together accounting for the large majority of world
production [1]. Good irrigated yields in northern Europe and North
America regularly exceed 40-50 t/ha, while rainfed and irrigated
yields in subtropical and cool-tropical regions run considerably
lower, roughly 5-25 t/ha [1]. Specific documented US state yields run
even higher in intensive irrigated production, with one state's
average converting to roughly 48 t/ha and another's to roughly 69
t/ha in a recent year [17]. Dry matter content runs roughly 20-25% of
fresh weight [1]. Documented nutrient removal is substantial: roughly
4-6 kg nitrogen, 0.6-1.1 kg phosphorus, and 7-11 kg potassium per
tonne of fresh tubers, with recommended fertilizer application rates
considerably higher than straight removal to account for losses [1].
The crop is rated moderately salinity-sensitive, with a documented
salinity threshold and total yield loss at a specific higher salinity
level [1].

**Finding 5 — propagation is almost always by seed tuber, though a
genuine true-seed hybrid route now also exists.** The crop is nearly
always planted as whole seed tubers or cut pieces carrying two to
three eyes, at a planting density of roughly 30,000-60,000 plants per
hectare, with emergence typically around seven days after planting
[1]. World trade in certified seed tubers runs to roughly 1.4 million
tonnes a year [1]. Detailed regional seed-sizing guidance recommends
discarding undersized pieces and planting cut seed immediately unless
the cut surfaces have already corked over in storage, with
cultivar-specific in-row spacing documented for several named
processing cultivars [8]. A genuinely new development is hybrid true
potato seed: diploid potato is normally self-incompatible, but a
specific gene discovered in a wild relative species overcomes this
self-incompatibility, allowing inbred diploid lines to be created
through roughly six generations of self-pollination and then crossed
to produce F1 hybrid true seed with hybrid vigor, at a documented seed
density of roughly 2,500 seeds per gram [11]. Ordinary clonal seed
tubers are also a documented route for carrying over plant viruses and
a bacterial ring-rot disease, which is why certified disease-free seed
stock is recommended [3].

**Finding 6 — new potatoes and mature storage potatoes are
genuinely separate harvest stages.** Standard commercial practice
kills the vines chemically or mechanically roughly 7-21 days before
harvest specifically to allow the tuber skin to set and resist
abrasion during digging and handling [9]. Early red-skinned cultivars
are documented as notoriously prone to poor skin set even with
extended time after vine killing; one California trial found a
two-week setting period gave unacceptably high skinning damage while a
four-week period kept it low, and killing vines too far ahead of
harvest was separately found to lower a quality measure (specific
gravity) in a Minnesota trial; an immature, unset tuber loses roughly
10-60 times more weight in storage than a properly matured one [9].
Because of this, "new potatoes" (deliberately lifted green, before
full maturity) and the mature storage crop are modeled here as
genuinely separate harvest stages, with different storage regimes and
different markets — new potatoes keep only a few months even under
refrigeration [3][9].

**Finding 7 — a specific weather pattern predicts late blight risk,
and light exposure raises a documented toxin.** A long-used UK blight-
warning rule defines a risk period as two consecutive days each
reaching a minimum temperature of 10°C and at least 11 hours at 90%
or higher relative humidity; a more recent, more sensitive revision of
this rule lowers the required humidity-hours and was found, in
retrospective testing, to correctly flag a much higher share of
historical disease outbreaks than the older rule [10]. Separately,
light exposure causes tubers to turn green and raises their content of
natural glycoalkaloid compounds (mainly one specific compound); a
documented food-safety guideline recommends market potatoes stay under
200 mg/kg, against normal tuber levels of roughly 12-20 mg/kg and
greened-tuber levels of roughly 250-280 mg/kg [3][7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season stem-tuber crop, grown as an
  annual across cool-temperate, temperate, subtropical (as a winter
  crop or at high elevation), and cool-tropical highland zones
  (Finding 3; climate detail below).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single dig per planting, at either the "new"
  (green-lifted) or "mature" (skin-set) harvest stage (Finding 6).
- **Climate:** Documented cultivation from lowland cool-temperate
  regions to tropical highland zones at elevation, with up to roughly
  180 days in a single cool-tropical rainfed season (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Never eaten raw; raw starch is poorly digested, and
  greened tubers carry a documented glycoalkaloid hazard (Finding 7).
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production — flowering plays
  no role in the ordinary clonal crop; pollination matters only for
  the newer hybrid true-seed route (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** A world average around 21 t/ha, with good
  irrigated yields regularly exceeding 40-50 t/ha and some intensive
  systems reaching close to 70 t/ha (Finding 4; see also Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** Sensitive to deficit specifically during stolon
  formation and tuber initiation, tolerant of moderate early-season
  deficit, and safely economized during senescence (Finding 2; water-
  use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from whole seed tubers or
  cut pieces carrying two to three eyes, planted 5-10 cm deep
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** Harvesting (digging and picking) is typically the single
  largest labor component in a fully manual production cycle, which
  can run to 1,600-2,000 hours per hectare in total (labor detail
  below).
- **Soil:** Deep, loose, well-drained soil is traditionally
  recommended; the crop is moderately salinity-sensitive and has a
  notably high potassium demand (Finding 4).
- **Stand life:** Annual.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Late blight (a weather-predictable fungal
  disease), plant viruses and a bacterial ring-rot disease carried in
  seed tubers, and a fungal storage rot are all documented concerns
  (Finding 5, 7; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Several market-type cultivar groups (russet/
  processing, waxy/salad, yellow-fleshed all-purpose, fingerling, and
  colored-flesh types), all clonal and tetraploid (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** Vines are killed chemically or mechanically roughly
  7-21 days before digging to set the tuber skin and reduce handling
  damage, except for the deliberately green-lifted "new potato" stage
  (Finding 6).
- **Storage:** Cured warm and humid for about two weeks after harvest,
  then held dark and cool — never truly cold — in one of three
  end-use-specific temperature regimes (seed, table, or processing
  stock) (Finding 1).

Climate detail: frost or cold stress sets in at 1°C and below, and
heat stress at 35°C and above; the crop's preferred growing
temperature is notably warmer by day than by night, since excess
night warmth actively suppresses tuber formation in favor of leafy
growth.

Water-use detail: no official rooting depth exceeds roughly 0.4-0.6 m
for practical water-management purposes, even though occasional roots
reach much deeper; the crop's most sensitive period for irrigation
scheduling is the window from stolon formation through tuber
initiation and shortly after.

Establishment detail: planting density runs roughly 30,000-60,000
plants per hectare, denser under irrigation; emergence typically takes
about seven days but is delayed by cold or dry soil, under-sprouted or
immature seed stock, or overly deep planting.

Labor detail: a fully manual production cycle documented in one
South Asian study ran to roughly 1,600-2,000 hours per hectare in
total, with harvesting generally the largest single component; a
separate smallholder survey found substantially lower total labor,
illustrating how widely labor requirements vary with mechanization
level and farm scale.

Cultivar detail: russet/processing-type cultivars are bred and managed
specifically for long storage life and processing quality (fries,
flakes, and chips); waxy/salad types, often red-skinned, are notably
prone to poor skin set and are generally used fresh rather than
stored or processed; yellow-fleshed all-purpose types sit in between;
fingerling types are small, elongated, and often late-maturing; and
colored-flesh types differ mainly in a natural pigment compound rather
than in agronomic behavior.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — tubers exposed to light turn green and
accumulate natural glycoalkaloid compounds (plant defense chemicals),
concentrated in greened flesh, sprouts, peel, and eyes, and also
present in the plant's foliage; a documented food-safety guideline
recommends market potatoes stay under a specific concentration
threshold. Mitigation is dark storage, hilling soil over exposed
tubers in the field, peeling, and discarding visibly green portions.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 7).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — tubers stored too cold for processing use
convert starch to sugar (cold sweetening), and frying these sugars
produces a documented process contaminant (acrylamide) in the
finished fried product; mitigation is end-use-appropriate storage
temperature and a warm reconditioning period before processing,
though reconditioning is not always fully effective. Deferred until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 1).

Late blight, a weather-predictable fungal disease with established
risk-forecasting rules, is the dominant field disease concern; plant
viruses and a bacterial ring-rot disease are documented risks carried
in seed-tuber stock, which is why certified disease-free seed is
recommended; a fungal storage rot is also documented (Finding 5, 7).

Discarded or surplus tubers fed to livestock still carry the same
glycoalkaloid hazard described above, along with a documented choking
risk and a laxative effect at high feeding rates, and should be fed
with appropriate care.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed tubers (whole or cut) → planted → mature tubers,
  dug 10-21 days after vine killing → graded, with culls removed →
  cured for 10-14 days → placed into one of three dark storage regimes
  by intended end use. Table stock → boiled, baked, or mashed.
  Processing stock → reconditioned if oversweetened → fried into
  fries or chips, or drum-dried into flakes. New potatoes → lifted
  green → sold within weeks, uncured. Culls → livestock feed, starch
  extraction, or cooked mash → fermented → distilled spirit. Seed-
  size tubers → stored cold → replanted the next season.
  Alternatively: hybrid true seed → nursery seedlings → seedling-grown
  tubers.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** frying, freezing, and
  canning in industrial detail; sprout-inhibitor chemistry; disease
  control and seed-certification schemes in detail; laboratory
  aeroponic minituber production.

A tradition in Jainism avoids this and other root vegetables, since
uprooting the plant is held to kill the whole organism along with
soil-dwelling life, and some traditions classify root vegetables as
holding, in a single body, what is considered an unlimited number of
minute life forms; a substantial majority of surveyed Indian Jains
report abstaining from root vegetables on this basis. No production
rule tied to this tradition was found — it governs consumption, not
farming practice. Passover-related use of potato starch and Hindu
fasting-food customs involving this crop were not independently
verified in this research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 21,000 kg/ha, good 45,000 kg/ha, exceptional
69,000 kg/ha, poor 5,000 kg/ha) are derived from a recent global
production-and-area estimate for the world-average tier, a documented
"more than 40-50 t/ha" figure for temperate irrigated regions for the
good tier, a specific high-yielding US state's documented average
yield in a recent year (converted to metric units) for the exceptional
tier, and the lower end of documented subtropical and cool-tropical
yield ranges for the poor tier.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a European region's
documented stage-length breakdown in a standard international
reference work, combined with that same work's documented mid- and
late-season crop coefficients; the resulting total seasonal water
estimate falls comfortably within an independently documented seasonal
water-requirement range for this crop. A days-without-water tolerance
of roughly 4 days is derived from standard soil-water-holding
assumptions combined with this crop's documented rooting depth and
moisture-depletion allowance. A yield-penalty-per-dry-day figure is
estimated from a recalled, not independently re-verified seasonal
yield-response factor, applied across a representative season length;
the true penalty is understood to be considerably more severe during
the tuber-initiation stage specifically than this single estimated
figure suggests.

A seed-rate and multiplication estimate is derived by multiplying a
documented planting-density range by a documented seed-piece weight
range, giving a seed-rate range in the low thousands of kilograms per
hectare; dividing a representative good-tier yield by a representative
seed rate gives a seed-to-yield multiplication ratio in the high
teens, while dividing the world-average yield by the same seed rate
gives a somewhat lower ratio.

Labor-hour tiers are derived from a documented total labor-hour figure
for a fully manual production cycle in one detailed regional source,
split across operations with harvesting (digging and picking) taken
as the largest single share, consistent with general field-practice
description; a separate smallholder labor survey showing considerably
lower total hours illustrates how widely this figure can vary by farm
scale and mechanization level, and is not used to set the tiers
directly.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh tubers) are derived as the midpoints of documented
removal ranges from an international crop-water-and-yield reference
work.

New-potato storage life (roughly 30 days) is estimated as a cautious
middle figure between a documented early-crop storage range and the
much faster spoilage rate documented for immature, unset tubers
relative to fully matured ones.

Hybrid true-seed mass (roughly 0.4 mg per seed) is derived from a
documented seed-count-per-gram figure for this crop's hybrid true-seed
production system.

Genome size (roughly 0.86 picograms per monoploid genome set) is
derived from a published monoploid genome-assembly size estimate using
a standard genome-size conversion factor; a tetraploid cultivar's full
genome content would run to roughly four times this monoploid figure.
