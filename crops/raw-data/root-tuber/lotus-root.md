# Lotus Root (*Nelumbo nucifera*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-26

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

The rhizome crop of sacred lotus, *Nelumbo nucifera* Gaertn., its own
small plant family (Nelumbonaceae); Japanese renkon, Chinese lian ou
[1]. This entry models the rhizome-lotus cultivar group specifically —
plants grown for the swollen underground rhizome — treating the
plant's other organs (leaves, stems, flowers, runners, and the seed
pods that rhizome-type plants also set) as secondary products of the
same crop.

Several related but distinct crops and plants are excluded from this
entry. A separate cultivar group of the same species, grown
specifically for its seed rather than its rhizome, is a distinct crop
this project has also researched as its own entry, since it differs
meaningfully in harvest method and product. A purely ornamental
flower-type cultivar group is not modeled here at all. American lotus,
a related but genuinely separate species with a much smaller trade and
a historical Native American food use, is likewise its own entry in
this project. This crop is not to be confused with water lilies
(an unrelated aquatic plant genus) or with the unrelated legume genus
that happens to share the common name "lotus" in some usages.

This entry is grounded against a closely related paddy-grown aquatic
root crop this project has also researched (for its flooded-field
parasite risk and starch-processing chain), and against a related
root-crop entry already migrated into this collection for formatting
precedent and for its Japanese-statistics-heavy research approach.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a flooded-paddy crop, dug from late summer through
spring, often straight out of standing water.** A typical Japanese
season runs: paddies prepared from early spring, seed rhizomes planted
in April to early May, pest spraying in early summer, and field
harvest running from mid-August through to May of the following year,
peaking around the New Year holiday market [5][6]. A southern Chinese
region plants mid-March to early April and digs from late October
through to mid-March [7]. Two distinct digging methods are documented:
"water digging," where the paddy stays flooded and the grower wades in
and locates rhizomes by touch, blasting mud away with a pump-fed hose
(a method that only became practical at scale once pumps became
available in the late 1970s, and that requires low-iron water, since
iron otherwise turns the root's natural tannins blue-black); and "hoe
digging," where the paddy is drained first and rhizomes are dug by
hand with a hoe or fork [2][3][6][10][11]. Both methods are slow and
delicate work, since the rhizomes snap easily [2][7]. Mature rhizomes
can simply be left in the wet mud over winter and dug to order as
needed, with one region leaving roughly a quarter of its rows entirely
undug to serve as next year's planting stock [7]. Mud must be washed
out of the rhizome's internal air channels immediately after digging,
before it sets [10].

**Finding 2 — yields: a Japanese national average around 13-16 t/ha,
with Chinese rhizome fields reporting considerably higher figures.**
Japan's national average ran 12.9-16.0 t/ha across different years in
the 2012-2024 period, with the leading producing prefecture reaching
14.8-18.8 t/ha and one top-producing cooperative reporting roughly
20 t/ha [2][3][4][5][6]. Chinese sources report a much wider range:
one reference gives 7.5-15 t/ha for an early July dig versus 30-45
t/ha for a September dig, while trade-press figures convert to roughly
22.5-37.5 t/ha [1][15]. Other regions report roughly 10-30 t/ha in
Korea and a much lower 3.5-4.5 t/ha figure from an older Indian source
[25]. China is documented as growing roughly 70% of world supply,
across a combined rhizome-lotus and seed-lotus planted area in the
hundreds of thousands of hectares as of a 2005 estimate [1].

**Finding 3 — rhizome swelling is a short-day response controlled by
a light-sensing plant pigment system.** Controlled seedling trials
found that rhizomes swelled under 8, 10, and 12-hour day lengths but
instead elongated into runners under 13- and 14-hour days; under
14-hour days, supplementing with blue, green, or far-red light still
produced swelling, while white, yellow, or red light supplements
produced elongation instead, implicating the plant's phytochrome
light-sensing system in the switch [8]. A brief red-light interruption
in the middle of an otherwise-short night under short days stopped
swelling, and a flash of far-red light immediately afterward reversed
that effect again, and the timing of maximum sensitivity to this
interruption itself follows a roughly day-length-dependent daily
rhythm [9]. Short days also induce dormancy in this species [23]. An
unresolved field puzzle: some early-maturing Japanese cultivars are
documented swelling rhizomes in early summer, when days are well over
14 hours long, suggesting cultivars differ meaningfully in their
photoperiod sensitivity — not resolved here.

**Finding 4 — a warm-season crop needing roughly six warm months.**
One Japanese source places the crop's northern production limit at a
site needing at least six months with a mean temperature of 15°C or
higher [5]. A Chinese regional cultivation standard specifies an
annual mean of 17-22°C and an accumulated-temperature requirement of
6,500-8,000 degree-days [7]. A general reference gives germination
above 13°C and a growing-season preference of 23-27°C, with a native
range stretching from the Amur river basin to India and northern
Indochina, up to roughly 1,400 m elevation [1]. Japanese cultivars
split into two broad groups: one group, introduced relatively
recently from China, matures late and grows fast in heat, while a
second, older native group is early-maturing and more cold-tolerant,
suited to early digging [3]. The plant's flowers are documented as
thermoregulating, holding 30-35°C even when surrounding air is only
10°C [1].

**Finding 5 — roughly 600 mm of true crop water use, but several
times that volume actually flows through a flooded field.** A single-
field measured water balance in Japan over a 140-day growing period
found roughly 1,609 cubic meters of irrigation, 722 cubic meters of
rainfall, 599 cubic meters of evapotranspiration (measured by a
standard method), and 1,731 cubic meters of drainage per 0.1 hectare —
working out to about 599 mm of true crop evapotranspiration (roughly
4.3 mm/day) against over 2,300 mm of total water passing through the
field [5]. Standing water depth is managed by growth stage in one
Chinese standard: shallow (roughly 8-10 cm) from planting to
sprouting, deeper (20-25 cm) once leaves stand upright, then shallow
again (3-5 cm) while rhizomes are actively swelling [7]; other
regional guidance gives a wider 5-50 cm range by stage [15]. Shallow-
water-grown rhizomes (roughly 10-20 cm deep) are documented as crisper,
while deep-water types (over 40 cm) run starchier, and wild or
ornamental stands can grow in anywhere from 30 cm to 2.5 m of standing
water [1]. A flooded lotus field is documented to lose more oxygen-
demanding organic matter and phosphorus per unit area in its runoff
than a comparable rice paddy, though a fertilizer-free lotus field used
specifically as a runoff filter still removed a meaningful share of
these pollutants from water passing through it [5].

**Finding 6 — a notably heavy feeder, several times the nitrogen
demand of rice.** One Japanese prefecture's conventional fertilizer
practice runs roughly 30 kg nitrogen and 21 kg phosphate per 0.1
hectare, versus a comparison figure of only 5-8 kg nitrogen for a
standard rice crop in the same units; a slow-release-fertilizer trial
cut this to roughly 24 kg nitrogen while maintaining yield, and a
nitrogen-containing lime treatment is applied roughly a month before
planting specifically to suppress rot [5]. A locally derived uptake
formula ties total absorbed nitrogen and phosphorus directly to yield
[5]. A separate regional trial on a specific cultivar documented a
similar nitrogen rate alongside a substantial poultry-manure
application and additional potassium and magnesium inputs [12]. A
Chinese regional standard specifies a heavy well-rotted-manure basal
application plus mineral fertilizers, followed by three separate
top-dressings timed to specific leaf-development stages [7]. Preferred
soils across sources are loam or clay loam at a pH of roughly 5.5-7.5,
with a specific silt-layer depth and organic-matter requirement in one
standard; soft soils are noted to give softer, whiter rhizomes
[7][10].

**Finding 7 — pests, diseases, and rot are a major production
constraint.** A vascular rot disease, caused by a soil-borne fungal
pathogen, causes browning inside the rhizome and scorched, rolled leaf
edges, and typically starts from infected seed rhizomes; a soil-
solarization trial in one Japanese region measurably reduced the
pathogen's soil density and raised yield by roughly 79% the following
season, with rot incidence tracking pathogen density directly [7][13].
The same fungal pathogen is also documented causing storage rot after
harvest [24]. Other documented diseases include a virus disease, a
bacterial leaf-spot disease, and a water-mold leaf blight [7]. Pests
include a snail (controlled with ducks, fish, or a plant-derived
feed cake), aphids, thrips, a cutworm moth species (controlled with
pheromone traps), and a root-feeding beetle (controlled using fish
predators) [6][7]. Standard field practice to manage disease pressure
includes rotating out of this crop with dry-land crops, sun-drying and
liming a drained field between crops, and avoiding damage to runners
and stalks, which is believed to spread rot [7].

**Finding 8 — cultivar groups and named cultivars differ in origin,
shape, digging method, and disease resistance.** Chinese cultivation
recognizes rhizome, seed, and flower cultivar groups, with several
named rhizome cultivars documented, one specifically early-maturing
at roughly 110-120 days to harvest [1][15]. Japanese cultivation
distinguishes an introduced Chinese type (disease-resistant, high-
yielding, documented since the late 19th century) from an older native
type (present since roughly the 8th century, slender and deep-rooted,
harder to dig, and now little grown); a specific hybrid cross between
the two dominates one leading prefecture's production [2][4][6].
Named cultivars documented by growing region differ in internode
length, root shape (short and round versus long), and preferred
digging method — some suited to water digging, others to hoe digging
— with eastern Japan generally preferring short, round rhizomes and
western Japan preferring longer ones [2][4][11].

**Finding 9 — storage: cool and humid, or simply left undug in the
paddy.** A Chinese regional storage standard specifies 5-10°C with no
more than roughly 1°C of fluctuation, 90-95% relative humidity,
protection from sun, rain, frost, and crushing, and refrigerated
transport ideally completed within 48 hours [7]. A general reference
gives a slightly different optimum of 5-8°C, with fresh stacked
rhizomes keeping about three weeks, rhizomes layered in sand keeping
up to two months, and a salt-solution treatment extending storage life
to as much as five months [1]. One Japanese region ships rhizomes
still coated in mud specifically because this is documented to extend
shelf life [11]. Consumer storage guidance recommends wrapping in damp
paper in a refrigerator with cut faces covered; naturally occurring
iron oxide in some soils can stain the rhizome's skin reddish-brown to
black without affecting eating quality [4]. Freshly cut slices brown
quickly from enzymatic browning; trial work found that anti-browning
dips, a brief heat treatment, and a carbon-dioxide-enriched package
together kept slices usable for 21 days at 5°C, and that near-freezing
storage (roughly -0.5°C) outperformed a standard 4°C over a shorter
trial [21].

**Finding 10 — nearly every organ of the plant is eaten or otherwise
used.** The rhizome is cooked in simmered dishes, stir-fries, tempura,
and soups, and stuffed with sticky rice in some Chinese preparations;
a Japanese New Year tradition eats it as a symbol of "a clear view
ahead," owing to its naturally hollow cross-section, and it appears in
Japanese Buddhist temple cuisine [1][2][3][4]. Mature rhizome is also
processed into a traditional starch product by grinding, repeated
settling and washing, and drying into cakes, with one specific
regional process yielding roughly 10% starch by weight of fresh
rhizome [14]. Leaves serve as traditional food wrappers and as a tea
ingredient; the fibrous stalks (petioles) are eaten in several
Southeast and South Asian cuisines, and their fibers can be drawn out
and hand-spun into a fine traditional textile used, in at least two
documented regional traditions, to weave ceremonial robes for Buddhist
religious images [1]. Flowers serve as offerings and a tea ingredient,
including a scented green tea tradition in Vietnam [1]. Seeds are
eaten fresh from green pods or dried after the pods mature and
blacken several months after planting, and are processed into cored
seeds, a sweet filling paste, popped snacks, and tea, including a
bitter tea made from the seed's green embryo [1]. Mature dry seed runs
roughly 60% starch and up to 24% protein by one composition estimate,
though the seed-type cultivar group bred specifically for this product
is a separate entry in this project [19]. Integrated farming systems
combining this crop with fish, crab, or crayfish, and using ducks to
control snail pests, are also documented [1][7].

**Finding 11 — hazards.** A parasitic flatworm whose larval cysts
attach to the surface of aquatic plants, including this one, can
infect people and pigs who eat raw or improperly cleaned plant parts,
or who drink untreated water from infested ponds — a hazard this
project has also documented for a related aquatic root crop. This
plant also takes up heavy metals from polluted water and sediment;
most of the uptake stays in the true roots rather than the harvested
rhizome, though one Indian lake study found a specific heavy metal
(chromium) giving the highest calculated risk value in rhizomes,
while the overall combined hazard measure stayed below a level of
concern [20]. The same uptake property is put to deliberate use in
water-treatment "phytoremediation" plantings, with one source
reporting substantial removal of several metals from contaminated
water [1]. The leaves and seed embryo contain two documented alkaloid
compounds that are used medicinally at tea-strength doses [1].
Discoloration from iron in wash water or soil, and fast browning of
cut tissue, are quality issues rather than safety hazards [4][10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A perennial aquatic rhizome crop grown as
  an annual in a flooded paddy; planted in spring, harvested from
  late summer through the following spring depending on maturity
  (Finding 1, 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** A rolling single harvest dug section by section
  over an extended autumn-to-spring window; a secondary new-rhizome
  harvest is possible in early-to-mid summer (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Subtropical, warm-temperate, and tropical zones needing
  roughly six months with a mean temperature of 15°C or higher; frost-
  sensitive foliage but frost-tolerant submerged rhizomes (Finding 4;
  climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible with care — crisp young rhizome is
  eaten raw in some cuisines, but see the parasite hazard below
  (Finding 11).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the rhizome crop; insect-
  pollinated (beetles and bees) where seed set matters (Finding 10).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable by region and digging
  method, from roughly 5 to 40+ t/ha of fresh rhizome (Finding 2; see
  also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Must stand in water all season — shallow at
  sprouting, deeper in full leaf, and shallow again while rhizomes
  swell; true crop water use is modest compared to the much larger
  volume that flows through a continuously flooded field (Finding 5;
  water-use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated entirely vegetatively from rhizome
  pieces carrying at least one growing bud, pushed bud-first into
  puddled mud (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** Harvest (hand digging, by water pressure or by hoe) and
  post-harvest washing and grading together dominate total labor hours
  (Finding 1, 9; labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Loam or clay loam with a meaningful silt layer, pH roughly
  5.5-7.5, and good organic-matter content; a notably heavy nitrogen
  and phosphorus feeder compared to other paddy crops (Finding 6).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as a one-season crop, though the plant is
  genuinely perennial and is lifted and replanted every year from held
  rhizomes (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A soil-borne fungal vascular rot is the
  dominant disease concern and a major driver of crop rotation
  practice; viral, bacterial, and water-mold diseases, plus a snail,
  several insect pests, and a root-feeding beetle, are also documented
  (Finding 7; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Distinct Chinese-origin and native Japanese cultivar
  lineages, plus named hybrid crosses between them, differing in
  shape, maturity timing, disease resistance, and preferred digging
  method (Finding 8).
- **Harvest:** Dug by hand, either by wading into standing water and
  locating rhizomes by touch with a pressurized water hose, or by
  draining the field first and digging with a hoe or fork; both
  methods are slow, delicate work (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Best left undug in the wet paddy mud; once harvested,
  wants roughly 5-10°C, high humidity, and packing in mud or damp
  sand, and the cut surface browns within minutes (Finding 9).

Climate detail: native range stretches from a cool temperate river
basin in the north to tropical South and Southeast Asia, up to roughly
1,400 m elevation; frost kills standing foliage, but rhizomes
submerged in mud survive and continue to be dug through the winter.

Water-use detail: no official crop-coefficient curve exists for this
species; one is estimated here by analogy to rice, the standard
flooded-paddy crop in international reference tables, adjusted upward
somewhat to reflect this crop's taller, broader leaf canopy and longer
late-season growth phase. The resulting estimated water-use figure is
checked against the one site's directly measured evapotranspiration
rate and found broadly consistent. The crop has essentially no drought
tolerance at all, being a true aquatic plant, and an extreme tolerance
for standing water; even a very short dry spell during the growing
season is treated here as a meaningful yield risk.

Establishment detail: rhizome pieces are planted bud-first at a shallow
angle into puddled field mud, with the growing tip left just above the
mud surface; row and in-row spacing varies by region, with edge rows
generally turned to face inward.

Labor detail: harvest dominates total labor hours by a wide margin,
reflecting the slow, delicate nature of hand digging in standing
water or soft mud; post-harvest washing, trimming, grading, and
packing is the next-largest labor component. One detailed single-farm
account documents a small family operation running several hectares
with digging work continuing across most of the year.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — a parasitic flatworm's larval cysts attach to
the surface of this and other aquatic plants; eating raw or
improperly cleaned plant parts, or drinking untreated water from
infested ponds, can infect both people and pigs. This hazard is shared
with other aquatic crops this project has also researched. Deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 11).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this plant takes up heavy metals from polluted
growing water and sediment; while most uptake stays in the true roots
rather than the harvested rhizome, at least one study found a specific
metal reaching a notable concentration in rhizomes from a polluted
site. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 11).

A soil-borne fungal vascular rot disease is the dominant disease
concern, typically starting from infected seed rhizomes and managed
through crop rotation, field solarization, and liming; viral,
bacterial, and water-mold diseases and several named insect and snail
pests are also documented (Finding 7).

Wading waist-deep in cold water to dig rhizomes by hand through the
winter months is documented as genuinely strenuous, heavy, muddy work
(Finding 1).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed rhizomes held over winter in the paddy mud →
  planted in spring → new rhizome (early-to-mid summer) or mature
  rhizome (autumn through spring). Mature rhizome → cool, humid
  storage, or simply left undug in the mud. Mature rhizome → salted
  and boiled product. Mature rhizome → pulped, settled, and washed →
  traditional starch, with a fibrous residue byproduct. Leaves →
  fresh or dried food wrappers, or tea. Stems → cooked food, or hand-
  drawn fiber thread for traditional weaving. Flowers → offerings,
  tea, or tea-scenting. Seed pods → dried seed, or shelled, cored
  seed plus embryo tea, further processed into a sweet filling paste.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen
  product; fresh-cut slices in anti-browning dips and modified-
  atmosphere packaging; popped seed snacks; integration with fish,
  crab, crayfish, or duck farming; use of the crop for wastewater
  treatment and metal phytoremediation.

Documented religious and cultural uses include Hindu use of the
flower in worship and as a seat for several deities; Buddhist flower
offerings and symbolic use of the flower form in religious art, plus
the ceremonial-robe weaving tradition noted above; Japanese Buddhist
temple cuisine; and a Japanese New Year food tradition tied to the
rhizome's naturally hollow appearance. A tradition in Jainism avoids
root vegetables and other underground storage organs generally, a
note this project has also recorded for other root crops. No
production rule tied to any of these traditions was found.

## Open research questions

- Chinese yield figures conflict between two source types — a general
  reference's 30-45 t/ha for a September dig versus a trade-press
  figure converting to roughly 22.5-37.5 t/ha — both apparently best-
  case claims, not reconciled here.
- Seed weight figures for this species conflict sharply between two
  sources by roughly an order of magnitude, likely reflecting
  different measurement bases or a unit error — not resolved.
- A large-area production claim for one specific Chinese cultivar
  appears implausible next to a documented national seed-lotus area
  figure from the same rough time period, and is probably a cumulative
  rather than a per-year figure — not resolved.
- How some early-maturing Japanese cultivars manage to swell rhizomes
  under long summer days, apparently contradicting the crop's general
  short-day rhizome-swelling response, is an open field question.
- Weakest estimates overall: a worldwide average yield figure (Japan's
  statistics are solid but China's, which dominates world production,
  rely on trade-press figures); a labor-hour split by operation (based
  on a single detailed farm account rather than a broad survey); the
  number of days a flooded field can go without standing water before
  yield is affected; and shelf-life figures for minor plant-part
  commodities, most of which are estimated rather than directly
  sourced.
- Whether rhizome-type cultivars set a meaningful pod/seed yield of
  their own, versus the dedicated seed-type cultivar group, was not
  found in any source consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 15,000 kg/ha, good 20,000 kg/ha, exceptional
40,000 kg/ha, poor 5,000 kg/ha) are derived by weighting Japan's well-
measured national statistics against higher Chinese trade-press
figures, since China accounts for the large majority of world
production but its figures are considered likely to skew toward best-
farm claims; the poor tier reflects an older, lower Indian figure
rounded upward slightly.

A dry-to-fresh weight ratio of roughly 5:1 is derived from a
documented raw-rhizome moisture content in the high 70s to low 80s
percent range.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are estimated by analogy to rice, the
standard flooded-paddy reference crop, adjusted upward to reflect this
crop's taller, broader leaf canopy and longer late-season growth
phase; the resulting estimate is checked against one site's directly
measured evapotranspiration rate over a comparable period and found
consistent. A days-without-water tolerance of roughly two days and a
correspondingly high per-day yield penalty are estimated given the
crop's status as a true aquatic plant with essentially no drought
tolerance.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 6 is derived by
dividing a representative Japanese yield figure by a documented
seed-rhizome planting rate; a higher-yielding specific site would imply
a somewhat higher ratio, and the planting-rate figure used here comes
from an unfertilized experimental field, which may run lower than
typical commercial practice.

Labor-hour tiers are derived from a single detailed small-farm
account's total annual labor input divided by its cultivated area,
split across operations using harvest and post-harvest handling as the
two dominant shares, based on the farm's documented year-round digging
schedule; the finer split across individual operations and across
mechanization tiers is estimated rather than independently sourced.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh rhizome) are derived from documented raw-rhizome protein
content (for nitrogen) and cross-checked against a locally derived
whole-plant nutrient-uptake formula tied to yield; the whole-plant
figures run somewhat higher than the rhizome-only removal figures,
with the difference attributed to nutrients retained in leaves and
stalks that are not harvested.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 7-8 g/m²/day) is derived
by dividing a representative yield figure by an estimated growing-
season length. Genome size (roughly 0.95 picograms, 1C) is derived
from a published genome-assembly size estimate using a standard
genome-size conversion factor.

A wind-damage risk note is recorded as a plausible but unquantified
effect, based on a documented recommendation to avoid breaking stalks
because doing so is believed to spread rot, combined with the plant's
tall, exposed growth habit; no source directly measured storm-driven
yield loss.
