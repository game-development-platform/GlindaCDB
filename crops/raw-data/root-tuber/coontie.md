## Naming and scope

*Zamia integrifolia* (coontie, koonti, Seminole bread), a small cycad
in family Zamiaceae — **not a flowering plant and not related to true
arrowroot** (*Maranta arundinacea*, a different crop this project has
also researched). Kew's Plants of the World Online accepts *Zamia
integrifolia* as the species name for Florida's native cycad, with a
native range spanning southeastern Georgia, Florida, the Bahamas,
Cuba, and the Cayman Islands [1]. A commonly cited alternative name,
*Zamia floridana*, is treated as a synonym at the species level but
survives as an accepted name for a botanical variety under an
alternative classification scheme; a second variety is also accepted
under that scheme. A separate species-level name sometimes used for
this plant in general references reflects a different taxonomic
treatment that lumps the wider Caribbean population differently, and
is not the name Kew's database carries for the Florida population.
This resolves, at the database level, a long-standing three-way
taxonomy conflict, though it does not settle whether the Cuban,
Bahamian, and Cayman populations are truly the same species as
Florida's — that remains a genuine dispute between competing
taxonomic treatments, not a database gap.

This crop shares the general "toxic raw, extracted by grating and
washing" starch-extraction pattern with tacca and arrowroot, two
related starch-root crops this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an extreme, decades-long growth cycle tied to a now-
vanished wild-harvest industry.** A wild plant might weigh around five
pounds and can take roughly 30 years to reach that size; a nursery
seed needs about a year to produce a 30 cm plant in cultivation.
Historical starch mills near Miami (running roughly from the mid-1800s
to the 1920s, with exact start dates conflicting across sources) relied
entirely on wild populations — one mill alone processed somewhere
between 10 and 20 tons of root a day, depending on which historical
source is used, and "between the starch factories and the building
boom in south Florida, only small remnants of these vast populations
remain." No cultivated starch yield for this crop has ever existed, and
the plant sits far outside any normal annual-crop model. The last
factory was destroyed by a major hurricane in 1926 [2][3].

**Finding 2 — poisonous raw, and the underlying toxicology remains
genuinely unsettled.** The whole plant, except the seed's fleshy outer
coating, contains cycasin (a carcinogenic, genotoxic compound, making
up 2-4% of seed) and a separate neurotoxic compound called BMAA;
as few as 1-2 seeds can be fatal [4]. Grating, repeated rinsing,
settling, and several days of fermentation remove most of the *free*
toxin, and the resulting wash water is itself toxic and must be
discarded — historical deaths from improperly prepared bread made from
this plant are documented [2][4]. However, the mechanism by which
cycad toxins cause a documented neurodegenerative syndrome among a
Pacific island population with historically high cycad-flour
consumption is actively disputed between two competing hypotheses,
neither fully confirmed: one holds that a cycasin breakdown product
causes lasting DNA damage in brain cells; the other holds that BMAA,
produced by cyanobacteria living symbiotically in cycad roots,
accumulates in brain protein as a slow-release toxic reservoir,
potentially explaining a documented multi-year disease latency after
exposure stops. A significant processing nuance: one primary source
reports that while washing removes most *free* BMAA, a meaningful
amount remains in a protein-bound form within prepared cycad flour —
meaning a study that measures only free BMAA in washed flour and
concludes the food is safe may be missing the dose that actually
matters. Further complicating the picture, a separate mouse study using
washed cycad flour found no appreciable cycasin, its breakdown product,
or BMAA by sensitive chemical analysis, and instead flagged a different
compound (a plant sterol glycoside) as possibly the more important
toxic component — an as-yet-unresolved alternative hypothesis. None of
this toxicology is specific to this particular species — it is drawn
from the broader cycad family, much of it from a different Pacific
cycad species — so it is recorded here as a hazard with real residual
uncertainty, not a solved problem. Separately, cycasin levels up to
75 µg/g have been found in traditionally washed cycad flour generally
(not species-specific), and in the United States, selling the starch
of this plant under the name "arrowroot" was banned by federal
regulators in 1925 [2].

**Finding 3 — starch yield is low and seasonal.** One ton of root
historically produced about 200 lb of starch (roughly 10% recovery).
Starch content in the root is reported to range from about 20% in
winter down to about 8% in summer, meaning harvest timing genuinely
matters; one informal source claims a much higher 38% starch content
without a documented basis, which conflicts with every other source
and is not used here [2].

**Finding 4 — the traditional processing chain.** Roots and
half-buried stems are peeled and washed, then chopped, grated, or
pounded; the resulting pulp is rinsed and strained many times (an
overnight soak followed by hours of rinsing in running water is also
described in some sources); the starch is allowed to settle and the
toxic water drained off; the paste is then left to ferment for several
days until it bubbles, and finally sun-dried into a powdery,
cornmeal-like flour used for a traditional gruel, bread, biscuits, and
pudding [2].

**Finding 5 — a dedicated landscape-horticulture publication confirms
and extends earlier findings.** A standard horticultural extension
fact sheet on this plant (originally published 1999, revised 2023),
drawn from a university extension's landscape-plant reference series,
reports: a height of 2-4 ft and spread of 3-5 ft, a round growth habit,
moderate density, and slow growth; USDA hardiness zones 8B through 11;
tolerance of part shade, part sun, and full sun; broad soil tolerance
(alkaline, sandy, acidic, loam); high drought tolerance; good salt
tolerance; and a recommended landscape spacing of 36-60 inches. On
pests, this source contains a genuine internal inconsistency: its
summary table states "no serious pests are normally seen on the
plant," while its body text separately states that a specific scale
insect "must be controlled by regular spraying because it can be fatal"
to the plant — an unresolved contradiction within the source itself. A
specific butterfly caterpillar (a name likely misspelled in the
source) is documented as devouring foliage rapidly for about two weeks
before disappearing, after which "plants look fine" once new foliage
grows back — a confirmed non-fatal defoliation event. This source also
adds that seed propagation is "usually difficult" with "very slow"
growth, that nursery trade availability is limited ("may have to go
out of the region to find the plant"), and that the plant is "rarely
used" in mass landscape plantings specifically "because of the high
cost of plants" — corroborating that this is a costly ornamental
plant today, never a food crop [7].

**Finding 6 — propagation and nursery practice.** The plant is
dioecious (separate male and female plants) and pollinated by weevils
and a specific beetle species; female cones are documented as most
abundant a few months after a wildfire in the plant's native pine-
scrubland habitat. Seeds have an orange fleshy coat that must be
removed by hand (with gloves, given the plant's toxicity); a float
test is used to discard non-viable seed; seed is sown at a shallow
depth in tall nursery tubes. Seed is recalcitrant (it cannot be frozen
for storage), but cleaned seed kept indoors stays viable for at least a
year. Reported germination time conflicts sharply across sources — one
gives about 6 weeks, another gives about a year. Vegetative propagation
by division is documented as working; stem or root cuttings are
reported as viable by one source but explicitly denied by another —
neither conflict is resolved here [4][5].

**Finding 7 — conservation status and a detailed legal framework
governing wild harvest.** This species carries an international
near-threatened conservation rating and a regional vulnerable rating,
driven mainly by the historical starch-mill overharvest and ongoing
habitat loss [2][3]. A specific state statute governing "commercially
exploited plants" (defined as native species removed from the wild in
significant numbers and sold or transported for sale) establishes a
tiered permission system: taking one or two plants from someone else's
private land, or from public land, requires written permission from
the landowner or managing agency; taking three or more plants
additionally requires a state agricultural-department permit. Anyone
transporting or selling such a plant harvested from their own property
must have a department permit on hand (threatened-species plants are
specifically excepted from this transport/sale requirement).
Misusing or forging such a permit is classified as a first-degree
misdemeanor under a related statutory provision. Critically,
nursery-propagated stock is entirely unaffected by this framework —
the statute targets wild removal only, which is why this plant is
freely sold as a landscape plant despite the permit regime around wild
harvest [8].

**Finding 8 — genome size is known only at the broader family and
genus level, not for this species specifically.** No species-specific
nuclear genome measurement for this plant was found, but broader data
exist: a large genome size is typical across cycads generally, with a
reported family-wide average. Within the plant's genus specifically, a
survey of 71 species found genome size varies relatively little across
the genus (roughly a 1.36-fold range between the largest and smallest
genomes measured), with species from a specific geographic region
running largest. One single-species data point from a related species
in the same genus implies a genome size somewhat above the broader
family average — though this is only one related species, not this
plant itself [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen, extremely slow-growing cycad
  grown on a multi-decade cycle; not an annual crop in any conventional
  sense (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single, destructive harvest per plant — the
  whole plant is dug once, at roughly the 30-year, 5 lb size mark; no
  separate maturity-stage products exist (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Subtropical, tropical, and warm-temperate zones, USDA
  hardiness zones 8B through 11 (Finding 5).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — the whole plant except the seed's
  fleshy outer coating is poisonous, and as few as 1-2 seeds can be
  fatal (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the historical root/starch
  harvest; the seed-propagation route requires both a male and a
  female plant and insect (beetle/weevil) pollination (Finding 6).
- **Yield / composition:** No cultivated field-yield figure has ever
  existed for this crop; starch recovery from root historically ran
  around 10%, with starch content in the root itself varying
  seasonally from roughly 8% in summer to 20% in winter (Finding 1, 3;
  see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Highly drought-tolerant once established, but
  genuinely intolerant of waterlogged or moisture-retentive soil,
  which causes root rot (Finding 5, 6).
- **Establishment:** Propagated either by seed (sown in tall nursery
  tubes, though documented as "usually difficult") or by division from
  a mature plant; sources conflict on whether stem or root cuttings
  also work (Finding 6).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for any operation on
  this crop; all labor estimates in this entry are rough analogies to
  other slow-growing perennial crops, not direct measurements.
- **Soil:** Broadly tolerant of soil texture (alkaline, sandy, acidic,
  loam) and of soil chemistry (acid, neutral, or alkaline pH), but
  strictly intolerant of poor drainage; native habitat includes
  limestone-based soils (Finding 5, 6).
- **Stand life:** The plant is destroyed by a starch harvest, so there
  is no repeat-harvest concept for food use; as an ornamental landscape
  plant it is long-lived (Finding 1, 7).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific scale insect can be fatal to the
  plant if untreated and requires regular spraying; a specific
  butterfly caterpillar causes dramatic but non-fatal defoliation,
  with the plant fully recovering; sooty mold can develop secondarily
  from scale or mealybug honeydew, a cosmetic issue (Finding 5).
- **Cultivars:** None found — this crop is not grown from named
  horticultural cultivars; the two accepted botanical varieties are
  taxonomic distinctions only, with no documented distinct cultivated
  form or trade name.
- **Harvest:** A single, destructive dig of the whole plant at
  maturity (around the 30-year, 5 lb size cue), historically timed to
  the winter season for higher starch content (Finding 1, 3).
- **Storage:** No sourced storage figure exists for the fresh root,
  which is toxic and was historically processed promptly after
  digging; dried starch keeps well; cleaned seed kept indoors retains
  viability for at least a year, but must never be frozen (Finding 2,
  6).

## Hazards

A specific scale insect can be fatal to the plant itself if untreated,
requiring regular spraying as prevention; this is an agricultural
hazard to the plant, not a human-food hazard. A specific butterfly
caterpillar causes dramatic but non-fatal, fully-recovering defoliation
(Finding 5).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the whole plant except the seed's fleshy outer
coating contains cycasin, a carcinogenic and genotoxic compound, and
BMAA, a neurotoxic compound linked (via a disputed mechanism) to a
serious neurodegenerative syndrome; as few as 1-2 seeds can be fatal.
Traditional processing (grating, repeated rinsing, fermentation,
drying) removes most of the *free* toxin, but a meaningful fraction of
BMAA may survive processing in a protein-bound form, meaning the
hazard is not fully eliminated by washing alone. The toxic wash water
itself must be discarded and never reused. This is a serious, only
partially understood hazard, deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 2).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — selling the starch of this plant under the name
"arrowroot" was banned by US federal regulators in 1925, and a state
statute imposes a tiered permit system on harvesting this plant from
the wild (landowner permission for 1-2 plants, a state agricultural
permit for 3 or more, and a separate permit requirement for
transporting or selling self-harvested stock), with permit misuse
classified as a misdemeanor. Nursery-propagated stock is unaffected.
This is a legal/regulatory constraint rather than a food-safety one,
but is flagged here since it governs real-world availability of this
crop (Finding 7).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** roots and half-buried stems dug (a single, destructive
  harvest per plant after a roughly 30-year growth cycle) → peeled,
  washed, chopped, and grated or pounded → rinsed and strained
  repeatedly (toxic wash water discarded) → starch settled and
  drained → paste fermented for several days → sun-dried into a
  powdery flour, used in a traditional gruel, bread, biscuits, and
  pudding. Separately: seed (coat removed by hand, float-tested) sown
  in tall nursery tubes, or mature plants divided, for ornamental
  landscape propagation.
- Not covered by this research: how wash water was historically
  disposed of, or how long the cycasin toxin persists in soil or
  water once discarded; effects of the wash water on fish or wildlife.

## Open research questions

- The exact historical start date of the Florida starch-mill industry
  is given differently across sources (variously the 1830s, 1845, or
  the 1880s) — not resolved.
- Historical mill daily output figures conflict across sources (10-15,
  15-20, or 18 tons per day) — not resolved.
- Starch content figures conflict sharply: most sources give an 8-20%
  seasonal range, while one informal source claims 38% without a
  documented basis — the higher figure is not used in this entry.
- Reported seed germination time conflicts sharply across sources (about
  6 weeks vs. about a year) — not resolved.
- Whether stem or root cuttings work as a propagation method is
  reported as true by one source and explicitly denied by another —
  not resolved.
- Minor hardiness-zone edge differences exist across sources (zone 8a
  vs. 8B as the cold-tolerance limit) — not treated as a significant
  conflict.
- One dedicated horticultural source contains its own internal
  contradiction: its summary table states no serious pests exist, while
  its body text describes a specific scale insect as potentially fatal
  to the plant — this contradiction is not adjudicated here.
- Whether Bahamian, Cuban, and Cayman Islands populations are truly the
  same species as the Florida population remains a genuine, unresolved
  dispute between two competing taxonomic treatments.
- The genuinely contested mechanism behind the cycad-linked
  neurodegenerative syndrome (a cycasin-derived DNA-damage hypothesis
  vs. a BMAA bioaccumulation hypothesis vs. a third sterol-glycoside
  candidate from a mouse study) remains unresolved in the broader
  scientific literature, not just in this entry.
- Whether washing fully removes protein-bound BMAA (as opposed to free
  BMAA) from prepared flour is a specific, documented point of concern
  not fully resolved by available sources.
- No species-specific genome-size measurement exists for this plant;
  the figures available are at the broader family or genus level only.
- All yield figures, caloric/protein content, water-use parameters,
  labor-hour figures, nutrient-uptake figures, root depth, storage
  duration, seed rate, and hydroponic viability in this entry are
  estimates, not direct measurements — there is essentially no
  sourced agronomic-yield data for this crop at all, since it was
  never cultivated as a field crop.
- What happened to the toxic wash water historically, and how long the
  toxin persists in soil or water, or affects fish and wildlife, were
  not established in this research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield figures (worldAvg roughly 30,000 kg/ha, good 60,000 kg/ha,
exceptional roughly 87,000 kg/ha, poor 8,000 kg/ha) are presented per
single harvest at the end of a roughly 30-year growth cycle, not per
year, since this crop has no annual yield concept. The exceptional
figure is derived from an assumed planting density at typical
landscape spacing combined with a roughly 5 lb mature-plant weight; all
other tiers are rough estimates, since no field-yield data exists for
this crop at any scale. Starch recovery at the documented roughly 10%
rate would imply roughly 3-9 t of starch per hectare per harvest.

Caloric content (roughly 350 kcal/kg) and protein (roughly 3 g/kg) are
derived specifically for the recovered starch per kilogram of raw root,
at the documented roughly 10% starch-recovery rate — not for the raw
root itself, which is poisonous and not something a person can eat
regardless of its own caloric content.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient values, seasonal water-use
estimate, drought-tolerance rating) are rough estimates based on this
being a documented drought-tolerant evergreen cycad; no standard crop-
water-use reference includes a dedicated figure for this plant.

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are rough, unsourced
analogies to other slow-growing perennial crops; no direct labor-hour
measurement exists for any operation on this crop, since it has not
been cultivated as a field crop in the modern era.

Nutrient-uptake figures, root depth, and storage duration are
similarly unsourced estimates based on general knowledge of
slow-growing perennials adapted to poor soils; no field measurement
exists.
