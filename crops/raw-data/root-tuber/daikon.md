# Daikon (*Raphanus sativus* var./subsp. *longipinnatus*)

**Category:** Root/Tuber · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/root-tuber/daikon.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-28

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

*Raphanus sativus* var./subsp. *longipinnatus*, the same species as
common radish — a different cultivar group grown for a much larger,
longer-season root. This project groups daikon with common radish as
the same underlying species/breeding system, while modeling the
distinct pickled/dried products of this cultivar group (described
below) separately, since the parent cultivar group has no equivalent
chain for them. This entry models a generic long-white daikon; the
named cultivars below (Sakurajima, Nerima, watermelon radish, Korean
mu) are documented as cultivar variations, not treated as separate
crops, since none differs in anything beyond size, color, or regional
pickling identity.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a notably longer growing cycle than common radish.**
Daikon reaches maturity in roughly 40-70 days depending on variety
(one source gives 40-45 days as typical, up to 70 for some varieties;
another gives 60 days for one specific variety) [1][2] — much longer
than common radish's typical 20-35 day cycle. If moisture is abundant,
the root can grow quickly; otherwise the flesh becomes tough and
pungent [3] — the same irregular-watering sensitivity documented for
the parent species generally.

**Finding 2 — substantial cultivar diversity, including one giant-
rooted type with its own genome sequencing study.** Named cultivar
forms include a very large Japanese type, a Chinese heirloom with a
bright rose/fuchsia interior ("watermelon radish"), a shorter, sturdier
Korean type with pale green shoulders ("mu"), and a Tokyo heirloom [3].
The giant-rooted Japanese type (roots often exceeding 20 kg) was
specifically sequenced in a 2020 genome study for its giant-root
trait (haploid genome estimated at roughly 498.5 Mb by k-mer analysis,
highly heterozygous), grown mainly in one volcanic-ash-soil region
credited with suiting the crop; the molecular basis of the giant-root
trait itself is described by the study's own authors as "largely
unknown" [4]. The Tokyo heirloom type was first documented in a
17th-century geography book; its roots reach 60-70 cm and are famously
difficult to pull, underlying an annual pulling contest. Production of
this heirloom type declined after a mid-20th-century peak, attributed
to disease, natural disasters, urbanization of farmland, the pulling
difficulty itself, and displacement by a commercially dominant F1
hybrid type; some contracted growers still hang-dry this heirloom type
after a late-autumn harvest for a specific traditional pickled product
[5].

**Finding 3 — composition and fresh uses.** Raw daikon is reported at
about 18 kcal, 0.6 g protein, 0.1 g fat, and 4.1 g carbohydrate (1.6 g
fiber, 2.5 g sugar) per 100 g, with modest vitamin C, potassium, and
calcium content, drawn from secondary nutrition sites reproducing a
government food-composition database entry not independently verified
in this pass [6]. It is eaten raw (in salads or as a sashimi
accompaniment) or cooked, and grated fresh into a traditional
condiment with no documented shelf life — treated here as same-day use
only.

**Finding 4 — a traditional sun-dried and pickled product with
unresolved salt ratios.** This product is made by sun-drying the
whole root for one to two weeks until dehydrated but still flexible,
then packing it in a pickling crock with salt, a seaweed, and rice
bran; optional flavor and color additions include dried citrus or
persimmon peel and sake lees [7]. Pickling takes about one month; a
shorter ferment gives a paler cream color instead of the traditional
bright yellow, which actually comes from turmeric, a specific fruit, or
safflower (mass-produced versions use food coloring instead) [7]. A
quick modern shortcut substitutes salt, sugar, water, and a pinch of
turmeric for the long traditional ferment. No source opened in this
research pass gives a specific salt-to-rice-bran ratio — this remains
an open question. This product is traditionally credited to a 16th-
century Japanese Zen Buddhist monk; a parallel Korean pickled daikon
product served with rice rolls and noodle dishes is a distinct but
related tradition, not modeled separately here. One source also notes
this product is high in sodium, with a recommendation to consume only
a few slices a day [7].

**Finding 5 — a traditional dried, shredded product with a strong
production-geography fact.** Shredded strips, harvested in late autumn
to winter, are cut and sun-dried; one Japanese prefecture supplies
about 90% of the national total of this product [8]. It is usually made
from two specific daikon types, and the shredding technique itself was
brought to that prefecture from a different region around 1897, with
the finished product reportedly reaching market around 1935 [8]. No
drying duration is given in the source opened this pass. Shelf life is
reported at up to 6 months stored airtight in a cool, dry place, used
within a few weeks once opened; the product is noted to brown in
summer heat and is best refrigerated from spring onward [8]. This
drying tradition dates to a historical famine-food practice, with
distinct regional production styles developing in different areas by
the late 19th century [8].

**Finding 6 — a Korean cubed-radish fermented pickle, documented only
at the recipe level, not as a food-science study.** Across multiple
recipes for this dish, salt amounts vary by grain size and are
generally measured to taste rather than a fixed ratio; a common
room-temperature ferment is 1-3 days (climate-dependent) before
refrigeration, where it continues to ferment slowly and keeps for
several weeks [9]. This entry's confidence in this process remains
lower than for sourced food-science findings, since none of the recipe
sources checked is a food-science reference.

**Finding 7 — seed production and pollination mirror the parent
species.** The crop is self-incompatible and insect-cross-pollinated
for seed production, using the same breeding-system mechanism
documented for common radish generally (a specific cytoplasmic male-
sterility system first identified in a Japanese radish line is now used
across the broader mustard-family crop group as well, though this
project treats that mechanism as general background rather than
daikon-specific). Documented seed count runs roughly 23,400-56,000
seeds per pound (averaging about 38,600), or roughly 12 g per 1,000
seeds — distinctly larger seed than other mustard-family root crops
this project has researched. A documented seeding rate is about 7
lb/acre in 18-inch rows, converting to roughly 7.8 kg/ha.

**Finding 8 — growing conditions.** Preferred soil pH is 5.8-6.8; deep,
loose soil worked to at least 18 inches is recommended, since forking
of the root ruins its marketable shape; preferred temperature is
roughly 50-65°F; a documented full-season water requirement under one
specific regional growing condition is about 12-15 inches.

**Finding 9 — storage data reveals a real conflict between long farm
storage and short retail handling, now resolved in favor of the
farm-storage figure.** Two independent sources agree on long farm or
root-cellar storage: one source gives about four months at 32-34°F and
95-100% relative humidity, attributed to a government agricultural
source; a university postharvest-technology reference independently
gives 3-4 months at the same storage regime, specifically for this
daikon-type radish [10][11]. A third, commercial produce-distribution
guide gives a much shorter 7-10 days at a similar temperature and
humidity range — on examination, this shorter figure describes a
retail short-term handling window, not the crop's actual achievable
storage life, so this entry treats the long figure as the anchor
value. The same university source notes that freeze injury is common
in this crop because its ideal storage temperature sits very close to
its own freezing point. Low humidity causes moisture loss, and
ethylene exposure yellows the tops (relevant mainly to bunched, leafy
sale rather than root storage) [10].

**Finding 10 — bolting follows the same mechanism as the parent
species.** No daikon-specific bolting trigger, threshold, or cultivar-
resistance data distinct from the parent species was found. This crop
shares its genus and species with common radish, which this project
has separately documented as bolting under long days combined with
high temperature, with some cultivars additionally requiring cold
exposure as seedlings first, and oriental types specifically bolting
on long days alone. The open question is only whether this cultivar
group's longer growing season shifts the practical timing of this
risk, not whether a different mechanism is involved.

**Finding 11 — this crop's own seed, sprouted, is a documented
foodborne-illness vehicle, not merely an unmodeled curiosity.** A
well-known 1996 Japanese *E. coli* O157:H7 outbreak, traced to
radish sprouts, actually involved sprouted seed of this specific
cultivar group (a Japanese term for daikon sprouts), not common
radish seed. This is a real, documented hazard distinct from the
general raw-root food status of this crop.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season root crop; some heat-tolerant
  types exist in warmer-climate traditions (Finding 1; climate zone
  detail below).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of the root; a leaf pick is a
  minor byproduct, not a repeat-cut regime.
- **Climate:** Cool temperate, temperate, and (for heat-tolerant types)
  subtropical zones; preferred temperature roughly 50-65°F (Finding 1,
  8).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, commonly eaten raw in salads or as a
  sashimi accompaniment, or cooked, or grated fresh (Finding 3).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  self-incompatible and insect-pollinated (Finding 7).
- **Yield / composition:** A documented yield anchor of roughly 15-20
  tons/acre (converting to roughly 34-45 t/ha) is independently
  corroborated by a national-level yield derivation from historical
  Japanese agricultural statistics, landing within the same range (see
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Low drought tolerance — inadequate moisture makes the
  root tough, pithy, and pungent rather than mild (Finding 1, 8).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded, with deeper spacing and sowing
  depth than smaller radish types, to accommodate the larger root; no
  daikon-specific sowing-depth source was found in this research pass.
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for any operation on
  this crop; estimates used here are rough analogies to other root
  crops in the same broader family.
- **Soil:** Preferred pH 5.8-6.8; deep, loose soil to at least 18
  inches is recommended to prevent root forking (Finding 8).
- **Stand life:** Annual, single-harvest root crop; the seed crop is a
  separate bolting-dependent cycle.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not independently sourced for this crop in
  this research pass; the general pest and disease profile of the
  broader mustard-family root-crop group (flea beetles, cabbage root
  maggot, clubroot, scab, black root, downy mildew) is assumed to apply
  by analogy, since this is the same species as other crops in that
  group.
- **Cultivars:** Several named forms exist with real differences in
  size, color, and regional pickling identity (Finding 2; cultivar
  detail below).
- **Harvest:** Single harvest of the root, generally timed to the
  40-70 day maturity window depending on variety (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Long farm/cellar storage of roughly 3-4 months at
  32-34°F and 95-100% relative humidity is well documented and
  independently corroborated across two sources; the crop's ideal
  storage temperature sits very close to its own freezing point, so
  freeze injury is a genuine risk (Finding 9).

## Hazards

Not independently sourced for pests and diseases in this research
pass; the general profile of the broader mustard-family root-crop
group is assumed to apply by analogy, since this is the same species.
Whole-plant and residue material carries the same genus-wide pungent-
compound (glucosinolate) chemistry as other pungent mustard-family
crops; no crop-specific livestock-feeding incident was found.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — sprouted seed of this cultivar group (a
Japanese-named sprout product) was the documented vehicle of a
well-known 1996 Japanese *E. coli* O157:H7 foodborne-illness outbreak.
This is a real, documented pathogen-contamination risk specific to
raw sprouted seed of this crop, deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 11).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop shares the same genus-wide pungent
mustard-oil compound (allyl isothiocyanate) chemistry found in
horseradish, wasabi, and other mustard-family root crops, at ordinary
food-pungency levels consistent with normal condiment use. Deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed sown directly → fresh root (40-70 days) → trimmed
  → eaten raw or cooked, or grated into a fresh condiment.
  Alternatively: fresh root → trimmed → sun-dried 1-2 weeks until
  flexible → pickled with salt, seaweed, and rice bran, about 1 month
  → a traditional yellow pickled product. Alternatively: fresh root →
  trimmed → shredded → fully sun-dried → a shelf-stable dried strip
  product. Alternatively: fresh root → trimmed → diced → fermented
  with a seasoning paste, 1-3 days then refrigerated → a Korean cubed
  fermented pickle. Separately: seed sown directly as sprouts → eaten
  raw (a documented pathogen hazard, see above). A separate seed crop
  (bee-pollinated, isolated) produces seed for the next planting.
- Not covered by this research: a cover-crop/soil-conditioning use of
  a related long-rooted type; several other regional pickle variants
  named in general references but not researched in process detail;
  the specific drying duration for the dried-strip product (conflicting
  figures exist and are not resolved here); the molecular basis of the
  giant-root cultivar trait itself.

## Open research questions

- No source opened in this research pass gives a specific salt-to-
  rice-bran ratio for the traditional sun-dried pickled product; an
  often-cited figure elsewhere is not well-sourced enough to state as
  fact here.
- The drying duration for the dried-strip product is given very
  differently across sources (a range of a few days to several weeks
  in one source, no duration at all in a more authoritative production-
  geography source) — not resolved.
- The seed-to-yield multiplication ratio used here is carried forward
  from an analogy to common radish's figure, which that crop's own
  research found and fixed as a likely unit error (food yield divided
  by seed rate, rather than seed-crop yield divided by seed rate); this
  crop's own seed-crop yield was not independently sourced, so the
  same likely error is flagged here rather than silently corrected.
- Whether this cultivar group's longer growing season shifts the
  practical timing of bolting risk relative to shorter-season radish
  types was not established in this research pass.
- No religious or ritual use specific to this crop was found, unlike a
  documented festival tradition specific to common radish elsewhere.
- Environment-envelope details (salinity tolerance, light requirements,
  controlled-environment trial data) were not independently researched
  for this crop; figures used elsewhere for common radish are the
  nearest analogy but were not re-verified for this specific cultivar
  group.
- A national-level yield-derivation cross-check used in this entry is
  based on a historical national radish total, not a cultivar-group-
  specific figure, so it is treated as a reasonable but imperfect
  proxy.

## Estimates and derivations

A genome-size estimate of roughly 0.52 pg (1C) is derived from a
documented primary genome assembly size (roughly 504.5 Mb) for the
giant-rooted Japanese cultivar, converted using the standard base-
pairs-per-picogram conversion factor; this is reasonably close to a
separately documented genome-size figure for a different daikon
cultivar, consistent with same-species expectations, though it reflects
one cultivar's breeding history (a landrace/heirloom with notably high
heterozygosity) rather than a daikon-wide genome trait.

A storage-duration estimate of 105 days is derived as the midpoint of
two independently sourced figures (roughly 4 months from one source,
3-4 months from a university postharvest reference), both describing
the same cold, high-humidity storage regime.

A yield cross-check derives roughly 36-37 t/ha from historical
Japanese national agricultural statistics (a documented national
radish-growing area and production total for a specific year), landing
within the independently sourced 34-45 t/ha yield-anchor band used
elsewhere in this entry — treated as a useful cross-check rather than
a replacement for the sourced anchor, since the national figure covers
radish generally rather than this cultivar group specifically (though
radish production in the source country is overwhelmingly of this
cultivar group).

Labor-hour estimates across all operations are rough, unsourced
analogies to other root crops in the same broader family; no direct
labor-hour measurement was found for this crop specifically.

Catalogue/product shelf-life estimates: a fresh grated condiment is
treated as same-day use (no sourced figure); the traditional sun-dried
pickled product is estimated at roughly 180 days as a long-keeping
preserved product by nature (not directly sourced); the dried-strip
product is documented at up to 6 months airtight, cool and dry; the
Korean fermented pickle keeps several weeks refrigerated per recipe
sources; sprout shelf life is estimated by analogy to other sprouted
seed products, not independently sourced for this crop.
