# Konjac (*Amorphophallus konjac*)

## Naming and scope

*Amorphophallus konjac* K.Koch, arum family (Araceae), diploid with 26
chromosomes; Japanese konnyaku, Chinese moyu ("demon taro"), also
called devil's tongue, voodoo lily, snake palm, and elephant yam [1].
This genus contains several other cultivated species that are kept as
separate crop entries in this project: the elephant foot yam, which
this project has already researched, plus two further species — one
called porang and one simply referenced by its scientific epithet —
that this project treats as their own rows, since all of them share
some of the same gum-flour commodity chain described below. China
alone is documented as growing nine species of this genus, and
Chinese national crop statistics are known to mix *A. konjac* with at
least one other closely related species, so any China-wide area or
production figure below should be read as covering more than this one
species.

The crop is distinctive enough among root and tuber crops to merit
its own entry: a three-year lift-and-replant cycle with indoor winter
storage of seed corms, a strong shade response, acrid raw corms that
require alkali processing, and a gum product (a glucomannan flour)
unlike any starch crop this project has documented. This entry is
grounded against a closely related aroid root crop this project has
also researched (for a water-use analogy and a shared calcium-oxalate
hazard), and against another already-migrated shade-grown root crop
of this collection for formatting precedent.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — commercial production is a three-year crop, lifted
every autumn and stored indoors every winter.** The typical Japanese
cycle runs: year one, small cormels (8-15 g) are planted in spring
and dug in autumn as "1-year" corms, too small to sell and entirely
replanted; year two, the corms are dug again, with some shipped and
most kept as seed stock; year three, corms are dug and shipped to
processors, with national statistics counting the harvest cycle at
about three years [4][5][6]. Some Fukushima-region growers keep corms
to five years to reach 4 kg corms used in farm-made product [16].
Every autumn the crop is dug, cormels sorted from corms, pre-dried,
and stored indoors until the following May [5][11]. As a result, most
of the planted area in any given year is actually raising planting
stock rather than a sellable crop: in 2023, Japan had 3,230 hectares
planted but only 1,950 hectares harvested for sale, the remainder
being seed-corm production [4]. Plants flower only from roughly 3-5
years of age, and commercial corms are always lifted well before
then, so true seed plays essentially no role in commercial
propagation [19]. A Fukushima field trial of leaving seed corms in
the ground over winter under polythene film found soil running
4-5°C warmer and over 97% survival after two full growing seasons,
saving labor but depending on weather, and documented only on warm,
south-facing slopes in a traditional unassisted form of the practice
[10].

**Finding 2 — yields: a national average around 26 t/ha of harvested
corms, with trial yields reaching 40-63 t/ha.** Japan's 2023
harvested-area average was about 26.1 t/ha, with a long-run national
range of 13.0-29.9 t/ha from 1970 to 2023 and one regional high of
about 34.8 t/ha in a single year [4][7]. One prefecture alone accounts
for roughly 96% of the national harvest [12]. A regional yield target
for an improved cultivar runs 37-40 t/ha [5]. Multi-year variety
trials found 2-year corms yielding roughly 21-33 t/ha depending on
cultivar, 3-year corms 42-63 t/ha, and 1-year corms grown from
cormels only 11-18 t/ha [8]. A separate multi-year storage trial found
15-26 t/ha [11]. A small New Zealand trial reported 11 t/ha in year
one and 30 t/ha in year two [3]. One Chinese regional figure under
walnut intercropping gives about 20.6 t/ha, though its measurement
basis is unclear [12]. A useful season-over-season "enlargement
ratio" — corm weight out divided by seed-corm weight planted — runs
roughly 3.3-6.0 depending on region, cultivar, and trial [8][9][11][18].

**Finding 3 — shade substantially raises yield.** A New Zealand trial
found that 50% shade cloth gave 66% more corm weight than full sun
(105 g seed corms grown out to 2,397 g under shade versus 1,442 g in
full sun, with the largest shaded corms nearing 2 kg); very small 13 g
offsets, by contrast, showed no shade response at all [3]. A review
source states that 50-70% shading is generally beneficial for this
genus [2]. Japanese growers prefer partial shade and shelter from
wind, and some spread straw over the ridges for this purpose [6][16].
A related cultivated species in the same genus showed a smaller but
still substantial 27.9% yield gain under 70% shade in a Chilean trial,
and one Chinese growing region grows the crop under walnut trees,
presumably for the same reason [12].

**Finding 4 — corms are acrid raw and are always processed with
alkali.** All parts of the plant are toxic raw, the corm most of all,
due to needle-shaped calcium oxalate crystal bundles reaching up to
650 micrometers [1][2]. Handling raw corms is documented to irritate
skin in folk and processing practice. The crop was domesticated in
southwest China roughly 2,000 years ago and has always been processed
by repeated boiling and alkaline ash-water treatment; it appears in
an early Chinese herbal pharmacopoeia, with cultivation recorded under
a dynasty roughly 1,700 years ago, and it has been eaten in Japan
since roughly the 6th century, originally as a medicinal food [1].
Soluble oxalate content falls sharply after processing, from over
130 mg per 100 g in raw powder to below 70 mg per 100 g [19].

**Finding 5 — processing runs from dried chips, through refined
flour, to the final gel product.** Washed corms are sliced and dried
into chips, which factories then sort and finish in a heated dryer;
the dried chips are pounded into flour, and wind-sifting separates
the hard glucomannan-rich particles (the refined flour) from a
lighter, starchy dust byproduct that carries almost no glucomannan and
is used instead as a soil amendment [8][13][14]. The final gel product
is made by stirring flour into water, letting it swell, kneading it
with a calcium-based alkali, molding it, and then boiling to remove
the alkali [13]. Processing ratios vary by cultivar and corm size, and
these ratios set the cultivar's trading price: across trials, dried
chips run roughly 16-19% of fresh corm weight, and refined flour runs
roughly 10-12% of fresh corm weight [8][9]. One industrial recipe
documents commercial gel product at about 3% flour by weight, with a
practical upper limit around 6-8%, set with a dilute calcium hydroxide
solution, a standing period, and a boiling-bath cure held overnight;
a frozen-and-thawed form of the gel is also a traditional product
[15]. A historical flour-milling process invented in the late 18th
century made year-round production of the gel possible for the first
time, and one Japanese prefecture alone makes over 90% of the
country's output [1]. A thin noodle form is made by pushing the
paste into hot limewater [1].

**Finding 6 — fresh-corm product is a distinct farm-made recipe.**
Trial work on fresh-corm product documents the key steps as grating
(almost always in a blender), standing to let the mixture gel,
adding alkali, and then molding and leaching out the alkali; pre-
boiling quartered corms for a few minutes before grating gives a
whiter, better-looking final product, and blending with hot water
above 80°C rather than cold water is much easier to work and avoids
scorching [17]. The ratio of water to corm varies by cultivar, and at
least one high-yielding cultivar makes a final product considered too
soft to be commercially useful [17]. A home-recipe version documents
peeling, cubing, blending with water, resting, kneading in a dissolved
alkali, then boiling and cooling [16]. Fresh-corm product carries
roughly 8 kcal per 100 g against about 5 kcal per 100 g for the
flour-based product [21].

**Finding 7 — seed-corm storage: traditionally a loft space over the
hearth fire, now more often a dehumidified shed.** Corms are dug on a
dry day and pre-dried for roughly 20-25 days in a plastic house, kept
at or below 30°C by day and at or above 10°C by night, until 10-20%
of their weight is lost; rod-shaped cormels of certain cultivars need
this step most [5][8]. After pre-drying, corms are stored at roughly
7-10°C and 70-80% relative humidity until shortly before sprouting,
then warmed slightly and held at higher humidity; cormels are kept
separately and somewhat warmer than seed corms [5]. Traditional
storage used the warm space directly above an indoor hearth fire, and
some farms still use wood-stove smoke specifically to deter mold; one
regional storage method buries seed corms in a pit inside a plastic
house, under soil and straw, to keep them from freezing, and the
regional spread of the crop in that area is tied historically to the
adoption of this method [11][12][16]. A multi-year dehumidified-
storage trial found no fatal cold injury even at 2°C for 120 days,
though weight loss was greatest at that temperature; corm moisture
stayed around 81% throughout storage, the crop's characteristic gum
content fell sharply once buds began moving in spring, and rot losses
ran only 1-2% at around 70% relative humidity, judged close to ideal;
one dehumidifier was found to serve roughly 5 tonnes of seed corms
[11].

**Finding 8 — disease pressure makes crop rotation effectively
compulsory.** Documented Japanese disease pressures include root rot
and dry rot (both requiring rotation to manage), a fungal leaf blight,
a bacterial rot, and aphids; seed corms are routinely dusted or
sprayed against dry rot, and some growers avoid replanting the same
field with this crop for five years or more [5][16]. A fungal
southern blight is also documented [16]. A regional survey found high
rates of disease in seed corms (dry rot, rot, and nematode damage)
and set a seed-corm quality standard of at least 50 g weight with no
more than 10% visible damage [18]. In China, a bacterial soft rot is
the dominant disease across several major growing provinces, with
reported losses ranging from 20-30% up to as much as 80-100% in some
sources, worst under continuous cropping and during storage [27].

**Finding 9 — field practice.** Preferred soil is organic, well-
drained loam, slightly sandy, at a pH of roughly 5.5-6.0 [5][16]. A
two-year crop's fertilizer program documented here runs roughly
15 kg/10a nitrogen, 10 kg/10a phosphate, and a split potassium
application; a separate trial found a specific economic nitrogen
optimum close to 100 kg/ha for a two-year crop of one cultivar, while
a New Zealand trial found that lime and potassium raised yield but
extra nitrogen (as urea) cut it [3][5][9]. Planting happens after a
threshold date once mean soil temperature reaches about 10°C, with
corms covered to roughly twice their own height and later earthed up
with a top-dressing; cormels are typically planted in two staggered
lines per ridge and seed corms in a single line, with documented row
and in-row spacing varying somewhat by region and crop age [5][9][18].
A detailed single-site timeline documents roughly 160 days from
planting to full leaf senescence, broken into an underground
sprouting period, a leaf-opening period, and then a long bulking
period before the leaves yellow and fall [8]. Straw mulch is spread
thickly after emergence and growers report little further care is
needed until harvest, which happens once about 80% of plants have
yellowed and fallen, typically mid-to-late autumn; digging too early
makes daughter corms hard to detach [5][16].

**Finding 10 — a gum crop, not a starch crop.** The corm's dry matter
is dominated by a gum called glucomannan — one source gives roughly
49-60%, another roughly 40% of the dried corm — alongside starch at
roughly 10-30%, protein at roughly 5-14%, soluble sugars at 3-5%, and
ash at roughly 3.4-5.3%, with corm moisture itself around 81% [1][2][11].
The glucomannan itself is a large, variable-length molecule (roughly
200-2,000 kilodaltons) with a glucose-to-mannose ratio of about
1:1.6-1.7, and it is used industrially as a food additive [2]. The
flour-based gel product and the fresh-corm gel product both carry
very low calorie counts (roughly 5 and 8 kcal per 100 g respectively)
and are mostly water and fiber by weight [21]. Beyond food use, this
gum is documented as a gelling agent, a vegan substitute for gelatin
and for certain seafood textures, a material for absorbent sponges, a
colon-targeted drug-delivery material, and a strengthening additive
for traditional paper, with oral supplement doses in trials ranging
from under 1 g to 15 g per day [1][2].

**Finding 11 — named cultivars differ meaningfully in yield,
disease resistance, cormel shape, and processing quality.** One late-
maturing bred cultivar is documented as notably more resistant to
leaf blight and root rot than older local types, though weaker to dry
rot, and gives the best chip and flour yields among the cultivars
compared, but produces mostly rod-shaped cormels that need pre-drying
[8]. A second bred cultivar emerges and opens its leaf somewhat later,
yields roughly 6% more corm, shows a notably higher season-over-season
enlargement ratio, produces mostly round (machine-plantable) cormels,
gives a higher flour yield, and makes a firmer final product, though
it is slightly weaker against leaf blight and some first-year plants
go dormant unexpectedly [5][9][17]. A third bred cultivar is high-
yielding and disease-resistant and was, by the late 1990s, the most
widely grown cultivar in one major growing prefecture, but it produces
rod-shaped cormels and a notably softer final product [8][9][17].
Traditional local (non-bred) types are documented as having good
processing quality and strong characteristic stickiness, but are
disease-prone and have mostly disappeared from commercial production
nationally, though some regions still grow them for the fresh-corm
product specifically [8][16][17]. A further named bred cultivar showed
a lower-than-expected enlargement ratio in one regional survey [18].

**Finding 12 — history and markets.** In China, this crop is made into
a tofu-style dish and a vegetarian substitute for tripe, and the
country is documented as producing roughly 60% of world output, though
area figures across sources conflict substantially (one source gives
roughly 174,000 hectares, another roughly 100,000), likely reflecting
the inclusion of other species of the same genus in national statistics
[1][27]. In Japan, the crop appears in simmered dishes and the gel
product is made from flour, water, and alkali, sometimes with a
seaweed addition, and also shaped into thin noodles [1]. Documented
Japanese trade prices show a wide spread between fresh corms and
refined flour, reflecting the substantial value added by processing
[7]. A well-documented food-safety hazard involves small cup-shaped
jelly confections made with this gum, which caused choking deaths and
led to a US regulatory warning, a European Union suspension and then
permanent ban, and multiple deaths recorded in Japan over roughly a
decade [1][22].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season perennial corm crop grown on
  a three-year (occasionally extended to five-year) lift-and-replant
  cycle; planted in late spring once soil reaches about 10°C, dug
  every autumn (Finding 1, 9).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single annual dig repeated across the multi-
  year cycle; most of the planted area in any season is producing
  replanting stock rather than a sellable crop (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Warm-temperate to subtropical highland zones, frost-
  intolerant, documented across a wide elevation range; prefers
  shelter from wind (Finding 3; climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Never eaten raw; always alkali-processed before
  consumption (Finding 4).
- **Pollination:** Not relevant to production — commercial corms are
  always lifted well before the crop reaches flowering age, which
  only begins at roughly 3-5 years (Finding 1).
- **Yield / composition:** A gum (glucomannan) crop rather than a
  starch crop, with national average yields around 26 t/ha and trial
  yields reaching 40-63 t/ha depending on corm age and cultivar
  (Finding 2, 10; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Needs steady moisture while its single leaf is open
  in summer, but the corm rots readily in wet, poorly drained ground;
  grown rain-fed without irrigation in the sources used here (water-
  use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated entirely vegetatively from small
  cormels or larger seed corms, planted on ridges and covered to
  roughly twice corm height, with straw mulch applied after emergence
  (Finding 1, 9).
- **Labor:** Harvest (digging, sorting corms from cormels) and post-
  harvest handling (pre-drying, storage tending) together make up the
  largest share of documented per-hectare labor hours; round-shaped
  cormels allow machine planting while rod-shaped ones require hand
  work (Finding 1, 5, 9; labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Prefers organic, well-drained, slightly sandy loam at a
  pH of roughly 5.0-7.0; crop rotation away from this crop for roughly
  five years is standard practice to manage soil-borne disease
  (Finding 8, 9).
- **Stand life:** A multi-year (typically three-year, sometimes
  extended to five-year) lift-and-replant cycle rather than a single-
  season crop (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Root rot, dry rot, a fungal leaf blight,
  a bacterial rot, a fungal southern blight, aphids, and nematode
  damage to seed corms; a separate bacterial soft rot is the dominant
  disease in major Chinese growing regions, with losses reaching as
  high as 80-100% in the worst cases (Finding 8; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Several named bred cultivars with documented
  differences in yield, disease resistance, cormel shape (round versus
  rod-shaped, which determines whether machine planting is possible),
  and processing quality, alongside disappearing traditional local
  types still valued for fresh-corm product (Finding 11).
- **Harvest:** Dug by hand or by a lifting implement once most plants
  have yellowed and died back, then hand-sorted by corm age and size
  (Finding 1, 9).
- **Storage:** Seed corms are pre-dried for roughly three weeks after
  digging, then held cool, dry, and frost-free until the following
  spring; dried chips and milled flour keep for years (Finding 7).

Climate detail: documented across a wide elevation range from near
sea level to over 2,000 m, with a preferred growing-season
temperature range in the 20-30°C band; frost kills both foliage and
unprotected corms, so corms are always dug and brought under cover
before frost arrives.

Water-use detail: no official crop-coefficient curve exists for this
species; one is estimated here by analogy to the closely related
aroid root crop this entry is grounded against, which has a similar
leafy summer canopy and underground storage organ, adjusted for this
crop's lower tolerance of wet soil (that related crop tolerates
flooding; this one does not) and for a somewhat shorter below-ground
sprouting period derived from one site's detailed field timeline. The
crop is considered to have generally low drought tolerance and low
waterlogging tolerance, the latter a direct consequence of its strong
preference for free-draining soil.

Labor detail: a national production-cost survey's two regional total
per-hectare labor figures were split by operation using the general
structure of harvest and post-harvest handling as the two largest
components, followed by planting, weeding and mulching, fertilizing
and hilling, and spraying; no detailed operation-level breakdown of
that survey itself was obtained in this pass.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw corms and other plant parts contain needle-
shaped calcium oxalate crystal bundles and are acrid and irritating,
particularly to the skin and mouth; the crop is always alkali-
processed before eating. This is a hazard shared with other crops in
the same plant family that this project has also researched. Deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 4).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — small cup-shaped jelly confections made from
this crop's gum, and dry glucomannan supplement tablets taken without
enough fluid, have both caused documented choking and intestinal-
obstruction deaths; regulators in multiple jurisdictions have warned
against or banned the jelly-cup product specifically. Deferred until
this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding
12).

Disease and pest pressure includes root rot, dry rot, a fungal leaf
blight, a bacterial rot, a fungal southern blight, aphids, and
nematode damage to seed corms, with crop rotation away from this crop
for roughly five years used to manage the worst of these; a separate
bacterial soft rot dominates in major Chinese growing regions, with
losses reaching as high as 80-100% in the worst documented cases
(Finding 8).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** cormels → year-one corms → year-two corms → year-three
  corms, each dug every autumn, pre-dried, stored indoors over winter
  and replanted each spring. Alternatively: seed corms left in the
  ground over winter under a protective film, in regions and seasons
  where this is practical. Mature corms → washed and sliced → dried
  into chips → pounded and wind-sifted → refined flour, with a lighter
  starchy dust byproduct returned to the soil as an amendment. Flour
  plus an alkali solution → the shaped gel product or thin noodles.
  Fresh corms plus alkali → a separate farm-made fresh-corm gel
  product, often made from larger, older corms.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** the frozen-and-thawed
  traditional product form; small jelly-cup confectionery (banned in
  the European Union); purified gum extraction for sponges, films, or
  drug-delivery uses; seed-corm disease-control dusting in detail.

A Chinese Buddhist vegetarian cooking tradition uses this crop's
processed gel as a substitute for tripe. A tradition in Jainism
avoids root vegetables and other underground storage organs
generally, a note this project has also recorded for other root
crops. An unverified claim that the gel product is a staple of
Japanese Buddhist temple cuisine was not confirmed against a primary
source in this pass.

## Open research questions

- Chinese production-area figures conflict substantially between two
  sources (roughly 174,000 hectares versus roughly 100,000 hectares),
  likely reflecting inclusion of other species of the same genus in
  national statistics — not resolved here.
- Glucomannan content of the dried corm conflicts between sources
  (roughly 40% versus 49-60%), with the higher figure's measurement
  basis unclear.
- Documented Chinese soft-rot loss figures vary widely across sources
  (20-30% in one source, up to 100% or total crop failure in others,
  30-70% in yet another) — not reconciled here.
- One detailed regional cultivar-trial table was extracted from a
  partially garbled source table; its yield figures are considered
  reliable but its enlargement-ratio and processing-ratio columns are
  less certain.
- Weakest estimates overall: a worldwide average yield figure (no
  broad Chinese provincial statistics were available to check it
  against); a labor-hour split by operation (only two regional
  per-hectare totals were found, with no operation-level breakdown);
  a fresh-corm calorie estimate (official food-composition tables
  list only the processed gel products, not the raw corm); and the
  estimated water-use curve, which rests on an analogy to an unrelated
  aroid crop rather than a measured study.
- Whether the plant actually skips leafing entirely in its flowering
  year, once it reaches flowering age, was not verified against a
  primary source in this pass.
- A seed-corm multiplication figure derived from one trial's table
  disagreed somewhat with a simple density-times-corm-weight cross-
  check; both are recorded, not reconciled.

## Estimates and derivations

Energy and protein content of the fresh corm (roughly 400 kcal and
15 g protein per kilogram) are derived from documented moisture
content and dry-matter composition shares (starch, the characteristic
gum, and protein), applying a standard calorie factor to starch and
protein and a lower, fiber-appropriate calorie factor to the gum
fraction, since it behaves as a largely non-digestible fermentable
fiber; this figure is explicitly not how the crop is ever eaten, since
it is always alkali-processed first, and no fresh-corm entry exists in
the food-composition tables consulted, only entries for the two
processed gel products.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 20,000 kg/ha, good 30,000 kg/ha, exceptional
60,000 kg/ha, poor 11,000 kg/ha) are derived mainly from the national
harvested-area average as the figure with the most reliable
statistics, adjusted downward somewhat to account for less intensive
practice and heavy disease losses documented in China, which accounts
for the majority of world production.

A dry-to-fresh weight ratio of roughly 5:1 is derived from documented
corm moisture content, cross-checked against documented dried-chip
yield as a fraction of fresh corm weight.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, root
depth, and a soil-moisture depletion allowance) are estimated by
analogy to the closely related aroid root crop this entry is
grounded against, using that crop's established coefficient values
but with stage lengths re-derived from one site's detailed single-
season field timeline (sprouting, leaf opening, bulking, and
senescence); the depletion allowance and days-without-water figure are
adjusted slightly from that analogy crop's values to reflect this
crop's better soil drainage and rain-fed production in the regions
studied.

A seeding-rate estimate of roughly 5,700-13,000 kg/ha is derived from
documented corm yields divided by documented season-over-season
enlargement ratios in one regional cultivar trial, cross-checked
against a simpler calculation of planting density times typical seed-
corm weight; the trial-derived figures and the density cross-check
do not fully agree, and the trial's own enlargement-ratio values were
read from a partially garbled source table.

Labor-hour tiers are derived from two regional per-hectare labor-hour
totals from a national production-cost survey, split across operations
using harvest and post-harvest handling as the two largest shares,
based on general field-practice descriptions; the finer split across
operations and across mechanization tiers is not independently
sourced.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh corm) are derived from the estimated protein content
above (for nitrogen) and from documented ash content assumed to be
mostly potassium, typical of this plant family's corms, cross-checked
against one region's documented fertilizer program relative to its
typical yield.

A conversion figure of roughly 30 kg of finished gel product per
kilogram of flour is derived from one industrial recipe's documented
flour concentration in the finished product, with a firmer, higher-
flour-concentration product implying a correspondingly lower
conversion figure.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 16 g/m² as fresh corm,
roughly 2 g/m² as flour) is derived by dividing a representative
yield figure by the detailed single-site growing-season length
described above; because only roughly a third of the total planted
area is actually harvested for sale in a given year under the
three-year cycle, the true land-use-adjusted figure runs
considerably lower than this per-season figure alone would suggest.

Genome size (roughly 5.7 picograms, 1C) is derived from a published
chromosome-level genome assembly size using a standard genome-size
conversion factor.
