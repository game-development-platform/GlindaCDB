## Naming and scope

*Pachyrhizus erosus* (L.) Urb., family Fabaceae (Leguminosae), diploid
with 22 chromosomes: the Mexican yam bean, jícama, singkamas or
sinkamas (Philippines), bengkuang (Indonesia), sengkuang (Malaysia),
man-kaeo (Thailand), củ đậu (Vietnam), sometimes called "Chinese
turnip" [2][3]. Older synonyms exist, and the genus name is often
misspelled with a double "r" [3].

Several name clashes are worth flagging. In Ecuador and Peru,
"jícama" can also refer to an entirely different plant, yacón
(genus *Smallanthus*, daisy family) [2]. The African yam bean is a
different legume genus altogether. The genus *Pachyrhizus* itself
contains two other cultivated yam beans that are separate species from
this one: the Andean yam bean, ahipa, which this project has already
researched as its own entry, and the Amazonian yam bean, which this
project has researched as two of its own entries — one for the
general type and one for a particularly starchy, cassava-like type
[5]. This entry is for *P. erosus* only.

Botanically this is a legume, but it is grouped here with the other
root and tuber crops because the swollen storage root is the crop
product, not the seed or pod. Two informal market/cultivar types
exist — a very watery, translucent-juiced type and a drier,
milkier-juiced type — but they share the same growing practice and
uses, so they are treated here as cultivar notes rather than separate
crops.

This entry is grounded against a closely related warm-season vine
crop grown for a chilling-sensitive storage root, which this project
has also researched but not yet migrated into this collection.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — removing the flowers roughly doubles the root crop.**
Pods and roots compete for the plant's sugars, so commercial growers
strip the flowers. In a Benin trial across 14 accessions with no
other inputs, pruning lifted yield from 23.4 to 44.6 t/ha (about
1.9×), and the fraction of dry matter going to the harvested root
rose from 17.6% to 50.3% [5]. A Borneo trial found that removing
flowers every 3-15 days raised root weight per plant from 481 g
unpruned to 744-1,000 g pruned, with a 7-day interval performing best
(root weight per plant rising to about 1,000 g and harvest index
rising from 8% to 46%) [8]. Other Benin accessions showed yield
multipliers of 1.5-5× depending on accession and harvest date (cited
secondhand in [5]). Mexican commercial practice removes flowers and
young pods weekly from first bloom onward, over a roughly 80-226 day
window after planting [6][7]. A broader regional reference describes
removing fertile shoots one to three times during flowering [3].
Pruning forecloses the young-pod and seed products, so a grower must
choose between a pruned-root path, an unpruned young-pod path, and a
seed-crop path.

**Finding 2 — only the root is safe to eat: a toxic insecticidal
compound (rotenone) concentrates in the seed and mature pods.** Seed
carries roughly 0.5-1.0% rotenone plus a similar amount of related
compounds, or about 1% of seed weight by an independent estimate
[3][5]. Foliage carries under 0.01% rotenone, and the roots carry
none [3]. Young, unformed pods are eaten cooked in parts of Southeast
Asia, but mature pods and seed are not [2][3]. Documented poisoning
cases include a fatal case in Thailand (death within two hours of
eating a seed meal, with measurable rotenone in the blood), outbreaks
of a dangerous metabolic acid imbalance in Taiwan, and one case where
cooked seed still contained a very high rotenone level, showing that
cooking does not make the seed safe [15]. Ground seed is deliberately
used as an insecticide and fish poison [3]. The general hazard shape —
the eaten organ is safe while the rest of the plant is dangerous — is
well established across sources.

**Finding 3 — a root crop that fixes a meaningful amount of its own
nitrogen.** In flower-pruned trials in central Mexico, nitrogen
fixation measured 175-215 kg/ha depending on cultivar and study, with
70-77% of the plant's nitrogen coming from the air rather than the
soil [6][7]. After the roots are harvested and removed from the
field, the net nitrogen balance — fixation minus the nitrogen exported
in the roots — still runs positive, at roughly 73-81 kg/ha [7]. Crop
residue left behind holds a further 127-150 kg/ha of nitrogen at
3.2-3.5% nitrogen content [6][7]. Commercial growers in Mexico apply
no nitrogen fertilizer at all [3]; one trial site found native
soil bacteria abundant enough that no inoculant was needed, though a
regional reference recommends inoculating new fields [3][6].
Phosphorus, not nitrogen, is the first limiting nutrient, and the
crop's potassium uptake is high — around 241-266 kg K2O/ha at
yields around 100 t/ha of roots [6][7].

**Finding 4 — short days trigger flowering and root swelling; long
days instead push the plant into vigorous vine growth.** This is a
documented short-day plant: flowering and root swelling are triggered
by shortening day length and falling night temperature, with a
preference for around 12-hour days and around 24°C by day [3][4]. A
Caribbean sowing-date study found that root swelling began 4-6 weeks
after sowing regardless of calendar date, but that plants raised
under long days built large vines first and delayed root growth into
an 8-9 month cycle, while short-day-grown plants finished in under
five months [18]. This species is more photoperiod-sensitive than its
Andean relative, ahipa, which is one reason breeders cross the two
together along with the Amazonian yam bean when seeking
photoperiod-neutral types [3][6].

**Finding 5 — a genuinely warm-climate crop with real chilling injury
below about 12.5°C.** Roots develop visible chilling-injury symptoms
within a week at 0°C, one to two weeks at 5°C, and two to three weeks
at 10°C, but stay in excellent condition after more than four weeks
at 12.5°C [9]. A cultivar-specific trial found that two weeks at 5°C
or 10°C caused chilling-driven decay, softening, and water loss,
while roots held at 13°C and 20°C stayed white and crisp [10]. At
10°C specifically, browning starts in the outer flesh after 7-14 days
and spreads inward, with colour change preceding texture loss [11].
Recommended storage temperatures across sources cluster in the
12.5-17.5°C range at 65-80% relative humidity, giving one to four
months of storage depending on the source [2][3][12]. In storage, the
root's starch content falls as it sweetens — down to roughly two-
thirds of its harvest value after two months at 12.5°C, and to about
a third after three months — and weight loss runs about 0.12% per day
at 12.5°C versus 0.21% per day at 22°C [3].

**Finding 6 — field yields run well below measured experimental
potential.** Mexico's 2024 national average was about 29.5 t/ha
across roughly 6,170 hectares, with the leading state reaching
38.2 t/ha [13]. A regional reference gives typical irrigated yields of
70-100 t/ha in Mexico and 40-90 t/ha in Thailand, against 40-60 and
20-40 t/ha respectively under rainfed conditions [3]. Experimental
highs reach 148-160 t/ha, and Mexican research-station trials on
flower-pruned crops reached 101-105 t/ha [3][6][7]. Lower-input
trials fall well short: around 45 t/ha pruned with no other inputs in
Benin, and 10-24 t/ha in Thailand and Sierra Leone (cited secondhand
in [5]). One Indian release reaches 36-40 t/ha in a 120-day cycle
[14]. Market-size roots run 0.2-0.4 kg in Southeast Asia and 0.5-2 kg
in Mexico and the US, with a documented record root at 23 kg [2][3].

**Finding 7 — a very watery root with a starch- and sugar-dominated
dry matter.** Per 100 g, one composition source gives 38 kcal,
0.72 g protein, 8.8 g carbohydrate, 4.9 g fiber and 90 g water, while
a regional reference gives a somewhat higher 47 kcal, 1.0-2.5 g
protein, and 10-17 g carbohydrate — a conflict left open [2][3]. Dry
matter itself ranges from about 7.6% in one Mexican field trial to
13-21% in a West African trial [5][6]. In the West African trial's
unpruned roots, dry matter broke down to roughly 43% starch, 15%
simple sugars (sucrose, fructose, and glucose combined), and 12%
protein [5]. A separate claim in older project material that the
crop's carbohydrate is partly a different fermentable fiber
(inulin, as in some other root crops) was not re-confirmed against a
primary source in this pass and is recorded as an open conflict.

**Finding 8 — products beyond the fresh root.** Young, unformed pods
are eaten as a cooked vegetable in parts of Southeast Asia [2][3].
Dried and sliced roots are milled into flour [2]. Older roots can be
processed into a digestible starch with granules in the 8-35
micrometer range [3]; in one West African trial, starch was washed
out of the root and the leftover pulp roasted into a cassava-style
staple food, a process this project has also documented for cassava
itself, reaching about 91% dry matter in the end product [5]. Seed is
22-30% oil by weight; one trial extracted it after heating the seed
in hot water, and a regional reference notes it can substitute for
cottonseed oil [3][5]. Ground seed meal doubles as an insecticide [3].
Vines serve as high-protein fodder or green manure, and old,
oversized roots go to cattle and pigs [3]. The crop is intercropped
with maize and beans in Mexico, coriander in Thailand, and a
long-podded bean in parts of Indonesia [3]. It has been cultivated
since roughly 1000 BC, with archaeological finds in Peru dated to
roughly 3000 BC, and it was carried from the Americas to the
Philippines and onward across Asia aboard colonial-era trans-Pacific
trade ships in the 17th century [2][3].

**Finding 9 — harvest timing, lifting, and the separate seed crop.**
Mature roots can be left in the ground for up to three months past
maturity under seasonal or irrigated conditions [3]. Harvest is by
hand after a plough or subsoiler loosens the soil [3]; roots crack or
split if lifted shortly after heavy rain or irrigation in hot weather
[3]. A dedicated seed crop, left unpruned, takes 8-10 months: pods are
dried in the open until they split, then shelled by hand because the
seed is fragile, and seed-feeding beetles can destroy up to 70% of
an unprotected seed crop [3]. Mexican breeders select the best roots
grown at normal commercial density under pruning, then replant those
selected roots at lower density specifically to produce seed — a
root-to-seed breeding route broadly similar to how some other root
crops produce their own seed stock.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season annual vine grown for its
  storage root; frost-tender, needing at least about five frost-free
  months for marketable roots and up to nine for very large ones
  (Finding 4, 6).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of the root crop, with an
  optional, separately timed young-pod harvest on unpruned plants and
  a separately timed seed-crop harvest (Finding 1, 9).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, warm-temperate, and semi-arid
  zones, up to roughly 1,400-1,750 m depending on source; a documented
  absolute temperature ceiling near 36°C and an absolute minimum near
  15°C, with frost fatal to the plant (climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible root; the seed and mature pods are
  toxic and must never be eaten (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the root crop, since flowers are
  deliberately removed in production; the plant is self-pollinated
  with a small 2-4% out-crossing rate when left to seed (Finding 1, 9).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable by input level and
  pruning, from roughly 10 to over 100 t/ha of fresh roots; dry matter
  is mostly starch and simple sugars, not a fiber carbohydrate
  (Finding 6, 7; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Needs moist but never waterlogged soil while vines
  grow and roots swell; lifting roots soon after heavy rain or
  irrigation in hot weather causes cracking (Finding 9; water-use
  detail below).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded on ridges; seed needs warm soil and
  germinates in 5-12 days; in cooler climates it can be started
  indoors under bottom heat several weeks before the last frost
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures were found for any
  operation; weekly flower removal from first bloom to near harvest is
  the main hand-labor operation specific to this crop (Finding 1).
- **Soil:** Tolerates a wide pH range (roughly 4.3-8), with
  well-drained, light to medium soils preferred; phosphorus is the
  first limiting nutrient and potassium demand is high (Finding 3).
- **Stand life:** A perennial root grown as an annual; tops die back
  each growing cycle (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A probable bean-mosaic-type virus spread
  by aphids and mealybugs, a witches'-broom disorder, several
  defoliating beetles, and seed-feeding beetles that can destroy a
  large share of an unprotected seed crop; nematode and insect damage
  to roots and various minor pests are also documented (Finding 9;
  hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Two informal market types (a very watery type and a
  drier, milkier type) plus several named bred cultivars from Mexico,
  one Indian release, and several documented trial accessions
  (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Hand-lifted after mechanical soil loosening; roots can
  be held in the ground for weeks to months past maturity if not
  needed immediately (Finding 9).
- **Storage:** Warm storage only, at roughly 12.5-17.5°C and 65-80%
  relative humidity, for one to four months depending on source; never
  refrigerated below about 12.5°C, where real chilling injury sets in
  (Finding 5).

Water-use detail: no official crop-coefficient curve exists for this
species, so one is estimated here by analogy to the same warm-season
storage-root vine this entry is grounded against, with growth stages
stretched somewhat longer to reflect this crop's generally longer,
short-day-sensitive cycle. A Mexican field trial's combined
irrigation-plus-rainfall total of roughly 960 mm over an April-to-
November season sits near the middle of the resulting estimated
range. The crop is not considered drought-tolerant in a strong sense,
but is documented in at least one source as not needing much water,
and has produced a crop across a documented absolute rainfall range
from roughly 250 mm to 7,000 mm per year.

Establishment detail: typical spacing is on ridges roughly 80-120 cm
apart with two rows per ridge, giving commercial stands in the
range of 100,000-170,000 plants/ha depending on region; sowing
generally follows the start of the rainy season or, where irrigated,
an early-spring date.

Cultivar detail: the two informal market types are a very watery,
translucent-juiced, top-shaped root favored for its crisp sweetness,
and a drier, milkier-juiced, more elongated root that is less
preferred commercially. Named bred Mexican cultivars documented here
reach 100-105 t/ha in research trials with measured nitrogen fixation
in the 175-199 kg/ha range; one Indian release reaches 36-40 t/ha in
a 120-day cycle with fusiform roots and light-blue flowers; and
several numbered trial accessions from West Africa reach roughly
80 t/ha with dry matter around 13%.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed and mature pods of this crop contain a
substantial concentration of a toxic insecticidal compound (rotenone
and related compounds), while the eaten root itself carries none.
Documented human poisonings include a fatal case and outbreaks of
dangerous metabolic acidosis, and cooking does not detoxify
contaminated seed. Ground seed is intentionally used as an
insecticide and fish poison, so seed and seed waste must be kept away
from food, feed, and fish ponds. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 2).

Disease and pest pressure includes a probable bean-mosaic-type virus
(aphid- and mealybug-spread, with losses of 20-30% documented in one
source), a witches'-broom disorder, several named defoliating
beetles, nematode and insect damage to roots, and various rodent and
minor insect pests by region. Seed-feeding beetles are a particular
risk to the separate seed crop, capable of destroying up to 70% of an
unprotected harvest (Finding 9).

Growth cracks and splitting at lifting are a quality hazard specific
to harvest timing: roots that are lifted soon after heavy rain or hot-
weather irrigation are prone to cracking (Finding 9). Storage below
about 12.5°C causes chilling injury — browning, softening, and decay —
and is a separate, well-documented postharvest hazard distinct from
the plant-toxin hazard above (Finding 5).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → direct sowing on ridges → weekly flower removal
  → harvested roots and vines. Seed → unpruned plants → young pods
  plus smaller roots. Seed → dedicated seed crop (8-10 months) → dried
  pods → hand-shelled seed. Roots → washed and graded → warm storage
  at 12.5-15°C. Roots → peeled, sliced, and dried → flour. Roots →
  milled and washed → starch, with the leftover pulp roasted into a
  cassava-style staple food. Seed → ground into insecticide; seed →
  heat-treated and pressed → oil plus press-cake. Vines → fodder or
  green manure; oversized and rejected roots → cattle and pig feed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned, pickled, and
  frozen product, and refrigerated fresh-cut sticks; harvest
  mechanization; industrial rotenone extraction; interspecific
  breeding between this species and its two cultivated relatives.

A low-quality source documents this root as one of several seasonal
fruits placed on home altars during a Mexican autumn holiday
observance; this is recorded here as a weak, unconfirmed cultural
note rather than a documented production practice. Separately, a
tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables and other underground
storage organs generally, a note this project has also recorded for
other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Composition figures conflict meaningfully between two sources (38
  kcal and 0.72 g protein per 100 g versus 47 kcal and 1.0-2.5 g
  protein), and dry-matter percentage conflicts even more sharply
  (7.6% at one Mexican site versus 13-21% in a West African trial) —
  neither is resolved here.
- A claim that this crop's reserve carbohydrate includes a significant
  fraction of a different fermentable fiber (as documented in some
  other root crops) was not re-confirmed against a primary source in
  this pass, while the sources used here report a starch- and sugar-
  dominated dry matter instead — an open conflict.
- Storage-life recommendations conflict modestly across sources
  (one to two months versus two to four months versus up to four
  months at similar temperatures).
- Seed-crop duration conflicts across sources (8-10 months in a
  regional reference versus 150 days to seed for one specific Indian
  release).
- The timing of root swelling relative to sowing versus germination is
  reported slightly differently across two sources (4-6 weeks after
  sowing versus about 7 weeks after germination).
- Recommended seeding rate varies enormously by region (roughly
  35 kg/ha in Mexico versus 60-180 kg/ha in Thailand), a gap wide
  enough that it may reflect differing sowing practice rather than
  measurement error.
- A regional reference lists this crop's foliage as poisonous, while
  the composition sources used here document only a very low, under-
  0.01% rotenone level in foliage and describe it as usable fodder —
  not reconciled here.
- Weakest areas overall: no sourced labor-hour figures exist for any
  operation; water-use parameters rely on an analogy to an unrelated
  crop rather than a measured crop-coefficient curve; and the
  starch-extraction yield used for processing estimates below is not
  based on a measured extraction trial.
- A monograph-length review of this species and a handful of other
  primary sources referenced secondhand in the material used here were
  not independently obtained or read in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 30,000 kg/ha, good 60,000 kg/ha, exceptional
100,000 kg/ha, poor 10,000 kg/ha) are derived mainly from Mexico's
2024 national average as the largest producer with reliable
statistics, cross-checked against regional irrigated/rainfed ranges
for Mexico and Thailand and against lower-input trial yields from
West Africa and Sierra Leone; a documented experimental maximum above
150 t/ha was treated as an outlier and excluded.

A dry-to-fresh weight ratio of roughly 7:1 is derived by averaging a
Mexican field trial's measured 7.6% dry matter against a West African
trial's 13-21% range and a regional reference's 10-20% estimate; the
true ratio could run anywhere from roughly 5:1 to 13:1 depending on
site and cultivar.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, root
depth, and a soil-moisture depletion allowance) are estimated by
analogy to the same warm-season storage-root vine this entry is
grounded against, since no dedicated reference curve exists for this
species; stage lengths are stretched somewhat longer than that
analogy crop to reflect this crop's longer, short-day-sensitive
growing cycle, and the resulting total water estimate sits close to
one Mexican field trial's measured combined irrigation-and-rainfall
total. A drought-buffer estimate of roughly 13 days before yield
penalty begins is derived from standard soil-water-holding assumptions
combined with these same estimated root-depth and depletion figures;
overall drought tolerance is rated only moderate, reflecting a mix
of a "doesn't need much water" field note against a documented
preference for consistent moisture during flowering.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 20 and a corresponding
seed-reserve fraction of about 0.05 are derived by dividing a
documented Mexican seed-crop yield by a documented Mexican seeding
rate; a West African unpruned trial's far higher seed yield was
treated as an outlier and excluded, and Thailand's much higher
seeding rate against its lower documented seed yield would imply a
far lower ratio, a discrepancy not resolved here.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh root) are derived from two independent Mexican field
trials' measured root-nutrient uptake at measured yields; the two
trials disagree on nitrogen removal by roughly 70%, a conflict kept
open, with phosphorus and potassium figures averaged across the two.

Optimal soil pH (roughly 7.0, with a tolerance range of roughly
4.5-8) is estimated from a documented optimum range of 6.5-8 and an
absolute tolerance range of 4.3-8, checked against one trial site that
grew a strong crop on pH 7.6 clay and another that grew a crop on
pH 4.6-4.9 soil.

A storage life of roughly 60 days at warm, humid conditions is
estimated from a cluster of sources giving one to four months,
anchored to the point at which stored roots have lost roughly a third
of their harvest starch content to sweetening; an ambient (non-
cooled) shelf life of roughly three weeks is estimated from a
documented daily weight-loss rate at warmer temperatures.

A starch-extraction yield of roughly 0.03-0.06 kg of starch per
kilogram of fresh root pulp is estimated from one West African
trial's measured dry-matter and starch-percentage figures, assuming
an unmeasured 60-90% recovery rate during washing; this has not been
checked against an actual measured extraction trial for this crop. A
flour-chain yield of roughly 0.08-0.20 kg of dried sliced product per
kilogram of fresh root is estimated similarly from documented dry-
matter ranges, assuming peeling loss and residual drying moisture
roughly cancel out.

A root-zone depth estimate of around 40 cm and a per-area daily yield
estimate in the range of roughly 15-46 g/m²/day are both derived from
typical root size and from dividing documented yield figures by
typical growing-season length.
