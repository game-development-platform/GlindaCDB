## Naming and scope

Celeriac (also called celery root or knob celery), *Apium graveolens*
var. *rapaceum*, family Apiaceae [5]. The same species as stalk celery
(var. *dulce*) and leaf celery; wild celery is their shared ancestor,
native to Europe and the Mediterranean [5]. The edible part is a
swollen hypocotyl and stem base, not a true taproot [5]. This entry
covers celeriac only; stalk celery is a distinct crop this project has
not treated here, though they share underlying species biology
(vernalization behavior, allergens, pest and disease pressure).

This crop shares general biennial Apiaceae-root-crop biology (humid
cold storage, ethylene sensitivity, umbel-based seed crops) with
parsnip and carrot, two related root crops this project has also
researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a transplanted root crop with a two-and-a-half-month
nursery period.** Unlike most root crops, celeriac is not typically
direct-sown in the field. Seed is sown once into trays roughly 10-12
weeks before transplanting (one home-garden source gives a shorter
4-6 week indoor period instead), with germination itself notably slow
(three weeks or more) [3][4]. Seedlings are transplanted once they have
at least two true leaves. Total time from seeding to mature root is
roughly 200 days, with named cultivars varying in maturity time from
transplanting [4].

**Finding 2 — cold exposure before planting out triggers premature
flowering (bolting), which destroys the root crop.** Plants that have
reached a minimum size (roughly 6-8 true leaves) can be induced to
flower by a period of cool temperature below about 10°C, with
vernalization proceeding faster at slightly colder temperatures and
potentially requiring up to 10 weeks depending on cultivar [8]. A
practical nursery-management rule is to avoid exposing young plants to
more than about 10 days at a specific mild-cool threshold temperature
[3]. The practical consequence is a genuine design tension: nursery
plants must be kept warm enough, for long enough, before transplanting
to avoid triggering bolting, which would otherwise destroy the root
crop entirely.

**Finding 3 — shallow-rooted, water-demanding, and salt-sensitive.**
A standard international crop-water-use reference (using data for the
same species) gives a notably low soil-moisture-depletion allowance
(among the lowest documented for any crop in that reference) and a
fairly shallow effective root depth [10]. Growth interruptions
"seriously decrease the size of roots," and uniform watering through
any dry period, along with frequent irrigation generally, is
specifically recommended [3][4]. The crop is rated moderately salt-
sensitive based on data for the same species' petiole tissue [11].

**Finding 4 — one of the longest-keeping vegetables, provided the air
stays very close to saturation.** Under ideal cold, very-high-humidity
storage (just above freezing, 95-99% relative humidity), celeriac can
keep for a remarkable 6-8 months; at a somewhat warmer storage
temperature, storage life drops to only about 4 months, illustrating
how sensitive this crop's keeping quality is to precise temperature
control [1][2]. Humidity just below saturation is specifically needed
to prevent shriveling, while freezing injury (water-soaked areas,
softening) sets in just below the crop's freezing point. The crop is
not considered chilling-sensitive and produces very little ethylene
itself, but is somewhat sensitive to ethylene exposure from other
produce, which toughens the root; controlled-atmosphere storage is not
considered advantageous for this crop [1][2]. Leaving the fine
secondary stems attached during storage can cause the center to rot,
and the center can also turn naturally hollow with age regardless of
storage conditions [5].

**Finding 5 — harvest and handling practices are well documented in at
least one commercial growing region.** Harvest typically occurs in late
autumn, with roots cut just below the soil surface by knife and tops
removed in the field; full flavor is often described as developing
only after the first autumn frost, and roots can be left in the ground
under mulch and dug progressively through the winter in milder climates
[3][4]. Documented hand-crew labor rates from one commercial organic
operation give specific harvest and washing throughput figures per
person [3]; that same source documents several hand-weeding and
cultivation passes needed because the crop's canopy provides little
weed-suppressing shade. Yield figures convert to roughly the mid-20s
t/ha under the growing practices documented [3][4]. Nutrient uptake and
soil pH targets are also specifically documented from commercial
growing guidance [3][4].

**Finding 6 — two significant food/skin hazards are shared with stalk
celery, as the same species.** A specific allergy syndrome links birch
pollen, mugwort pollen, and celery-family allergens: one allergenic
protein in the tuber is closely related to a well-known birch-pollen
allergen, while a second, distinct allergen is specifically associated
with a more severe, mugwort-linked form carrying a substantially
elevated risk of severe allergic reaction; dried celeriac is also
sometimes present, without obvious labeling, in spice blends and
prepared soups [7]. Separately, celery-family plants infected with a
specific fungal disease (pink rot) produce elevated levels of
furocoumarin compounds that cause a phototoxic skin reaction
(phytophotodermatitis) when skin contact with the plant is followed by
sun exposure; a documented historical survey found that over half of a
large group of commercial celery harvesters experienced this
blistering skin reaction, and at least one review specifically
identifies the root as holding the highest concentration of the
responsible compound [12].

**Finding 7 — documented disorders and pests.** A boron-deficiency
disorder causes "brown heart" rot in the root, worsened by alkaline or
dry soil conditions (since boron becomes less available to the plant
under both conditions); hollow centers can also develop simply with
age, and soft, spongy roots are a distinct quality defect [1][5][15].
Documented pests and diseases include a fungal leaf-spot disease
favored by humid conditions at moderate temperature and carried on
seed and crop residue, a specific celery-feeding fly, carrot rust fly,
aphids, carrot weevil, cutworms, root-knot and lesion nematodes, and
voles [16].

**Finding 8 — seed production and hybrid breeding.** The plant is
biennial, with seed crops requiring substantial isolation distance from
other seed crops of the same species — and notably, celery and celeriac
readily cross-pollinate with each other, so the two must be isolated
from one another as well as from other plantings of their own type
[8][9]. Documented seed yield (from the same species generally) is
roughly 500 kg/ha, with very small individual seed size; seed shatters
readily, so harvest timing is based on most seed on the main umbels
turning grey-brown, followed by drying on tarpaulins or in windrows and
screen cleaning [9]. F1 hybrid celeriac does exist commercially, but
hybrids remain a small share of the market relative to open-pollinated
cultivars, since the cytoplasmic male-sterility systems needed for
reliable hybrid seed production are held proprietarily by seed
companies and are described as unstable; a small number of known
male-sterile genetic sources exist but are not yet used at commercial
scale [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial grown as an annual
  root crop, established exclusively via nursery-raised transplants
  rather than direct seeding (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest, typically in late autumn after
  the first frost, with the option to leave roots in the ground under
  mulch and dig them progressively through winter in milder climates
  (Finding 5).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate, temperate, and Mediterranean (cool-
  season) zones; genuinely bolt-prone if young plants experience
  prolonged cold before transplanting (Finding 2; see also climate
  zone detail).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (commonly shredded raw in salads), though
  the root should be peeled first.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop
  requires insect pollination and substantial isolation, including
  from stalk celery, with which this crop readily cross-pollinates
  (Finding 8).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields in one well-
  sourced growing region convert to roughly the mid-20s t/ha; no
  broader national or international yield statistic was found in this
  research pass (see Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Shallow-rooted and notably water-demanding — a
  standard reference gives this crop one of the lowest soil-moisture-
  depletion allowances documented for any crop, meaning yield is
  sensitive to even brief water stress; growth interruptions directly
  and seriously reduce final root size (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Exclusively nursery-raised: seed is sown into
  trays roughly 10-12 weeks before transplanting (germination is slow),
  with transplants moved to the field once they reach a minimum leaf
  count; direct seeding is not practiced commercially, owing to slow
  germination and the crop's long total growing season (Finding 1).
- **Labor:** Documented hand-harvest and washing throughput rates exist
  from at least one commercial organic growing operation; several
  hand-weeding passes are typically needed since the crop's canopy
  provides little weed suppression (Finding 5).
- **Soil:** Well-drained loam at a moderately acidic to neutral pH is
  preferred; boron availability is a specific concern, since boron
  becomes unavailable to the plant above a certain soil pH, directly
  causing a named root disorder (Finding 7).
- **Stand life:** Grown as an annual for root production, though the
  plant itself is biennial, with a distinct seed-crop cycle requiring
  overwintering.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A boron-deficiency root disorder, a fungal
  leaf-spot disease, a specific celery-feeding fly, carrot rust fly,
  aphids, carrot weevil, cutworms, root-knot and lesion nematodes, and
  voles are all documented (Finding 7).
- **Cultivars:** Several named open-pollinated cultivars exist, varying
  mainly in root shape, size, maturity time, and storage quality; at
  least one F1 hybrid cultivar exists but hybrids remain uncommon
  relative to open-pollinated types (Finding 8; cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Cut just below the soil surface by hand, with tops
  removed in the field; can be done as a single autumn harvest or
  extended through winter via mulched in-ground storage (Finding 5).
- **Storage:** Exceptionally long-keeping under precisely controlled
  cold, near-saturated-humidity conditions (6-8 months), but storage
  life drops substantially at only slightly warmer temperatures,
  illustrating unusually high sensitivity to precise storage-condition
  control; the crop is also somewhat sensitive to ethylene exposure
  from other produce during storage (Finding 4).

## Hazards

A boron-deficiency disorder causes "brown heart" rot in the root
(worsened by alkaline or dry soil); hollow centers can develop simply
with age; soft, spongy texture is a distinct quality defect. A fungal
leaf-spot disease, a specific celery-feeding fly, carrot rust fly,
aphids, carrot weevil, cutworms, root-knot and lesion nematodes, and
voles are all documented pests and diseases (Finding 7).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop shares a notable allergy risk with
stalk celery: an allergenic protein in the tuber is closely related to
a well-known birch-pollen allergen, and a separate allergen is linked
to a more severe, mugwort-pollen-associated reaction carrying
substantially elevated anaphylaxis risk; dried celeriac can also be
present, without obvious labeling, in spice blends and prepared soups.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data (Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — celery-family plants infected with a specific
fungal disease (pink rot) produce elevated phototoxic compounds that
can cause significant skin blistering (phytophotodermatitis) in people
handling the plant who are then exposed to sunlight; a historical
survey found this affected over half of a large group of commercial
harvesters, and the root is specifically identified as holding the
highest concentration of the responsible compound. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 6).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed sown into trays for 10-12 weeks → transplanted →
  harvested in autumn → washed, trimmed, and sized → stored cold at
  near-saturated humidity for up to 6-8 months. Alternatively,
  transplants can be mulched in the field and dug progressively through
  winter. Trimmed roots can also be peeled, sliced, and dried into a
  powder used in soups and spice blends. For seed production, roots are
  kept as overwintering stock and replanted, producing seed umbels that
  are threshed and cleaned.
- Leaves are used as a seasoning; stalks are of only marginal culinary
  use for this particular variety group (the plant is grown primarily
  for its root).
- Not covered by this research: frozen and canned celeriac in detail;
  the specific cytoplasmic male-sterility systems used in commercial
  F1 seed production; traditional cooked dishes (rémoulade, soups,
  purée) beyond what is noted above.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, since
harvesting them kills the whole plant and disturbs soil organisms
along with it. No other dedicated religious or ritual use specific to
this crop was found; a commonly cited ancient Greek literary reference
to a plant with a similar name actually refers to wild celery rather
than any ritual use of the cultivated root crop.

## Open research questions

- Nursery duration before transplanting is given quite differently
  across sources (10-12 weeks in one commercial source vs. 4-6 weeks
  in a home-garden source) — likely reflecting different growing
  conditions or transplant-size targets rather than a true conflict.
- The precise bolting trigger (cold duration and temperature threshold)
  is given somewhat differently across sources, though all agree on
  the general mechanism (cold exposure after a minimum plant size
  triggers flowering).
- Storage humidity and freezing-point figures differ very slightly
  between two authoritative storage references — likely within normal
  measurement variation rather than a true conflict.
- Seed-crop isolation distance is given quite differently across
  sources (a longer isolation distance in one formal seed-production
  reference vs. a notably shorter distance in informal seed-saver
  guidance) — not reconciled.
- In-row spacing recommendations differ between sources; at least one
  trial found a wider spacing produced larger roots than narrower
  commercial spacing.
- No broader national or international yield statistic for this crop
  specifically was found in this research pass; the yield figures used
  here are derived entirely from two documented US growing-region
  sources (see Estimates below).
- Documented hand-harvest labor rates from one commercial source
  imply a considerably higher harvest labor requirement than a rough,
  unsourced estimate would otherwise suggest — this entry treats the
  sourced figure as more reliable, but a broader labor study across
  different growing systems would be valuable.
- Whether to model celeriac and stalk celery as sharing any commodity
  items (since they are the same species) was considered; given that
  their edible products (root vs. stalk) and commercial cultivars are
  entirely distinct, they are treated here as fully separate in terms
  of harvested product, despite shared underlying species biology.
- Several topics were not researched in this pass: broader national
  yield statistics, waterlogging tolerance, photoperiod sensitivity,
  seed viability over storage time, and losses specific to mulched
  winter field-holding.

## Estimates and derivations

A good-yield figure of roughly 27,000 kg/ha is derived by converting
documented US commercial yield figures (roots per row-foot at a
specific bed/row spacing from one source, and a minimum per-row-foot
yield target from a second source) into a per-hectare basis; the two
independent conversions agree closely, at roughly 26-28 t/ha. Plausible
range: 20,000-35,000 kg/ha, depending on bed width and root size.
National agricultural statistics for this crop (not found in this
research pass) would replace this estimate. World-average, exceptional,
and poor-yield figures are carried as rough, unsourced estimates
pending better data.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived from a standard international crop-
water-use reference's data for the same species, combined with an
estimated growth-stage breakdown for a temperate, transplanted growing
season of roughly 165 days; this produces a seasonal water-use index
of about 157 (days-equivalent). A drought-buffer estimate of only about
2 days before yield penalty begins is calculated from standard soil-
water-holding, root-depth, and depletion-allowance assumptions —
notably short compared to many other root crops, reflecting this
crop's shallow root system and documented sensitivity to any growth
interruption.

A seed rate of roughly 0.25 kg/ha (reflecting modern plug/tray
nursery practice) and a resulting seed-to-yield multiplication ratio
of roughly 2,000 are derived from standard transplant density and a
documented seed yield figure for the same species; a traditional
garden-scale direct seeding rate, by contrast, works out to a
considerably higher seed weight per hectare and a correspondingly
lower multiplication ratio, reflecting the much lower seeding
efficiency of direct garden sowing compared to precision nursery
plug production.

Days from sowing to seed harvest (roughly 450-550 days, or about 15-18
months) are derived from the root-growing period, a winter storage and
overwintering period, and a subsequent flowering-to-seed-ripening
period in the following growing season.

Documented hand-crew harvest and washing throughput rates (from one
commercial source) convert to roughly 580 person-hours per hectare for
harvesting and roughly 170 person-hours per hectare for washing, at a
representative yield level — both figures reflect small-scale,
hand-labor-intensive production rather than mechanized harvest.

Nutrient uptake per tonne of root (roughly 4.5 kg nitrogen, 0.68 kg
phosphorus, and 6.8 kg potassium) is derived from a documented whole-
crop nutrient-uptake figure (including tops, not just the harvested
root) divided by the estimated yield above; this represents total crop
uptake rather than nutrient removal in the harvested root alone.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 16 g/m²/day) is derived
from the estimated good yield divided by a typical field-growing
period of about 165 days, not counting the separate nursery period.

Genome size (roughly 3.4 pg, 1C) is derived from a published genome-
assembly size for the same species, converted using the standard
base-pairs-per-picogram conversion factor; this reflects an assembly
size rather than a directly measured flow-cytometry value, and an
earlier, different assembly of the same species produced a somewhat
smaller genome-size estimate.
