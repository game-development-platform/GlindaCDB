## Naming and scope

*Brassica napus* L. Napobrassica Group — rutabaga, swede, or neep,
also simply called "turnip" in some regions despite being a distinct
species — an allopolyploid formed from a cross between turnip and
cabbage, with 38 chromosomes [5][13]. This entry covers the table
rutabaga, grown and harvested as a root vegetable for direct human
consumption; a separate grazed-in-field fodder type of the same crop,
raised specifically for livestock grazing rather than table use, is
planned as its own distinct entry, since it differs meaningfully in
practice and purpose. The same broader species also includes an
oilseed crop, a leafy vegetable type, and a fodder type grown for
leaves rather than roots, none of which are covered by this entry.

This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research into
the closely related turnip, the parent species that lends half this
crop's genome, since both share the same standard water-use reference
table row and the same storage-handbook treatment.

Shared background across both rutabaga and its grazed-fodder
relative: seed-crop practice (biennial life cycle requiring an
overwintered root before flowering, strict isolation from all other
members of the same broader species), self-incompatibility-based
hybrid seed breeding, a soil-borne clubroot disease, a fungal dry-rot
disease, a specific boron-deficiency root disorder, and genome size.
This entry's own distinguishing features are the vegetable root
itself, retail waxing and pre-peeling practice, long cold storage, and
protective netting against a root-feeding pest.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a field-held winter root crop with very long storage
life.** Commercial growing guides document 90-100 days from sowing to
harvest [3][11]. In a detailed Irish extension guide, the main crop is
sown in spring and harvested from mid-summer all the way through to
the following spring, with roots standing in the field through winter;
cold-stored roots, placed into bulk storage bins in late winter, cover
the remaining months so that year-round supply is possible [4]. Stored
roots keep four to six months for fresh use, and six to nine months
when destined for processing, at near-freezing temperature and very
high humidity (98-100%, with a documented floor of at least 95%); below
that humidity threshold, roots shrivel and develop a surface defect
called storage burn, with measurably lower weight loss and no increase
in decay at the higher humidity range [2][3]. Roots tolerate slight
freezing without injury, but severe freezing water-soaks and browns
the flesh, producing a mustard smell and fermentation [2][3]. The
practical consequence is a long harvest window combined with in-field
holding over winter, followed by the same cold, humid storage regime
documented for related root crops.

**Finding 2 — waxing is a retail coating step with a real trade-off.**
Roots are commonly hot-dipped in paraffin wax just before marketing,
to keep them clean and prevent shrivel [2][3]. However, a heavy wax
coat blocks gas exchange and causes internal breakdown (a condition
called suboxidation); waxing roots intended for storage beyond about
two months is specifically not recommended, and in sufficiently humid
storage using perforated bags, waxing may be unnecessary altogether
[2][3]. Pre-peeled roots packed in film bags keep three to four weeks
when hand-peeled, but only about one week when abrasively peeled
[2][3]. The practical consequence is a post-harvest coating treatment
that extends shelf life at retail but actively harms long-term storage
quality.

**Finding 3 — a boron-deficiency disorder with documented genetic
resistance.** A disorder called brown heart (also known as water core
or mottled heart) appears as brown, water-soaked discoloration in the
root's inner tissue; it is linked to boron deficiency and is not
caused by cold storage itself [2][10]. The disorder is worse on
high-pH soils, since boron becomes less available above roughly pH
7.0; it is prevented with a boron-containing base fertilizer plus
several foliar boron sprays starting early in the plant's growth, and
larger roots show the disorder first [4]. A detailed UK field trial
found that incidence depended on the specific cultivar as well as on
boron availability, with resistance to the disorder appearing to be a
dominant genetic trait; at least two specific cultivars bred for
brown-heart resistance were introduced commercially in 2022 [10]. The
practical consequence is a soil-chemistry disorder that is modified by
both soil pH, moisture conditions, and the specific cultivar grown.

**Finding 4 — pests and crop rotation shape how this crop is
grown.** The cabbage root fly is documented as the major pest, with
two generations per year and the only genuinely effective control
being insect-proof netting laid at sowing time and removed roughly six
months later; such netting is documented lasting at least ten years,
cutting incoming light by roughly 12%, and giving better root quality
with fewer side roots despite that light reduction [4]. A fungal dry-
rot disease can destroy whole crops; growers are advised to keep this
crop well away from oilseed rape (a related crop highly susceptible to
the same disease) and to use wide crop rotations — at least four to
five years between any brassica-family crops, and seven to eight years
specifically between plantings of this crop — while avoiding fields
known to carry clubroot disease [4]. The practical consequence is a
reusable protective field-covering input plus strict rotation rules.

**Finding 5 — F1 hybrid seed of this crop is produced using self-
incompatibility rather than cytoplasmic male sterility.** Self-
incompatible parent lines produced close to 100% true hybrid seed when
the two parent lines' flowering time and flower color coincided, but
only about 61% hybrid purity when they did not; self-incompatibility
is documented as the preferred breeding method for this crop over
cytoplasmic male sterility, and resulting F1 hybrids outyielded their
better parent by roughly 12-29% in dry matter in trial data [9]. Two
named F1 hybrid cultivars are documented in commercial trade [4]. An
older, unsourced claim that this crop is normally 70-80%
self-pollinating was not confirmed by any source consulted in this
research pass; the sources instead document that genuinely self-
incompatible populations of this crop exist and are specifically used
in hybrid breeding [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial root crop grown as an
  annual, taking roughly 90-110 days to reach harvestable size
  (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per planting, but with roots
  typically held and lifted gradually from the field across a long
  window spanning mid-summer through the following spring (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible; a taste-genetics study found tasters
  with a specific bitter-taste-receptor genotype perceive this crop as
  roughly twice as bitter as other tasters do, a taste difference
  rather than a safety concern.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  insect-pollinated, with self-incompatible lines specifically used in
  hybrid breeding (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields span roughly
  12,000 to 50,000 kg/ha depending on region and production intensity
  (see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Needs steady, even moisture on well-drained ground;
  dry conditions during root bulking worsen the boron-related brown-
  heart disorder, while wet conditions combined with excess nitrogen
  cause splitting and wet rot (Finding 3; water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Mostly direct-drilled in the field, with transplant
  modules sometimes used for an earlier crop under protective covers
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour survey exists for this crop
  specifically; one detailed Irish extension source notes direct
  drilling requires notably less labor than transplanting.
- **Soil:** A well-drained medium-to-heavy loam or clay loam is
  preferred; tolerant of acid soils, though growth is restricted below
  a specific low pH, and boron becomes less available above a
  specific higher pH (Finding 3).
- **Stand life:** Biennial in its natural life cycle but grown
  commercially as an annual root crop.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Cabbage root fly (the major pest,
  controlled mainly by protective netting), flea beetle, aphids,
  caterpillars, slugs, and larger wildlife pests; downy mildew, dry rot,
  powdery mildew, ring spot, a Rhizoctonia-caused crater spot, and
  clubroot are documented diseases (Finding 4; hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** A range of open-pollinated and F1 hybrid cultivars
  spanning fresh-market and processing use, bolting resistance, and
  brown-heart resistance (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Lifted from the field over an extended window spanning
  mid-summer through the following spring in some growing regions
  (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Kept at near-freezing temperature and very high
  humidity, giving four to six months of storage life for fresh use
  and six to nine months for processing stock (Finding 1).

Water detail: a standard international reference work's crop-
coefficient curve for this crop group gives a water-use shape similar
to the related turnip, with stage lengths and the resulting total
seasonal water figure estimated in the absence of a documented
official stage-length table; a US extension source documents an
applied irrigation total of roughly 200-300 mm in one specific growing
region.

Establishment detail: direct-drilled crops are spaced roughly 11-20 cm
apart in rows on beds, giving a plant population in the tens of
thousands per hectare; an early transplanted crop is raised from
modules sown under protection in late winter and set out under
temporary plastic or fleece cover in early spring; sowing before a
certain date in spring risks a cold check that triggers premature
bolting.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include two long-established
open-pollinated purple-top yellow types used for both fresh market and
processing; a Danish-bred open-pollinated cultivar noted for high dry
matter, frost hardiness, and resistance to bolting, powdery mildew,
and splitting, though more prone to dry rot and downy mildew; a globe-
shaped open-pollinated type known for good color and shape but prone
to an internal brown-discoloration disorder managed by tight spacing
and a specific late-autumn harvest deadline; two F1 hybrid cultivars
increasingly used commercially; an open-pollinated type reported with
clubroot tolerance in European trials; and two specifically bred
brown-heart-resistant cultivars introduced commercially in 2022.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — culls and tops of this crop are commonly fed to
livestock, and grazing-related disorders (a glucosinolate-related liver
condition and photosensitization) are documented for the related
grazed-fodder type of this same broader crop; this health/safety
material is deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
data, and is primarily relevant to that separate fodder-type entry
rather than to the table root covered here.

Cabbage root fly, flea beetle, aphids, caterpillars, slugs, and larger
wildlife pests are documented field pests; downy mildew, dry rot
(sharing susceptibility with oilseed rape), powdery mildew, ring spot,
a Rhizoctonia-caused crater spot, and clubroot are documented
diseases, along with bacterial soft rot, a fungal black rot, and gray
mold in storage. No notable human food-safety hazard was found beyond
the bitterness taste-genetics note above.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → drilled directly, or raised as early transplants
  under protective netting against cabbage root fly → roots lifted
  over an extended window, mid-summer through the following spring,
  tops and culls going to livestock feed. Roots → topped, washed,
  graded, and cooled → stored at near-freezing temperature and very
  high humidity. Stored roots → hot-waxed shortly before marketing,
  or hand-peeled and bagged for a shorter shelf life. Seed crop:
  overwintered biennial root stock, replanted and isolated from all
  related crops → seed stalks → threshed, cleaned, and hot-water
  treated before resale.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen
  product; specific regional dishes made from this crop; a folk
  tradition of carving this root into lanterns, and a related
  competitive-rolling folk game; protective netting as a reusable
  field input; the separate grazed-fodder type of this crop.

A Halloween-season tradition in parts of the British Isles and the
Isle of Man uses carved roots of this crop as lanterns, a folk-festival
custom attached to the Christian calendar and, on the Isle of Man,
tied to a distinct Celtic new-year observance. A Finnish Christmas
tradition features a baked casserole made from this crop as a festival
food. A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally,
consistent with notes this project has recorded for other root crops.
A well-known Scottish national celebration meal also features this
crop prepared alongside a traditional dish, though this is a secular
rather than religious observance.

## Open research questions

- An older, unsourced claim that this crop is normally 70-80% self-
  pollinating was not confirmed; documented sources instead show
  genuinely self-incompatible populations used in hybrid breeding —
  an open conflict between an older figure and newer sourced findings.
- Documented storage humidity guidance conflicts across sources (a
  98-100% figure from one detailed storage manual versus a somewhat
  lower figure from another regional extension source, versus yet
  another figure from a national cold-store operator).
- Pre-peeled shelf life conflicts between two sources (three weeks at
  one specific cold temperature versus four weeks at a slightly
  different temperature).
- Documented days-to-harvest figures for specific cultivars conflict
  with the general 90-110-day range given elsewhere.
- Seed-isolation distance guidance conflicts sharply between two
  sources (roughly 400 m in one pedigreed-seed standard versus roughly
  800 m, about half a mile, in general seed-saving guidance).
- No swede-specific seed-crop yield figure was found in any source;
  the seed-yield estimate in this entry is carried over by analogy
  from the related turnip's own documented seed-crop yields.
- Weakest areas overall: seed yield and seed-to-harvest multiplication
  ratio; world-average and poor-tier yield figures; all labor-hour
  figures (unsourced); water-use stage lengths (estimated, not
  directly documented); the extended field-holding harvest window;
  and soil salinity tolerance (carried over by analogy from the
  related turnip, since no documented salinity figure exists for this
  crop specifically).
- Whether nitrate or other goitrogenic compounds in this crop's root
  matter for human health was not researched in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 25,000 kg/ha, good 35,000 kg/ha, exceptional
50,000 kg/ha, poor 12,000 kg/ha) are derived from a documented Irish
average packed-out yield for the good tier, cross-checked against a
separately documented US regional yield converted to metric units; the
world-average tier is estimated between a documented lower-bound
regional figure and the good tier, since no single global statistic
separates this crop from related root crops in standard international
agricultural statistics; the poor tier is estimated at roughly a third
of the good tier, representing unnetted crops affected by the pest and
disease pressures described in Finding 4.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a standard international
reference work's documented crop-coefficient values for this crop
group, combined with an estimated stage-length breakdown (since no
official stage-length table exists for this specific crop in that
reference), cross-checked against a documented applied-irrigation total
from a US regional growing guide. A days-without-water tolerance of
roughly 9 days is derived from standard soil-water-holding assumptions
combined with this crop's documented rooting depth and moisture-
depletion allowance.

A seeding rate of roughly 1.5 kg/ha is derived from a documented US
regional seeding-rate range, with a considerably lower rate documented
for precision-drilled plantings in a different growing region. Seed
mass (roughly 2.4 mg per seed) is derived from a documented seed count
per unit weight.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 670 is estimated by
dividing an estimated working seed-crop yield figure — itself carried
over by analogy from the related turnip's own documented seed-crop
yield range, since no sourced seed-yield figure exists for this crop
specifically — by the derived seeding rate.

Seed viability and germination-rate figures are carried over by close
analogy from the related turnip, based on shared genus and similarly
small seed size.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 380-420 days from sowing to seed
harvest is derived from this crop's documented biennial life cycle,
assuming spring or summer sowing, a root overwintered in the field or
in storage, and seed harvested the following summer.

A harvest window of roughly 120 days is estimated from a documented
Irish main-crop lifting period spanning mid-summer through the
following spring, reflecting that one planting can be held in the
field for several months past initial maturity.

A soil pH ceiling of roughly 7.5 and a rooting depth of roughly 75 cm
are derived respectively from the documented point at which boron
availability falls (treated as a working ceiling assuming
supplemental boron sprays) and from the middle of a standard
international reference work's documented rooting-depth range for
this crop group.

Storage life (roughly 150 days cool and dry) is derived as the middle
of the documented four-to-six-month fresh-storage range, with
processing stock documented separately at six to nine months.

A plant population of roughly 90,000-140,000 plants per hectare is
derived from a documented plants-per-square-meter range for direct-
drilled crops.

Soil salinity tolerance is carried over by analogy from the related
turnip's root, since no dedicated salinity figure exists for this crop
in the standard international water-quality reference consulted.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 25 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing the estimated world-average yield tier by a representative
growing-season length.

Genome size (roughly 1.15 picograms, 1C) is derived from a documented
genome-assembly size for a related oilseed cultivar of the same
broader species, converted using a standard genome-size conversion
factor; this crop's own genome has not been separately measured.
