# Cocoyam / Tannia (*Xanthosoma*, mainly *X. sagittifolium*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-19

## Naming and scope

The cultivated *Xanthosoma* of tropical America, mainly *X.
sagittifolium*, plus *X. nigrum* (purple types) [2]. Also known as
tannia, tania, yautia, tanier, malanga, and "new cocoyam." In West
Africa, "old cocoyam" refers to taro, a different aroid root crop this
project has also researched separately — the two share general
oxalate-hazard and feed-use characteristics but are treated as entirely
separate crops here. The genus has 40-60 species and a poorly resolved
taxonomy [2].

Two cultivar groups within this species are treated as part of this
entry rather than as separate crops: a Brazilian leaf-production type
(grown for its leaves rather than its cormels) and a group of
main-corm types (where the main corm, rather than the smaller offshoot
cormels, is the eaten part) — see Finding 9.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two distinct ways to harvest one plant.** A full lift
takes place at 8-11 months for white types, or up to 13 months for
purple types, once the older leaves yellow and dry [3][5].
Alternatively, "milking" takes the largest cormels every 3 months
starting around 6 months, without uprooting the plant, for about 1.5
years — the planting is then renewed after about 2 years [2][3].
Smallholders commonly practice a third pattern: harvesting only what is
needed and leaving the rest in the soil, since cormels do not spoil if
left 6 to 10-12 months — though once fully mature they will sprout with
rain and turn watery [5].

**Finding 2 — the offshoot cormels are the food; the main corm is
planting stock.** In the two main species groups covered here, the
cormels are eaten; in two other, less common species within the genus,
the main corm itself is the food instead (see Finding 9). Average
cormel weight is 125-150 g [4]. Planting material options include
"head-setts" (the top of the plant with trimmed petioles, giving the
highest yields), cut pieces of the main corm (each with a bud-eye,
sprouted in moist sawdust or a shaded nursery over about 1.5-2 months),
suckers, seed cormels, and tissue-culture plantlets [2][3][4]. Planting
material is routinely dipped in fungicide and should come only from
healthy plants [3].

**Finding 3 — never waterlogged.** This crop cannot tolerate
waterlogged conditions the way taro can; wet soil favors a serious
root-rot pathogen [3]. Documented rainfall requirements across sources
converge on roughly 1,400-2,000 mm through the growing season [2][3][4].
Cultivation typically uses raised ridges with drains below furrow
level, and dry spells cause real stress — leaves burn and water becomes
essential during droughts [2][4]. No standard international crop-water-
use reference includes a dedicated row for this crop [1].

**Finding 4 — a forest-floor plant usually grown in full sun
commercially.** Its native habitat is under rainforest canopy, but
commercial production is usually in full sun [3]. One source reports
better yields specifically under 25-50% partial shade, with plants
surviving only as dormant tubers under heavy shade [2]. It is commonly
grown as an intercrop under coconut, banana, and rubber, though yield
under an established coconut canopy runs noticeably lower than as a
sole crop [2][13].

**Finding 5 — climate requirements.** Average daily temperature above
21°C is needed, with 25-29°C preferred; the crop is not frost-tolerant
[2]. One regional technical source describes the crop as preferring
"cool" temperatures around 21°C specifically [3] — a genuine conflict
with the broader reference's higher preferred range, not resolved here.
It is a lowland crop, grown up to about 2,000 m with falling yields at
altitude [2]. Preferred soil pH is 5.5-6.5, and the crop tolerates
slightly saline soils [2][4].

**Finding 6 — a specific root rot disease is the crop's defining
threat.** A soil-borne water mold causes a serious root-rot disease
(locally called "rapid yellowing disease" or "burning disease"): outer
leaves yellow from the margin inward, roots die, and marketable
cormels are severely affected [3]. This disease is credited with a
significant historical decline of a related cocoyam type in Cameroon
[9]. Susceptibility varies by type — white-fleshed types are very
susceptible, while purple/red types show some field tolerance; one
screening trial in Cameroon identified dozens of resistant and
tolerant accessions [9]. Control is entirely preventive: clean planting
material, crop rotation of at least 3 years, planting after a fallow
period, good drainage, and fungicide dips — once a plant is diseased it
cannot be restored [3][4]. Other documented problems include a viral
disease, bacterial blight, two further fungal tuber rots, and specific
beetle pests [2].

**Finding 7 — storage is short and cool, but never cold.** Eating
cormels keep up to about 3 months if stored dry, cool, and well
ventilated without losing eating quality [2]. Retail storage guidance
specifies about 7°C and 70-80% relative humidity for short holds (about
30 days' shelf life), with a hard floor — storage must never go below
7°C, since chilling injury shows as water-soaked soft spots and faster
decay [6]. Seed cormels are handled differently: sun-cured for 4-5
days, then buried in dry soil or sand in a ventilated, semi-dark place
for 4-5 months [5]. A separate propagule-specific cold-storage trial
found browning injury occurring even at a notably warmer 10°C after 6
months, and at 5°C after 3 months [6] — a second data point on this
crop's general cold sensitivity, slightly in tension with the "never
below 7°C" retail guidance.

**Finding 8 — products and composition.** Per 100 g of cormel from the
primary species, composition is roughly 70-77 g water, 1.3-3.7 g
protein, 17-26 g carbohydrate, with an unusually high reported vitamin
C value treated here as a likely outlier [2]. Other composition
sources report substantially lower calorie and protein figures for the
same general product under different database names, a conflict not
fully resolved here [11]. Leaves are higher in protein and notably rich
in phosphorus and vitamin C, and are harvested from about 6 weeks after
planting [2]. All parts of the plant carry calcium oxalate raphides and
must be cooked before eating; oxalate content is higher in leaf blades
and petioles than in cormel flesh [2][12]. The cormels of this crop
(the "new cocoyam" type) pound well into a traditional fufu-style
dish, while taro cormels (the "old cocoyam" type) go too soft for this
use [8]. Flour is also made via soaking, fermenting, and steaming
pretreatments, reportedly giving better economic returns than either
the pounded dish or fried crisps [8]. Cooked roots are fed to pigs, and
fresh leaves have been shown in at least one trial to replace up to
half the soybean protein in pig feed [10].

**Finding 9 — two cultivar groups with distinct harvested products.**
A Brazilian leaf-production type is grown specifically for its leaves,
cut from 60-75 days after planting and bunched; the leaves lose market
quality within just 24 hours and are always pre-cooked before sale.
Documented leaf yield runs around 2.0 kg per plant per cycle, converted
by one source to roughly 40 t/ha. This type needs partial shade
specifically — full sun plus heat burns the leaves and raises calcium
oxalate content further, which is also higher in the dry season.
Critically, cutting the leaves prevents the underground rhizome from
filling out: rhizomes can only be harvested at 7-8 months from plants
whose leaves were never cut, creating a genuine either/or tradeoff
between the leaf harvest and the root harvest on the same plant.
Separately, a group of main-corm types exists (including a yellow-
fleshed Puerto Rican type used grated into traditional fried dough
dishes) where the main corm itself, rather than offshoot cormels, is
the eaten part; no separate cycle, yield, or storage data specific to
this group were found, so the general tannia values are assumed to
apply.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season, vegetatively propagated root
  crop; one regional source describes a cooler temperature preference
  that conflicts with the broader reference range (Finding 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Either a single full lift at 8-13 months, or
  repeated partial "milking" of the largest cormels from a standing
  plant every 3 months over about 1.5-2 years before renewal; a
  separate leaf-only harvest pattern applies to the leaf-production
  cultivar group (Finding 1, 9).
- **Climate:** Tropical and subtropical lowland zones, up to about
  2,000 m with falling yields at altitude; not frost-tolerant (Finding
  5).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — all parts carry calcium oxalate
  raphides and must be cooked (Finding 8).
- **Pollination:** Not required; the crop is propagated vegetatively
  and flowers only rarely, with fruiting and seed set rarer still
  (gibberellic acid can be used to induce flowering for breeding
  purposes) (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Sole-crop yields in the general range of
  12-20 t/ha are documented, with the best selected clones reaching up
  to roughly 33 t/ha and intercropped yields under an established
  coconut canopy running noticeably lower; national agricultural
  statistics typically lump this crop together with taro, making a
  reliable species-specific global figure hard to obtain (Finding 4;
  see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Rainfall need is roughly 1,400-2,000 mm through the
  growing season; the crop cannot tolerate waterlogged soil at all,
  since wet ground directly favors the crop's defining root-rot disease
  (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Planted on raised, well-drained ridges with drains
  below furrow level, using head-setts, main-corm pieces, suckers, seed
  cormels, or tissue-culture plantlets; planting material is
  fungicide-dipped and sourced only from healthy plants (Finding 2, 3).
- **Labor:** Weeding is critical in the first several months after
  planting; ridging, earthing-up, and the periodic "milking" harvest
  technique are all distinct hand-labor operations beyond the land
  preparation and full-harvest work common to other root crops.
- **Soil:** Preferred pH 5.5-6.5; tolerates slightly saline soils;
  requires drained, non-waterlogged conditions and benefits from a crop
  rotation of at least 3 years to manage the crop's major soil-borne
  disease (Finding 3, 5, 6).
- **Stand life:** About 1 year under a full-lift harvest pattern, or
  roughly 2 years when milked periodically before the planting is
  renewed (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A serious soil-borne root-rot disease is
  the crop's defining threat, with resistance varying by type; other
  documented problems include a viral disease, bacterial blight, two
  further fungal tuber rots, and specific beetle pests (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** White and purple/red color types differ mainly in
  maturity timing and disease tolerance; a Brazilian leaf-production
  type and a group of main-corm types represent genuinely distinct
  cultivation and harvest patterns within the same species (Finding 1,
  6, 9; cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Either a single full lift, or periodic partial
  "milking" of the largest cormels without uprooting the plant; a
  leaf-only harvest pattern applies to the leaf-production cultivar
  group, which forecloses the rhizome harvest on the same plant (Finding
  1, 9).
- **Storage:** Eating cormels keep roughly 3 months stored dry, cool,
  and well ventilated, but must never be stored below 7°C, as chilling
  injury sets in below that threshold; seed cormels are handled
  separately via sun-curing and burial in dry sand or soil for 4-5
  months (Finding 7).

## Hazards

A serious soil-borne root-rot disease is the crop's defining
agricultural threat, controlled only through prevention (clean planting
material, rotation, drainage, fungicide dips); other documented
problems include a viral disease, bacterial blight, two further fungal
tuber rots, and specific beetle pests (Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — all parts of this plant carry calcium oxalate
raphides and must be cooked before eating; raw consumption causes
irritation. Oxalate content is higher in leaves and petioles than in
cormel flesh, and is further elevated by dry-season growing conditions
and by sun/heat stress in the leaf-production cultivar group. Deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 8, 9).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** head-setts, corm setts, or seed cormels → planted on
  drained ridges → full lift at 8-13 months, or periodic milking of
  the largest cormels from 6 months onward over roughly 1.5-2 years,
  with tops and main corms retained as planting stock. Leaves are cut
  from about 6 weeks after planting. Harvested cormels are graded,
  rinsed, and dried, then cool-stored for about 3 months, or (for seed
  cormels specifically) sun-cured and buried in dry sand for 4-5
  months. Cormels are also boiled and pounded into a traditional
  fufu-style dish, or peeled, sliced, and dried into flour.
- Not covered by this research in detail: deep-frozen pulp processing;
  a traditional pounded dish with a side soup whose exact genus
  identity the sources do not settle; tissue-culture propagation
  technique.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, since
harvesting them kills the whole plant and disturbs soil organisms
along with it. No other dedicated religious or ritual use specific to
this crop was found in this research pass.

## Open research questions

- The crop's preferred temperature range is given quite differently
  across sources — one broad regional reference gives 25-29°C
  preferred, while a specific technical growing guide describes the
  crop as preferring notably cooler temperatures around 21°C — not
  resolved here.
- Whether commercial production favors full sun or partial shade is
  given differently across sources: one source describes commercial
  growing as typically full-sun, while another reports measurably
  better yields under 25-50% partial shade.
- Calorie and protein composition figures differ substantially across
  three independent food-composition sources for what should be the
  same general product — not reconciled here, and the reported very
  high vitamin C figure in the primary source is treated as a probable
  outlier.
- Cold-storage guidance conflicts somewhat: retail guidance treats 7°C
  as a hard floor below which chilling injury sets in, while a separate
  propagule-storage trial found browning injury occurring even at a
  notably warmer temperature after extended storage — these may reflect
  different plant tissues (eating cormels vs. seed propagules) rather
  than a true conflict.
- No per-pick yield data exists for the periodic "milking" harvest
  technique; the per-pick yield figures used in this entry are a rough
  estimate based on the total yield over a full 2-year milking cycle
  compared to a single full-lift harvest.
- No reliable species-specific global yield statistic exists, since
  national agricultural statistics for this crop are commonly
  aggregated together with taro rather than reported separately.
- The exact leaf-yield penalty on rhizome size caused by leaf cutting
  in the leaf-production cultivar group was not quantified in any
  source found during this research pass.
- No cycle, yield, or storage data specific to the main-corm cultivar
  group were found; this entry assumes the general cormel-type values
  apply, which may not be accurate.
- Genome size for this crop was not found in this research pass.
- Frost and extreme-heat thresholds beyond the general "not frost-
  tolerant" characterization were not researched in detail.
- Whether any wild-gathered use of this genus exists was not
  established in this research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric content of roughly 1,340 kcal/kg and protein of roughly 25
g/kg are derived from one source's composition figure (about 134
kcal/100g, with protein at the midpoint of a 1.3-3.7 g/100g range),
scaled up to a per-kilogram basis; two other independently sourced food-
composition database entries report substantially lower calorie and
protein figures for nominally the same product, giving a much wider
plausible range of roughly 980-1,340 kcal/kg and 8-37 g protein/kg.

A good-yield figure of roughly 16,000 kg/ha is derived as the midpoint
of a documented 12-20 t/ha sole-crop yield range, broadly consistent
with a separate regional source's reported 14-15 t/ha figure.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage length, seasonal
water-use index) are estimated by analogy to upland taro grown in a
similarly humid tropical climate on similarly drained land, since no
standard crop-water-use reference includes a dedicated row for this
crop; this analogy likely understates this crop's actual water
requirement somewhat, since this crop is often grown under shade
(which would lower evapotranspiration) while its documented rainfall
need includes an allowance for runoff and drainage, not just crop
water use directly. A root-depth estimate of 0.35 m and a moisture-
depletion-allowance estimate of 0.40 (between taro's lower tolerance
and more drought-tolerant root crops generally) together give a
drought-buffer estimate of about 4 days before yield penalty begins.

Planting density (roughly 9,000-12,800 plants/ha) is derived from
several documented spacing patterns across sources, converted to a
per-hectare basis; a separate regional source independently reports a
similar 9,000-10,000 plants/ha figure.

A storage-duration estimate of 75 days is derived conservatively from
a documented "about 3 months" figure for eating cormels, weighted down
somewhat since separately documented retail shelf life is only about
30 days.

A per-pick yield estimate for the periodic "milking" harvest technique
(roughly 2-5 t/ha per pick, across about 6 picks over a 2-year cycle)
is derived by assuming total yield over the full milking cycle runs
1.0-1.5 times a single full-lift harvest, since no direct per-pick
yield trial was found.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 5.3 g/m²/day) is derived
from the estimated good yield divided by a representative 300-day
growing period.
