## Naming and scope

Cultivated carrot, *Daucus carota* subsp. *sativus* (Apiaceae), 2n = 18
[3]. The broader *D. carota* species complex includes 13 subspecies,
12 of them wild, including wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) [3]. The
cultivated carrot itself splits into two groups [3][15]:

- **Western (carotene) carrots** — unbranched yellow, orange, or red
  roots; by far the more commercially important group worldwide.
  Normally biennial, but often grown as an annual in the tropics.
- **Eastern (anthocyanin) carrots** — branched roots, yellow to
  purple-black, flowering in their first year without needing a cold
  period. Believed to originate in Afghanistan, spreading to the
  Mediterranean and Western Europe in the 11th-14th centuries and to
  China, India, and Japan in the 14th-17th centuries.

Both groups are treated here as cultivar groups within one crop entry.
The eastern group has its own distinctive product (a fermented black-
carrot drink, kanji) and flowering behavior.

This crop shares general biennial-root-crop biology (cold-triggered
bolting, a distinct seed-crop cycle) with sugar beet, a related root
crop this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one root, three harvest stages, and a long cold store.**
Bunched carrots (harvested with tops still attached) keep only about
8-12 days under refrigeration at high humidity, since the tops
themselves limit shelf life [5]. Young, immature fresh-market carrots
(topped) keep 4-6 weeks near freezing, or a somewhat shorter window at
slightly warmer refrigeration, especially if promptly hydrocooled in
chlorinated water after harvest [5]. Mature topped carrots keep
substantially longer — roughly 5-9 months near freezing at very high
humidity if cooled promptly, with slightly different ranges given by
different sources [3][5][8]. Humidity must stay just below 100% to
avoid condensation damage. Sprouting is minimal for the first few
months of cold storage but becomes objectionable after about 7 months;
at warmer storage temperatures, decay and sprouting both develop much
faster [5]. A particularly distinctive hazard is that ethylene exposure
during storage makes carrots bitter, through formation of a specific
bitter compound (an isocoumarin); even a very low ethylene
concentration is enough to cause perceptible bitterness within about
two weeks, and common ethylene sources include other stored produce
like apples and pears, as well as engine exhaust; immature carrots and
cut pieces are the most sensitive to this effect [5][8]. Controlled-
atmosphere storage is not recommended for this crop, since both very
low oxygen and elevated carbon dioxide levels promote decay [5].

**Finding 2 — salt-sensitive and shallow-watered.** Carrots are rated
salt-sensitive, with yield loss beginning at a fairly low salinity
threshold and increasing steeply per unit of additional salinity beyond
that point [2]. A separate field study found yield loss setting in at
a somewhat higher salinity threshold but an even steeper loss rate per
unit beyond it — the two findings are not fully reconciled [2]. A
standard international crop-water-use reference gives root-zone depth
of roughly 0.5-1.0 m with a fairly conservative soil-moisture-depletion
allowance, implying that the crop responds to water stress relatively
early rather than tolerating prolonged drying [1]. Irrigating during
dry spells specifically helps prevent irregular, malformed root shape
[3].

**Finding 3 — cold triggers flowering, but only once the plant has
enough leaves.** Plants only become sensitive to a cold-triggered
flowering response after reaching a minimum leaf count (roughly 8
leaves in one source, 7.5 leaves in another) [3][7]. Cultivars bred to
resist premature flowering (bolting) under temperate conditions
generally need several weeks of sustained cool temperature to flower
at all, and the flower stalk only develops after exposure below a
specific cool-temperature threshold [3][4]. One trial found cultivated
carrots flowered only after a full 12 weeks of cold exposure, while
wild carrots growing alongside them flowered under every tested cold
treatment — a notable difference in cold requirement between the wild
and cultivated forms [6]. Tropical and subtropical carrot types behave
quite differently: they can bolt simply under long day length, even
before their roots have thickened at all, and local tropical cultivars
specifically bolt once nighttime temperatures drop below a fairly mild
threshold — a genuinely different triggering mechanism from the cold-
accumulation response of temperate types [3]. Eastern-group carrots
flower in their first year regardless. Successful seed set additionally
requires average daytime temperatures to stay below a moderate
threshold; even a modest rise in minimum temperature during the cold-
exposure period has been shown in one model to measurably reduce final
seed yield [3][6].

**Finding 4 — temperature governs root color and quality.** The
optimal air temperature range for growth is moderate (roughly 16-24°C);
soil temperatures above about 25°C slow growth and produce fibrous
roots with reduced carotene content [3]. In tropical regions,
economically viable yields generally require growing at meaningfully
higher elevation. Roots typically mature 70-120 days after sowing
(somewhat faster, 70-85 days, in some Asian growing conditions), with
maturity indicated by orange coloration extending all the way to the
root's blunt tip [3].

**Finding 5 — a seed crop that readily crosses with a common wild
roadside weed.** Carrot seed can be produced either by a seed-to-seed
method (sowing and overwintering plants directly in the field) or a
root-to-seed method (harvesting roots, selecting against poor color,
splits, forking, and rough skin, then replanting the best roots) [4].
Isolation distances from other carrot seed crops are substantial, and
crucially, cultivated carrots cross readily with wild carrot (Queen
Anne's lace), whose pollen is identified as a major cause of seed-stock
quality deterioration over time [4]. Seed forms on umbels in a specific
order (primary, secondary, tertiary), with the primary umbel producing
the most and heaviest seed; seed ripens 40-50 days after petals drop,
and harvest timing is based on the earliest primary-umbel seed turning
ripe and beginning to drop, with harvest often done while dew is still
on the plants specifically to reduce seed shattering [3][4][6]. A
distinctive processing step called "debearding" (removing spines from
the seed coat) must happen before the seed can be properly screened
[4]. Documented seed yields vary substantially by type: open-pollinated
temperate types produce considerably more seed per hectare than
tropical or Asiatic types, and hybrid seed production yields notably
less than open-pollinated seed production [3][4][6]. F1 hybrids are
steadily replacing older open-pollinated cultivars across several major
production regions, using one of two different cytoplasmic male-
sterility (CMS) systems favored respectively by European and American
seed companies; commercial CMS seed production has historically been
plagued by unpredictable failures, attributed to non-random foraging
behavior of pollinating insects between the different parent rows
needed for hybrid seed production [3][9].

**Finding 6 — labor: hand harvest dominates in smallholder systems.**
Hand digging and pulling in Indian smallholder conditions has been
measured at several hundred person-hours per hectare, with substantial
variation between regions; introducing a tractor-drawn mechanical
digger cut harvest labor dramatically, by well over an order of
magnitude, in at least one documented comparison [12]. A single
weeding-with-earthing-up pass several weeks after sowing, plus several
irrigation events, was documented in one Indian field trial [12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial root crop grown as an
  annual for root production; eastern-group and many tropical types
  flower in their first year without needing a cold period (Finding 3,
  5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest, taken at one of three distinct
  market stages from the same sowing — bunched (tops on, very young),
  young topped (immature fresh market), or mature topped — with tops
  and immaturity directly determining post-harvest shelf life (Finding
  1).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate to Mediterranean and arid (irrigated)
  climates for western-type carrots; tropical highland cultivation
  (generally requiring meaningful elevation) for warm-climate
  production (Finding 2, 4).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop
  specifically requires insect pollination and careful isolation from
  both other carrot seed crops and wild carrot populations (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** A world-average yield figure for this crop
  is difficult to source precisely, since international agricultural
  statistics commonly lump carrots together with turnips; recent
  combined global figures suggest a world average in the mid-30s
  t/ha, with well-managed crops reaching considerably higher and
  smallholder tropical production often much lower (see Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** Root-zone depth is moderate (roughly 0.5-1.0 m) with
  a fairly conservative soil-moisture-depletion allowance before yield
  is affected; irregular or split roots result from uneven
  soil moisture, particularly dry spells followed by sudden heavy
  watering (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded, shallow (1-2 cm deep) in close
  drills or bands, into a deep, loose, stone-free seedbed; seedlings
  are later thinned to a specific in-row spacing (Finding 2; see
  general cultivation practice below).
- **Labor:** Hand harvest in smallholder systems is labor-intensive,
  measured at several hundred person-hours per hectare in at least one
  well-documented regional study, though mechanical digging equipment
  can cut this very substantially (Finding 6).
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained, stone-free, fertile sandy soil is
  strongly preferred; heavy clay produces malformed roots. Optimum pH
  is moderately acidic to neutral; the crop is rated salt-sensitive
  (Finding 2, 4).
- **Stand life:** Grown as an annual for root production (the plant
  itself is biennial, with a distinct multi-season seed-crop cycle)
  (Finding 3, 5).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Several fungal leaf blights, a root-knot
  nematode, numerous viral diseases, a soil-dwelling water-mold genus
  causing cavity spot and root forking, and a specific root-feeding
  fly pest (notably absent from at least one major tropical growing
  region) are documented (see Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** Several distinct western market-type groups (short
  and round, half-long, and long storage types) plus the entirely
  distinct eastern anthocyanin group; F1 hybrids are steadily
  displacing open-pollinated cultivars in several major production
  regions (Finding 5; cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Taken at one of three stages from a single sowing —
  bunched, young, or fully mature — each with its own harvest method,
  commodity use, and shelf life (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Shelf life depends heavily on harvest stage and whether
  tops remain attached, ranging from just over a week for bunched
  carrots up to many months for properly cooled, high-humidity mature
  topped-root storage; ethylene exposure during storage is a
  distinctive and significant quality hazard, causing bitterness even
  at very low concentrations (Finding 1).

## Hazards

Documented pests and diseases include fungal leaf blights, a root-knot
nematode, numerous viral diseases (including diseases spread by
aphids), armyworm, a root-feeding fly pest (absent from at least one
major tropical growing region), and a soil-dwelling water-mold genus
causing cavity spot and root forking, whose inoculum can persist in
soil for years, making crop rotation an important management tool.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw carrot contains an allergenic protein related
to a well-known birch-pollen cross-reactive allergen family, most often
causing mild oral allergy symptoms in sensitized individuals, though
systemic reactions have been documented; cooked carrot is often well
tolerated by the same individuals. Deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — excessive carrot consumption (very roughly on the
order of several carrots a day, sustained over weeks) can cause a
benign, dose-dependent yellow-orange skin discoloration (carotenemia),
which resolves once intake decreases; and carrots contain bitter
polyacetylene compounds that show cytotoxic effects in laboratory cell
studies at high doses, though a majority of this compound is lost
during a typical boiling preparation. Both are deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

A visually similar wild plant family includes genuinely lethal
look-alikes (poison hemlock and water hemlock) that pose a real
foraging hazard distinct from the cultivated crop, worth noting as a
general caution.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → sown → bunched, young topped, or mature roots,
  depending on target market and harvest timing. Mature roots are
  washed, hydrocooled, and cold-stored; a portion of washed roots is
  processed into "baby-cut" carrot pieces (a specific, well-documented
  commercial product involving cutting, peeling, and polishing
  irregular roots into a uniform small shape); culls go to juice
  production or animal feed. Eastern-group black carrots are
  traditionally fermented with mustard seed and salt into a tangy
  drink (kanji) over several days. The seed crop itself follows a
  separate cycle: roots are either selected and replanted, or plants
  are sown and overwintered directly, with mature seed eventually
  threshed, debearded, and cleaned; carrot seed oil can also be
  produced by steam distillation.
- Not covered by this research: canned and frozen carrot preparation
  in detail; cooked dishes generally; dehydrated carrot, carrot powder,
  or carrot-derived colorants; the technical details of CMS hybrid seed
  production beyond what is noted in Finding 5.

A long-standing Ashkenazi Jewish tradition includes sliced carrots
(often cut into round, coin-like pieces symbolizing prosperity, tied to
a Yiddish word meaning "increase") as a festival food at the Jewish
New Year, including in a traditional sweet stew preparation. A separate
tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, since harvesting
them kills the whole plant and disturbs soil organisms along with it.
Black-carrot kanji is also specifically associated with a North Indian
spring festival.

## Open research questions

- Salinity-tolerance figures conflict between a standard international
  reference (a lower threshold, steeper loss rate per unit beyond it)
  and a separate field study (a higher threshold, even steeper loss
  rate) — not reconciled.
- Storage duration for mature topped roots is given somewhat
  differently across three separate sources (a range of roughly 100
  days up to about 9 months) — likely reflecting different storage
  conditions or quality endpoints rather than a true contradiction, but
  not fully reconciled here.
- Seed yield figures for open-pollinated types vary meaningfully
  across sources (from a few hundred up to around a thousand kg/ha) —
  likely reflecting real differences between growing regions and
  cultivar types rather than a single representative figure.
- Potassium removal per tonne of root is estimated two different ways
  from two different sources, producing substantially different
  figures — not reconciled; a dedicated nutrient-removal study specific
  to a given growing region would be more reliable than either
  estimate used here.
- Root-zone depth is described two different ways across sources — a
  shallow taproot depth figure versus a deeper effective "available
  water" rooting depth figure used in standard water-use references;
  these likely describe different things (the visible taproot versus
  the effective water-extraction zone) rather than a true conflict.
- Whether a specific cold-temperature range is actually more effective
  at triggering flowering than a wider range of cool temperatures is
  disputed between at least two cited studies.
- World-average yield statistics for this crop specifically are
  difficult to source cleanly, since major international agricultural
  databases commonly report carrots and turnips as a combined category
  — the estimate used below should be treated as approximate for this
  reason.
- Whether the eastern (anthocyanin) carrot group is distinct enough
  — in flowering behavior, pigment biology, and regional market/product
  identity — to warrant treatment as an entirely separate crop entry
  rather than a cultivar group within this one is a genuine open
  question; it is kept here as a cultivar group for now.
- Labor-hour figures for every operation besides harvest are not well
  sourced and should be treated as rough estimates.
- Whether avoidance of carrots is a genuine dietary practice in some
  Hindu religious communities (as some popular sources suggest) was
  not independently verified.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric and protein content (roughly 410 kcal/kg and 9.3 g protein/kg)
are derived from a detailed commodity composition analysis: available
carbohydrate, protein, and fat content give an energy density toward
the lower end of a plausible range (roughly 290-440 kcal/kg across
different composition sources), with the adopted figure sitting
comfortably within that range.

A world-average yield of 36,000 kg/ha is adopted from a recent combined
global statistic (which, as noted above, lumps carrots with turnips).
Good yield is estimated at 50,000 kg/ha and exceptional at 80,000
kg/ha, both consistent with documented yield ranges for well-managed
production in Europe and the United States; poor yield is estimated at
8,000 kg/ha, consistent with the low end of documented smallholder
tropical Asian yields.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived from a standard international crop-
water-use reference's regional stage-length tables, using a
Mediterranean-climate stage breakdown as the representative case; this
produces a seasonal water-use index of about 139 (days-equivalent) and
a drought-buffer estimate of about 7 days before yield penalty begins,
calculated from standard soil-water-holding and root-depth assumptions.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of about 133 is derived by
dividing a representative open-pollinated temperate seed-crop yield by
a standard root-crop seeding rate; the true range spans roughly 56
(lower-yielding Asiatic seed types) to 250 (top open-pollinated seed
yields), reflecting real differences between seed-production systems
rather than a single fixed figure.

Days from sowing to seed harvest (roughly 365-420 days) are derived
from the root-to-seed production cycle: an initial 70-120 day root-
growing period, followed by winter storage and replanting, several
months to flowering and seed-ripening, giving a total cycle of roughly
12-14 months for a spring-sown, overwintered, summer-harvested seed
crop.

Potassium removal per tonne of fresh root is estimated at roughly 0.7
kg/t, derived from a tropical-highland nutrient-uptake study; this
figure conflicts with a separate, higher estimate derived by a
different method from a different composition source, and both are
presented here as open questions rather than reconciled into one
figure.

Edible yield per unit area per day is derived at roughly 36 g/m²/day,
based on the adopted world-average yield divided by a typical 100-day
growing season; at higher yield and a longer season, this rises to
roughly 42 g/m²/day.

Genome size (roughly 0.48 pg, 1C) is derived from a published genome-
assembly size estimate for a sequenced doubled-haploid reference line,
converted using the standard base-pairs-per-picogram conversion factor.
