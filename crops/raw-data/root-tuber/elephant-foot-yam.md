# Elephant Foot Yam (*Amorphophallus paeoniifolius*)

## Naming and scope

*Amorphophallus paeoniifolius* (whitespot giant arum; also known by
many regional names including suran, jimmikand, ol, and chena), family
Araceae [1][3]. **Not** a true yam (*Dioscorea*) and **not** konjac
(*A. konjac*), though both are sometimes loosely called "elephant
yam." A 2017 genetic study places the species' origin in Island
Southeast Asia, with three independent domestication events, rather
than in India as traditionally assumed [1].

This entry treats two distinct cultivar groups: traditional,
high-oxalate landrace types that require extended boiling or alkaline
leaching to be safely edible, and modern bred selections (chiefly one
widely grown commercial variety) specifically bred to be "free from
acridity" [3][4]. Both groups are modeled together here, with the
non-acrid delta marked throughout, since they differ mainly in
oxalate/acridity level, processing requirements, and named yield
figures rather than in overall crop biology.

Two related species in the same genus — one grown in a multi-year
cycle for gum production, and another wild-collected or semi-
cultivated type — are treated as entirely separate crops by this
project and are not covered here. This crop shares the general aroid
calcium-oxalate hazard and water-use pattern with konjac and taro, two
related root crops this project has also researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the opposite of konjac: a true starch-and-food corm,
not a gum crop.** The corm is roughly 71-74% moisture with 17.3-20.1%
starch, only about 0.14% sugar, 4.19-4.50% crude protein, and
324-374 kJ per 100 g [3]. A separate regional reference gives
somewhat different figures: water 75-79 g, protein 1-5 g, carbohydrate
18 g (of which starch is 4.5-18 g and a gum-like mannan component only
0-9 g), and 420 kJ per 100 g [2]. Crucially, konjac's near-non-caloric
glucomannan-gum story does not apply here: in this species, the
mannan-type compound is only a minor fraction, and the corm is eaten
as a genuine starchy vegetable staple — in curry, mashed, as kebab,
chips, or pickle [1][3]. The leaf (20-22% protein) and the petiole/stem
(12-14% protein) are also used as protein-rich minor vegetables [3].

**Finding 2 — acridity is cultivar-dependent, and the standard fix is
extended boiling.** Traditional landrace types are "highly acrid and
cause irritation in the throat and mouth due to the excessive amount
of calcium oxalate," with needle-shaped calcium-oxalate crystals as
the physical irritant [3][5]. Boiling for 10 minutes cuts soluble
oxalate by roughly 35% and total oxalate by roughly 45%; longer
boiling gave no further significant reduction in one study [5].
Traditional cooking with tamarind (an acid), salt, and turmeric is
standard practice; a brief citric-acid soak during processing also
limits browning and acridity [3]. The leading commercial non-acrid
variety is described as genuinely "free from acridity," and a separate
bred variety has "very good cooking quality" — this distinction is the
basis for treating the two cultivar groups separately in this entry
[3][4]. One earlier source's oxalate figures (a large difference
between acrid and non-acrid types) were not independently verified in
this research pass; a separate pre-soak-plus-boiling study reported
much lower post-treatment oxalate levels [6]. The raw corm and all
plant parts are irritating regardless of cultivar group — this crop
is not raw-edible in either group, though the non-acrid types are
measurably lower-risk, not risk-free.

**Finding 3 — very high yield, with planting-material size as the
main driver.** National average yield in one major producing country
runs about 24.76 t/ha across a large growing area [3]; a regional
reference gives a rainfed average of 22.5 t/ha and a potential of up
to 85 t/ha using large (4 kg) seed pieces [2]. A dedicated tuber-crop
research institute names a realistic range of 40-50 t/ha and a
potential ceiling of 100 t/ha [3]. Yield scales strongly with
seed-corm size (larger seed pieces documented giving dramatically
higher yields) and with plant spacing (documented trials at three
different spacings gave markedly different per-plant corm weights and
per-hectare totals) [2][3]. Single mature corms normally weigh
4-13 kg, occasionally up to 15-25 kg [1][2]. These yield figures
conflict somewhat across sources and are not averaged here, since they
likely reflect genuinely different growing conditions and inputs.

**Finding 4 — the lowest propagation multiplication rate of the
tropical tuber crops, with a specific technique to improve it.**
Because the planting material and the harvested food organ are the
same corm, "there is competition between ware corm and seed corm,"
and the multiplication rate is described as the lowest among tropical
tuber crops generally, at roughly 1:4 — meaning each seed corm planted
yields only about four times its weight in harvestable corm, most of
which must then be set aside as the next planting's seed [3]; roughly
25% of the harvest is typically held back as seed stock under
conventional practice [2]. A specific "minisett" technique developed
by a tuber-crop research institute — using small (roughly 100 g) cut
pieces, each carrying part of the corm's central bud ring, planted at
closer spacing — raises the multiplication rate to roughly 1:15,
producing daughter seed corms averaging around 417 g. Conventional
planting instead uses whole roughly 500 g corms or 750 g cut pieces at
wider spacing [3].

**Finding 5 — a single-season crop gated by a 45-60 day corm
dormancy.** The crop takes roughly 8-9 months under rainfed conditions,
or 6-7 months under irrigation [3]. Harvested corms are dormant for
45-60 days after harvest, so rainfed crops are planted in late winter
to early spring and sprout with the arrival of seasonal pre-monsoon
rain [3]. The corm has good in-ground and post-harvest storage life,
giving growers a long practical harvest window [3]. Unlike konjac, this
crop does not require a multi-year field cycle — figures suggesting
multi-year cycles in some reference sources apply to seed-grown plants
or to a different species in the same genus, not to this crop's normal
clonal production cycle [2].

**Finding 6 — a serious disease complex, all carried in clonal seed
stock.** A specific soil- and seed-borne fungal collar rot disease is
described as "the most prevalent and harmful disease" of this crop,
worst under warm, humid weather, heavy wet soils, and physical
wounding; it is managed through clean seed, good drainage, crop
rotation, and biological or chemical fungicide treatment [3][12]. A
mosaic virus, transmitted by two named aphid species and carried in
seed corms, can cut yield by up to 38%, with roughly 5% field incidence
documented in one major producing country; it is managed through
healthy or meristem-cultured seed stock and aphid control [3]. A
post-harvest/storage corm rot, caused by 13 different fungi plus a
bacterial pathogen, causes 25-30% loss [3]. Two nematode species make
this crop one of the most nematode-susceptible tuber crops studied,
causing a further 25-30% storage weight loss that continues even after
harvest [3].

**Finding 7 — warm, humid, rainfed, shade-tolerant, and intolerant of
waterlogging.** Optimum growing temperature is roughly 25-30°C (with
mean annual temperatures up to 30-35°C still suitable); the crop wants
roughly 1,000-1,500 mm of rain over a 6-8 month season, and is
documented growing up to roughly 700-900 m elevation, on well-drained
sandy loam at near-neutral pH, rich in organic matter — heavy clay and
swampy ground are explicitly unsuitable [2][3]. The crop has no
drought resistance and no waterlogging tolerance at all, but is
genuinely shade-tolerant — a major reason it is widely grown as an
intercrop under coconut, banana, areca palm, and coffee [3]. Steady
soil moisture during the corm-bulking period (roughly 4-6 months after
planting) is critical, while a dry spell later in the season actually
helps the corm mature properly [3].

**Finding 8 — every organ of the plant is used, and it is a
genuinely good cash and intercrop.** The corm is eaten fresh, made
into flour, chips, and pickle, and used in traditional medicine for
digestive complaints; the leaf, petiole, and flower bud are all cooked
as vegetables or used as livestock fodder [1][3]. The crop generates
roughly 410 person-days of employment per hectare under one documented
production system [3]. Intercropped with soybean, the leading
commercial non-acrid variety reportedly reached a corm-equivalent
yield of 66.4 t/ha in one trial [3]. The plant's thermogenic, carrion-
scented flowering structure is insect-pollinated and sets red berries
within 24-36 hours of pollination, but the cultivated crop is
routinely lifted for harvest before or regardless of whether it
flowers, and is propagated entirely clonally — flowering plays no role
in the commercial crop cycle [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season, single-harvest corm crop;
  rainfed crops are planted in late winter to early spring and
  harvested roughly 8-9 months later, timed around a 45-60 day
  post-harvest corm dormancy (Finding 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest; one leaf cycle per
  season; the minisett seed-production technique is a distinct
  cultivation path, not a separate maturity stage (Finding 4, 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical and subtropical zones, frost-intolerant, grown
  up to roughly 700-900 m elevation; genuinely shade-tolerant, a
  standard intercrop under tree canopies (Finding 7).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible in either cultivar group — the raw
  corm and all plant parts are irritating due to calcium-oxalate
  crystals; non-acrid bred varieties carry measurably lower, but not
  zero, risk (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not required — the crop is entirely clonal, and
  flowering is irrelevant to the harvested yield (Finding 8).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable, strongly driven by
  seed-piece size and planting density; national averages run around
  22-25 t/ha, with realistic managed yields of 40-50 t/ha and
  documented potential up to 85-100 t/ha; the non-acrid commercial
  variety's developer-claimed yield (50-60 t/ha) runs notably higher
  than measured trial yields (29-37 t/ha) (Finding 3; see also
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No drought tolerance and no waterlogging tolerance
  at all; steady moisture is critical during corm bulking, but a late
  dry spell actually aids maturation (Finding 7).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from whole corms, cut
  setts, minisetts (a specific small-piece technique that
  substantially improves multiplication rate), or daughter cormels,
  planted into prepared pits (Finding 4; establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** A documented whole-crop figure of roughly 410 person-days
  of employment per hectare anchors the labor estimates in this entry;
  pit digging, hand harvesting of multi-kilogram corms, and repeated
  monthly weeding-and-earthing are the heaviest individual operations.
- **Soil:** Near-neutral pH (roughly 6-7.5) on well-drained sandy loam
  rich in organic matter; the crop is described as a "voracious
  feeder," especially of nitrogen and potassium; crop rotation is
  necessary to manage the serious disease complex, since this crop
  should not be grown repeatedly in the same field (Finding 6, 7).
- **Stand life:** Annual lift-and-replant; the corm is dug every
  season, with no standing perennial habit in cultivation.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A severe fungal collar rot disease is the
  most damaging threat; a mosaic virus, a multi-fungus post-harvest
  corm rot, and two damaging nematode species round out a significant
  disease and pest complex, all of which travel with clonal seed stock
  (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** A clear split exists between traditional high-oxalate
  landraces and modern bred non-acrid selections; several named
  commercial varieties exist with documented yield, cooking quality,
  and disease-tolerance differences (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single annual hand harvest of multi-kilogram corms,
  timed to avoid wounding, which invites storage disease (Finding 6,
  8).
- **Storage:** Corms have genuinely good in-ground and post-harvest
  keeping quality when stored upright in a cool, shaded, ventilated
  place at 60-70% moisture; seed corms are specifically cured and then
  covered with dry sand, ash, or rice bran to further reduce storage
  loss (Finding 5; storage detail below).

## Hazards

A severe fungal collar rot disease is described as the most
prevalent and damaging disease of this crop; a mosaic virus, a
multi-fungus post-harvest corm rot, and two damaging nematode species
further threaten both field and stored crop, all traveling with
clonal seed stock (Finding 6). These are agronomic hazards to the
crop, not direct human-food hazards.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the raw corm and all other plant parts of this
crop contain needle-shaped calcium-oxalate crystals that cause real
physical and chemical irritation to the throat and mouth if eaten raw
or undercooked. Traditional high-oxalate landrace types require
extended or repeated boiling, or treatment with acid or alkali, to
become safely edible; even boiling has documented limits (10 minutes
achieves most of the reduction, with little further benefit from
longer boiling). Modern bred non-acrid varieties carry measurably
lower risk but are not confirmed safe to eat raw by any source found.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data (Finding 2).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** corm set, minisett, or cormel planted into a prepared
  pit → grown roughly 6-9 months → harvested by hand → peeled →
  (traditional acrid types) detoxified by repeated or extended boiling
  → cooked food. Alternatively: corm → peeled → sliced → dried into
  chips → milled into flour, used in further value-added products.
  Leaf, petiole, and flower bud are cooked as vegetables or used as
  livestock fodder. A portion of the harvest (roughly 25% under
  conventional practice, less under the minisett technique) is set
  aside and cured as seed stock for the next planting.
- Not covered by this research in detail: specific traditional
  cracker-style and quick-cooking dehydrated corm products; canning or
  freezing (not documented as a notable product for this crop).

In a Hindu festival tradition in parts of eastern South Asia, dishes
made from this crop's corm are customarily eaten during the autumn
festival season. Separately, a tradition in Jainism avoids root
vegetables and underground organs generally, including this crop's
corm, since harvesting them kills the whole plant and disturbs soil
organisms along with it.

## Open research questions

- Energy and protein content figures conflict meaningfully across
  three independent composition sources (roughly 77-89 kcal vs. about
  100 kcal vs. about 61 kcal per 100 g, with correspondingly different
  protein figures) — likely reflecting different moisture bases across
  samples, not resolved here.
- Yield figures conflict across sources by a wide margin (a national
  average around 22-25 t/ha vs. documented potential of 50-100 t/ha)
  — treated here as reflecting the real range between unmanaged
  smallholder production and intensively managed trial conditions,
  not averaged.
- Absolute oxalate content figures for acrid versus non-acrid
  cultivar types differ substantially between an older, less-verified
  source and a more recent treatment study — not fully resolved here.
- The documented elevation ceiling differs slightly across sources
  (roughly 700 m vs. 900 m) — not a major discrepancy.
- Preferred soil pH range differs slightly across sources (6-7 vs.
  6-7.5) — not a major discrepancy.
- No standard international crop-water-use reference includes a
  dedicated row for this crop; the water-use parameters used in this
  entry are estimated by analogy to other aroid root crops.
- Labor-hour figures beyond the single documented whole-crop
  person-days total are estimates, split across operations by
  judgment rather than direct measurement.
- Genome size for this species was not checked in this research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Caloric content (roughly 850 kcal/kg) and protein (roughly 44 g/kg)
are derived from one source's composition figures (324-374 kJ per 100
g, taking the midpoint, and 4.19-4.50% protein), converted to a
per-kilogram basis; this conflicts with a regional reference giving a
notably different energy figure and a wider protein range, and with a
national food-composition table giving a much lower calorie figure for
a higher-moisture sample. The plausible range is roughly 600-1,000
kcal/kg and 20-45 g/kg protein.

Yield tiers for the traditional/acrid cultivar group (worldAvg roughly
25,000 kg/ha, good 45,000 kg/ha, exceptional 70,000 kg/ha, poor 12,000
kg/ha) are anchored on the documented national average and regional
rainfed average, with the "good" and "exceptional" tiers drawn from a
tuber-crop research institute's documented realistic and potential
ranges; the "poor" tier is an estimate for low-input or stressed
conditions below the sourced average.

Yield tiers for the non-acrid cultivar group (worldAvg roughly 40,000
kg/ha, good 50,000 kg/ha, exceptional 60,000 kg/ha, poor 25,000 kg/ha)
are estimated from a developer-claimed yield for the leading commercial
variety (50-60 t/ha) set against measured trial yields running
considerably lower (29-37 t/ha), with the average set at the low-to-
mid point between the claim and the trial data.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water-use index) are derived by analogy to taro and konjac, two
related aroid root crops this project has also researched, combined
with this crop's documented 8-month rainfed growing cycle and its
specific 4-6 month critical corm-bulking window; this crop differs
from taro specifically in being intolerant of flooding, where taro
tolerates it.

Seed-rate figures (roughly 9,000 kg/ha under conventional planting,
or roughly 3,700 kg/ha under the minisett technique) are derived from
documented planting density and piece-weight figures for each method,
cross-checked against a separately documented seed-retention fraction
under conventional practice.

Labor-hour estimates across individual operations are derived by
splitting a single documented whole-crop person-days figure across the
heaviest named operations (pit digging, hand harvest, and repeated
weeding-and-earthing), since no operation-by-operation labor budget
was found.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 15 g/m²/day) is derived
from the estimated yield range divided by the roughly 240-day growing
season.
