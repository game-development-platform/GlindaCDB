# Maca (*Lepidium meyenii*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-26

## Naming and scope

Cultivated *Lepidium meyenii* Walp., mustard family (Brassicaceae)
[1][3]. There is a genuine taxonomic dispute in the literature: some
authors treat the cultivated plant as a distinct species and reserve
the name used here for a wild relative, while a widely cited review
treats wild and cultivated forms as a single species; this entry
follows the latter, more conservative view [1][3]. The crop's
harvested organ is technically a swollen hypocotyl with the upper
taproot attached, usually called simply the "root" in both trade and
research literature, and this entry follows that convention [3][6].

This entry is grounded against two other Brassicaceae storage-root
crops this project has also researched, used as the nearest available
analogy for water-use and rooting parameters, since no dedicated
reference data exists for this species.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a frost-tolerant high-Andean root crop, harvested once
and mostly sun-dried for years of storage.** The crop's traditional
heartland is a high plateau around Lake Junín in Peru, at roughly
3,950-4,450 m elevation, with cultivation reported up to 4,500 m
[3][5][6]. A more recently established Chinese growing region near
Lijiang, Yunnan sits at roughly 3,100 m, with growers there observing
a rule that roots go hollow below about 2,800 m [14]. On the Peruvian
plateau, mean temperatures run 4-7°C with nights down to -10°C, annual
rainfall around 1,400 mm, and relative humidity around 80%; the crop
is documented tolerating night temperatures as low as -7 to -10°C and
midday temperatures of 15-22°C, with photosynthesis peaking at
15-25°C [3][6][7]. A grower report from outside the Andes gives a
notably different cold tolerance (only to about -4°C, with hard
freezes killing the foliage) alongside poor growth above roughly 24°C
in summer — a real conflict with the Andean figures, not resolved
here [2]. Most of the crop is traditionally sun-dried: unwashed roots
are laid out on open ground for roughly 9-10 weeks, or about two
months by another account [3][6][9]. This freeze-thaw cycling during
drying is documented to macerate the root's tissue, releasing
compounds that then combine into the specific alkamide compounds
valued in the dietary-supplement trade — fresh, undried roots contain
almost none of these compounds [9]. Drying also reduces the root's raw
pungency and converts starch to sugars [3]. Properly dried roots
become very hard and keep for years — up to two years in cloth bags,
or four years or more in sacks in a dry storage loft [2][6].

**Finding 2 — a two-year cycle: roots are harvested in year one, and
seed is produced from replanted roots in year two.** In the root
phase, seed is broadcast at the start of the rainy season, seedlings
emerge about a month later, and the plant forms a flat rosette of
10-25 leaves roughly 15 cm tall; frost and the onset of the dry season
kill the leaves 260-280 days after sowing, at which point roots are
pulled, giving an average vegetative-phase length of roughly 8-9
months [1][2][3][6]. In the separate seed phase, growers select
healthy roots of the desired color type, trim off the leaves, and hold
the roots dormant for roughly 2-2.5 months in pits layered with soil
and turf, or in crates in a cool, shaded place; once sprouts reach a
few centimeters and the rains return, the roots are replanted at wide
spacing in small, heavily manured plots, flowering shoots grow to
roughly 60-80 cm, and the plants are cut before their seed pods
shatter, roughly five months later, then shade-dried and threshed —
the plant dies after seeding [3][4][6]. Seed numbers per plant are
reported very differently across sources — from under a thousand
seeds up to roughly 16,000 by weight, described elsewhere simply as
"hundreds to thousands" — an open conflict, though in any case only a
few dozen seed-producing plants are enough to seed an entire
smallholding [1][2][3]. The flowers are self-pollinating in a way that
keeps the pollen from ever leaving the unopened flower, so the various
named color types breed fairly true from seed [2]. Documented
historical seed prices fluctuated considerably, and Peru maintains a
legal export ban on seed, other propagating material, and primary-
processed product of this crop [3].

**Finding 3 — sowing on long-rested grassland, and a traditional
"exhausted soil" belief.** Traditional cultivation uses virgin or
long-rested high-altitude grassland; a 17th-century chronicler wrote
that this crop "burns the earth," leading fields to be rested for over
a decade, with an even longer rest period claimed by another colonial-
era writer [3]. A 2006 farmer survey found only 17% resting land for
ten years or more, with 58% replanting within five years, and Chinese
plantings are documented requiring their own rotation practice — the
true underlying cause of the "exhausted soil" effect (nutrient
depletion, disease buildup, or something else) was not established in
any source read here [3][14]. Land preparation is now usually done by
contract tractor, followed by traditional clod-breaking and leveling
to prevent waterlogging [3][6]. Seeding rates of roughly 3-5 kg/ha are
documented, with the tiny seed mixed with soil, sand, or plant debris
to spread it evenly and then covered by trampling livestock, branches,
or rakes [3][6][11]. Traditional practice on the high plateau uses
neither irrigation nor agrochemicals, though at least one lower-
altitude trial used manure and a full NPK fertilizer program, and
pesticide-residue problems led to a documented shipment confiscation
by Japanese authorities in the early 2000s [3][6][11]. Weeding
requirements are reported as minimal at high altitude but the crop is
documented to compete poorly with weeds when grown at lower elevations
[2][3].

**Finding 4 — yields vary roughly tenfold across sources.** National
Peruvian statistics from the mid-2000s gave a roughly 6.9 t/ha average,
with later national statistics from the mid-2020s implying a slightly
lower average of roughly 5.7 t/ha across thousands of farming families
[3][8]. Documented trial and research yields range far more widely: as
low as 2-3 t/ha under extensive traditional management on one end,
up to 15-16 t/ha on fertile land, and one controlled trial recording
nearly 15 t/ha with a very high fraction of total plant mass going
into the harvested root [3]. A separate regional trial recorded yields
from under 1 t/ha up to nearly 20 t/ha depending on treatment and
sowing month, and a greenhouse trial at high altitude using fermented
alpaca manure as fertilizer reached an estimated 22-23 t/ha [7][11]. A
rough rule of thumb converts about 15 t/ha fresh to about 5 t/ha
dried [1]. Two sources citing the same original 1992 trial give
sharply different yield figures for it — one roughly five times the
other — an unresolved conflict, possibly reflecting a unit
misinterpretation somewhere in the citation chain [3][7]. Up to 20% of
cultivated plants are documented forming no swollen root at all [3].

**Finding 5 — composition, pungent plant compounds, and traditional
cooked preparation.** Fresh-root composition figures vary somewhat by
source, with one quality-standard specification giving moisture below
74%, protein above 3.0%, fat above 2.2%, fiber below 1.6%, and
carbohydrate below 33%, while another source gives fresh moisture over
80% — a real conflict, likely reflecting different cultivars or
measurement timing [6][10]. Dried-root composition is more consistent:
roughly 10% residual moisture, 55-65% carbohydrate, 10-13% protein,
about 2.2% lipid, and 8.5% fiber, with a trade quality standard
requiring dried protein above 8%, moisture below 20%, and energy above
290 kcal per 100 g [3][6]. Two related pungent compounds (plant
defense chemicals typical of this plant family) are responsible for
raw maca's spicy flavor, with one of the two making up roughly 80% of
the total and roughly 1% of fresh root weight [3][4][10]. The root is
traditionally eaten cooked rather than raw: dried roots are soaked
overnight and boiled, or ground into flour; roasted roots serve as a
coffee substitute; and fresh roots are traditionally roasted in
earth ovens built at the edge of the field during harvest [3][4].
Eaten raw, the root tastes spicy-sweet, somewhat like radish [2]. A
documented household consumption pattern describes a family eating
roughly a kilogram of dried maca per week, with smaller roots
preferred for home use [3].

**Finding 6 — an export-driven supplement boom, and a major Chinese
growing expansion.** Only a very small area was under cultivation as
of 1980, prompting international conservation concern over possible
extinction of the crop as a distinct cultivated form [3]. Dietary-
supplement demand drove a sharp boom and then a crash in the early
2000s, leaving substantial unsold dried stock; exports grew
substantially over the following decade, and a formal geographical-
indication protection for the traditional Peruvian growing region was
registered under an international system in 2012 [3][5][20]. A
specific Chinese growing region expanded from a very small planted
area before 2011 to a dramatically larger one by 2014, eventually
accounting for roughly 80% of China's total production, on a calendar
of spring nursery sowing, transplanting about a month later, and
harvest in early winter [14]. Documented trade prices for Chinese
dried maca fell sharply within the space of a single year as the
market adjusted [14]. Peruvian institutions have publicly accused
Chinese firms of illegally exporting seed and other propagating
material in violation of Peru's export ban, and a rapid Chinese
production expansion driven by domestic demand was followed by a
price collapse later in the decade [7][18].

**Finding 7 — history and ritual use.** Archaeological evidence places
roots at a high-altitude cave site from as early as roughly 1900-1600
BC, growing measurably larger by roughly 1000-300 BC, with a separate
site's evidence for actual cultivation (rather than wild gathering)
dated to roughly 700-600 BC — the two dating estimates for first
cultivation conflict somewhat, not resolved here [1][3][10]. Colonial-
era Spanish records document a substantial annual tribute payment made
in this crop by one Andean district in the late 16th century, and a
mid-17th-century religious document records the crop being used
alongside maize and potato in ritual practice in the same region [3].
A modern legislative proposal asserts that the crop was offered to
Inca-era deities alongside maize and potato, though this specific
claim was only found in a low-quality secondary source [21]. A
traditional folk belief, recorded by a mid-20th-century researcher,
holds that the root is eaten by women wishing to conceive, and a
majority of farmers surveyed in the 2000s reported believing in some
fertility-related effect of the crop [3]. A modern secular festival
celebrating the crop is held annually in its traditional growing
region and in the national capital [21].

**Finding 8 — documented health and regulatory concerns.** A European
food-safety authority ruled raw maca flour unsafe in 2002, though EU
regulators later agreed the crop itself does not require novel-food
authorization [3]. A specific trace compound found in the root has
been proposed as a weak enzyme inhibitor and co-mutagen, though at
very low concentration [1][10]. A clinical trial of a substantial
daily dose of fresh micro-pulverized maca over 60 days found no
toxicity, and adverse events in the wider literature are described as
rare and generally mild [10]. Market adulteration has been documented
in two forms: wheat flour substituted into Peruvian product in the
early 2000s, and a prescription erectile-dysfunction drug compound
found mixed into Chinese product in 2015 [3][14]. The crop is also fed
to livestock as a fertility supplement, and leftover leaves are left
in the field as animal feed, with no documented animal-health hazard
found in the sources used here [1][3].

**Finding 9 — pests and diseases.** A downy mildew pathogen specific
to this crop was formally described in 2017; it is restricted to the
crop's native growing area but has since been introduced to South
Korea, and quarantine measures have been suggested to prevent further
spread [13]. The mildew is not considered a serious threat under
traditional high-altitude growing conditions, though fungicide use is
now occasionally reported even there [3]. Other documented pests
include a root-feeding fly, aphids, thrips, a moth whose larvae also
attack potato, and a powdery mildew in one regional trial, along with
birds, rodents, and (in one non-Andean growing report) crickets
[2][11]. Waterlogged, sodden soil is documented to damage the roots
directly, which is why fields are carefully leveled against standing
water [2][6].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A biennial root crop grown in a two-year
  cycle — roots harvested in year one, seed produced from replanted
  roots in year two; a frost-tolerant rosette crop of the high Andean
  plateau, also grown in at least one lower-latitude, higher-altitude
  region elsewhere (Finding 1, 2).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single annual root harvest at the end of the
  growing season, when frost and the onset of the dry season kill the
  foliage (Finding 2).
- **Climate:** Tropical highland (the traditional high Andean
  plateau) and subtropical highland (the newer high-elevation Chinese
  growing region) zones; only marginal in cool-temperate gardens at
  low elevation (Finding 1; climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible but pungent; traditionally eaten cooked,
  dried, or roasted rather than raw (Finding 5).
- **Pollination:** Not required — flowers self-pollinate before
  opening, keeping named color types breeding true (Finding 2).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable across sources, from
  roughly 2 to over 20 t/ha of fresh root depending on management
  intensity and site (Finding 4; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Traditionally rain-fed with no irrigation; standing
  water or waterlogged soil damages the root directly, so fields are
  leveled and drained (Finding 3, 9; water-use detail below).
- **Establishment:** Traditionally direct-seeded by broadcasting over
  rested grassland and covering by trampling livestock; also raised
  in nursery trays and transplanted in greenhouse and some Chinese
  production (Finding 3).
- **Labor:** The least well-documented area of this crop's agronomy;
  only one low-quality cost table was found, with harvest and
  extended sun-drying as the largest apparent labor components
  (labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Acidic soils, often below pH 5.5, light sandy loam, low
  organic matter; traditionally grown without fertilizer on long-
  rested land (Finding 3).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as a single-season root crop; the plant
  itself is biennial, with the seed-production phase occurring in a
  separate replanted crop the following year (Finding 2).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A crop-specific downy mildew (normally
  minor at high altitude but now sometimes treated with fungicide), a
  root-feeding fly, aphids, thrips, a shared potato pest moth, and
  bird and rodent damage to drying roots (Finding 9; hazard detail
  below).
- **Cultivars:** No named cultivars in the conventional sense; growers
  instead maintain a range of named color ecotypes (cultivar detail
  below), plus a distinct Chinese growing population established from
  Peruvian seed.
- **Harvest:** Hand-pulled once foliage dies back at the end of the
  growing season, over a harvest window of roughly six weeks
  (Finding 2).
- **Storage:** Fresh roots keep only a few months refrigerated at
  high humidity; the overwhelming majority of the crop is instead
  sun-dried for weeks in the field and then stored dry for years
  (Finding 1).

Climate detail: the traditional growing region sits at a specific
southern latitude band and a very high elevation range; the crop's
cold tolerance is disputed between sources (a very cold-tolerant
figure from Andean research versus a notably less cold-tolerant figure
from a non-Andean grower), and it performs poorly in warm summers
outside its native highland climate.

Water-use detail: no official crop-coefficient curve exists for this
species; one is estimated here by analogy to two related Brassicaceae
storage-root crops this project has also researched, since this
crop's flat, low rosette canopy shades the ground less than either
analogy crop and its traditional growing environment has notably low
evapotranspiration demand. The crop is considered moderately drought-
tolerant, consistent with its traditional rain-fed cultivation and a
growing cycle that finishes in the dry season.

Labor detail: the only labor-cost data found comes from a single low-
quality historical student field-trial report, whose harvest-labor
figure appears implausibly low relative to the same report's own cost
breakdown; harvest and the extended multi-week sun-drying and
winnowing process are judged the largest true labor components based
on the sheer duration of hand digging and the sustained field
attention drying requires.

Cultivar detail: growers maintain named color-based ecotypes ranging
from white through yellow, cream, red, purple, black, and grey, plus
several intermediate root shapes, differing mainly in seed-set
success and specific end use (one dark-colored type, for instance, is
specifically used for a traditional distilled liquor) rather than in
any other agronomic trait documented here.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw roots contain pungent plant defense
compounds (glucosinolates) typical of this plant family, which cause
the characteristic spicy raw flavor; the crop is traditionally always
cooked, dried, or roasted before eating. A trace compound with weak
enzyme-inhibiting activity is also present at low concentration, and
rare, generally mild adverse events have been documented in clinical
use. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 5, 8).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — market adulteration of dried and powdered
product has been documented in two separate forms (wheat flour
substitution, and an undisclosed prescription drug compound mixed
into supplement powder), alongside pesticide-residue problems severe
enough to trigger at least one international shipment confiscation.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of market-
safety data (Finding 6, 8).

A crop-specific downy mildew pathogen, generally minor pressure under
traditional high-altitude growing conditions but now sometimes
fungicide-treated, is the main documented disease; a root-feeding fly,
aphids, thrips, a shared potato pest moth, and bird and rodent damage
to drying roots are also documented pest pressures (Finding 9).

Peru maintains a legal export ban on seed, other propagating material,
and primary-processed product of this crop, a regulatory constraint
worth noting for anyone sourcing planting material (Finding 2).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → broadcast onto rested high-altitude grassland at
  the start of the rains → fresh roots, with leaves left in the field
  for livestock. Alternatively: seed → nursery tray or bed seedlings →
  transplanted → fresh roots. Fresh roots → color-selected → held
  dormant for roughly two months in pits → replanted at wide spacing →
  cut before seed pods shatter → shade-dried → threshed into seed plus
  chaff. Fresh roots → unwashed, multi-week sun-drying → winnowed →
  dried roots stored in sacks. Fresh roots → oven-dried, as an
  alternative to sun-drying. Fresh roots → roasted in a traditional
  earth oven at harvest. Fresh roots → washed → cold storage.
  Dried roots → ground into flour, or heat-extruded into a milder
  "gelatinized" flour, or roasted into a coffee-substitute meal, or
  fermented into a traditional lightly alcoholic beverage; dark-
  colored roots specifically → a traditional distilled liquor.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned or frozen product
  and a traditional jam preparation; various rehydrated dishes, drinks,
  and baked goods; pharmaceutical-grade extracts, pills, and capsules;
  traditional storage-structure design.

Documented religious and cultural uses include a colonial-era Andean
religious record pairing this crop with maize and potato in ritual
use, and a modern legislative claim (from a lower-quality source) of
an Inca-era offering tradition using the same three crops together. A
tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables and other underground
storage organs generally, a note this project has also recorded for
other root crops. A widespread folk belief in a fertility-related
effect, and a modern secular harvest festival, are recorded as
cultural rather than strictly religious practices.

## Open research questions

- Whether rhizome swelling in this crop is triggered by day length
  (short-day) or is day-neutral is a genuine, unresolved conflict
  between two cited sources.
- Cold tolerance conflicts sharply between Andean research sources
  (night temperatures as low as roughly -10°C tolerated) and a non-
  Andean grower report (only to about -4°C, with hard freezes killing
  the foliage) — not resolved here, and possibly reflecting a real
  difference between acclimatized highland ecotypes and plants grown
  outside their native climate.
- Two sources citing the same original 1992 research trial report
  yield figures that differ by roughly a factor of five — likely a
  unit-conversion or citation error somewhere in the chain, not
  resolved here.
- Seed number per plant is reported wildly differently across sources
  (from under a thousand up to tens of thousands by one weight-based
  estimate) — not reconciled.
- Documented drying duration varies by a few weeks across sources, and
  documented annual rainfall for the traditional growing region
  differs by several hundred millimeters between two sources — neither
  conflict resolved here.
- Two archaeological dating estimates for the earliest cultivation of
  this crop differ by roughly a thousand years between two different
  excavation sites, which may simply reflect genuinely different sites
  rather than a true conflict.
- Weakest estimates overall: labor hours (only one low-quality
  historical student report was found); water-use parameters (based
  entirely on analogy to unrelated crops); seed-crop yield and
  multiplication ratio; nutrient-removal figures; and soil pH
  tolerance limits, none of which were found directly sourced.
- A substantial body of likely foundational literature on this crop
  (a national-academy review chapter, several national agricultural-
  institute technical bulletins, and at least one frequently cited
  1992 field trial) could not be obtained directly in this research
  pass and was only read through secondary citation.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 6,000 kg/ha, good 13,000 kg/ha, exceptional
20,000 kg/ha, poor 2,500 kg/ha) are derived mainly from two Peruvian
national production statistics roughly two decades apart, since Peru
is the dominant producer outside China and no Chinese national yield
statistics were found; the good and exceptional tiers draw on
documented fertile-land and trial yields, and the poor tier reflects
documented yields under extensive traditional management.

Energy and protein content of the fresh root (roughly 680 kcal and
26 g protein per kilogram) are derived from documented dried-root
composition figures, converted back to a fresh-weight basis using a
documented dry-matter percentage from one regional trial; a quality-
standard specification's fresh-composition figures imply a somewhat
higher dry-matter content and would give a notably higher calorie and
protein figure, a discrepancy not resolved here.

A dry-to-fresh weight ratio of roughly 3-5:1 (equivalently, a sun-
dried-product yield of roughly 20-36% of fresh root weight) is derived
by combining a regional trial's measured oven dry-matter percentage,
a separate field study's measured dry-to-fresh weight ratio, and a
general reference's rough fresh-to-dried conversion figure; a quality
standard's maximum allowed dried-product moisture content is also
factored in.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, root
depth, and a soil-moisture depletion allowance) are estimated by
analogy to two related Brassicaceae storage-root crops this project
has also researched, with the mid-season coefficient placed between
the two analogy crops' values and the late-season coefficient lowered
somewhat to reflect this crop's foliage drying out as the growing
season ends in the dry season; growing-season stage lengths are
derived from documented sowing and harvest date ranges.

A seed-crop yield of roughly 47 kg of seed per hectare of seed plot,
and a corresponding seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 12,
are derived from a documented seed-plot planting density combined with
an estimated typical seed yield per plant (itself a rough middle
estimate, since documented per-plant seed-yield figures vary by more
than an order of magnitude across sources) divided by a documented
root-crop seeding rate.

Labor-hour tiers are derived from a single low-quality historical
field-trial cost table, with the harvest figure specifically adjusted
upward from that source's own number, which appears inconsistent with
the same source's reported harvest labor cost share; the extended
multi-week sun-drying, turning, and winnowing process is estimated
without any direct source.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh root) are derived from documented dried-root protein
and mineral content converted to a fresh-weight basis using an
estimated dry-matter percentage, with phosphorus assumed at a typical
root-crop level in the absence of a direct figure; nutrients retained
in leaves left in the field are not counted in this removal estimate.

Soil pH tolerance (optimum roughly 5.5, with an estimated range of
4.5-7.5) is derived from a documented quality standard noting that
most traditional growing-region soils sit below pH 5.5, cross-checked
against a separate regional reference giving a similar acidic figure;
the tolerance limits themselves are estimated rather than directly
sourced.

Genome size (roughly 0.77 picograms, 1C) is derived from a published
genome-assembly size estimate using a standard genome-size conversion
factor, adjusted for this species' documented eight-fold chromosome
multiplication (octoploidy).

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 2 g/m²/day under field
conditions, up to roughly 9 g/m²/day under greenhouse conditions) is
derived by dividing representative field and greenhouse yield figures
by their respective documented growing-season lengths.
