# Andean Potato (*Solanum tuberosum*, Andigenum Group, including Phureja types)

## Naming and scope

"Andean potato" here means the cultivated potatoes of the Andes other
than the bitter, frost-resistant species. Under modern taxonomy this is
*Solanum tuberosum* Andigenum Group, which absorbs several older
classification groups (Phureja, Stenotomum, Chaucha) and spans diploid,
triploid, and tetraploid forms. One major water-use reference calls it
subspecies *andigena* [1]. The separate bitter potato species are a
distinct crop outside the scope of this entry.

This entry covers two production systems, kept apart below as cultivar
groups:

- **native landrace mixtures** (*papas nativas*) — mostly floury
  tetraploid Andigena, grown rainfed at about 3,500-4,200 m [3][4];
- **papa criolla** — diploid Phureja types, a commercial crop in
  Colombia at 2,000-3,500 m [6][12].

This crop shares its general potato biology — glycoalkaloid/greening
hazard, late blight risk, curing behavior, and general water-use shape
— with the common potato, a related crop this project has also
researched. This entry focuses on what is genuinely distinctive about
the Andean types: short-day tuberization, a highland growing
environment with specific frost limits, landrace-mixture farming with
its own seed system, and the lack of tuber dormancy in Phureja types.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a short-day potato that will not tuberize well in long
summer days.** *S. tuberosum* subspecies *andigena* and the other
cultivated Andean types tuberize under the 10-12 hour photoperiods of
the tropics [1]. Grown under long days, these types start tubers late,
grow a vigorous canopy, flower profusely, and mature late with reduced
tuber yield potential [1]. Breeding a long-day-adapted population from
this germplasm in the mid-20th century produced large yield increases
[1][22]. The underlying genetics involve specific gene variants that
allow long-day tuberization by escaping normal long-day suppression of
a key tuberization signal, and separately, a light-signaling pathway
(phytochrome B) that normally blocks tuber formation under non-
inductive day lengths [22]. The practical consequence is a photoperiod
gate on tuber initiation, with a critical day length around 12 hours:
in long days the plant instead becomes a late, leafy, heavily flowering
crop with low tuber yield — a key constraint for growing this crop
outside the tropics or under artificial lighting.

**Finding 2 — two seed systems: dormant landrace mixtures vs. non-
dormant papa criolla.** Phureja types have no tuber dormancy: they
sprout at harvest, so they cannot be stored as ordinary seed and
instead suit year-round planting in mild climates [2]. In Colombia this
lack of dormancy causes postharvest losses of up to 40% [11]. Treatments
tried to slow sprouting include fumigation and growth regulators [11];
a 2025 breeding cross with a different Peruvian line doubled the rest
period, implying the ordinary type keeps for only about two weeks by
comparison [11]. Andigena landraces, by contrast, are dormant and are
stored at home in a dark room to prevent greening [5]. A distinct
"diffused-light" seed-storage method (developed by international potato
research and widely adopted across many countries) keeps seed tubers in
thin layers on shelves or trays in indirect daylight with good
ventilation; compared with dark storage, this cuts weight loss and pest
losses, produces short, sturdy sprouts (avoiding the need for manual
de-sprouting), and raises yields by 15-30% [5]. The tubers deliberately
turn green under this method and become unfit to eat, so it is used for
seed stock only — a genuinely deliberate greening practice, the
opposite of how greening is normally treated as a hazard to avoid.

**Finding 3 — landrace mixtures, many cultivars, and occasional true-
seed use.** Thousands of native varieties are grown at 3,500-4,200 m
across the Andes, mostly with little or no agrochemical input [3]. They
are mainly grown for subsistence and cultural/heritage reasons, rarely
reaching a formal market, and are often traded between communities or
given as gifts [3]. A detailed morphological study in one Peruvian
region, sampling nearly 2,500 tubers across several dozen farm
collections, found several hundred unique cultivars, with tetraploids
most abundant, and cultivar diversity peaking at a specific elevation
band within the overall range [4]. Floury landraces are typically grown
as deliberate blends of anywhere from a handful to several dozen
cultivars in a single field, with single-cultivar fields a minority
practice. Separately, Andean farmers have raised potatoes from true
(sexual) seed for centuries as a way to clean out disease, renew stock,
and create new cultivars; a market survey in one region found well over
a hundred distinct cultivars among just a few hundred sampled tubers,
suggesting one large, interconnected gene pool with ongoing gene flow
between cultivars [8]. The practical consequence is that a single field
of this crop is often not a single cultivar but a deliberate mixture
with its own internal cultivar count.

**Finding 4 — a highland growing season with frost limits and
generally low yields.** In the cool tropics, this crop is usually grown
as one rainfed crop per year with a long growing season of about 180
days, grown alone or rotated with maize, legumes, or quinoa [1]. Flat
(unridged) planting gives higher yields at higher, semi-arid elevations
by conserving soil moisture [1]. Planting generally follows the onset
of seasonal rains. A traditional multi-year communal land-use system
documented across dozens of Andean communities crops a given field for
a few years (potatoes typically planted first) before resting it for
roughly a decade, with only a fraction of the total communal land under
cultivation at any time [16]. Frost tolerance varies: most native
varieties show leaf damage below about −2.5°C with associated yield and
quality loss, while at least one Phureja variety was shown in a trial to
be damaged by a much milder, brief frost — a polyamine spray treatment
substantially restored yield after such damage in one documented case
[14]. Yields are generally low by modern-breeding standards: native
landrace yields around 5 t/ha are typical in one well-documented
region, compared to 15-16 t/ha for blight-resistant bred cultivars
derived from native germplasm in the same area; in a severe blight
year, local landrace yields fell to roughly 2 t/ha [21]. Papa criolla,
by contrast, is reported at around 15 t/ha in a commercial reference
budget, and 13-25 t/ha across named commercial cultivars [6][12].

**Finding 5 — papa criolla as a commercial market crop in Colombia.**
A detailed commercial reference budget for a specific growing zone in
Colombia [6] describes: a site at 2,000-3,500 m, 12-15°C mean
temperature, 600-800 mm annual rainfall; fine-textured soils over 40 cm
deep at pH 5.5-7.0; sensitivity to both water shortage and excess water,
as well as to frost; a roughly 4-month growing cycle at a specific bed
spacing; a total yield around 15 t/ha, sorted into four size/quality
grades; and a labor requirement of roughly 860 hours per hectare, with
planting-stage work (clearing, plowing, bed-making, planting) making up
about 41% of labor and harvest about 40%; seed itself is reported as
the single largest input cost, at roughly half of total input costs.
Processing-grade material of this type is reported to have unusually
high dry matter (over 22%), making it well suited to pre-cooked frozen
potato products, dehydrated purée, and starch extraction [15].

**Finding 6 — composition, color, and Andean-specific pests.**
Composition studies of native varieties report wide ranges: dry matter
roughly 14-26%, with anthocyanin pigment content and carotenoid content
both varying enormously by cultivar and in some studies showing a
negative correlation with each other (cultivars high in one tend to be
lower in the other) [15]. Color itself is treated as a cultivar-level
trait rather than a distinct crop type. Two pests are specifically
associated with Andean potato cultivation: the Andean potato weevil,
which causes losses above 50% in the field without control and up to
80% in storage, though simple plastic barriers around fields can cut
field damage by 65-70% [13]; and the Guatemalan tuber moth, which causes
roughly 30% average loss in one country's production and more than 25%
in papa criolla specifically, though at least one native landrace shows
less damage than improved cultivars in trials [13]. A traditional
dehydrated product made by boiling, peeling, dicing, and sun-drying
native potatoes is also documented, distinct from the freeze-dried
products associated with the bitter potato species [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual tuber crop; cool highland tropics,
  rainfed, one crop per year at roughly 180 days for native landrace
  mixtures, or a shorter roughly 4-month cycle for commercial papa
  criolla (Finding 4, 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest, dug once by hand; no distinct
  "new potato" market stage identified for this crop (Finding 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical highland (cool tropics), 2,000-4,200 m
  depending on cultivar group; native landrace mixtures at the higher,
  colder end (3,500-4,200 m), papa criolla at the lower end
  (2,000-3,500 m, optimum around 2,300-2,800 m) (Finding 4, 5).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible; shares the general glycoalkaloid/
  greening hazard common to potatoes generally.
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production; true (sexual)
  seed from open-pollinated berries is used only occasionally, mainly
  for breeding and disease-free stock renewal (Finding 3).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable by production system — rough
  native-landrace yields around 5 t/ha, commercial papa criolla around
  15 t/ha, bred blight-resistant landrace-derived cultivars up to 15-16
  t/ha, and named commercial papa criolla cultivars ranging 13-25 t/ha
  (Finding 4, 5).
- **Water use:** Rainfed, planted with the onset of seasonal rains;
  papa criolla is specifically documented as sensitive to both drought
  and excess/standing water (Finding 4, 5).
- **Establishment:** Native landrace mixtures are propagated from small
  whole seed tubers kept back from harvest, often as a deliberate
  multi-cultivar blend; papa criolla uses seed tubers at a specific
  commercial bed spacing (Finding 2, 3, 5).
- **Labor:** A detailed commercial reference budget for papa criolla
  gives roughly 860 hours per hectare, split roughly 41% planting-stage
  work and 40% harvest-stage work, with seed as the dominant input cost
  (Finding 5). No comparable labor study was found for native landrace
  mixture farming.
- **Soil:** Papa criolla reference conditions call for fine-textured
  soil over 40 cm deep at pH 5.5-7.0 (Finding 5). Native landrace
  mixture fields are managed under traditional multi-year communal
  fallow systems rather than continuous fertility management (Finding
  4).
- **Stand life:** Annual; native landrace fields are part of larger
  multi-year communal fallow rotations rather than continuously
  cropped (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** The Andean potato weevil (up to 80% storage
  loss uncontrolled) and the Guatemalan tuber moth (roughly 30% average
  loss) are the two pests specifically associated with this crop, in
  addition to the general late-blight risk shared with potatoes
  generally (Finding 6).
- **Cultivars:** Thousands of native landraces exist, commonly grown as
  deliberate multi-cultivar field blends; named commercial papa criolla
  cultivars and a small number of bred, blight-resistant cultivars
  derived from native germplasm are also documented (Finding 3, 4, 5).
- **Harvest:** Single-stage, dug once by hand; papa criolla output is
  commonly sorted into several size/quality grades after harvest
  (Finding 5).
- **Storage:** Two genuinely distinct regimes — dormant native-landrace
  tubers kept dark at home to prevent greening (for food), versus seed
  tubers deliberately kept in diffused light to induce controlled
  greening and short, sturdy sprouts (for planting stock); papa criolla,
  lacking dormancy entirely, sprouts within weeks of harvest regardless
  of storage method and must generally be replanted or sold quickly
  (Finding 2).

## Hazards

The Andean potato weevil can cause losses above 50% in the field
without control and up to 80% in storage, though simple plastic
barriers can substantially reduce field damage; the Guatemalan tuber
moth causes roughly 30% average loss, more in papa criolla specifically
(Finding 6). Late blight is a major yield risk in wet years, consistent
with the general blight risk shared across potato types; it has driven
severe landrace yield crashes in documented outbreak years (Finding 4).

This crop shares the general greening/glycoalkaloid hazard common to
potatoes — exposed or deliberately light-stored tubers accumulate
glycoalkaloids and are not fit to eat, which is why the diffused-light
seed-storage method is explicitly a seed-only practice (Finding 2).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** native landrace mixtures — harvest → small tubers kept
  back as seed → diffused-light storage → replanted with the seasonal
  rains, while eating stock goes to dark home storage; native potatoes
  are also boiled, peeled, cut, and sun-dried into a traditional
  dehydrated product. Papa criolla — harvest → sorted into grades →
  sold within weeks, since the crop lacks storage dormancy; seed tubers
  are typically replanted immediately. True (sexual) seed from berries
  is occasionally raised into seedlings for breeding and disease-free
  stock renewal.
- Not covered by this research: freeze-dried/dehydrated products
  specific to the separate bitter-potato species; traditional communal
  fallow-rotation management in agronomic detail; dedicated weevil/moth
  control methods; earth-oven cooking methods; cultural/ritual
  practices beyond what is noted below.

A long-standing Andean religious tradition venerates potatoes as part
of broader harvest-season observances tied to earth-deity worship,
including offerings of various potato types after harvest and before
the next planting; this is a cultural note rather than an agronomic
one and is not pursued further here.

## Open research questions

- No reliable national or regional production statistics exist that
  separate Andean-type potatoes from potatoes generally — the
  world-average yield figure used in the estimate below is a rough,
  weighted approximation rather than a sourced statistic.
- Labor requirements for traditional native-landrace mixture farming
  (as opposed to the well-documented commercial papa criolla budget)
  are not well sourced and are estimated only by analogy.
- Storage life for native landrace tubers under traditional dark home
  storage, and for papa criolla under ordinary ambient storage, are
  both estimated rather than directly measured in a dedicated study.
- Caloric and protein content figures for this crop were not
  independently re-sourced for this entry and should be treated as
  provisional.
- Whether papa criolla (the Phureja types) is better treated as an
  entirely separate crop entry from the native landrace mixtures is a
  genuine open question: the two differ in dormancy, elevation range,
  cycle length, and commercial market structure, though they share
  species-level biology.
- Planting density and seeding rate for traditional native-landrace
  mixture fields were not found in available sources.
- Chilling-injury thresholds specific to Andigena-type tubers were not
  found.
- The traditional multi-year communal fallow-rotation system is
  documented narratively but not quantified in a way that supports
  a precise agronomic model.
- A specific genetic basis for long-day tuberization adaptation (allelic
  variants that escape long-day suppression of the tuberization signal,
  and phytochrome-B-mediated photoperiod sensing) is documented at the
  level of research-literature search snippets, not from a fully opened
  primary source, and would benefit from direct verification.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 8,000 kg/ha is estimated: native landrace
mixtures give roughly 5 t/ha, commercial papa criolla roughly 15 t/ha;
weighting toward the (areally larger) native-mixture system at
roughly 3:1 gives about 7.5 t/ha, rounded to 8,000 kg/ha. Good yield is
estimated at 15,000 kg/ha (the papa criolla reference budget and bred
blight-resistant landrace-derived cultivars); exceptional at 25,000
kg/ha (top-performing named commercial cultivars in trials); poor at
2,000 kg/ha (native landrace yield in a severe blight year). Plausible
range: 5,000-10,000 kg/ha. Dedicated national statistics for native
potato production specifically would replace this estimate.

Water-use stage lengths are derived by scaling a standard European
potato growth-stage breakdown up to the roughly 180-day Andean rainfed
season, giving stage lengths of roughly 37/43/62/38 days
(initial/development/mid/late). Using standard potato crop-coefficient
values gives a seasonal water-use index of about 160 (plausible range
140-170). At high altitude, actual reference evapotranspiration is
lower than in the temperate case this scaling is based on, so real
seasonal water use likely sits closer to a directly measured highland
range of roughly 350-650 mm. A dedicated highland water-balance study
would replace this estimate.

Drought-sensitivity parameters (days without water before a yield
penalty, and the per-day penalty rate) are estimated by analogy with
potatoes generally, adjusted slightly for papa criolla's documented
sensitivity to shortage; the real difference is likely a slower
evapotranspiration demand at altitude, implying a somewhat larger
buffer than at lower elevation. Plausible range: 3-7 days before
penalty, 0.02-0.06 yield penalty per additional dry day.

Papa criolla seed-tuber requirements are derived from standard
commercial planting density and an estimated small-seed-tuber weight,
giving roughly 1,000-1,700 kg/ha of seed tubers against a 15,000 kg/ha
crop — a seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 9-15, consistent
with seed being reported as the single largest input cost in the
commercial reference budget.

Labor-hour estimates for native landrace mixture farming (as opposed
to the well-documented papa criolla commercial budget) are built by
analogy: a traditional foot-plow-based tier is estimated with somewhat
higher land-preparation labor than the mechanized commercial case,
scaled from the shape of labor figures for potatoes generally. Plausible
range: estimates ±50% around the analogy figure. A dedicated time study
of traditional Andean mixture farming would replace this estimate.

Native landrace home-storage life is estimated at roughly 120 days: the
harvest (roughly April-June in one documented calendar) must feed the
household until the next planting season begins (roughly September-
November), and dark storage at highland ambient temperature is assumed
to hold edible quality for about four months, acknowledging that
storage-pest damage (the Andean potato weevil) can destroy up to 80% of
stored tubers in poorly managed stores. Plausible range: 60-180 days. A
dedicated storage-loss survey of traditional Andean farm stores would
replace this estimate.
