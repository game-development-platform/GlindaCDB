# Hamburg Parsley / Root Parsley (*Petroselinum crispum* var. *tuberosum*)

## Naming and scope

A cultivar group of ordinary parsley, *Petroselinum crispum* var.
*tuberosum* (also described in some sources as *P. crispum* conv.
*radicosum*), bred for a thickened, carrot-shaped storage root rather
than for leaf production [1][2]. Trade names include Hamburg parsley,
root parsley, parsnip-rooted parsley, turnip-rooted parsley, Dutch
parsley, and rock parsley [3][4].

This entry's scope is the root crop only. This crop's own leaves are a
minor, unmarketed byproduct of growing the root, not an independent
product line (see Finding 8). This is treated as a distinct crop from
common leaf parsley, a crop this project has already researched,
despite being the same species — the two differ in harvested organ,
growth cycle, storage regime, and market identity. This entry is
grounded against that leaf-parsley research for shared seed-crop and
seed biology, and against carrot and parsnip, two related root crops
this project has also researched, for general root-crop storage
regime, forking-disorder, and soil-need analogies — carrot has already
been migrated into this collection; parsnip is still pending.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the same species as leaf parsley, but a distinct
cultivar group with multiple trade synonyms.** This crop is the same
species as ordinary leaf parsley, placed by one major horticultural
reference in a specific cultivar group bred for root production; a
separate scientific trial uses an alternative botanical name for the
same group [1][2]. Common trade names include Hamburg parsley, root
parsley, parsnip-rooted parsley, turnip-rooted parsley, Dutch parsley,
and rock parsley [3][4].

**Finding 2 — field yield is documented across three independent
trials, not fully reconciled into one figure.** A 15-cultivar Polish
trial (2004-2006) gives total root yield of 14.7-20.9 t/ha and
marketable yield of 13.0-13.4 t/ha, at a documented seed rate and row
spacing [5]. A separate Czech cultivar trial gives a mean yield of
about 13.5 t/ha [2][6]. A nitrogen-rate trial gives forked-root yield
of 3.5-7 t/ha depending on cultivar and nitrogen application rate [7].
This spread (roughly 3.5-21 t/ha across all three trials) reflects
genuinely different cultivars, nitrogen regimes, and differing
definitions of "marketable" yield, rather than a single disagreement
— it is not reconciled into one figure here.

**Finding 3 — a single-harvest root crop, unlike leaf parsley's
repeated cutting.** This crop reaches root maturity in roughly 85-90
days and is lifted once the root reaches about 1 inch (2.5 cm) in
diameter, by consistent agreement across multiple independent seed-
company and extension sources [8]. Leaf parsley, by contrast, is cut
repeatedly through the season from about the same plant age onward;
this root crop is instead dug once, like a carrot or parsnip.

**Finding 4 — multi-month cold storage, unlike leaf parsley's much
shorter fresh shelf life.** Topped roots keep "several months" at
0°C/32°F, the same storage regime documented for carrot and parsnip
[8]. This is a materially different storage asset from leaf parsley's
much shorter fresh shelf life and its drying-only long-term form.

**Finding 5 — soil needs and a forking disorder mirror carrot and
parsnip.** This crop wants deep, loose, well-drained soil; heavy clay
or dry soil causes the root to fork into two smaller roots — the same
disorder documented for carrot and parsnip [9].

**Finding 6 — seed-crop biology is shared with leaf parsley, with one
unresolved cross-pollination risk.** This crop shares the same
biennial life cycle, vernalization-gated seed year, slow and erratic
germination, short seed viability, and insect-pollinated, protandrous-
floret cross-pollination pattern documented for leaf parsley generally
— this was not independently re-derived for this specific cultivar
group, since it is the same species. A 1-mile isolation distance is
commonly given by seed retailers, though no source found derives this
figure from an actual trial [10]. Because this crop and leaf parsley
are literally the same species, a farm growing both for seed risks
cross-pollination between them; no source addresses this specific
scenario directly — the 1-mile figure is given per crop type generally,
not specifically for crossing between these two cultivar groups of the
same species.

**Finding 7 — a distinct, centuries-old market identity as a root
vegetable, not an herb.** This crop is a named ingredient in German
soup-greens mixtures and in Central/Eastern European cooking generally,
with documented use traced back to at least a 16th-century cookbook
reference; it remains little known in US and UK markets [3][4][11]. It
is eaten as a cooked root vegetable (in soups and roasts, with a
parsnip- or celery-like flavor), not used as the culinary herb that
leaf parsley provides.

**Finding 8 — the leaves are edible but a minor byproduct, not this
crop's actual product.** Most grower sources describe this crop's
leaves as tasting close to ordinary leaf parsley; one informal online
opinion dissents, calling them inferior — no measured flavor comparison
was found either way [12]. The leaves are not sold or marketed
separately from leaf parsley.

**Finding 9 — root nutrition figures disagree meaningfully across
non-government food databases, with no comprehensive government
database entry found.** One major government food-composition
database has no dedicated entry for this crop's root (only for leaf
parsley, at 36 kcal and 2.97 g protein per 100 g) [13]. Two
independent non-government food databases give the root as anywhere
from roughly 46 kcal/2.6 g protein to roughly 71 kcal/1.3 g protein per
100 g — a real, unresolved range of nearly 50% [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial grown as an annual
  for its root, reaching maturity in roughly 85-90 days (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest — one root lift per plant,
  unlike leaf parsley's repeated cutting pattern (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Temperate, cool-season zones, by analogy to carrot and
  parsnip; not independently sourced for this specific cultivar group.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, though normally cooked in soups and
  roasts (Finding 7).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; only the
  separate seed crop requires insect pollination (Finding 6).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented yield spans a wide range across
  three independent trials (roughly 3.5-21 t/ha), reflecting different
  cultivars, nitrogen regimes, and marketable-yield definitions, not a
  single disagreement (Finding 2; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Water-use parameters are not independently sourced
  for this specific cultivar group; this entry carries them by
  analogy from parsnip, a similarly deep-rooted temperate root crop of
  comparable size and growing duration.
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded, with the same slow, erratic
  germination pattern documented for leaf parsley generally, since it
  is the same species.
- **Labor:** No labor-hour or labor-operations data was sourced for
  this crop in this research pass.
- **Soil:** Deep, loose, well-drained soil is wanted; heavy clay or
  dry soil causes root forking, the same disorder documented for
  carrot and parsnip (Finding 5).
- **Stand life:** Not applicable — a single-season annual-grown
  biennial, not a multi-year stand.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not independently sourced for this
  specific cultivar group; a disease complex similar to leaf parsley's
  (a specific leaf-spot disease and a crown rot) is assumed by species
  identity, not independently confirmed for the root-grown type.
- **Cultivars:** Documented cultivars vary significantly in both total
  and marketable yield across independent trials (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** A single lift once the root reaches roughly 1 inch in
  diameter, at about 85-90 days from sowing (Finding 3).
- **Storage:** Multi-month cold storage at 0°C/32°F for topped roots,
  the same regime documented for carrot and parsnip — a materially
  different storage asset than leaf parsley's much shorter fresh shelf
  life (Finding 4).

## Hazards

A disease complex similar to leaf parsley's (including a specific
leaf-spot disease and a crown rot) is assumed to apply to this crop by
species identity, though this was not independently confirmed for the
root-grown type in this research pass.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop is assumed, by species identity, to
share the same furanocoumarin (phototoxic) and apiole/myristicin
seed-oil hazard profile documented for leaf parsley, though no source
found in this research pass specifically tested the Hamburg/root form
for either hazard. If confirmed, this would extend the same hazard
notes already applicable to leaf parsley rather than represent a new,
distinct hazard. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind
of health/safety data, and pending confirmation that the hazard
actually applies to the root form specifically (Finding 6 cross-
reference; not independently sourced for the root form this pass).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed sown directly → grown roughly 85-90 days → root
  lifted once it reaches about 1 inch in diameter → topped → eaten
  fresh/cooked, or cold-stored for several months at 0°C/32°F. A
  separate seed crop (biennial, vernalization-gated, insect-pollinated)
  produces seed for the next planting.
- Not covered by this research: a measured flavor comparison between
  this crop's leaves and ordinary leaf parsley; any formal study of
  cross-pollination risk between this crop and leaf parsley when grown
  for seed on the same farm.

This crop is a named ingredient in German soup-greens mixtures and in
Central/Eastern European cooking generally, with documented use traced
to at least a 16th-century cookbook reference.

## Estimates and derivations

A nutrition estimate of roughly 46-71 kcal and 1.3-2.6 g protein per
100 g is carried as an unsourced range: no comprehensive government
food-composition entry for this crop's root was found, and the range
comes from two independent non-government food databases that disagree
by roughly 50%. A government or other peer-reviewed food-composition
entry specifically for this crop's root would replace this range.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient values, root depth) are carried
by analogy from parsnip's own water-use figures, on the basis that
both are deep-rooted temperate root crops of broadly similar size and
growing duration; they likely differ in degree, since parsnip is a
larger, longer-season root. A dedicated water-use source for this
specific cultivar group would replace this analogy.

A seed-crop isolation distance of 1 mile is carried without
independent derivation, sourced only from seed-retailer pages with no
stated basis; shatter risk is carried by analogy from leaf parsley's
own seed-crop biology, since both are the same species with the same
floret structure.

A general weather-response pattern is chosen by analogy to carrot and
parsnip (both deep-rooted temperate biennials grown as annuals for a
cold-stored root), since no crop-specific frost, heat, or bolting
threshold was found for this cultivar group.

A hazard-profile assumption (that this crop shares leaf parsley's
furanocoumarin and apiole/myristicin hazards) rests only on shared
species identity; no source tested either compound in the root form
specifically.
