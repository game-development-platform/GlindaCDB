# Yam — Guinea Yams, White and Yellow (*Dioscorea cayenensis*, incl. subsp. *rotundata*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-18

## Naming and scope

True yams: the genus *Dioscorea*, family Dioscoreaceae, a monocot
genus entirely distinct from the North American "yam" (which is
usually sweet potato, a crop this project has already researched
separately, and botanically unrelated). This entry covers the West
African Guinea-yam complex specifically — white yam (*D. cayenensis*
subsp. *rotundata*, formerly classified as its own species *D.
rotundata*) and yellow yam (*D. cayenensis*) — which together account
for over 95% of world yam production.

A naming conflict exists across sources: one regional technology
package calls a different species (the lesser yam, *D. esculenta*)
"Chinese yam," while a general encyclopedia source reserves that name
for yet another species (*D. polystachya*). This entry follows the
encyclopedia convention. Several related *Dioscorea* species —
including water yam/greater yam, a separate Chinese yam species, a
bitter/trifoliate yam, an aerial yam, and a South American cush-cush
yam — are related crops this project has not yet researched as their
own entries; a brief cush-cush note appears in this entry's cultivar
section, but it is not modeled as a separate row here.

This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research into
potato and sweet potato, two other root/tuber sibling entries in this
project's research.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one plant can give either one harvest or two, through a
practice called milking.** White yam, and to a lesser extent yellow
yam, can give an early crop in addition to the main harvest: the tuber
is cut below the head and removed while the plant is still growing,
leaving the top portion to grow again and produce another tuber or
tubers. In practice, this "milking" technique takes the ware (market)
tuber while the leaves are still green, and the plant then grows seed
tubers for a second harvest [6]. A documented Nigerian field trial
using a specific white-yam cultivar found that the later the first
harvest was taken, the bigger its yield and the larger the share of
mature, marketable tubers — but the smaller the resulting second
harvest [6]. This creates a genuine timing trade-off between a single
ware harvest, a milked first harvest, and a milked second (seed-yam)
harvest.

**Finding 2 — seed yams represent a large share of the crop, and a
technique called minisetts reduces that share.** Planting material
consists of whole seed tubers or cut pieces (setts), planted into
mounds or ridges at the start of the rainy season; farmers often keep
up to 30% of the harvest specifically for replanting [1]. Documented
sett preparation involves cutting sprouting tubers with a disinfected
knife (head and tail into two or more pieces, then the body into
disks and wedges, each piece kept with as much skin as possible),
drying them overnight in a shaded, ventilated place, and planting
within about two days, since holding them longer lowers sprouting
success; very small tubers are planted whole [3]. A specific small
planting-piece technique called minisetts uses roughly 2 cm cube
pieces of about 25 g, dusted with insecticide and fungicide [5]. One
regional technology package documents that white-yam minisetts of
roughly 25-30 g at one spacing yield roughly 100-300 g seed yams,
while larger minisetts of roughly 70-100 g at a wider spacing yield
roughly 400-500 g seed yams. A separate research institute trial found
that raising minisett size from roughly 30 g to 60 or 90 g raised seed
yield by 61% and 103% respectively, but lowered the overall
multiplication ratio; early planting yielded 137% more than late
planting in the same trial [5]. A documented review gives
multiplication ratios (harvested weight divided by planted weight) of
roughly 2.8-5.1 for white yam [5].

**Finding 3 — mounds, ridges, stakes, and a long wet season define
cultivation.** Documented optimal climate conditions run roughly
25-30°C (best around 30°C), with roughly 1,500 mm of rain spread over
five to six months, on deep, well-drained, fertile sandy loam soils;
lime is applied to bring acidic soil up to roughly pH 5.5, with an
ideal range of roughly 5.5-6.5 [3]. Land is prepared either as mounds
(where mechanization is unavailable) or as ridges (roughly 45 cm high
and 90 cm apart, allowing roughly 50 cm between plants and generally
higher yields); white and yellow yams are planted at the start of the
rainy season (documented as June-July in one Caribbean region) [3].
The vines of white and yellow yam reach roughly 10-12 m and are spiny
near the base; a stake roughly 2-5 m long and about 5 cm thick is set
beside each plant as vines emerge, though plastic mulch combined with
drip irrigation along the ridge can eliminate the need for staking
[3]. Fertilizer is applied at roughly four and seven to nine weeks
(marking the tuber-bulking stage), banded roughly 10 cm from the
plant; virus-infected plants are rogued out. Yield depends on mound
size, spacing, staking method, species, and target tuber size [1]. A
documented Colombian growing region for a related white-yam cultivar
grows at roughly 250 m elevation, about 33°C, and roughly 1,100 mm of
rainfall on clay-loam soils [13].

**Finding 4 — curing and barn storage carry heavy losses.** Curing
conditions are documented at roughly 29-35°C and 90-95% relative
humidity for four to eight days in one Caribbean source, or roughly
30-40°C and 70-90% relative humidity for one to four days under a
tarpaulin, started immediately after harvest, in a separate FAO
source; curing heals wounds through a natural corky-tissue-formation
process and reduces weight loss and decay [3][4]. Storage temperature
and dormancy guidance vary: storage at roughly 12-15°C with aeration
and regular inspection is documented in one source, while a separate
source documents roughly 14-16°C as best with chilling injury below
roughly 12°C, and a third FAO source recommends roughly 20°C to
inhibit sprouting while never storing below 15°C. Dormancy length in
white yam is documented at roughly 14-112 days; water yam (a related
species this project has not yet researched) stores better than white
yam, and white yam stores better than yellow yam, which has a shorter
dormancy period [1][4][11]. Traditional West African storage practice
documents a harvest period of September-November, with yams kept up
to six months in bamboo barns with tubers tied individually under
shade (in forested regions), in sorghum-stalk shelters (in savanna
regions), or in simple earthen clamps; losses are documented around
25% conservatively, though other sources report roughly 10-15% weight
loss after three months and near 50% weight loss by six months [4][7].
Sprout handling differs between food and seed stock: food yams have
their heads broken off before storage to delay sprouting, while seed
yams keep their heads intact, with sprouts cut off as they elongate
[4].

**Finding 5 — documented yields and production figures.** Global
production is documented at roughly 74.8 million tonnes in 2020, with
Nigeria alone accounting for roughly 50.1 million tonnes (about 67%),
and over 95% of world production concentrated in Africa [1]; a
separate secondary FAO-based source documents Nigeria at roughly 61.2
million tonnes in 2022 [9]. White and yellow yams typically give one
large tuber per year, roughly 2.5-5 kg (occasionally up to 25 kg), over
a 7-12 month growing period; most edible yams mature roughly 7-11
months after planting [1][3]. A documented FAOSTAT-based review found
Nigeria achieving only roughly 17.3% of an estimated potential yield of
50,000 kg/ha; a documented Colombian white-yam cultivar averages
roughly 13,300 kg/ha at roughly 10,000 plants/ha, against a separately
claimed (and conflicting) figure of roughly 25,000 kg/ha for African
producers in the same source [9]. Documented raw-yam composition per
100 g includes roughly 118 kcal, 27.9 g carbohydrate, 1.5 g protein,
4.1 g fiber, 816 mg potassium, and 17.1 mg vitamin C [1].

**Finding 6 — processing, feed use, and a cultural new-yam rule.**
Yam flour production peels and slices the tubers, parboils them at
roughly 40-60°C for one to three hours, soaks and sun-dries them, then
mills them into flour; this flour is stirred into boiling water to make
a staple dish, and some processing methods also ferment the slices for
roughly 72 hours [7]. Peeling removes roughly 15-30% of harvest
weight; peelings are fed to pigs and cattle, with white-yam peel
documented at roughly 11.2% crude protein on a dry-matter basis, and
cooking is documented destroying the bitter alkaloid compound present
in raw yam [8]. Bitter compounds can build up in the immature tissue
of Guinea yams specifically, though the more serious toxic compounds
documented in certain other *Dioscorea* species belong to the bitter/
trifoliate and aerial yam species this project has not yet researched
as separate entries. A documented Igbo cultural tradition, the New Yam
Festival, is held at the start of the new-yam harvest, typically in
early August at the end of the rainy season; tasting new yam before
the community festival is documented as taboo (some sources describe
this as a historical rule); yams are first offered to the gods and
ancestors, accompanied by palm-wine libations, kola nut, and prayers
to specific deities [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season vegetatively propagated vine
  grown as an annual crop from tubers, needing a long rainy season.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single ware harvest, or a double (milked)
  harvest from one planting, modeled as distinct harvest stages
  (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Tropical zones.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — must be cooked; bitter compounds
  can occur in immature tissue (hazard detail below).
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production, since this is a
  vegetatively propagated crop; wild and breeding populations are
  dioecious (separate male and female plants), with true seed used
  only in breeding.
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields vary
  considerably, roughly 3,000-25,000 kg/ha/year depending on region and
  management (Finding 5; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-water-use reference
  work documents this crop; water-use parameters are estimated by
  analogy to sweet potato, the closest tropical tuber-forming vine with
  a similarly rainy-season-dependent growing pattern (water detail
  below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from whole seed tubers or
  cut setts, planted into mounds or ridges at the start of the rains
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for this crop; all
  labor-tier figures carried in internal planning material are
  unsourced estimates.
- **Soil:** Deep, well-drained, fertile sandy loam, with a documented
  optimal pH range of roughly 5.5-6.5 (soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** An annual crop grown from the tuber of a perennial
  vine.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A fungal anthracnose disease, nematodes,
  insects, and viral diseases are documented (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Over 200 Guinea-yam varieties are documented to
  exist, though only a handful are named in the sources consulted,
  including specific white-yam cultivars, the yellow-yam type, and a
  South American cush-cush relative (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single ware harvest (7-12 months after planting), or
  two harvest stages from one planting under the milking technique
  described in Finding 1 (harvest-stage detail below).
- **Storage:** Traditional barn storage keeps cured tubers for roughly
  six months with substantial but gradually accumulating weight loss
  (Finding 4).

Water detail: this crop needs to be planted at the start of the rains
and kept moist while the tuber bulks; it needs a long, well-watered wet
season. Documented total seasonal rainfall of roughly 1,500 mm spread
over five to six months is considered most suitable in one Caribbean
region, while a separate documented growing region in Colombia receives
only roughly 1,100 mm. This crop needs deep, well-drained soil and does
not tolerate waterlogging; ridges are used partly for drainage.

Establishment detail: setts or whole seed yams are planted on mounds
or ridges at the start of the rains, at a documented planting depth of
roughly 7.5 cm; a documented ridge spacing of 90 cm between rows and
50 cm within rows gives roughly 22,000 plants/ha, while closer
minisett spacing can reach roughly 40,000 plants/ha, and a documented
Colombian mound spacing gives roughly 10,000 plants/ha.

Soil detail: fertilizer is applied in two documented applications
timed to early growth and the tuber-bulking stage, banded near the
plant.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a specific white-yam
cultivar used in the double-harvest research trial described in
Finding 1; two further white-yam cultivars that appeared anthracnose-
resistant in a regional disease screen; a Caribbean regional name for
a white-yam type with documented vine length and planting timing; the
yellow-yam type, noted for carotenoid-yellow flesh, firmer skin,
shorter dormancy, and generally poorer storage than white yam; a
Colombian white-yam cultivar with a documented national average yield
figure; a savanna-adapted white-yam cultivar group noted for longer
dormancy and later sprouting in storage; and a South American cush-
cush yam relative, noted as less tied to seasonal timing with a
shorter dormancy period, mentioned here as a cultural and botanical
note rather than as a separate row in this entry.

## Hazards

Bitter compounds can build up in the immature tissue of this crop's
species specifically, though this is considered a minor concern
compared to the more serious toxic compounds documented in certain
other, not-yet-researched *Dioscorea* species. Calcium oxalate,
present at the genus level in some yam species (particularly noted for
Japanese yam types), can cause skin itching during food preparation.

A fungal anthracnose disease, nematodes, various insect pests, and
viral diseases are documented pests and diseases, with virus-infected
plants typically rogued out of the field.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed yams → setts (dried overnight) → mounds or ridges →
  staked → ware tubers at 7-12 months → cured → barn storage → cooked
  (boiled, roasted, or pounded). Double-harvest path: early planting →
  first ware harvest with the head left intact → regrown seed yams
  harvested at season end. Minisett path: roughly 25-30 g minisetts →
  planted at close spacing → roughly 100-300 g seed yams. Stored yams
  → peeled (peels fed to pigs and cattle) → sliced, parboiled, soaked,
  sun-dried → milled into flour → cooked into a boiled staple dish.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** staking material as a
  distinct tracked input; detailed cooking methods; detailed pest and
  disease management; post-harvest treatments such as irradiation or
  growth-regulator application in storage.

An Igbo cultural tradition (and related traditions among other West
African communities) marks the start of the new-yam harvest with a
New Yam Festival; eating the new crop before this community festival
is documented as taboo. First yams are offered to the gods and
ancestors, followed by palm-wine libations, kola nut, and prayers,
typically timed to early August at the end of the rainy season. A
separate Ashanti yam festival tradition exists but was not researched
in detail. A Jain dietary tradition avoids root vegetables generally,
consistent with notes this project has recorded for other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Documented storage chilling-injury thresholds conflict between
  sources (roughly 12°C in one source versus roughly 15°C in another),
  as do documented optimal storage-temperature ranges (roughly 12-15°C,
  14-16°C, or about 20°C across three sources).
- Documented curing conditions conflict between two sources (roughly
  29-35°C for four to eight days versus roughly 30-40°C for one to
  four days).
- Documented yield figures conflict considerably: a Nigerian yield
  figure of roughly 8,600 kg/ha sits far below a separately claimed
  figure of roughly 25,000 kg/ha for unspecified "African producers"
  cited within the same source.
- The common name "Chinese yam" is applied inconsistently across
  sources, naming different species in different regional contexts.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour figures (entirely unsourced
  estimates); water-use parameters (estimated entirely by analogy to
  sweet potato, since no dedicated crop-water-use study exists for
  this crop); the lower (poor) yield tier (unsourced); soil nutrient-
  uptake figures (unsourced); and effective rooting depth (unsourced).
- True-seed production and flowering biology were not researched in
  detail, since commercial production is entirely vegetative.
- Yam beetle pests were noted as a concern but not researched in
  detail.
- Frost and cold-tolerance limits were not researched, since this is
  considered a strictly tropical crop.
- A separate Ashanti yam-festival cultural tradition was noted but not
  researched in detail.

## Estimates and derivations

World-average yield (roughly 8,600 kg/ha) is derived directly from a
documented FAOSTAT-based review finding that Nigerian yam production
runs at roughly 17.3% of an estimated potential yield of 50,000 kg/ha;
since Nigeria is documented producing roughly two-thirds of world
output, this figure stands in for a world-average estimate. The
reporting period for the underlying review was not specified in the
source consulted.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are estimated entirely by analogy to sweet
potato, the closest tropical tuber-forming vine with a similarly
rainy-season-dependent growing pattern, since no standard reference-
table row exists for this crop; this analogy likely differs in season
length and canopy structure, since this crop's vines climb considerably
higher on stakes than sweet potato's. Sweet potato's documented
tropical stage lengths are scaled up proportionally to match this
crop's longer roughly 270-day season. The resulting estimated total
seasonal water-use figure falls reasonably close to the documented
1,500 mm rainfall figure from Finding 3, lending some support to this
estimate despite its indirect derivation.

Planting density figures (roughly 10,000-40,000 plants/ha depending on
method) are derived directly from documented ridge and minisett
spacing figures, cross-checked against a documented Colombian mound-
spacing figure.
