## Naming and scope

*Helianthus tuberosus* (sunroot, sunchoke, topinambur, earth apple),
family Asteraceae, the same genus as sunflower [2]. It is hexaploid
(six sets of chromosomes). Genome work points to this species being a
natural hybrid of two wild sunflower relatives, not the common
cultivated sunflower [2]. The common English name is probably a
corruption of an Italian word for sunflower; "sunchoke" is a 1960s
marketing name [2]. It is not related to globe artichoke (a flower
bud from an entirely different plant family).

This entry treats food, forage-top, and inulin/energy uses as a single
crop, since they all come from the same clones and are cultivation
paths rather than genuinely separate crops. This entry is grounded
against potato (for stem-tuber biology, seed pieces, and mechanical
harvest comparison) and parsnip (for in-ground winter storage and
humid cold storage practice), two related root crops this project has
also researched but not yet migrated into this collection.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the reserve carbohydrate is inulin, not starch.**
Carbohydrates make up roughly 75-80% of tuber dry matter, mostly
inulin [1]; a separate feed-composition source gives fructans (mainly
inulin) at about 50% of dry matter and sugars at about 27% [4]; a
general reference gives inulin at 8-13% of the fresh tuber [2]. Humans
cannot digest inulin directly — colon bacteria ferment it instead,
which causes gas and sometimes digestive discomfort [2]. Inulin is
itself a chain of molecules of varying length, and it depolymerizes
both in storage and in the ground over winter: the ratio of fructose
to glucose within the inulin falls substantially over time, meaning
syrups made from stored tubers end up much higher in simple glucose,
and the longer molecular chains needed for certain industrial uses are
progressively lost [5]. Tubers harvested in spring have less inulin
and soluble solids, and more simple reducing sugars, than tubers
harvested in autumn [16]. Storing tubers under elevated carbon-dioxide
levels slowed this inulin breakdown, while gamma irradiation sped it
up [5]. The practical consequence is that this crop's processing
quality depends meaningfully on harvest date and storage time, not
just on raw yield.

**Finding 2 — a stem tuber that keeps well in the ground but shrivels
badly in dry air.** Three storage approaches exist: refrigerated
storage, common storage (root cellars, clamps, pits), and in-situ field
storage, where tubers are simply dug as needed through the winter [5].
Field storage works where the soil stays cold through winter but the
surface rarely freezes solid; sandy, well-drained soils make winter
digging practically feasible [5]. Tubers can survive extremely cold
air temperatures (down to roughly -30°C) while insulated in the soil
[2]. The tuber tissue itself freezes at about -2.2°C; a brief exposure
to -5°C does little damage, but -10°C causes rapid deterioration [5].
Cold storage at 0-2°C and 90-95% relative humidity gives 6-12 months
of storage life depending on cultivar, with some cultivars performing
much worse than others; an older reference gives a somewhat shorter
4-5 month figure at a slightly colder temperature, with up to 20%
loss to decay and shriveling after 5 months [3]. Unlike a potato, this
crop's tuber has no corky protective skin — its thin, easily damaged
surface loses water quickly, making high-humidity storage essential
[5]. The tubers wilt and soften faster than potatoes and should never
be left in dry air before storage [1]. The crop is not ethylene-
sensitive [5]. The main storage rot organisms include two common
fungal pathogens plus a third fungal pathogen that is the most serious
rot specifically at low storage temperature [5].

**Finding 3 — the crop that will not leave the field.** "Leftover
tubers sprout the next year," creating overly thick volunteer stands;
volunteers can persist for several years, so fields are generally
replanted fresh each year rather than relying on regrowth [3].
Volunteers overwinter well and can even outgrow corn in competition
[1]. In trials, repeated mechanical or chemical control still left
several volunteer shoots per square meter the following season, and
full elimination of a volunteer stand typically requires control
efforts across the second and likely third subsequent year [14].
Documented uncontrolled yield losses in a following crop vary hugely
by crop type: insignificant in wheat, oat, rape, and ryegrass, but as
high as 81% in maize and 91% in sugar beet in one study [14]; a
separate feed-reference source gives somewhat different but still
severe figures (25% loss in maize, 91% in soybean) [4] — this
discrepancy is recorded but not resolved here. In soybean specifically,
even a few leftover tubers per meter of row cut yield by 31-71%
depending on density, with control required within 6 weeks of planting
for best effect [14]. This species is documented as one of the
fastest-spreading invasive species in parts of Central Europe; it
spreads along waterways, via animal movement, and by seed, and
suppresses neighboring plants through shading and possibly through
allelopathy (chemical suppression) [2]. This crop joins a small group
of root crops this project has researched that share this volunteer/
invasive persistence risk.

**Finding 4 — yield, and a genuine trade-off between tops and
tubers.** Typical yield is documented around 34 t/ha with an overall
range of roughly 11-56 t/ha; a general reference gives a typical range
of 16-20 t/ha, while other regional sources report considerably higher
figures (roughly 41-70 t/ha), and one specific three-year regional
trial averaged roughly 65 t/ha [1][2][3][4]. One experimental salinity
trial under controlled sand-tank conditions reached roughly 83 t/ha
[8]. Yield trials show that closer plant spacing raises total yield
but produces smaller individual tubers [1]. Critically, cutting the
green tops for forage dramatically reduces tuber yield: in one trial,
a late-season top cut produced a large forage harvest but left tuber
yield at only a small fraction of the no-cut control [1]. Green tops
themselves yield roughly 18-28 t/ha of green matter, or a much smaller
figure on a dry-matter basis, and are best cut just before flowering,
as the plant tissue turns woody after that point [4].

**Finding 5 — short-day flowering and tuber formation, with a
disputed day-neutral cultivar claim.** This species is a documented
short-day plant: in one controlled study, day lengths of 6-13 hours
induced flowering while 14 hours or more kept plants vegetative, with
a critical day length around 13 hours and at least 17 consecutive
short days required [10]. A breeder's practical guide gives a similar
threshold (below roughly 13-13.5 hours for at least 16 days, once
plants are mature enough), noting that most varieties flower in
mid-to-late autumn [11]. Tuber formation itself is also short-day
triggered [11]; one study found that artificially shortening day
length sped up the overall growing cycle but reduced both tuber number
and total yield while raising the fraction of total plant mass that
went into the harvested organ [12]. One specific widely grown cultivar
is described by growers as day-neutral and notably early-maturing
(about 90 days), but a controlled research trial instead classified
the same cultivar as "intermediate-flowering" with a specific inductive
day-length threshold, flowering at a fixed number of days after
planting and completing its full cycle in 128 days under California
growing conditions [8][11][13] — this entry keeps this as an
unresolved conflict. The crop generally needs at least 125 frost-free
days, with an optimum growing temperature of roughly 18-27°C, and soil
warm enough (at least about 7°C) for sprouting within 10-17 days of
planting [1].

**Finding 6 — a low-input crop, but not a drought-proof one.** This
crop has a generally low nitrogen requirement and is a strong weed
competitor in its own right [2]; it needs little fertilizer, with
excess nitrogen potentially reducing tuber formation, and it tends to
deplete soil fertility for whatever crop follows it [4]. It tolerates a
wide soil and pH range, favoring slightly alkaline soils, and performs
poorly on heavy clay, especially when waterlogged [1][4]. Soil moisture
falling below about 30% of field capacity during tuber formation
measurably limits yield [1]; a dedicated regional water-requirement
study found irrigation raised tuber yield by roughly 59% in trial
conditions [7]. There is a genuine, unresolved conflict in the
literature over overall drought tolerance: some agronomy reviews
describe this crop as drought-tolerant, while a general reference
describes it in its ecology discussion as preferring moist habitats
and being relatively intolerant of drought [2][7]. Separately, one
multi-year regional trial found that cooler, drier growing seasons
actually produced the highest total biomass, with heat and high
cumulative-temperature conditions reducing it [20]. Salinity testing
under controlled conditions found that moderate salinity reduced tuber
yield only modestly, while high salinity cut both tuber and shoot
yield substantially; the salt-driven damage was specifically traced to
chloride rather than sodium, and inulin content actually rose slightly
under salinity stress [8].

**Finding 7 — harvest is genuinely harder than harvesting potatoes.**
The dead, woody tops are cut and removed first [3]. The tubers
themselves are smaller than potatoes and cling more tightly to the
underground rhizomes; a standard potato-harvesting machine can lose
roughly half the crop unless modified with extra chains and agitation
to shake tubers free [1]. Separate mechanization research similarly
found that this crop's tuber clusters attach more firmly to the plant
than a potato's tubers do [19]. Documented contract rates from one
historical cost study show harvest costing notably more per acre than
either planting or cultivation combined — no direct labor-hour figure
was found, only these cost figures [1]. Harvest timing is generally
late autumn, after the first frost kills the foliage [5].

**Finding 8 — uses well beyond the table.** The tuber is eaten raw
(crisp, sweeter and nuttier than potato), cooked, or pickled; it turns
mushy if boiled [2]. A notable regional spirit is distilled from this
crop in parts of Germany, where over 90% of the regional crop is used
for distillation; both EU and Swiss regulations for this specific
spirit require it to be made from this crop alone, at a minimum
alcohol strength, with no added alcohol or flavoring beyond permitted
caramel coloring [2]. Roasted tubers are used as a coffee substitute,
and a leaf-protein concentrate can be extracted from the tops [4]. As
livestock feed, the tubers provide a documented energy value for both
ruminants and pigs, while the tops provide variable crude protein that
falls as the plant ages; dairy cattle can be fed substantial
quantities of fresh tops daily, tuber meal can make up a meaningful
share of cattle diets, poultry can be fed only dried tubers in modest
amounts, and pigs allowed to root in a field with this crop should be
limited in daily rooting time and given supplemental protein — no
toxicity to any of these animals has been reported, though the
fructan content does raise methane production in ruminants [4].
Industrial extraction of inulin, fructose syrup, and ethanol from this
crop have all been investigated, though ethanol production was found
uncompetitive economically [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A herbaceous perennial grown as an annual
  dig crop; planted in early spring, with tubers overwintering in the
  ground if left undug (Finding 2, 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest, either as a specific autumn dig
  after frost kills the tops, or dug progressively through winter
  directly from the field (Finding 2, 7).
- **Climate:** Cool temperate, temperate, and warm-temperate zones,
  with semi-arid cultivation possible under irrigation; poorly suited
  to humid tropical lowlands, where tubers can rot if not lifted
  promptly (Finding 5; climate detail below).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible, with a crisp, sweet, nutty flavor;
  turns mushy if boiled (Finding 8).
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production; true seed is
  produced only for breeding purposes and requires insect cross-
  pollination between compatible clones (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable, from roughly 10 to over
  65 t/ha depending on cultivar, management, and region; cutting the
  tops for forage dramatically reduces tuber yield, representing a
  genuine either/or tradeoff between the two products (Finding 4; see
  also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Soil moisture below about 30% of field capacity
  during tuber formation measurably limits yield; the crop tolerates
  waterlogged clay poorly. Overall drought tolerance is genuinely
  disputed across sources — some describe it as drought-tolerant,
  others as relatively drought-intolerant (Finding 6; water-use detail
  below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from whole small tubers
  or seed-tuber pieces carrying several buds, planted as early in
  spring as practical; hilling helps retain moisture and ease later
  digging (Finding 6; establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No direct labor-hour figures were found; only historical
  per-acre contract cost figures exist, showing harvest costing
  substantially more than planting or cultivation (Finding 7).
- **Soil:** Tolerates a wide pH range (roughly 4.5-8), favoring
  slightly alkaline soils; performs poorly on heavy, especially
  waterlogged, clay; needs relatively little fertilizer and can
  actually lose yield under excess nitrogen (Finding 6).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as an annual despite being a genuinely
  perennial plant that regrows from any tuber fragment left in the
  soil; fields are generally replanted fresh each year because
  volunteer stands become too thick to manage well (Finding 3).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal white mold is the main
  disease concern, managed through crop rotation away from several
  specific susceptible crop families; a separate fungal stem rot can
  cause up to 60% loss; rust, downy and powdery mildew, and a minor
  leaf-spot disease also occur; stalk borers are a minor pest; soil-
  borne diseases largely prevent commercial production in some warm,
  humid regions (Finding 6, 8).
- **Cultivars:** Numerous named cultivars exist with documented
  differences in maturity timing, tuber shape and size, and yield;
  taxonomic identity among some named cultivars is uncertain, with
  several possibly representing the same underlying clone under
  different trade names (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Tops are cut and removed first, then tubers are dug by
  modified mechanical digger or by hand; a standard unmodified potato
  digger can lose roughly half the crop (Finding 7).
- **Storage:** Long cold storage of 6-12 months at 0-2°C and 90-95%
  relative humidity is achievable, though storage life depends
  heavily on cultivar and on maintaining very high humidity, since
  this crop's thin skin makes it prone to rapid desiccation in dry air
  (Finding 2).

## Hazards

A specific fungal white mold is the crop's main disease concern,
managed through rotation away from several susceptible crop families
(not dry beans, sunflower, safflower, mustard, or soybean); a separate
fungal stem rot can cause up to 60% loss, and soil-borne disease
largely prevents commercial production in some warm, humid regions.
Rust, downy and powdery mildew, and a minor leaf-spot disease also
occur; stalk borers are a minor pest (Finding 6, 8).

This crop is a genuinely significant agronomic hazard in its own
right: it is a volunteer weed and locally invasive species, capable of
infesting several subsequent growing seasons if tubers are left in the
field, with documented yield losses in susceptible following crops
ranging up to roughly 81-91% (Finding 3).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's tubers are rich in inulin, a
fermentable fiber that humans cannot digest directly; large servings
can cause gas, bloating, and digestive discomfort as gut bacteria
ferment it, though tolerance generally rises with habitual fiber
intake. Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 1).

No toxicity to livestock has been reported for any part of this
plant, though the fructan content raises methane production in
ruminants, pigs need supplemental protein alongside it, and rooting
time should be limited for pigs grazing a field of this crop (Finding
8).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed tubers or pieces (with several buds each) planted
  early spring → tops killed by autumn frost → stalks removed → tubers
  dug → sorted and washed → cold-stored at 0-2°C and 90-95% relative
  humidity. Alternatively: same planting left in the ground → dug
  progressively through winter until spring regrowth begins.
  Alternatively: green tops cut before flowering → used fresh as feed
  or made into silage (at the cost of most of the tuber yield).
  Washed tubers → crushed and fermented → distilled into a regional
  spirit. Washed tubers → sliced → dried into chips → milled into
  flour (for food or feed) or roasted as a coffee substitute. Washed
  tubers → pickled in vinegar. Sliced tubers → hot-water extraction →
  inulin-rich juice (further refining not covered here). Compatible
  clones interplanted → short-day-triggered flowering → crossed seed
  heads → true seed used only for breeding.
- Not covered by this research in detail: frozen or canned product;
  inulin powder, oligofructose, or fructose-syrup industrial refining;
  fuel ethanol, butanol, biogas, or direct combustion of the tops;
  leaf-protein-concentrate extraction process; the practical cost of
  eradicating an established volunteer infestation.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables and underground organs
generally, including this crop's tuber. Separately, a story in
Iroquois tradition holds that the first plants of this crop grew from
the earth itself in a mythic account — a story rather than a
documented ritual production practice.

## Open research questions

- Documented volunteer-crop-loss figures conflict somewhat between two
  independent sources for the same following crops (maize and soybean)
  — both describe severe loss but give different percentages, not
  reconciled here.
- Storage-life figures conflict across sources (roughly 4-5 months in
  one source vs. 6-12 months in another vs. a much shorter 1-5 month
  range in informal gardening sources) — likely reflecting different
  storage-condition precision rather than a true conflict, but not
  fully resolved.
- Overall drought tolerance is genuinely disputed in the literature —
  some agronomy sources describe this crop as drought-tolerant, a
  general reference describes it as relatively drought-intolerant,
  and trial data shows both a clear yield response to irrigation and a
  surprising biomass advantage in cooler, drier growing seasons — not
  resolved here.
- One widely grown cultivar is described as day-neutral by growers but
  was classified as "intermediate-flowering" with a specific measured
  day-length threshold by a research trial — a real conflict, not
  resolved.
- Recommended row spacing differs across sources by a meaningful
  margin — not a major discrepancy but not reconciled.
- The number of buds recommended per seed-tuber piece differs slightly
  across sources (2-3 vs. 3-5) — not a major discrepancy.
- Crop-water-use (crop coefficient) figures differ sharply between a
  region-specific lysimeter-derived curve and a sunflower-genus
  analogy used as a cross-check, likely because the two were derived
  using different underlying methods rather than reflecting a true
  water-use difference.
- Two independent genome-size estimates for this species disagree by
  a meaningful margin, apparently depending on how a very large genome
  assembly is interpreted — not resolved here.
- No sourced labor-hour figures exist for any operation on this crop;
  only historical per-acre contract cost figures were found.
- Seed-crop (true-seed) yield per hectare was not found in any source,
  since true seed is used only for breeding, not commercial
  propagation.
- Detailed tuber composition beyond one feed-reference source, labor
  broken down by operation, distilling yields for the regional spirit
  product, ensiling details, and light/CO2 response data were not
  researched in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

A growing-season length of roughly 110-180 days is derived from a
documented minimum frost-free requirement of 125 days, combined with
cultivar-specific cycle lengths ranging from about 90 days (grower-
reported, for an early cultivar) to 128 days (research-trial-measured,
for the same cultivar) and a documented regional growing-season length
of 164 days in one specific study region.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 20,000 kg/ha, good 34,000 kg/ha, exceptional
65,000 kg/ha, poor 10,000 kg/ha) are derived from a combination of a
general reference's typical range, a widely cited agricultural-manual
figure for typical yield, and a specific three-year regional trial
average for the exceptional tier; the poor tier reflects the low end
of a documented overall range, close to one specific unfertilized
trial-plot result.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, root depth, moisture-
depletion allowance) are estimated primarily by analogy to sunflower,
the same genus, given its similarly tall annual canopy and comparable
growing season; a region-specific, lysimeter-derived monthly crop-
coefficient curve from one study is used as a dated alternative,
though it was derived using a different underlying calculation method
and gives notably lower values than the sunflower analogy. A drought-
buffer estimate of roughly 13 days before yield penalty begins is
derived from standard soil-water-holding assumptions combined with the
sunflower-analogy root depth and moisture-depletion figures.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 27 and a corresponding
seed-reserve fraction of about 0.04 are derived by dividing a
documented typical fresh-tuber yield by a documented seeding rate.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh tuber) are derived from a feed-composition reference's
documented dry-matter, protein, phosphorus, and potassium content,
converted to a removal-per-tonne basis; these reflect tuber removal
only, since removing the tops for forage would add further nutrient
removal beyond this figure.

Optimal soil pH (roughly 7.0) is estimated as the neutral point at the
low end of this crop's documented preference for "slightly alkaline"
soil, within its broader documented tolerance range of roughly 4.5-8.

Harvest index (roughly 0.35 on a fresh-weight basis) and edible yield
per unit area per day (roughly 22 g/m²/day) are derived from
documented per-plant organ weights (tops, tubers, and fine roots) and
the typical overall yield divided by a representative 150-day storage/
harvest window.

Genome size estimates diverge meaningfully depending on interpretation
of a very large genome assembly: a flow-cytometry-based figure implies
roughly 13.5 pg (1C), while interpreting a specific haplotype-resolved
genome assembly as covering only half the full hexaploid chromosome
complement implies a notably lower figure of roughly 10.7 pg — this
entry records both without resolving the discrepancy.
