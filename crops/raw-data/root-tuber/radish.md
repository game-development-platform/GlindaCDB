# Radish (*Raphanus sativus*, spring and winter radishes)

## Naming and scope

*Raphanus sativus* L., mustard family (Brassicaceae), grown for its
swollen hypocotyl and root. This entry covers the spring (salad)
radish as the main crop, with a winter radish cultivar group (hardy,
long-storing types sometimes called black radish or German Rettich
types) carrying its own growing calendar and storage regime within
the same entry. A long East Asian white radish type, grown and used
quite differently, is a separate entry this project has also
researched; Asian round radish types (such as a Korean round type)
remain grouped with that entry for now rather than with this one, an
unresolved boundary question. Two further distinct uses of this same
species — a type grown for its edible seed pods, and a type grown
purely as a cover crop for soil improvement — are each planned as
their own separate entries, since they differ meaningfully in product
and purpose from the root crop covered here.

This entry is grounded against a related fast-growing Brassicaceae
root crop this project has also researched, for its similar humid
cold-storage practice.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one of the fastest crop cycles documented in this
project, and one that needs to be sown again and again to keep a
harvest coming.** Typical time from sowing to harvest runs just 20-35
days, with some sources giving three to four weeks to reach a
marble-sized root; a controlled-environment space-research program
specifically chose this crop partly because it matures in only 27
days [4][5][15]. Roots stay in prime eating condition for only a few
days once mature, and must be harvested promptly or they turn pithy
[5]. As a direct consequence, commercial growers sow this crop in
succession — repeatedly, through the growing season, rather than as a
single planting — starting as soon as soil temperature allows in
spring and continuing into autumn [4][5].

**Finding 2 — two genuinely different storage worlds exist within one
species.** Spring radishes, topped (leaves removed), keep three to
four weeks at 0°C and 95-100% relative humidity, with a freezing point
of about -0.7°C [3]. Bunched radishes still carrying their tops keep
only one to two weeks under similar cold, humid conditions with
package and top ice [3]. Winter radishes, by contrast, keep a full
three to four months at the same 0°C and high humidity — the same
storage regime documented for several other related root crops — with
a notably lower freezing point around -2.2°C [3]. Radishes of either
type cannot withstand even brief freezing without serious quality loss
after thawing [3]. Cooling promptly after harvest is essential, since
roots otherwise go spongy from water loss; hydrocooling with chilled
water brings roots down to safe storage temperature within about five
minutes [3]. A very low-oxygen storage atmosphere (roughly 1%) slows
top regrowth, root growth, and softening, but only works above 0°C
[3][4]. Clipping the tops close to the crown is documented to greatly
retard top regrowth and reduce decay [3].

**Finding 3 — water needs are modest in total but must be applied
evenly.** A detailed US extension guide describes this crop's total
water requirement as very low, with peak demand during the rapid
root-growth phase [4]. Splitting of the root is caused mainly by
erratic water supply, especially heavy rain following a long dry
spell, or by leaving roots in the ground too long past maturity; one
detailed UK postharvest-loss study puts typical grower losses from
splitting at 5-30%, with the underlying causes "not yet fully
understood" [11]. Separately, any check to steady growth — whether
from drought, heat, or other stress — makes roots tough, pithy, and
excessively pungent, and hot, dry weather is specifically documented
as harmful to crop quality [5].

**Finding 4 — the crop is salinity-sensitive.** A standard
international water-quality reference rates this crop's storage root
as moderately sensitive to salinity, with a documented salinity
threshold and a notably steep yield-loss rate for each unit of
salinity above that threshold [2].

**Finding 5 — bolting is driven by long days and heat, sometimes
after a period of cold.** Long days combined with high temperature
cause the plant to bolt (switch to flowering) before the root
develops properly, cutting short the crop's usefulness [10]. Flavor,
texture, and root size are all best in a relatively cool temperature
band, roughly 10-18°C depending on source [4][10]. Like many mustard-
family plants, this crop generally needs some period of cold on
young seedlings combined with subsequently lengthening days before it
will bolt, though salad-type radishes are documented needing
considerably more cold exposure to trigger bolting than some other
mustard-family relatives; a distinct group of Asian cultivars bolts
naturally simply as days lengthen, without needing the cold period
first [10]. At least one breeding line selected specifically for
late bolting was found less sensitive to cold-triggered bolting than
standard cultivars [10].

**Finding 6 — the seed crop is self-incompatible, bee-pollinated, and
is the original source of a widely used male-sterility breeding
system.** Most commercial cultivars of this crop are self-
incompatible, meaning a single plant's own pollen cannot fertilize its
own flowers; honeybee visitation is documented adding roughly 22-45%
to seed yield, with one trade source estimating roughly 6.5 honeybees
per thousand flowers needed for full pollination [8]. Documented
isolation-distance requirements vary widely by source: a classic study
found outcrossing falling from roughly 30-40% at a very short distance
down to just 0.1% at about 73 m, while a general gardening guide
recommends a much larger isolation distance of roughly 450 m — a
substantial, unreconciled gap between practical seed-saving guidance
and measured outcrossing rates [8]. Documented seed yields from small
Indian field trials of different cultivars ranged from roughly 600 to
over 2,500 kg/ha depending on cultivar, planting date, and spacing,
with closer spacing giving the highest seed yield and wider spacing
giving the best seed quality [7]. Most significantly, a specific
cytoplasmic male-sterility system was first discovered in 1968 in a
Japanese radish of unknown cultivar, arising in the progeny of a
spontaneous cross between wild and cultivated plants; though only
lightly used in Japanese radish breeding itself, this sterility system
was transferred into European radish breeding (where a corresponding
fertility-restoring gene now exists in some cultivars) and,
significantly, was also transferred into rapeseed and other related
crops, where it became a major commercial tool for producing F1 hybrid
seed across the wider mustard family; the underlying sterility trait
is tied to a specific mitochondrial gene [14].

**Finding 7 — a space-grown crop.** A dedicated plant-growth facility
aboard the International Space Station grew and harvested a crop of
this species in late 2020, with the harvested plants returned to
Earth frozen for analysis [15]. This crop was specifically chosen for
the experiment because it is a well-understood, fast-maturing,
nutritious crop that is also genetically close to a standard
laboratory model plant [15]. The space experiment supplied minerals in
precise, measured amounts rather than through a slow-release fertilizer
medium [15]. Ground-based controlled-environment research has found
that root size responds strongly to total daily light exposure, with
one cultivar producing its largest roots at a specific daily light
integral within a wider tested range, and that elevated carbon dioxide
improves water-use efficiency only up to a point, with very high
carbon dioxide levels potentially harmful to some cultivars [15].

**Finding 8 — sprouted seed of this crop was implicated in the
largest food-poisoning sprout outbreak on record.** A 1996 outbreak of
a dangerous strain of *E. coli* affecting over 6,000 schoolchildren in
Japan was most likely carried by sprouted seed of a specific radish
type eaten raw; the resulting Japanese food-safety hygiene guidance
names seed and water as the critical control points for preventing a
recurrence [13].

**Finding 9 — history and culture.** A historical Greek account
reports an inscription recording expenditure on vegetables including
this crop for workers who built a major ancient Egyptian monument,
though the account itself was received secondhand through an
interpreter and the original author expressed some doubt about the
figure; translations of the passage differ on exactly which vegetables
were named [19]. The crop's exact origin of domestication is uncertain
— truly wild forms are documented only in Southeast Asia, and Asian
and European cultivated types are genetically distinct from one
another; the crop enters the written historical record by the 3rd
century BC, and Greek and Roman writers of the 1st century AD already
describe a wide range of forms (small, large, round, long, mild, and
sharp) [19]. A well-known Mexican festival held annually in late
December features a radish-carving contest with an officially
recognized competition dating back to the late 19th century, built
around nativity-scene themes [20]. A regional German beer-garden
tradition serves a specific winter radish type spiral-cut and salted
as a savory snack [9].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A fast-growing, cool-season annual root
  crop; the spring type completes its cycle in as little as 20-35
  days, while the winter cultivar group takes considerably longer (50-
  80 days) and is sown later in the season for extended storage
  (Finding 1; cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per sowing, with growers
  typically making many successive sowings through the season rather
  than one large planting (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones; a genuine cool-
  season crop even where grown in warmer regions, needing cool soil
  at sowing in both spring and autumn plantings.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  bee-pollinated and mostly self-incompatible (Finding 6).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields span roughly
  4,500 to over 30,000 kg/ha depending on region and intensity (see
  also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** A low total water requirement, but needing even,
  consistent moisture — erratic supply is the leading documented cause
  of root splitting (Finding 3).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded shallowly in close bands or rows;
  longer-rooted cultivars are typically sown somewhat thicker and
  deeper than round types (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** Harvesting (hand-pulling and bunching) is the largest
  documented labor component; no fully sourced labor-hour figures
  were found for this crop specifically.
- **Soil:** Friable, well-drained, light sandy loam at a moderately
  acid-to-neutral pH; the crop is moderately sensitive to salinity
  (Finding 4).
- **Stand life:** Modeled as an annual.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Flea beetles, cabbage root maggot, aphids,
  and loopers are common pests; clubroot (worse in more acid soil),
  a soil-borne scab disease (reduced by irrigation), a separate root-
  rot disease, downy mildew, white rust, and damping-off are
  documented diseases (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** European round and long spring types, a controlled-
  environment-tested spring type, a hardy winter radish group, and an
  Asian field-trial type (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Hand-pulled and bunched, or topped and trimmed for
  bulk packing, typically within days of reaching maturity to avoid
  pithiness (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Spring radishes keep three to four weeks near freezing
  in very high humidity; the winter cultivar group keeps three to four
  months under the same conditions (Finding 2).

Establishment detail: direct-seeded shallowly (roughly 0.5-1.25 cm
deep depending on source) in narrow bands or closely spaced rows;
longer-rooted cultivars such as some European heirloom types are
documented sown roughly 15-20% thicker and somewhat deeper than round
cultivars.

Cultivar detail: European round and long spring cultivars include
several well-known heirloom types, one dating to before 1885 and
another a mid-20th-century award-winning introduction; a controlled-
environment-tested round red cultivar performed best at a specific
daily light integral in greenhouse trials; the winter radish cultivar
group includes a long-grown European black-skinned type sown in mid-
to-late summer for autumn harvest and months of storage, with flavor
documented mellowing further in storage, and a white-skinned type
specifically associated with a regional German beer-garden tradition;
and a heat-tolerant Asian field-trial cultivar was used in several of
the seed-yield studies cited in this entry.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw sprouted seed of this crop was implicated in
the largest documented food-poisoning sprout outbreak on record,
affecting thousands of schoolchildren in a single 1996 incident;
resulting food-safety guidance names seed and water as the critical
control points for sprout production. Deferred until this project has
a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 8).

Flea beetles, cabbage root maggot, aphids, and loopers are common
field pests; clubroot (worse in more acid soil), a soil-borne scab
disease (measurably reduced by irrigation, not improved by soil
acidification), a separate root-rot disease, downy mildew, white
rust, and damping-off are documented diseases. A related wild form of
this species can poison livestock if its seed is eaten in quantity;
tops and surplus roots fed to livestock otherwise fall under general
feeding guidance for this plant family.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → bunched radishes with tops, harvested 20-35 days
  after sowing. Alternatively: seed → roots → topped, trimmed, and
  hydrocooled → cold storage for three to four weeks. Winter cultivar
  group: mid-to-late-summer sowing → winter radishes → topped →
  cold storage, or packed in damp sand, for three to four months.
  Seed → sprouted seed (raw use; see hazard above). Seed crop: a
  bolting crop, bee-pollinated and grown in isolation → seed stalks →
  threshed and cleaned seed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** quick pickles, canned or
  frozen product; the regional German spiral-cut beer-garden
  preparation, beyond noting its existence; edible seed pods from
  bolted plants, and a separate cover-crop type of the same species,
  both treated as their own distinct crops by this project; oversized
  radishes grown specifically for festival carving.

A well-known Mexican Christmas-season festival features a radish-
carving contest using deliberately oversized roots, with an officially
recognized competition tradition dating to the late 19th century. A
tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables and other underground
storage organs generally, a note this project has also recorded for
other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Documented seed-isolation guidance conflicts sharply: a classic
  measured-outcrossing study implies isolation of under 100 m is
  largely sufficient, while general gardening guidance recommends an
  isolation distance several times larger — not reconciled here.
- Composition figures conflict between two source types for fat
  content specifically (a notably higher figure from one industry
  storage manual against a much lower figure from standard nutrition-
  database sources), likely reflecting an error in the higher source.
- Recommended soil pH range differs somewhat between two extension
  sources.
- Documented bunched-radish storage life conflicts between sources
  (one to two weeks at near-freezing temperature versus about one
  week at a notably warmer temperature in a different source).
- A historical account of ancient Egyptian monument-worker provisions
  mentioning this crop differs across translations on exactly which
  vegetables were named, and the original source himself expressed
  doubt about the figures reported.
- Weakest areas overall: seed-crop timing and field seed yield rely
  only on small-plot trial data rather than broad commercial figures;
  labor-hour and nutrient-removal figures are unsourced; and sprout-
  product shelf life is an unresearched estimate.
- Where exactly the boundary lies between this crop and its long,
  white East Asian relative — specifically for Asian round and
  tropical radish types — remains unresolved; this entry treats them
  as belonging with the other, separately researched entry for now.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 10,000 kg/ha, good 22,000 kg/ha, exceptional
30,000 kg/ha, poor 4,500 kg/ha) are derived from several documented
regional commercial-yield figures — a national average and a "good"
figure from a standard vegetable-growing reference, a documented low-
end regional figure for the poor tier, and a documented high-end
regional figure for the exceptional tier — cross-checked against a
further regional figure that falls comfortably within the resulting
range.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a standard international
reference work's documented stage lengths and mid- and late-season
crop coefficients for this crop, with the initial-stage coefficient
taken from that same reference's general small-vegetables group value
since this crop's own specific coefficient cell could not be
recovered from the source; the resulting total seasonal water estimate
is cross-checked against a US extension guide's documented "very low"
total water requirement and found consistent. A days-without-water
tolerance of roughly 3 days is derived from standard soil-water-
holding assumptions combined with this crop's documented rooting
depth and moisture-depletion allowance.

A seeding rate of roughly 17 kg/ha is derived by averaging a
documented US field-bed seeding-rate range; a considerably higher
seeding rate is documented for dense market-garden row plantings.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 60 is derived by
dividing a representative working seed-crop yield figure (itself
estimated as a middle value among several documented small-plot trial
results) by the derived field seeding rate.

Seed mass (roughly 9.5 g per 1,000 seeds) is derived from a documented
average seed count per unit weight from a commercial seed supplier.

Seed viability figures (roughly 5 years ideal, 4 years ambient, 6
years in dry barn storage) are derived by combining a general
gardening guide's documented viability range with a seed-saving
organization's longer documented viability under cool, dry storage,
and a scientific long-term storage study's documented high germination
rate after 25 years under sealed, refrigerated conditions.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 150-180 days from sowing to seed
harvest is estimated from a documented Indian seed-crop planting date,
assuming a harvest several months later; spring-type cultivars are
assumed to behave as true annuals for seed production within a single
season, while the winter cultivar group likely behaves more like a
biennial, though this was not independently sourced.

Fertilizer application figures (roughly 56 kg/ha each of nitrogen and
phosphate, and roughly 112 kg/ha of potash) are derived by converting
a documented US extension recommendation from imperial units.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 75 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing a representative commercial yield figure by a representative
growing-season length.

Genome size (roughly 0.56 picograms, 1C) is derived from a documented
flow-cytometry genome-size measurement for a specific reference
cultivar, cross-checked against a range of documented genome-assembly
sizes for this species.
