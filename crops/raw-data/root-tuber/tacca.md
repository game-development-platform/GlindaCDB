# East Indian / Polynesian Arrowroot (*Tacca leontopetaloides*)

## Naming and scope

*Tacca leontopetaloides* (L.) Kuntze — widely known across the Pacific
under many regional names (pia in Hawaii, Tahiti, Niue, and the Cook
Islands; masoa in Samoa; mahoaʻa in Tonga; yabia in Fiji; gapgap in
Guam; taka in Indonesia), and in English as Fiji, East Indian, or
Polynesian arrowroot — family Dioscoreaceae (historically placed in
its own now-defunct family). This is a pan-Pacific cultivated plant
that Austronesian voyagers carried as a canoe plant roughly 5,000 years
ago, from West Africa through South and Southeast Asia to northern
Australia, Micronesia, Polynesia, and Madagascar [4].

This entry covers a single cultivated starch species with one
economic organ, the tuber. It is clearly distinct from two other
starch-tuber crops this project has already researched under the
"arrowroot" name: its raw tuber is bitter and genuinely poisonous, and
the starch-washing process is itself the detoxification step — the
same shape as cassava's raw-toxic-then-leached pattern, not the simple
starch-separation washing used for the other two arrowroot-type crops.
It is also a semi-wild, non-perishable, drought-hardy coastal crop
unlike either of those siblings. This entry is grounded against this
project's own prior research into the other two arrowroot-type crops
(the same starch-tuber niche and extraction chain) and into cassava
(the precedent for a raw-toxic-then-leached root crop).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — starch extraction is itself the detoxification step.**
The raw tuber is bitter and almost inedible, holding a bitter
poisonous compound (plus steroidal compounds of a related class); the
peels are unfit to eat [1][3]. Every traditional processing route
grates the tuber, kneads and washes the resulting mash, lets the
starch settle, and repeats this cycle — during this pounding and
leaching, the starch loses its bitterness [1]. A regional flora
reference states plainly that thorough washing is essential because of
the presence of this bitter substance, which is said to be poisonous
[3]. So unlike the other two arrowroot-type crops this project has
researched, where washing merely separates starch from fiber, here
washing is the actual safety step — the same pattern documented for
cassava.

**Finding 2 — a semi-wild crop with no documented cultivated per-
hectare yield in any source found.** Traditional Pacific Island
harvesters generally did not plant this crop systematically — large
tubers were dug and the small secondary "eggs" were thrown back into
the digging hole to serve as next year's planting stock [1]. A
regional plant-resource reference gives tuber size (roughly 70-340 g,
up to about 900 g, and 10-15 cm across) and a planting spacing, but no
per-hectare field yield [2]. A very large wild-harvest production
figure found in one regional source describes wild gathering rather
than a measured field yield, and is not used here. The yield tiers in
this entry are derived from spacing and tuber-weight figures rather
than measured, and are the weakest-sourced yield figures in this
project's root-tuber research so far.

**Finding 3 — the full traditional processing chain, with documented
timing from a Marshall Islands ethnobiological study.** Tubers are
rinsed, optionally peeled (one documented regional processing
tradition peels for a whiter flour, while another leaves the skin on),
grated on a hard abrasive surface, then kneaded in a strainer while
water is sprinkled over the mass — traditionally seawater, with fresh
water used for the final wash. The mixture is left to settle for one
to two hours, sifted again a few hours later, and sometimes sifted a
third time; the spent fiber is discarded. The settled starch lump then
drips dry in shade for two to three days, is crushed, and sun-dried
for a further two to four days. A separate Southeast Asian tradition
documents grating the tuber underwater specifically to prevent
browning that would otherwise discolor the starch. A documented batch
of roughly 57 kg of tubers takes about two days to dig, about half a
day to wash and grate, a day to sift, and about three days to dry [1].

**Finding 4 — starch yield is high and directly sourced.** A
documented batch of roughly 57 kg of tubers yielded roughly 11-14 kg
of pure starch, a 20-24% recovery rate; tuber starch content is
documented in a range of roughly 10-25% by one source and roughly
20-30 g per 100 g fresh weight by another, with the remainder being
fiber, skin, water, and processing waste [1][2]. Flour made from this
starch is documented with a specific calorie, water, protein, and
carbohydrate composition per 100 g. Starch granules are documented in
the 8-40 micron range (averaging roughly 20 microns) — smaller than
the granules of one sibling arrowroot-type crop's starch, but larger
than wheat starch granules [2].

**Finding 5 — no perishability deadline at all, a genuine departure
from most root crops this project has researched.** Unprocessed tubers
keep up to six months in cool, dark pits before sprouting, and dry
starch keeps well over a year — indefinitely if protected from
weevils, ants, and cockroaches [1]. This contrasts sharply with the
very short raw-rhizome shelf life documented for one of this crop's
sibling arrowroot-type crops. Tubers and dried starch are traditionally
wrapped in leaves or mat bags for storage, and a dried starch lump can
be stored whole and broken up as needed [1].

**Finding 6 — annual top dormancy with a renewed tuber, and drought-
triggered die-back.** Leaves die back and the tuber renews itself each
year; in one documented Pacific region the plant is dormant for
several months in the cooler part of the year [6]. The overall growth
cycle runs roughly 8-10 months, including 2-4 dormant months; harvest
is timed to when the leaves wither [2]. Drought triggers the same
top die-back response seen in the natural dormancy cycle — the tuber
survives underground and resprouts once rain returns [1]. Plants grown
from true botanical seed take roughly three years to flower and form a
mature tuber, and this method was almost never used traditionally in
the Pacific [2].

**Finding 7 — a hardy, coastal, almost pest-free crop.** Usually found
below roughly 200 m elevation (occasionally up to roughly 1,100 m), on
marginal soils in coastal beach vegetation alongside other hardy
coastal plants, well drained, tolerating only slight shade, and
benefiting from some protection from direct salt spray; no serious
diseases or pests are documented for this crop [2]. It withstands
drought relatively well [1]. This stands in clear contrast to a
virus-burdened sibling starch-tuber crop and a soft-rot-prone konjac,
both researched elsewhere in this project.

**Finding 8 — an understorey crop under trees, a tentative
agroforestry case.** Since at least the early 20th century, this crop
has traditionally been grown in coconut groves, intercropped with
papaya, banana, breadfruit, and other hardy coastal plants; a mid-20th-
century regional survey specifically proposed interplanting it under
coconut once competing vegetation had been cleared [1]. This is the
second tentative agroforestry case this project has documented
alongside konjac, though this crop's shade tolerance is only slight,
not a true requirement.

**Finding 9 — it spreads like a weed, yet collapses without regular
weeding.** This crop is documented tending to spread like a weed, and
has naturalized across tropical Africa, South Asia, Australia, and
Oceania [4]; yet an unweeded stand is quickly overgrown by competing
vegetation, documented as a contributing factor in this crop's decline
in one Pacific region [1]. This makes it a fourth crop in this
project's research with a volunteer/invasive-persistence pattern, but
with an interesting twist: a weedy spreader that nonetheless competes
poorly in a managed, unweeded stand.

**Finding 10 — a twice-collapsed commercial market, and a non-food
fiber use.** A 19th-century export starch trade existed across several
Pacific and Southeast Asian regions; in the 20th century the starch
became mainly a laundry-starch product and lost its food role to
imported rice, and the crop is documented as rarely cultivated today
[1][4][8]. The flower stalk (peduncle) yields a fiber that is
traditionally plaited into hats and twisted into fishing line in parts
of Polynesia [3] — a secondary non-food product alongside the starch.
Documented food uses include coconut-cream-based puddings known under
several regional names; documented traditional Hawaiian medicinal use
applies the raw tuber or starch mixed with water and red clay for
diarrhea, dysentery, and bleeding [6].

**Finding 11 — cytology.** This species carries a chromosome count of
2n = 30, documented by a historical chromosome count and a modern
chromosome-count database [2][7]. No nuclear genome size figure has
been published for this species specifically, though a genome assembly
exists for a related ornamental species in the same genus [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A tuberous perennial grown and dug as an
  annual, planted at the start of the rains and harvested when the
  leaves wither; warm-season only.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single annual harvest; the tuber is renewed
  each year from the previous season's growth (Finding 6).
- **Climate:** Tropical and subtropical coastal zones, usually below
  roughly 200 m elevation, occasionally up to roughly 1,100 m.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — the raw tuber is bitter and
  genuinely poisonous until processed (Finding 1).
- **Pollination:** Not required for tuber production, since this is a
  clonally propagated tuber crop; the plant's flowers are insect-
  pollinated trap flowers, a trait of the genus, but this is not
  relevant to normal cultivation.
- **Yield / composition:** No measured field yield was found in any
  source; derived yield figures span roughly 2,500-25,000 kg/ha of
  fresh tuber (Finding 2; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** A notably drought-hardy crop tolerating deep soil-
  moisture depletion before its tops die back; needs free-draining
  soil and performs poorly on waterlogged ground (water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from small secondary
  tubers planted one or two per hole; true botanical seed is a very
  slow, rarely used alternative (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated field-operation labor survey was found; one
  documented wild-tuber digging rate is the only sourced labor anchor,
  with all other labor figures estimated by analogy.
- **Soil:** Indifferent to soil type, grows well on marginal, well-
  drained sandy coastal and calcareous atoll soils; no documented
  fertilizer use in traditional practice (soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** A perennial geophyte dug as an annual; productive
  indefinitely if left in place and allowed to self-propagate from
  small tubers, but quickly outcompeted by other vegetation if
  unweeded (Finding 9).
- **Disease/pest profile:** No serious diseases or pests are documented
  for this crop (Finding 7); the only documented hazard is the raw
  tuber's own natural toxin (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** No named cultivars exist — this is a wild-type, pan-
  Pacific landrace spread by Austronesian voyagers; what varies
  regionally is processing practice and local naming, documented here
  as two named Marshall Islands processing traditions plus a general
  pan-Pacific wild-type grouping (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single harvest stage — the tuber is dug when the
  leaves wither; the flower stalk is a separate secondary product, not
  a maturity stage of the tuber itself.
- **Storage:** No perishability deadline at all for either the raw
  tuber or the processed starch (Finding 5).

Water detail: no standard international crop-water-use reference
documents this species directly; water-use parameters are estimated by
analogy to the two sibling arrowroot-type crops this project has
researched, with a notably lower late-season value reflecting this
crop's natural leaf die-back before harvest. Documented total seasonal
water requirements were not found in any source; this crop is
generally rainfed and rarely irrigated.

Establishment detail: small secondary tubers (traditionally under
roughly 25 mm) are planted one or two per hole at a documented spacing;
no nursery stage is used. True botanical seed germinates slowly and
erratically and takes roughly three years to produce a mature tuber,
and was almost never used in traditional Pacific practice.

Soil detail: no documented fertilizer use exists in traditional
practice; this crop is described as indifferent to soil type and
tolerant of calcareous, salt-spray-exposed coastal sand, though it
needs genuinely free-draining ground and performs poorly if
waterlogged.

Cultivar detail: documented processing-tradition groupings include a
western-Marshall-Islands tradition that peels the tuber before grating
for a whiter flour; an eastern-Marshall-Islands tradition that grates
with the skin left on for a darker flour; and a general pan-Pacific
wild-type landrace grouping covering the crop's broader regional names
and uses, including starch, puddings, laundry starch, and craft fiber.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the raw tuber and its peel contain a bitter
poisonous compound plus related steroidal compounds; the extracted,
properly washed starch is safe, but eating the raw or unleached tuber
or peel is hazardous. The traditional grate-knead-wash-and-settle
processing chain is itself the mitigation, with the peel discarded;
residual risk is low after proper leaching. Deferred until this
project has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 1).

No diseases or pests are otherwise documented for this crop (Finding
7). A processing-quality issue (browning of the grated pulp, not a
genuine hazard) is addressed by grating the tuber underwater.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** small secondary tuber → planted → tuber grown through a
  season, tops dying back at harvest time → dug tuber → rinsed,
  optionally peeled → grated → kneaded and washed repeatedly in water
  → settled, sifted → starch dried in shade, then sun-dried → dried
  starch (the washing-and-settling step is simultaneously the
  detoxification step, per Finding 1). Flower stalk (peduncle), a
  separate secondary product → fiber stripped and plaited → craft
  items including hats and fishing line.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** specific modern
  commercial processing or laundry-starch manufacturing; detailed
  nutritional or chemical analysis of the discarded peel beyond its
  documented bitter-compound and anti-nutrient content.

Documented food uses include coconut-cream-based puddings under
several regional names. Documented traditional Hawaiian medicinal
practice applies the raw tuber or starch mixed with water and red clay
for diarrhea, dysentery, and bleeding. No crop-specific religious or
ritual production rule was found in this research pass; a coconut-
cream pudding made from this crop's starch is documented as a feast or
celebratory food in Hawaiian and broader Polynesian culture, which is
cultural rather than a sect-specific ritual requirement.

## Open research questions

- Documented starch-content figures conflict somewhat between two
  sources (a 10-25% range from one source versus a 20-30 g per 100 g
  figure from another).
- Documented typical elevation range conflicts somewhat between
  sources (usually below roughly 200 m versus occasionally up to
  roughly 1,100 m).
- No measured field yield exists in any source found; the yield figures
  in this entry are derived only from spacing and tuber-weight
  estimates, and should be replaced with a measured trial figure if one
  becomes available.
- Labor-hour figures beyond one documented wild-tuber digging rate are
  estimated by analogy rather than sourced; a genuinely low-input,
  semi-wild production mode (no bed preparation, no fertilizer, seed
  tubers left in the digging hole) does not map cleanly onto a
  conventional labor-tier framework.
- No nuclear genome size figure has been published for this species,
  though its chromosome count is documented.
- Detailed chemical analysis of the discarded peel's anti-nutrient
  content was not researched in depth here, beyond a general note that
  the peel is unfit to eat.
- Whether this crop has been tested in any space-agency or controlled-
  environment life-support research was checked and no such trial was
  found.
- Soil-chemistry tolerances beyond salt and calcareous-soil tolerance
  (boron, aluminum, manganese, and general micronutrient demand) were
  not researched.
- Detailed light-response and photoperiod data were not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

Fresh-tuber calorie and protein content (roughly 850 kcal/kg, 10 g
protein/kg) are derived from documented fresh-tuber starch content
(roughly 22 g per 100 g) converted at a standard starch-to-calorie
ratio, plus a small allowance for fiber and protein, since the only
directly documented composition table found applies to dried flour
rather than the fresh tuber; a dedicated fresh-tuber food-composition
analysis would be a better source if one becomes available.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 6,000 kg/ha, good 12,000 kg/ha, exceptional
25,000 kg/ha, poor 2,500 kg/ha) are derived entirely from documented
planting spacing (translating to roughly 25,000-37,000 plants/ha) and
documented typical and maximum tuber weights, multiplied together at
different weight assumptions for each tier; no field yield trial was
found in any source, making this the weakest-sourced yield figure in
this project's root-tuber research so far.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve) are estimated by
analogy to the two sibling arrowroot-type crops this project has
researched, reflecting a broad-leaved starch tuber, with a notably
lower late-season coefficient reflecting this crop's natural leaf
die-back before harvest. A soil-moisture-depletion allowance of
roughly 0.6 is estimated reflecting this crop's marked drought
hardiness, tolerating deep depletion before its tops die back.
Documented total seasonal water-use figures were not found in any
source; estimated seasonal water-use totals are set low, reflecting
growth on drought-prone coastal sand. A days-without-water tolerance
of roughly 10 days and a small, slow yield penalty per dry day are
estimated, reflecting that drought in this crop mainly triggers early
dormancy with the tuber surviving underground rather than causing
direct yield loss.

A planting-tuber seeding rate of roughly 450 kg/ha, an individual
planting-tuber weight of roughly 15 g, and a seed-to-yield
multiplication ratio of roughly 13 are derived from documented
planting density combined with a documented small-tuber weight
threshold from one Pacific regional practice; a directly sourced
planting-tuber weight figure would be a better source if one becomes
available.

Harvesting labor at the smallest production tier (roughly 1,000 h/ha)
is derived by analogy from the one directly sourced wild-tuber-digging
labor rate, scaled to assume somewhat faster digging per unit of
tuber mass in a denser, deliberately planted stand compared to wild
gathering; all other labor-tier figures and all other labor operations
are estimated without any directly sourced anchor.

Soil nutrient-uptake figures are estimated by analogy to other starch-
tuber crops, since no removal-rate figures were found and no
fertilizer is documented in traditional use. Soil pH range (optimum
roughly 6.5, minimum roughly 5.0, maximum roughly 8.5) is estimated
from this crop's documented indifference to soil type and its
tolerance of calcareous coastal and atoll sand, which pushes the
upper pH tolerance unusually high. Effective rooting depth (roughly
30 cm) is estimated from documented tuber size and typical planting
depth.
