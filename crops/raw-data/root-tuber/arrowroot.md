# Arrowroot (*Maranta arundinacea*)

**Category:** Root/Tuber · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/root-tuber/arrowroot.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-24

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

True, West Indian, or Bermuda arrowroot, *Maranta arundinacea*
(Marantaceae), also called the "obedience plant" [1][4]. Several other,
botanically unrelated starchy crops are also commonly sold under the
name "arrowroot" — including Queensland arrowroot (edible canna),
Polynesian arrowroot (tacca), and Florida arrowroot (coontie) — all of
which are distinct crops this project treats separately; a starch
sometimes called "Guyana arrowroot" is actually a yam product. This
entry covers only true *Maranta* arrowroot.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a detailed Brazilian cost study gives sourced labor
figures, with harvest the largest single cost.** A 2017 study in
Dourados, Brazil, tracked a full season of a named commercial cultivar
and found total labor of roughly 84-92 man-days per hectare, broken
down across set preparation, planting (rising with planting density),
irrigation, weeding, hilling, and harvesting, with harvesting alone the
single largest component [4]. This matters because labor cost,
specifically the cost and difficulty of hand-digging, is widely cited
as the main reason the historically dominant arrowroot-exporting
industry (in St. Vincent) has shrunk over recent decades, as younger
generations are unwilling to take up this demanding manual harvest
work, and mechanized harvest remains difficult [2][8][13].

**Finding 2 — rhizomes rot fast: they must be processed within about
48 hours of harvest.** Multiple sources describe a very narrow
post-harvest window before rhizomes begin rotting, generally cited as
around 48 hours, though specific cultivar-level figures vary somewhat
[2][8]. A dedicated 30-day storage trial found substantial mass loss
even under refrigeration (around 28% at room temperature, rising to
roughly 42% at colder refrigerated temperatures), along with a large
drop in recoverable starch content after storage — the clear
conclusion being that rhizomes intended for starch extraction should
never be stored, while rhizome pieces intended as planting material
actually keep reasonably well at room temperature for about a month
[6]. The practical consequence is that field harvest rate is directly
constrained by processing capacity — a plot can only be dug as fast as
it can be processed.

**Finding 3 — yields: roughly 10-35 t/ha in general references, 20-60
t/ha in recent research trials.** General references give a broad
range of roughly 10-35 t/ha of fresh rhizomes and 2.5-7.5 t/ha of
extracted starch [2]. More recent, better-documented research-station
trials in India report average yields in the low-to-mid 20s to low 30s
t/ha across several named varieties, with documented yield potential up
to about 49 t/ha under good management, and notably higher potential
(35-40 t/ha) when intercropped under coconut or areca palm [3]. A
separate Indian trial across several genotypes averaged around 27
t/ha [7]. Brazilian density trials found yield rising substantially
with closer in-row spacing, reaching nearly 33 t/ha at the closest
tested spacing [4]. A separate Brazilian trial combining irrigation and
nursery-raised seedlings achieved the highest yield found in this
research (just over 60 t/ha), while a non-irrigated seedling treatment
at higher density gave the best starch yield specifically; one
treatment in that same trial produced an anomalously low yield far
below every other treatment, likely reflecting an unexplained stand
failure rather than a real treatment effect [5]. A Philippine trial
showed yield rising substantially with nitrogen fertilization rate
[9], and an Indonesian source reports roughly 36 t/ha at a 12-month
harvest [10].

**Finding 4 — starch content is roughly 20% of the fresh rhizome, with
8-18% actually recovered in practice.** Rhizome starch content is
consistently reported around 19-21% across several sources, with
commercial extraction efficiency typically well below the theoretical
maximum — figures from various studies and historical commercial
practice range roughly 6.6-18.4% recovered starch per 100 g of fresh
rhizome, depending heavily on processing method and freshness
[2][5][6][7][10]. A Philippine pilot-scale processing study found that
adding a granulating step raised recovery meaningfully over a baseline
method [12]. The resulting starch has distinctive qualities — easily
digested, gluten-free, and very fine-grained — and commands a price
far above commodity starches like maize or cassava starch in both
Brazilian and Caribbean markets [4][10][13].

**Finding 5 — processing follows a consistent wash-peel-grate-strain-
settle-dry sequence.** Typical processing steps are: soaking in hot
water and peeling off the fibrous outer scales; grating or rasping
(historically done with saw-graters at industrial scale, or blended
with water at lab scale); straining through cloth or screens, which
separates out a coarse/fine fibrous residue by-product; settling the
resulting starch "milk" (repeatedly resuspending and resettling until
the starch runs white, or using a centrifuge at industrial scale); and
finally drying, either in the sun on racks or in a heated dryer
[1][2][5][12]. Historically, the St. Vincent processing method was
criticized for using excessive amounts of clean water, and grinding
and settling operations could not run simultaneously in at least one
historical factory, creating a processing bottleneck [2][12].
Household-scale starch extraction is also documented as a viable, if
smaller-scale, practice [3].

**Finding 6 — the plant tolerates shade and is commonly intercropped
under coconut.** The plant naturally forms thickets in shade and
benefits from shade specifically on light soils; it is recommended as a
shade-tolerant intercrop under coconut and areca palm in at least one
major growing region [2][3]. Climate requirements are generally warm
tropical to subtropical, with substantial rainfall (sources give
somewhat different but overlapping ranges in the 950-2,000 mm annual
range), grown from sea level up to roughly 900-1,000 m in most areas,
though one historical archaeological growing site was at a much higher
elevation [1][2]. Dormant rhizomes can tolerate temperatures as low as
about 5°C, but the plant is otherwise frost-tender; in the continental
United States it survives outdoors only in the warmest subtropical
areas [1]. Preferred soil is deep, friable, and slightly acidic loam
[2].

**Finding 7 — planting, spacing, and fertilizer practice vary by
region.** Planting material ("sets") is typically a rhizome piece a
few centimeters long weighing roughly 15-60 g with 2-4 growth buds, cut
directly from a prior harvest [3][4][5]. Planting density and resulting
seed-rate figures vary substantially by region and target spacing, from
roughly 1 to over 5 tonnes of sets per hectare [3][4][8]. Timing and
layout differ by region: raised beds at the start of the rainy season
in one major growing region, versus ridged, irrigated beds in a
different season in a South American growing region, versus a
double-sucker-per-hill planting method in a Southeast Asian growing
region [3][4][9]. A dedicated nursery-seedling method (growing sets in
bags for roughly 70 days before transplanting) substantially improved
final stand survival compared to planting sets directly at wide
spacing, and achieved the highest yields in that trial [5]. Typical
fertilization combines farmyard manure with an NPK blend; yield
responds positively to nitrogen rate in at least one trial [3][9].
Keeping the field weed-free for the first several months and earthing
up (hilling) the row with each weeding is widely recommended, though
one Brazilian trial found no measurable yield benefit from hilling
specifically, in contrast to recommendations from two other growing
regions [3][4][9].

**Finding 8 — harvest comes at leaf senescence, roughly 9-12 months
after planting.** The harvest signal is consistently leaf senescence —
sources describe harvesting once over half to over 60% of the leaves
have browned and bent to the ground, generally occurring somewhere in
a 9-12 month window depending on source and growing conditions
[1][2][3][4][5]. Earlier harvest (around 8-10 months) is possible but
a full-season harvest (11-12 months) gives meaningfully more total
yield and higher starch content [9]. Harvest method ranges from fully
manual plow-and-lift to a mixed approach using machinery to loosen the
row followed by hand lifting [4][9]. Successive crops are commonly
grown on the same land for 5-6 years before replanting is needed,
since the plant naturally regrows from residual rhizome material [2].

**Finding 9 — foliage and processing residue are useful, safe animal
feeds.** The above-ground foliage is notably high in protein (over a
quarter of dry matter in one analysis) and has been shown in feeding
trials to support sheep and cattle diets well, including as a majority
or even sole forage component for lambs without loss of digestibility,
though the root crown itself was refused by test animals due to high
mineral content [2]. Leftover aerial biomass can also be ensiled,
though this somewhat reduces its protein value and digestibility [2].
Leaves have also been used as fish feed for farmed catfish and tilapia
in several countries [2]. The fibrous processing residue left over
from starch extraction has documented feed uses for pigs and poultry,
including after fermentation treatment in at least one country's
broiler-feed research; no antinutrients have been reported in any part
of this plant [2].

**Finding 10 — composition data shows an internal conflict between a
standard nutrient-database figure and separately measured starch
content.** A commonly cited nutrient-database figure for raw rhizome
gives a relatively low energy and carbohydrate content, which is
difficult to reconcile with separately measured starch content figures
of around 19-21% (or even higher carbohydrate figures from a different
regional composition study) — these figures are not fully consistent
with each other, and the discrepancy is not resolved in available
sources [1][7][10]. A separately measured protein figure from one
regional study is notably close to the nutrient-database figure, but
this is unusually high for a starchy rhizome and it is unclear whether
it reflects a fresh- or dry-weight basis [1][7]. A dried-powder
composition figure from a different regional study shows much higher
carbohydrate content, consistent with what would be expected after
removing most of the moisture [11].

**Finding 11 — pests and diseases are relatively few.** A specific
insect pest (an arrowroot leaf roller) has historically been
controlled with arsenical sprays, and the resulting historical
pesticide use in at least one major growing region's monoculture
practice left lasting soil contamination from lead arsenate residues
[2][8][9]. Documented diseases include a bacterial wilt, a banded leaf
blight, root rot caused by two different fungal pathogens, and an
aphid-spread mosaic virus [8][9]. A specific rhizome deformity
("cigar roots," short and swollen) is noted without a clearly
established cause [8]. Overall pest and disease pressure appears
relatively low — one research group attributes general pest resilience
to peroxide compounds in the plant, and at least one commercial trial
required no pesticide applications at all [3][4].

**Finding 12 — a long history of use, including a major colonial-era
export industry.** Archaeological starch-grain evidence from a
highland South American site dates use of this plant back roughly
10,000 years, with early domestication possibly motivated by use as a
wound poultice [1]. The plant has long been used in food and medicine
by at least one Caribbean Indigenous people [13]. A Caribbean island
nation was the dominant world exporter of arrowroot starch from
roughly 1900 to 1965, at times representing close to half of the
nation's total export earnings; the industry declined sharply from the
mid-1950s onward as competing land uses expanded and as a major
historical US buyer switched to cheaper corn starch in the 1960s (a
specialty paper industry use for arrowroot's fine grain was also
eventually displaced by wheat starch) [14]. Production fell drastically
by the late 1990s compared to historical peaks, though this nation
remained the largest single producer into recent decades [1][2][7][14].
A natural disaster destroyed the last traditional processing factory in
2021, with international development funding supporting construction
of a replacement facility as of the mid-2020s [13]. Other current
producers include India, Brazil, the Philippines, and Indonesia, among
others [2][3][9][10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A tropical perennial grown as an annual
  rhizome crop, planted at the start of the rainy season; the field
  stand is commonly left in place and redug yearly for 5-6 successive
  years before replanting (Finding 6, 8).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual harvest per planting cycle, timed
  to leaf senescence; the same planted stand supports repeated yearly
  harvests over several years (Finding 8).
- **Climate:** Tropical to subtropical, generally 20-29°C with
  substantial rainfall (roughly 950-2,000 mm depending on source),
  grown from sea level up to about 900-1,000 m in most current growing
  regions; shade-tolerant, suiting it to intercropping under tree
  crops (Finding 6).
- **Food status:** Not typically eaten raw; the vast majority of
  production goes to starch extraction rather than direct vegetable
  use.
- **Pollination:** Not required — flowers are reported as sterile or
  rarely setting seed; propagation is entirely vegetative, from
  rhizome pieces (Finding 12 context; see also Hazards/Open questions
  below).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable by growing region and
  management — general references cite 10-35 t/ha, while modern
  research-station trials report 20-60 t/ha depending on variety,
  density, irrigation, and propagation method (Finding 3).
- **Water use:** Benefits from steady moisture through the rhizome-
  filling period, with irrigation typically stopped roughly two months
  before harvest; at least one trial found irrigation increased total
  yield but reduced starch recovery percentage, a genuine yield-vs-
  quality tradeoff (Finding 3; Estimates below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from cut rhizome pieces
  ("sets") with 2-4 buds, either planted directly or raised first as
  nursery seedlings in bags for about 70 days before transplanting —
  the nursery method notably improves stand survival (Finding 7).
- **Labor:** A detailed Brazilian cost study found roughly 84-92
  person-days of labor per hectare per season, with harvest the single
  largest component; hand-harvest cost and difficulty are the
  specifically documented driver behind one major historical producing
  region's industry decline (Finding 1).
- **Soil:** Deep, friable, slightly acidic loam is preferred (Finding
  6).
- **Stand life:** Commonly left in place and redug for 5-6 successive
  years without replanting, though at least one source instead
  describes the crop as managed strictly as an annual — this is a
  genuine difference in regional practice rather than a resolved
  conflict (Finding 8).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A leaf-rolling insect pest, bacterial wilt,
  banded leaf blight, two root rot pathogens, and an aphid-spread
  mosaic virus are documented; overall pest/disease pressure is
  relatively low (Finding 11).
- **Cultivars:** Few named clonal varieties exist, reflecting generally
  low genetic variability in the crop; named varieties differ mainly in
  yield, starch content, and (for historical West Indian types)
  post-harvest keeping time before processing is required (Finding 3,
  12; cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** Dug once per season at leaf senescence (roughly 9-12
  months after planting), either fully by hand or with mechanical
  assistance to loosen the row; harvest rate is constrained by how
  quickly dug rhizomes can be processed (Finding 2, 8).
- **Storage:** Fresh rhizomes must be processed within roughly 48
  hours and should never be stored if starch extraction is the goal, as
  both total mass and recoverable starch content drop sharply within
  days, even under refrigeration; rhizome pieces intended as planting
  material, by contrast, keep reasonably well at room temperature for
  about a month (Finding 2).

## Hazards

A specific insect pest (an arrowroot leaf roller) and several fungal
and bacterial diseases are documented (Finding 11), but no toxin or
antinutrient has been reported in any part of this plant — it is
considered a safe, bland food crop with safe feed by-products as well
(Finding 9, 11).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — historical arsenical pesticide use against the
leaf-roller pest left lasting lead arsenate soil contamination in at
least one major historical growing region's farmland. This is an
environmental/soil contamination legacy issue rather than a hazard of
the crop itself, deferred until this project has a place for this kind
of health/safety-adjacent data (Finding 11).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** rhizome sets planted at the start of the rains → weeded
  for the first several months → dug at leaf senescence (9-12 months)
  → washed and peeled within the 48-hour processing window → grated →
  strained (yielding a fibrous residue by-product usable as animal
  feed) → settled and rewashed → dried into finished starch. The same
  planted stand is commonly redug for several successive years. Sets
  can alternatively be raised in nursery bags before transplanting.
  Foliage goes to livestock feed (fresh or ensiled); the rhizome itself
  is occasionally cooked and eaten as a minor vegetable use.
- Not covered by this research: starch hydrolysis into glucose syrup
  (a documented but specialized industrial process); fermenting the
  processing residue for broiler feed in detail; starch-based films or
  fruit coatings; canned or frozen product (none documented for this
  crop).

## Open research questions

- A substantial internal conflict exists between a standard nutrient-
  database energy/carbohydrate figure and separately measured starch
  content figures that imply meaningfully higher carbohydrate content
  — not resolved in available sources.
- Whether this crop is better modeled as a true multi-year perennial
  stand (redug for 5-6 years) or as a strict annual depends on the
  growing region and source, and is not a fully resolved question.
- Whether flowers are properly described as fully sterile or merely
  rarely setting seed is given inconsistently across sources.
- A dramatic outlier yield result in one otherwise well-documented
  Brazilian trial (far below every other treatment in the same study)
  is reported as-is by the original researchers without a clear
  explanation and was not independently resolved here.
- Whether hilling (earthing up) meaningfully improves yield is
  disputed: no effect was found in one Brazilian trial, while
  cultivation guidance from two other growing regions recommends it.
- No dedicated crop-water-use study exists for this crop; the water-
  use estimates below are built entirely by analogy to a different
  tropical root crop with broadly similar growth duration and leaf
  cover, despite a meaningful difference in flood tolerance between
  the two.
- Historical production-volume figures for the dominant historical
  exporting region show unresolved unit-conversion inconsistencies
  between different secondary sources.
- Nitrogen-removal estimates derived from nutrient-database protein
  content may overstate true nitrogen uptake, given the broader
  composition conflict noted above; a documented regional fertilization
  program applies far less nitrogen per hectare than simple removal
  arithmetic would suggest is needed, which is more consistent with a
  lower true protein content or a larger soil/manure nitrogen
  contribution than with the nutrient-database figure being accurate.
- No dedicated labor study exists for the starch-extraction process
  itself (as opposed to field labor), which remains a real gap in the
  labor picture for this crop.
- Genome size was not found in a primary source; only a chromosome
  count and a broader family-level range were available.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 15,000 kg/ha is estimated: smallholder,
largely unfertilized production likely sits near the bottom of the
commonly cited 10-35 t/ha general range (around 12-15 t/ha), while
research-station trials average meaningfully higher (20-32 t/ha); 15
t/ha is chosen as a value between a historical world-reference figure
and the research-station averages. Good yield is estimated at 30,000
kg/ha and exceptional at 49,000 kg/ha, both drawn from documented
research-station potential figures; poor yield is estimated at 10,000
kg/ha, from the low end of the general reference range. Plausible
range: 12,000-20,000 kg/ha. National production statistics from a major
growing region would replace this estimate.

A fresh-to-dry weight ratio of roughly 4:1 is derived from regional
moisture-content figures, with starch content alone (around 20% of
fresh weight) setting a rough floor on plausible dry-matter content;
plausible range 3.7:1 to 4.8:1.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are derived entirely by analogy to a different
tropical starchy-rhizome crop with a similar roughly 9-12 month growth
duration and heavy leaf cover, adjusted for arrowroot's documented
preference for free-draining soil (versus the other crop's flood
tolerance) by using a somewhat larger soil-moisture-depletion
allowance before yield penalty. This produces a seasonal water-use
index with a plausible range of roughly 200-300 (days-equivalent) and
a drought-buffer estimate of roughly 3-6 days before yield penalty
begins. A dedicated water-balance or crop-coefficient study for this
crop (none was found) would replace this estimate.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio ranging from roughly 5.5 to 31 is
derived from documented combinations of harvested yield and seed-rate
figures across different growing regions; the wide range reflects
differences in planting density and individual set size between
regions, rather than a single representative figure.

Labor-hour estimates across different mechanization levels are built
partly from the sourced Brazilian cost study (for a moderately
mechanized tier) and partly by analogy and estimation for less- and
more-mechanized tiers, since no comprehensive labor study spanning the
full range of practice was found; a plausible range of roughly ±40%
around these estimates is assumed. A harvest window of about 90 days is
estimated from the documented range of viable harvest timing and a
major historical producer's documented harvest season length,
acknowledging that each plot must still be processed within the
48-hour post-harvest window once dug.

Nutrient removal per tonne of fresh rhizome is derived from the
standard nutrient-database composition figure: roughly 6.8 kg
nitrogen, 1.0 kg phosphorus, and 4.5 kg potassium per tonne, with the
caveat (noted above) that the nitrogen figure may be an overestimate
given the broader composition-data conflict and the comparatively
modest fertilization rates documented in actual high-yield cultivation
guidance.

A harvest index of roughly 0.66 (fresh-weight basis) and an edible
yield rate of roughly 10 g/m²/day are derived from a Brazilian trial
that separately measured rhizome, leaf, and root biomass at harvest.
