## Naming and scope

*Beta vulgaris* L. subsp. *vulgaris*, Altissima Group — the sugar-
producing cultivar group of a species that also gives table beet and
Swiss chard, both covered as their own separate entries by this
project, plus a fodder-type cultivar group grown for livestock feed
that this project has not yet researched. The species' wild ancestor
is sea beet, a coastal wild plant. This entry covers only the sugar
crop. The plant has 18 chromosomes. This entry is grounded against
this project's own prior research into potato, a similarly temperate
root crop covered by the same kind of standard international water-
use reference tables and with its own substantial processing chain.

Shared background with other members of this species (candidates for
future consolidation when the related fodder-type cultivar group is
researched): the biennial seed-crop practice (overwintered root stock,
wind pollination, strict isolation from every other cultivar group of
the same species), and a set of livestock-feed hazards around oxalate
and nitrate content.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the harvested root loses its own sugar every single day
until it reaches the factory.** The product is sucrose, making up
roughly 14-20% of fresh root weight, most commonly 14-18%; roughly
80% of that sucrose can be crystallized into white sugar, meaning
roughly 7.7 tonnes of beets at a typical sugar content are needed to
produce one tonne of refined sugar [1][6]. After harvest, the root
keeps respiring and literally burns away its own sugar content; a
general industry estimate puts this loss at roughly a quarter of a
kilogram of sugar per tonne of roots per day [4]. Documented loss
rates vary enormously with handling: undamaged, carefully hand-dug,
fungicide-treated roots lost under a third of that rate over a 140-day
storage trial, while machine-harvested and piled roots lost
considerably more, with or without fungicide treatment [4]. Loss rates
are documented running high immediately after harvest, falling for a
period, then climbing again late in storage [4]. Bruising is
specifically documented as the dominant driver of this loss, and
bruising rises sharply once roots are handled below a specific cool
threshold temperature; each handling step (mechanical harvesting,
transfer from harvester to truck, and transfer from truck to storage
pile) is documented contributing roughly equal shares of total damage
[4]. Covered outdoor storage piles (clamps) lost substantially less
sucrose per day than uncovered piles in one set of German trials, with
respiration accounting for roughly half to three-fifths of total
loss, and dehydration being a particular concern in the outer portion
of an exposed pile [5]. Processing factories run a short annual
campaign of roughly 90 days, fed continuously from these storage
piles, which are ventilated with cold air immediately after formation
and, in colder climates, deliberately frozen solid to slow sugar loss
further [5][6].

**Finding 2 — water needs are most acute at establishment, peak at
mid-season, and are deliberately cut off before harvest.** Total
seasonal water use runs roughly 500-800 mm, with peak daily use of
roughly 6-9 mm during the month of highest demand [1]. The crop is
most sensitive to water deficit at emergence and for about a month
afterward, when frequent light irrigation is recommended both for
establishment and to counter soil crusting and topsoil salinity;
notably, early over-watering is documented slowing leaf growth and
potentially triggering premature first-year flowering (bolting) [1].
Mid-season water deficits, occurring during vegetative growth and
yield formation, cut sugar yield more than deficits occurring later in
the season; late-season water availability instead raises root fresh
weight while lowering sucrose concentration, so its net effect on
total sucrose yield is comparatively small [1]. Irrigation is
typically stopped two to four weeks before harvest specifically to
raise sucrose concentration, though soil must remain moist enough for
the roots to be lifted without excessive damage [1]. Roughly a quarter
of the world's sugar beet crop is irrigated, with irrigation rates
varying from 80-100% of cultivated area in some regions to under 40%
in others; where irrigation is essential, considerably more water is
applied than where it is merely supplemental [1]. When water supply is
limited but land is not, the documented strategy is to cut irrigation
earlier and plant a larger area instead, since sucrose yield per unit
of water actually rises under restricted late-season watering [1].

**Finding 3 — this is a biennial crop that must be prevented from
flowering in its first year.** The plant stores sugar in its storage
root during the first growing season and flowers only in its second
year, after experiencing winter cold [1][6]. Modern commercial
cultivars are documented as almost completely insensitive to day
length for triggering first-year flowering under normal growing
conditions [1]. Premature first-year flowering (bolting), which cuts
sucrose yield substantially, is induced by a combination of low
temperatures and long day length, with documented vernalizing
temperature ranges varying somewhat across sources [1][17]. Breeders
specifically select for very strong flower-induction requirements so
that bolting-resistant cultivars can be sown unusually early in spring
or even in autumn as a "winter beet" type [1]. The separate seed crop,
by contrast, specifically requires an overwintering cold period to
induce flowering [17].

**Finding 4 — the crop is broadly salt tolerant, except as a very
young seedling.** Documented yield loss begins at a specific salinity
threshold, reaching 50% loss at roughly double that threshold and
100% loss at a considerably higher level; during early seedling growth
specifically, a much lower salinity threshold applies [1]. Sodium is
documented partly substituting for potassium as a plant nutrient, with
research finding sodium acting as a genuine direct nutrient with a
significant negative interaction with potassium uptake, such that both
elements are needed together for maximum yield; sodium fertilizer is
documented raising yields in drier growing seasons, but at the cost of
raising sodium impurities that interfere with sugar extraction during
processing [20].

**Finding 5 — a heavy industrial processing chain exists alongside a
genuine farm-scale alternative.** The factory process begins with
washing in water flumes, slicing into V-shaped strips called
cossettes, and countercurrent hot-water diffusion at a specific
elevated temperature for roughly an hour, yielding a raw sugar juice
plus wet fibrous pulp [6][7]. Purification uses lime and carbon
dioxide from the factory's own lime kiln in a two-stage carbonation
process at elevated temperature, producing a clarified thin juice; the
resulting filter cake, made mostly of calcium carbonate, is returned
to agricultural fields as a soil amendment [15]. Concentration and
crystallization proceed through multi-effect evaporation to a thick
juice, vacuum-pan boiling with seed crystals, and centrifuging across
two or three successive boilings, with the final residual liquid
becoming molasses [7]. By-products include pressed wet pulp (fed to
livestock or further dried and pelleted with added molasses) and
molasses itself, which is used for animal feed, yeast production, or
alcohol distillation, or further processed to extract residual sugar
and a valuable extract compound; a traditional Central European rum-
like spirit is documented distilled from beet molasses [6][7][9][15].
Notably, unlike sugarcane processing, a beet sugar factory has no
fibrous plant waste to burn as fuel and must instead rely entirely on
fossil fuel for its energy needs [6]. A genuine farm- and cottage-
scale alternative exists in several European culinary traditions: root
slices are cooked in roughly their own weight of water, pressed, and
the resulting juice boiled down into a thick syrup, used much like a
treacle or molasses substitute [9][10].

**Finding 6 — historically, this crop's labor story centered on
multigerm seed and extensive hand-thinning.** Traditional seed takes
the form of a multigerm cluster that produces several seedlings from
a single sown unit, meaning every stand historically had to be
laboriously thinned by hand after rough mechanical spacing [8][16]. A
detailed 1913-15 farm-account survey from Minnesota documented a
total labor requirement of roughly 155 hours of human labor and 111
hours of draft-animal labor per acre, with hand-thinning,
hand-hoeing, and hand-pulling-and-topping together making up roughly
two-thirds of total labor; specialized contract workers performed
these hand operations notably faster than ordinary farm labor [8]. A
separate Canadian historical study documented a broadly similar total
of roughly 115 hours of hand labor per acre, with workers crawling
along rows to thin seedlings by hand [27]. The breakthrough came in
1948, when several single-seeded (monogerm) plants were discovered
among hundreds of thousands of ordinary plants in an Oregon seed
field, traced to a single recessive gene; combined with mechanical
thinning and herbicide use, this is documented reducing total labor to
roughly 5% of what hand-singling a traditional multigerm crop required
in English field trials conducted in the early 1960s, though yields
were somewhat lower under this early mechanized system [16].

**Finding 7 — hybrid seed production relies on a single historical
cytoplasmic male-sterility discovery.** Research in the 1940s
discovered naturally occurring male-sterile plants within one specific
breeding line, combining a sterilizing cytoplasmic factor with two
recessive nuclear genes; "maintainer" lines carrying normal cytoplasm
but the same recessive genes were developed to propagate the sterile
seed parent. Essentially all commercial hybrid sugar beet seed in use
today traces back to this single original cytoplasmic male-sterility
source [16]. The monogerm trait was subsequently combined with this
male-sterility system to produce monogerm hybrid cultivars; triploid
hybrids are also produced by crossing diploid male-sterile lines with
tetraploid pollinator lines, or the reverse [16]. Since the late 1960s,
essentially all commercial sugar beet planted in the United States has
used monogerm hybrid seed [16]. Seed itself is grown as a genuinely
separate two-year crop, mostly using overwintered root stock
("stecklings"): basic seed is sown in nursery beds in mid-to-late
summer, with the resulting young roots lifted and transplanted the
following winter to early spring in regions with mild enough winters
[12]. A detailed regional seed-certification standard documents
isolation-distance requirements that scale with the type of
cross-contamination risk, with by far the largest isolation distance
required from the related chard, red beet, and fodder beet cultivar
groups of the same species, reflecting how easily this crop
cross-pollinates with its closest relatives [13].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial root crop, grown as
  an annual for its root, either spring-sown in temperate regions or
  autumn-sown in Mediterranean, arid, and subtropical regions (Finding
  3).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest per planting; the plant is
  topped and lifted once, well before it would naturally flower.
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate, temperate, and Mediterranean zones
  (autumn-sown), semiarid and arid zones (irrigated), and subtropical
  zones (as a winter crop), spanning a wide latitude range.
- **Food status:** Grown almost exclusively for industrial sugar
  extraction rather than direct human consumption; not typically eaten
  as a fresh vegetable.
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production, since the crop is
  harvested before it would naturally flower; the separate seed crop
  is wind-pollinated and largely self-incompatible (Finding 7).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial yields span roughly
  15,000-100,000 kg/ha of fresh topped root depending on region and
  era, with sucrose content typically 14-18% of fresh weight (Finding
  1, 4; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Most sensitive to deficit at establishment; mid-
  season deficits cut sugar yield, while a late-season irrigation
  cutoff two to four weeks before harvest actually improves sucrose
  concentration (Finding 2).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded shallowly in rows; historically
  sown thickly with multigerm seed and thinned by hand, now typically
  precision-sown with monogerm hybrid seed to a final stand
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** Historically dominated by hand-thinning, hoeing, and
  hand-harvesting; modern mechanized production with monogerm seed and
  herbicide use requires only a small fraction of historical labor
  (Finding 6; labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Nutrient-rich, moist, well-drained, deeply plowed soil;
  broadly salt-tolerant once established, though seedlings are much
  more salt-sensitive (Finding 4).
- **Stand life:** Biennial in natural life cycle, harvested as an
  annual; the seed crop alone takes a full second growing season.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific soil-borne virus causing
  "root madness" (many small, malformed roots), a fungal leaf-spot
  disease with well-documented weather-based infection forecasting, and
  two further viral diseases are documented concerns (hazard detail
  below).
- **Cultivars:** A long breeding history moving from early open-
  pollinated multigerm types through the monogerm discovery to modern
  cytoplasmic-male-sterility-based hybrids, raising sugar content from
  roughly 5-6% around 1800 to roughly 18% today (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** A single lifting event per planting, with documented
  monthly yield and sugar-content changes through the autumn harvest
  window in one western European study (harvest detail below).
- **Storage:** Roots are piled in outdoor storage (clamps) or
  ventilated factory piles, where sugar content actively decays every
  day until processing; covered and deliberately frozen piles lose
  sugar more slowly than exposed, unfrozen ones (Finding 1).

Establishment detail: direct-seeded 1-2 cm deep in rows spaced
roughly 0.4-0.76 m apart; historically sown in considerably wider rows
with multigerm seed requiring hand-thinning, documented giving a
measurable yield benefit for each additional well-spaced plant per
unit of row length; modern monogerm seed is precision-drilled to a
final stand with comparatively little or no thinning required.

Labor detail: a historical measured labor survey found land
preparation, planting, weeding (including hand-thinning and hoeing),
harvesting (pulling and topping), and post-harvest hauling together
totaling roughly 155 human labor-hours per acre under horse-drawn
cultivation with multigerm seed; modern monogerm hybrid seed combined
with mechanical thinning and herbicide use is documented reducing
total labor to a small fraction of this historical figure.

Cultivar detail: an early 18th-century German open-pollinated
multigerm cultivar is documented as the ancestor of essentially all
later sugar beet cultivars; several further named 19th-century
European open-pollinated multigerm cultivars followed, including the
specific breeding line in which male-sterile plants were later
discovered; the single-seeded (monogerm) trait was discovered in a
single Oregon seed field in 1948 and is controlled by one recessive
gene; modern commercial production is now dominated by monogerm
cytoplasmic-male-sterility hybrids, alongside triploid hybrids, disease
-tolerant hybrids bred against the soil-borne root-madness virus,
specifically bolting-resistant ("winter beet") hybrids for early or
autumn sowing, and hybrids bred specifically for tropical and
subtropical growing conditions.

Harvest detail: one western European study found yield and sugar
content both still rising through early autumn, roughly level through
mid-autumn, and declining somewhat if harvest is delayed into early
winter, illustrating a fairly narrow optimal harvest window for
balancing total yield against sugar content.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's tops and byproducts carry documented
livestock-feed hazards: oxalate in the leaves, nitrate/nitrite risk in
leaves following a dry spell and subsequent rain, a rumen-acidosis risk
from excessive root feeding, and a choking risk specifically from
dried pulp fed to horses; deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

No unusual human health hazard was found; the refined white sugar
product is essentially pure sucrose. A specific soil-borne virus
causes a disease sometimes called "root madness," producing many
small malformed roots; a fungal leaf-spot disease has well-developed
weather-based infection-risk forecasting models; two further viral
diseases affect foliage; and storage rot following bruising or
freezing is a documented post-harvest concern.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → sown to a precision stand (monogerm) or drilled
  thickly and hand-thinned (historical multigerm) → topped roots and
  tops at a single lifting. Roots → piled in a clamp or ventilated
  storage pile → washed → sliced into cossettes → hot-water diffusion
  → raw juice plus wet pulp. Raw juice → limed and carbonated → thin
  juice → evaporated to thick juice → boiled and crystallized → white
  sugar plus molasses. Wet pulp → pressed → combined with molasses →
  dried pulp pellets for livestock feed. Molasses → fermented →
  distilled into alcohol, or desugared to recover residual sucrose.
  Alternatively, on a farm or cottage scale: washed roots → sliced →
  cooked in water and pressed → juice boiled down into a traditional
  thick syrup; press cake fed to livestock. Tops → wilted feed or
  silage. Seed crop: basic seed → nursery beds → overwintered root
  stock transplanted the following season → seed stalks → threshed,
  cleaned, graded, and pelleted.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** advanced molasses-
  desugaring chemistry and extraction of a specific valuable compound;
  early rasping-and-pressing extraction methods used by historical
  factories; thick-juice intermediate storage; seed pelleting and
  priming treatments in detail; detailed disease-control and
  herbicide-tolerant cultivar chemistry. No canning or freezing product
  form exists for this crop.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, consistent
with notes this project has recorded for other root crops, though
whether refined beet sugar itself (as opposed to the whole root) is
avoided under this tradition was not researched.

## Open research questions

- Root dry-matter content conflicts across sources by several
  percentage points.
- World average yield figures conflict depending on data vintage,
  reflecting genuine yield improvement over roughly a decade between
  the sources consulted, rather than a true conflict.
- Documented vernalizing temperature ranges for triggering first-year
  bolting vary somewhat across sources (several overlapping but
  non-identical ranges reported).
- Documented critical seedling-frost damage thresholds differ between
  a Turkish study and a German study by roughly two degrees Celsius.
- Documented daily storage sugar-loss rates vary by roughly a factor
  of two between sources, likely reflecting different handling and
  damage conditions rather than a true conflict.
- Documented diffusion-process timing in the factory chain varies
  somewhat between an industry handbook and an encyclopedia source.
- A farm-scale syrup yield figure from one specific regional study
  conflicts with a simple theoretical calculation based on typical
  root sugar content and syrup concentration — not reconciled here.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour figures for less-mechanized and
  highly mechanized production tiers (estimated rather than directly
  sourced); a seasonal yield-response factor and corresponding yield-
  penalty-per-dry-day figure (recalled rather than independently
  verified); seed viability by year (carried over by analogy from an
  unrelated root crop); and days-to-seed-harvest timing (estimated
  from documented nursery-bed sowing and transplanting dates rather
  than directly sourced).
- Waterlogging tolerance, detailed nutrient-uptake-per-tonne figures,
  carbon-dioxide response, light saturation, and spaceflight trial
  information were not researched for this crop.
- Whether refined beet sugar carries any religious dietary
  restrictions (kosher, halal, or Passover status, or an analogous
  question to the bone-char filtration question sometimes raised for
  cane sugar) was not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

Energy and protein composition figures (roughly 700 kcal/kg, 15 g
protein/kg) are derived from documented sucrose content converted to
energy using a standard conversion factor, with a smaller contribution
from protein content and fiber energy deliberately excluded given its
low and uncertain human-digestible energy value; no direct food-
composition-table entry for raw sugar beet root was found in the
national databases consulted.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 60,000 kg/ha, good 75,000 kg/ha, exceptional
100,000 kg/ha, poor 15,000 kg/ha) are derived from a combination of
recent global production statistics for the world-average tier,
documented recent national yield figures from two major European
producing countries for the good tier, a standard international
reference work's own documented "good commercial yield" range
(somewhat lower, reflecting an older data vintage) cross-checked
against the good tier, the same reference work's documented upper
yield figure for the exceptional tier, and documented low national
yield figures from a specific historical year for the poor tier.
Historical yield figures from the 1913-15 Minnesota labor survey, when
converted to metric units, fall considerably below all of these
modern tiers, illustrating substantial yield improvement over the
intervening century.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived from a standard international
reference work's documented crop-coefficient values and one specific
regional stage-length breakdown, cross-checked against the broader
seasonal water-use range documented in a separate international
water-response reference work. A days-without-water tolerance of
roughly 12 days is derived from standard soil-water-holding
assumptions combined with this crop's documented rooting depth and
moisture-depletion allowance, though seedlings need water considerably
sooner than this figure would suggest. A yield-penalty-per-dry-day
figure is estimated from a recalled (not independently re-verified)
seasonal yield-response factor; the true penalty is understood to be
strongly stage-dependent, with late-season deficits costing
considerably less sucrose yield than early- or mid-season deficits.

A seeding rate of roughly 1.2 kg/ha (monogerm, precision-drilled) or
roughly 17 kg/ha (multigerm, historically drilled thickly) is derived
from documented seed mass and target plant population figures, or
from a historical seeding-rate figure converted to metric units.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of roughly 2,300 (monogerm) or
roughly 170 (multigerm) is derived by dividing a documented seed-crop
yield figure by the corresponding seeding rate for each seed type.

Seed viability and germination-rate figures are carried over by close
analogy from an unrelated root crop (turnip), reflecting a general
assumption that this is a small, dry seed stored similarly to other
root-crop seed; a likely but unverified difference is that this
crop's seed has a corky fruit wall and, when pelleted, may not store
as well as that analogy would suggest.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 330-390 days from sowing to seed
harvest is derived from a documented mid-to-late-summer nursery-bed
sowing date and an assumed following-summer seed-harvest date.

Isolation distances for seed production and documented seed-crop
yield figures are derived by converting a detailed regional seed-
certification standard's imperial-unit figures to metric units.

Labor-hour tiers are derived primarily from the historical 1913-15
Minnesota farm-account survey, converted to metric units, for an
intermediate mechanization tier; a fully manual tier is estimated by
analogy to potato's own documented hand-labor figures, with hand
cultivation, lifting, and carrying added; more heavily mechanized
tiers are scaled down from a documented English trial finding that
monogerm seed combined with mechanical thinning and herbicide use
required only a small fraction of traditional hand-singling labor,
though these more mechanized tiers remain estimates rather than
directly sourced figures. A harvest window of roughly 45 days is
derived from the historical survey's documented pulling, topping, and
hauling period, considerably shorter than a modern factory's full
roughly 90-day processing campaign.

A salinity yield-loss slope of roughly 5.9% per unit of salinity is
derived from the documented span between the salinity threshold where
yield loss begins and the point of complete yield loss, cross-checked
against the documented 50%-loss salinity threshold for consistency.

Storage life (roughly 100 days cool and dry) is derived from a
documented storage-trial period and from the length of a typical
factory processing campaign, acknowledging that roots in deliberately
frozen piles keep considerably longer, albeit still losing a
substantial share of their sugar content over an extended storage
period at the documented typical daily loss rate.

Edible yield per unit area per day is derived separately for fresh
root and for sucrose specifically, by dividing representative yield
figures for each by a representative growing-season length.

Genome size (roughly 0.73-0.78 picograms, 1C) is derived from a
documented genome-size estimate from a landmark genome-sequencing
study, converted using a standard genome-size conversion factor.
