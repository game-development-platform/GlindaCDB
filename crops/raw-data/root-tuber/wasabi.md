# Wasabi (*Eutrema japonicum*)

## Naming and scope

*Eutrema japonicum* (Miq.) Koidz. (with historical synonyms placing it
under other genus names), family Brassicaceae. This is a single
species, not a lumped entry, and is distinct enough from horseradish
(a related but separate pungent-root condiment crop this project has
already researched) to warrant its own entry — chiefly because of its
semi-aquatic, flowing-water growing method. This entry is not split
into separate water-grown and soil-grown crop entries; these are
treated as two cultivation paths of one crop rather than separate
entries. This entry is grounded against this project's own prior
research into horseradish (the same family, the same pungent-rhizome
condiment model, and the same core pungent-compound chemistry — the
closest sibling entry), with the major differences being water regime,
growing-cycle length, and thermal tolerance.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a semi-aquatic crop grown in constantly flowing cool
water.** The premium water-grown cultivation method grows this crop in
gravel or sand beds continuously flushed year-round by cool spring
water at roughly 10-15°C with dissolved oxygen above roughly 9 ppm,
under heavy shade [1][4][5][6]. This is a continuous clean flow-
through water regime, not intermittent irrigation — a water-management
pattern unlike any other crop this project has researched so far. A
separate soil-grown cultivation method is a more conventional wet,
shaded-bed crop that fits a standard irrigation-based water model more
readily.

**Finding 2 — a narrow thermal envelope; heat is the main threat.**
Optimum air temperature runs roughly 8-20°C, with a seasonal optimum
around 10-16°C; above roughly 28°C the plant takes heat damage and
becomes susceptible to a specific bacterial soft rot [1][8]. Below
roughly 8°C, growth stalls. This narrow thermal tolerance is a major
factor behind a documented climate-driven production decline in Japan,
with harvests in one well-known growing region down roughly 55% over
a decade [12].

**Finding 3 — a long growing cycle, grown like a perennial but
harvested like a biennial.** This crop takes roughly 18-24 months to
produce a harvestable rhizome of roughly 10-15 cm (with a documented
range of 14-36 months across different sources), after which the whole
plant is pulled by hand and its side shoots are divided and replanted
[8][9][10]. This is roughly double the one-year growing cycle
documented for horseradish.

**Finding 4 — propagated by clonal side-shoot offsets, not seed.**
Commercial propagation is by side-shoot offsets (small rooted
plantlets) divided from the parent plant's crown at harvest, with
several produced per plant; true seed is strongly dormant and slow to
germinate, and is used mainly for breeding rather than commercial
planting [1][8][9]. Clonal planting lines are typically renewed every
roughly three years to limit disease buildup in the stock [9].

**Finding 5 — multiple plant organs yield multiple distinct products.**
The rhizome is the primary product, grated into a pungent paste, but
the leaves, leaf stalks, and spring flower stalks are all eaten as
well, with the soil-grown cultivation method yielding proportionally
more leaf relative to rhizome [1][6]. Removing flower stalks serves a
dual purpose: the stalks themselves are sold as a separate product, and
removing them keeps the plant from diverting energy away from the
rhizome through premature flowering.

**Finding 6 — pungency chemistry richer than horseradish's, and even
more short-lived.** Grating the rhizome triggers an enzyme that acts on
glucosinolate compounds to produce the same core pungent compound found
in horseradish, plus additional longer-chain pungent compounds that
give this crop's pungency a more aromatic, "nose not tongue"
character; this aroma and heat fades within roughly 15-20 minutes of
grating [1]. The same core irritant hazard documented for horseradish
applies here as well. Unlike horseradish, there is no distilled
essential-oil product made from this crop in the trade.

**Finding 7 — most "wasabi" sold worldwide is actually horseradish.**
Because the real crop is difficult and slow to grow, the bulk of paste
and powder products sold as wasabi worldwide are actually made from
horseradish and mustard with green coloring added, a well-documented
market substitution [1]. This is a market-authenticity fact that this
entry notes specifically to distinguish genuine products made from
this crop from the horseradish-based substitute already covered in
this project's horseradish entry.

**Finding 8 — short storage life, unlike horseradish.** The fresh
rhizome keeps only a matter of weeks: grated rhizome keeps up to 30
days refrigerated, while the whole rhizome keeps roughly one to two
months under cool, humid conditions [8]. This is dramatically shorter
than the 10-12-month storage life documented for horseradish root.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** An evergreen, cool-climate perennial grown
  for roughly two years before being fully lifted — effectively dug
  like a long-season biennial crop.
- **Harvest pattern:** The rhizome is harvested once, with the whole
  plant pulled at maturity; leaves and leaf stalks can be cut
  repeatedly as a separate product, and flower stalks are harvested
  seasonally as yet another separate product — these are distinct
  plant organs on separate harvest schedules, not different maturity
  stages of a single product.
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones, montane or maritime
  cool conditions; needs shade and cool water, and is intolerant of
  heat (Finding 2).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible — the grated rhizome eaten raw is the
  entire point of the crop; leaves are also eaten raw but can have a
  mild laxative effect if eaten in quantity.
- **Pollination:** Not required for rhizome or leaf harvest, since the
  crop is harvested regardless of flowering; pollination matters only
  for the minor, breeding-focused true-seed route.
- **Yield / composition:** A documented regional growing-guide figure
  gives roughly 10,000-15,000 kg/ha for a well-managed stand, used as
  the basis for this entry's yield tiers (Finding 1; see also Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** The water-grown cultivation path does not fit a
  standard irrigation-based water-use model at all — it is a
  continuous flow-through system rather than metered irrigation; the
  soil-grown path fits a standard model more closely (water detail
  below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from side-shoot offsets
  divided from the parent plant at harvest; true seed is rarely used
  commercially due to strong dormancy (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for this crop; all
  labor figures are rough placeholder estimates. The water-grown
  cultivation method is documented as notably labor- and capital-
  intensive, involving hand planting, hand weeding in flowing water,
  hand harvest, and the substantial labor of building and maintaining
  stone or gravel streambed infrastructure.
- **Soil:** A documented pH range of roughly 6.0-7.0; the crop prefers
  low nutrient concentrations in the growing medium (soil detail
  below).
- **Stand life:** An evergreen perennial grown to size over roughly 1.5-
  2 years, then fully lifted; clonal planting lines are typically
  renewed every roughly three years for disease control (Finding 4).
- **Disease/pest profile:** No well-sourced non-pest physiological
  disorder was found; the main documented losses come from rot diseases
  and heat damage (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Several named Japanese water-grown cultivars spanning
  a range of pungency and rhizome size, a disease-aware bred Japanese
  cultivar line, a general soil-grown cultivar group, and a modern
  genome-sequenced Korean cultivar (cultivar detail below).
- **Harvest:** A single harvest stage for the rhizome; leaves, leaf
  stalks, and flower stalks are harvested separately as distinct
  products on their own schedules (Finding 5).
- **Storage:** A notably short storage life compared to horseradish —
  only weeks to a couple of months for the whole rhizome, and up to 30
  days refrigerated once grated (Finding 8).

Water detail: this crop needs constant cool (roughly 10-15°C), well-
oxygenated water at all times; a warm spell or any interruption in
water flow stalls rhizome growth and brings on soft rot. The hazard in
the water-grown cultivation method is not water volume but water
temperature and stagnation — still or warm water drops dissolved
oxygen below the level the roots need and causes rot. Documented
management practice for the water-grown method centers on maintaining
continuous cool spring-water flow year-round, often filtered and
temperature-buffered by a specific traditional sand-and-gravel bed
design; the soil-grown method instead keeps beds saturated with clean,
cool irrigation water and never lets them dry out.

Establishment detail: propagated from side-shoot offsets at a
documented spacing of roughly 30 cm within rows and 30-60 cm between
rows; offsets are replanted promptly after division, since they remain
viable for only a few weeks if wrapped and kept cool. Clean planting
stock is best achieved through tissue-culture propagation, though
division of healthy mother plants remains the more common practice.

Soil detail: documented fertilizer practice favors balanced, modest
nutrient application with added sulfur specifically, since sulfur-
containing fertilizer is documented improving this crop's pungency and
flavor (consistent with its pungent compounds being sulfur-containing
by nature); under-fertilizing is documented as safer than over-
fertilizing. Growing medium should be loose, highly organic, free-
draining yet constantly wet.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a traditional Japanese
water-grown benchmark cultivar with clean, balanced heat and a large
rhizome; a hotter, more aromatic Japanese water-grown cultivar; a
further balanced Japanese water-grown cultivar close to the benchmark
type; a disease-aware bred Japanese cultivar series with improved yield
reliability; a general soil-grown cultivar group with a slightly more
bitter or sharp flavor, a coarser and smaller rhizome, and a larger
leaf and leaf-stalk share, grown in soil rather than flowing water; and
a modern genome-sequenced Korean cultivar.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this crop's pungent sulfur-containing compounds
(including the same core compound found in horseradish, plus
additional longer-chain compounds unique to this crop) are released by
grating or crushing and can irritate eyes and airways in concentrated
amounts; large quantities of raw leaf can also cause gastric upset.
Normal culinary amounts are safe; ventilation and moderate intake are
the documented mitigations. Deferred until this project has a place
for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — pungent culls, trimmings, and foliage fed to
livestock carry the same sulfur-compound and glucosinolate-residue
risks documented for other brassica-family condiment crops, including
goiter-related and gastrointestinal effects; composting rather than
feeding these residues to livestock is the documented mitigation.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data.

No well-sourced non-pest physiological disorder was found for this
crop; the main documented losses come from a specific bacterial soft
rot (worsened by heat and stagnant warm water), further root and
rhizome rot diseases, viral disease carried in clonal planting stock,
and several documented insect and mollusk pests.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** side-shoot offset → planted into a flowing-water gravel
  bed or a saturated shaded soil bed → grown roughly 18-24 months →
  whole plant pulled by hand at harvest, side shoots divided off for
  replanting. Rhizome → grated → pungent paste (aroma fades within
  roughly 15-20 minutes). Rhizome → dried and ground → powder. Leaves
  and leaf stalks → eaten raw or pickled. Spring flower stalks →
  harvested as a separate seasonal product.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** a distilled essential-oil
  product (none exists in the trade for this crop, unlike horseradish);
  detailed commercial processing beyond grating, drying, and pickling;
  the breeding-focused true-seed propagation route in detail.

No crop-specific religious or ritual production rule was found for
this crop; it appears as a culinary condiment in various Japanese
cultural and culinary contexts, but no source establishes a ritual
production or quality rule comparable to horseradish's documented role
in a Jewish Passover ritual food.

## Open research questions

- Documented genome-size figures conflict considerably between a wild-
  type genome assembly and a specific modern Korean cultivar's genome
  assembly (roughly 1,512 Mb versus roughly 795 Mb) — treated as a
  range in this entry rather than resolved.
- Documented time-to-harvest figures conflict across sources, ranging
  from roughly 14 to 36 months; roughly 18-24 months is taken as
  typical in this entry.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour figures (entirely unsourced
  placeholders, despite the water-grown cultivation method being
  documented as unusually labor- and capital-intensive); yield figures
  (anchored on a single regional growing-guide stand figure, with no
  official agricultural statistic found); raw-rhizome composition
  (based on a rough, user-submitted nutrition-database figure rather
  than a standard food-composition-database entry); soil nutrient-
  uptake figures (estimated); genome-size range (unresolved cultivar
  conflict, noted above); and self-incompatibility and pollination
  details for the minor true-seed route (not established for this
  species specifically, though common in this plant family generally).
- Whether this crop has been tested in any space-agency or controlled-
  environment life-support research was checked and no such trial was
  found; its continuous cool-flowing-water requirement makes it a
  difficult candidate for such research.
- Detailed chill-unit requirements for breaking seed dormancy and
  triggering flowering were not quantified in any source, beyond a
  general statement that "a cold winter" is required.
- Detailed seed-crop figures (seed yield, isolation distance, self-
  incompatibility status, thousand-seed weight) were not researched,
  since commercial production is entirely clonal.

## Estimates and derivations

Calorie and protein content (roughly 1,090 kcal/kg, 48 g protein/kg)
are estimated from a rough, user-submitted nutrition-database entry for
raw rhizome, since no standard food-composition-database entry for raw
rhizome of this specific crop was found; this figure should be treated
as provisional pending a better-sourced composition analysis.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 8,000 kg/ha, good 13,000 kg/ha, exceptional
18,000 kg/ha, poor 4,000 kg/ha) are anchored on a documented regional
growing-guide stand-yield figure of roughly 10,000-15,000 kg/ha, with
the good tier set at its midpoint, the exceptional tier set above it,
and the world-average and poor tiers set below it to reflect stressed
or heat-affected stands, informed by the documented climate-driven
production decline described in Finding 2. No official national or
international yield statistic was found for this crop.

Water-use parameters for the soil-grown cultivation path are estimated
by analogy to watercress, the closest cool, shallow-water leafy
member of the same plant family with documented crop-water-use data;
the water-grown cultivation path does not fit this kind of model at
all, since it is a continuous flow-through system rather than metered
irrigation, and is instead described through site-specific water-
regime notes (temperature and flow-rate requirements) rather than a
seasonal water-use total. A days-without-water tolerance of roughly 1
day is estimated, reflecting that even a brief flow interruption is
documented quickly stressing the plant.

A planting density of roughly 28,000-110,000 plants/ha is derived from
documented spacing figures ranging from a tighter single spacing to a
wider row-and-in-row spacing combination.

Soil nutrient-uptake figures are estimated as generally modest, since
this crop is documented preferring low nutrient-solution strength in
hydroponic trials, suggesting correspondingly modest nutrient uptake
per unit of harvested rhizome.

Ambient (room-temperature, uncooled) shelf life of roughly 7-14 days is
estimated from the documented perishable nature of the fresh rhizome
and its considerably longer shelf life under proper refrigeration.

A light-requirement description of low daily light exposure is
estimated by analogy to other shade-tolerant understory crops,
reflecting this crop's documented requirement for at least 75% shade.
