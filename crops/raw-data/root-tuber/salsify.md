# Salsify (*Tragopogon porrifolius*)

## Naming and scope

*Tragopogon porrifolius* L., daisy family (Asteraceae): purple or
common salsify, also called oyster plant, vegetable oyster, or
goatsbeard. A diploid plant with 12 chromosomes. This entry covers one
species with one main product; white- and purple-flowered cultivars
differ only in root shape and size, so no splitting of this entry was
needed. A different plant entirely — black or Spanish salsify, a
different genus — has its own separate entry in this project and has
largely replaced common salsify as a commercial crop; the common name
"goatsbeard" is also used for two wild relative species not covered
here. This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research
into parsnip, a similarly cool-season biennial taproot crop with
humid cold storage, in-ground overwintering, and short-lived seed; its
inulin content is grounded against this project's prior research into
Jerusalem artichoke.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the first well-sourced yield and inulin figures for this
crop come from a three-year Polish field trial.** A detailed three-
year field trial at a Polish agricultural research site grew a named
cultivar on silty loam soil, documenting a mean total root yield of
23.5 t/ha, ranging 15.4-30.7 t/ha across different treatments, with
17.5 t/ha under a baseline treatment using no preceding cover crop
[5]. Growing a preceding cover crop (vetch, phacelia, or oats sown the
previous summer and plowed into the soil) raised yield to roughly
25-26 t/ha against the 17.5 t/ha baseline — an increase of roughly
45% — with plowing the cover crop in during spring outperforming
plowing it in during autumn [5]. Planting on ridges outperformed flat
planting in total yield, and a follow-up study from the same research
group found ridge planting also gave the largest marketable root
share and longer roots [6]. Composition analysis found the roots held
roughly 15.2% inulin by fresh weight, 22.3% dry matter, and 3.4%
protein — meaning inulin makes up roughly two-thirds of the root's dry
matter, with a documented inulin yield of roughly 3.6 t/ha [5]. This
establishes that this crop, like Jerusalem artichoke, stores its
carbohydrate reserve as inulin rather than starch.

**Finding 2 — this crop stores much like topped carrots, but dry air
quickly ruins it.** A detailed commodity storage manual recommends
storage at near-freezing temperature and 95-98% relative humidity for
three to four months; the crop is described as highly sensitive to
shriveling from moisture loss, requiring perforated film liners in
storage and perforated bags at market, and is not usually precooled
before storage [4]. Its freezing point is documented at roughly
-1.7°C; slight freezing does not injure the root, but roots must not
be handled while still frozen, since severely frozen roots thaw out
flabby, brown-black, and water-soaked when cut [4]. Other sources
document similar storage conditions with a somewhat wider humidity or
duration range [2][3].

**Finding 3 — up to four distinct harvests come from a single
planting, each one ending the usefulness of the one before.** Roots
are typically lifted in late autumn, ideally after a frost, which is
documented to improve their flavor; the long, thin roots are hard to
lift whole, and broken roots discolor and spoil quickly [2]. Roots can
also be left in the ground and dug as needed through winter, though
mice, voles, and in some regions burrowing rodents can consume a
substantial share of unharvested roots over winter [2]. Roots left in
the ground resprout in spring; a recommended home-garden method cuts
back the old leaves and covers the new growth with soil, straw, or an
upturned container, producing pale blanched shoots that are then cut
as they emerge [10]. In the plant's second year, flowering shoots are
eaten much like asparagus, raw or cooked, and the flowers themselves
are used in salads [2]. Each later stage ends the usefulness of the
earlier ones because, once the plant has flowered, the roots become
stringy and inedible, so roots and leaves are best harvested before
the flower stalk forms [2]. The plant's other edible parts include the
young leaves of the first-year rosette, eaten raw or boiled with other
greens, and sprouted seed, eaten in salads and sandwiches [2].

**Finding 4 — seed life is documented as "about one year" in ordinary
practice, but considerably longer under controlled storage.**
Standard growing advice recommends sowing fresh seed each year, since
stored seed is documented remaining viable for only about one year
under typical growing conditions [7]. A seed-science database instead
documents this as an orthodox (storable) seed type with a measured
half-life of roughly 2.6 years in open storage under a temperate
climate, with roughly 13% germination remaining after 15 years in an
open warehouse, and 100% germination maintained after 12 years under
proper genebank refrigerated conditions [7]. In soil, the seed bank is
short-lived: a field study found under 3% of buried seed remaining
viable after one year, since most seed instead germinates on the soil
surface in autumn [7]. Germination itself takes roughly 8-10 days at
a moderate soil temperature [2]. Seed size is documented at roughly
9-14 g per 1,000 seeds across several sources; the seed itself is a
long, beaked achene with a plume adapted for wind dispersal [2][9].

**Finding 5 — the seed crop is biennial, self-compatible, and crosses
with wild relative species.** This crop flowers in its second summer
and is harvested from midsummer to early autumn; breeders actively
select against premature flowering (bolting) [2]. It is documented as
self-compatible and able to self-pollinate, though it is also insect-
pollinated [7]. A documented isolation distance of roughly 150 m
between cultivars is recommended [2]; a genebank collection for the
separately researched black salsify uses a closer isolation distance
with small accession plots. This species hybridizes with two wild
goatsbeard relatives where their ranges overlap; notably, a cross
between this species and one specific wild relative gave rise to an
entirely new allotetraploid (doubled-chromosome) species in western
North America within roughly the last 80 years — a well-documented
case of rapid new-species formation through crop-wild hybridization
[8]. No seed yield per hectare figure was found for this crop in any
source consulted.

**Finding 6 — this is a weak domesticate that escapes cultivation
easily.** This crop's level of domestication is rated as weak or
essentially absent in a scholarly domestication-syndrome assessment
[7]. Historically, it was mentioned in classical Roman writing;
European cultivation began in 16th-century France and Italy, became
popular in 18th-century Britain before declining, and today it is
grown mainly in a handful of European countries [2]. A formal weed-
risk assessment prepared for Hawaiian biosecurity purposes rated this
species a "high risk," documenting that it has naturalized across
North America, Australia, New Zealand, southern Africa, and parts of
South America, and is recognized as an environmental weed of
roadsides, orchards, and vineyards in several regions [7]. Its native
range is southeastern Europe, the Mediterranean, North Africa, and the
Caucasus [2][7]. The species is independently documented as a wild-
harvested food plant in Italy today, and historically, North American
indigenous peoples are documented eating the root and chewing the
milky stems for digestive complaints [7][12]. No toxicity to people or
animals is recorded in any source consulted, and livestock are
documented grazing it without apparent harm [7].

**Finding 7 — growing conditions.** This crop needs deep, loose,
stone-free soil with good water-holding capacity, loosened to roughly
45-60 cm depth; stony or waterlogged ground reduces yield and
complicates harvest, even watering prevents forked roots, and fresh
(unaged) manure is documented as unhelpful [2][3]. A soil pH of 6.0 or
above is recommended, with other sources giving somewhat different
preferred ranges [3][7]. Documented time to maturity ranges from
roughly 90-150 days depending on source, generally understood as a
notably longer season than many other root crops [3][9]. Seeding rates
and row spacing vary somewhat by source and growing system. Plants
tolerate light frost but foliage freezes at a specific low
temperature, while roots themselves overwinter successfully in the
ground and are hardy across a wide range of cold-climate zones — a
difference most likely explained by the foliage being more cold-
sensitive than the insulated root, though this exact explanation is
not directly sourced. In the wild, this species has been observed
tending to prefer partial shade.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial taproot crop, sown
  in spring and typically lifted in late autumn after frost, though
  roots can also be left in the ground and dug through winter
  (Finding 3).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single root harvest per planting, but with up
  to three further harvest stages possible from the same planting
  (overwintered roots, blanched spring shoots, and flowering shoots)
  before the plant's usefulness as a root crop ends (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate, temperate, and Mediterranean zones,
  consistent with its native range in southeastern Europe, the
  Mediterranean, North Africa, and the Caucasus.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (young roots grated raw; leaves, shoots,
  and flowers also eaten raw).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the seed crop is
  insect-visited and self-compatible (Finding 5).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented commercial trial yields span
  roughly 15.4-30.7 t/ha depending on treatment (Finding 1; see also
  Estimates below). The root's carbohydrate reserve is stored mainly
  as inulin rather than starch, at roughly 15% of fresh weight
  (Finding 1).
- **Water use:** Needs even watering throughout summer in deep, loose
  soil; both dry spells and waterlogging cause forked, stunted roots
  (water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded shallowly in rows, with ridge
  planting shown to outperform flat planting (establishment detail
  below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-hour survey exists for this crop;
  labor estimates are carried over by analogy from the related
  parsnip, adjusted upward for harvest since the long, thin root
  breaks easily and broken roots spoil quickly.
- **Soil:** Deep, loose, stone-free loam, silt loam, sandy loam, or
  muck, not stony or waterlogged, at a pH of 6.0 or above (Finding 7).
- **Stand life:** Biennial in natural life cycle, grown as an annual
  for root production, with roots able to overwinter in the ground.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A white rust disease (shared with wild
  relatives and some other Asteraceae crops), several additional
  fungal rust and rot diseases, and rodent damage to in-ground roots
  over winter are documented concerns (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Mostly open-pollinated heirloom cultivars from North
  American and European seed trade, plus the specific Polish research
  cultivar used in the yield trial described in Finding 1 (cultivar
  detail below).
- **Harvest:** Up to four distinct harvest stages from one planting:
  autumn roots, overwintered roots dug as needed, blanched spring
  shoots, and second-year flowering shoots, each stage ending the
  usefulness of the one before it (Finding 3).
- **Storage:** Kept like topped carrots at near-freezing temperature
  and very high humidity, in perforated liners to prevent shriveling,
  for roughly three to four months (Finding 2).

Water detail: no dedicated standard water-use reference-table row
exists for this crop; its water-use parameters are estimated by
analogy to the related parsnip's documented crop-coefficient curve
and rooting depth, since both are similarly deep, stone-free, direct-
seeded cool-season taproots.

Establishment detail: direct-seeded roughly 1.3-2 cm deep, in rows
spaced roughly 45-60 cm apart with seed thinned to a final in-row
spacing of a few centimeters; germination takes 8-10 days at a
moderate soil temperature; sown in spring, or in late summer/early
winter in milder climates; a preceding cover crop plowed into the soil
in spring, and planting on ridges rather than flat ground, both
measurably raise yield.

Cultivar detail: the dominant cultivar in North American trade is a
white-rooted open-pollinated heirloom type dating to 19th-century
seed trade, along with a selection of the same type bred for larger or
better-shaped roots; several European open-pollinated cultivars exist
with limited individually documented differences; and the specific
Polish research cultivar used in the yield trial described in Finding
1 is documented with detailed yield, inulin, dry-matter, and protein
figures.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — this root contains inulin at roughly 15% of
fresh weight, a fructan fiber that can cause dose-dependent digestive
discomfort (flatulence) in some people; deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data (Finding 1).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — sprouted seed of this crop, like sprouted seed
of other crops, carries a general food-safety consideration around
sprout pathogens; deferred until this project has a place for this
kind of health/safety data (Finding 3).

No toxicity to people or livestock is recorded in any source
consulted, and livestock are documented grazing this plant without
apparent harm. A white rust disease (shared with wild relatives and
some other Asteraceae crops), several further fungal rust and rot
diseases, and rodent damage to unharvested roots over winter are
documented pest and disease concerns.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → spring sowing (on ridges, after a plowed-in cover
  crop) → autumn roots → topped and graded → cold storage at near-
  freezing temperature and very high humidity. Alternatively: seed →
  roots left in the ground → dug through winter as needed. Overwintered
  roots → cut back and covered in spring → blanched spring shoots.
  Roots left in place or replanted → second-year flowering shoots
  (eaten) and seed heads → seed. Seed → sprouts. Young first-year
  rosette leaves → salad or boiled greens.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen
  product; commercial inulin extraction, despite a documented inulin
  yield of roughly 3.6 t/ha in the trial referenced in Finding 1; an
  unsourced claim that the root's milky latex can be chewed like gum;
  detailed cover-crop rotation chemistry beyond the yield effect noted
  in Finding 1.

A tradition in Jainism avoids root vegetables generally, consistent
with notes this project has recorded for other root crops.

## Open research questions

- Seed life is documented two very different ways: roughly one year
  under ordinary growing practice, versus a measured 2.6-year half-
  life under controlled open storage conditions — these likely
  reflect different storage conditions rather than a true conflict,
  but are not fully reconciled here.
- Documented seeding rates conflict across sources by nearly a factor
  of two.
- Documented storage duration and humidity figures vary somewhat
  between sources (three to four months at one humidity range versus
  two to four months at a slightly different range).
- Documented foliage frost tolerance (freezing at a specific low
  temperature) sits in some tension with the root's much greater cold
  hardiness and ability to overwinter in the ground; a likely but
  unsourced explanation is that the insulated root is simply far less
  cold-sensitive than exposed foliage.
- Documented maturity timeframes conflict somewhat between seed-trade
  sources and university extension sources.
- No seed-crop yield per hectare was found for this crop in any
  source consulted.
- Weakest areas overall: all labor-hour figures (carried over by
  analogy from parsnip, with no dedicated labor study found for this
  crop); water-use parameters (estimated by analogy, since no
  standard reference-table row exists for this crop); world-average
  yield figure; seed viability by year; and optimal soil pH, all
  estimated or carried by analogy rather than directly sourced.
- How to represent a repeat-cut secondary harvest stage (the blanched
  spring shoots) occurring on an otherwise single-harvest root crop
  remains an open modeling question, since no yield data exists for
  that secondary stage.

## Estimates and derivations

Yield tiers (worldAvg 15,000 kg/ha, good 25,000 kg/ha, exceptional
30,000 kg/ha, poor 8,000 kg/ha) are derived from the Polish field
trial's documented baseline (no-cover-crop) yield, rounded down to
reflect probable lower-input garden-scale growing that likely
dominates production of this minor specialty crop, since no national
production statistics exist for it; the poor tier is estimated at
roughly half the baseline figure, representing stony or waterlogged
growing conditions.

A fresh-to-dry weight ratio of roughly 4.5:1 is derived from the
trial's documented dry-matter percentage.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are derived entirely by analogy to the
related parsnip's documented crop-coefficient values and rooting
depth, since no standard international reference-table row exists for
this crop specifically; this crop's sparser, grass-like foliage may
mean its true mid-season water-use coefficient runs somewhat lower
than parsnip's. A days-without-water tolerance of roughly 8 days is
derived from standard soil-water-holding assumptions combined with
the analogous rooting depth and moisture-depletion allowance.

Seed viability and germination-rate figures are estimated by combining
the documented one-year practical seed-life figure with the
documented 2.6-year open-storage half-life figure, assuming
germination halves roughly every 2.6 years from a representative
fresh-seed germination rate.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium per
tonne of fresh root) are derived from the trial's documented protein
percentage (converted to nitrogen using a standard protein-to-nitrogen
conversion factor) and from documented phosphorus and potassium
content figures per 100 g of root; a cross-check against the trial's
documented yield and applied fertilizer rates shows reasonable
consistency.

Storage life (roughly 90 days cool and dry) is derived from the
overlapping range across several documented storage-duration figures,
set toward the lower end to allow for roots not kept at the very
highest documented humidity.

Labor-hour tiers and a harvest window of roughly 60 days are carried
over by analogy from the related parsnip, with harvest-tier hours
raised somewhat to reflect this crop's long, thin, easily broken root
and the resulting spoilage risk; no dedicated labor study, cost study,
or piece-rate figure was found for this crop specifically.

A seed-crop timeline of roughly 430-500 days from sowing to seed
harvest is derived from a documented spring sowing date, an
overwintering period, and a documented seed-harvest window in the
second year's midsummer to early autumn.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 18 g/m²/day) is derived by
dividing the trial's mean yield by a representative growing-season
length.

Genome size (roughly 3.3 picograms, 1C) is derived from a single
documented flow-cytometry genome-size measurement for one plant
grown in a US research study, divided by its documented ploidy level.

Thousand-seed weight (roughly 9-14 g) is derived from documented seed-
count-per-unit-weight figures from multiple seed-trade sources.

Soil pH optimum (roughly 6.5, with a minimum of 6.0 and maximum of
7.8) is estimated between a documented minimum figure from one
extension source and a documented wider range from general garden-
reference sources.
