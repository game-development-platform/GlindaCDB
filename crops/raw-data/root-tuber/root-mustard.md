# Root Mustard (*Brassica juncea* var./subsp. *napiformis*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-28

listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced

## Naming and scope

*Brassica juncea* var./subsp. *napiformis*, also called tuberous-root
mustard or turnip-root mustard: a large-root, tuberous-root cultivar
group of the same mustard species that also includes a widely grown
leafy-greens type and a separate stem-vegetable type, but a distinct
cultivar group within that species grown specifically for a conical,
turnip-like storage root rather than for leaves or stem. It is
explicitly not the same plant as a well-known Chinese pickled stem
vegetable made from a different cultivar group of the same species —
this non-match is independently confirmed in this entry (Finding 2).
This entry is grounded against a closely related already-researched
*Brassica* root crop this project has also researched, for its
committed field-shape figures, though that comparison crop's own
entry is not reopened here.

Data for this specific cultivar is genuinely thin: it is covered
mainly by seed-catalog and general plant-reference sources rather
than agricultural-extension or national statistical sources, and no
sourced yield, storage-life, or nutrient-uptake figure was found for
this cultivar despite a deliberate second search pass, including
non-English search terms.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the root is the main product, with the young leaves as
a genuine secondary one.** A biennial plant grown as an annual root
crop, this cultivar reaches a full root in roughly 70-90 days from
sowing according to a commercial seed catalog; the mature root is
described as smooth and shiny, "scarcely distinguishable from white
turnips in appearance, texture and flavor," but carrying a mild spicy
mustard flavor [1][2]. A general plant-reference source separately
lists the leaves as edible raw or cooked, with a peppery flavor
ranging from mild to hot; the same seed catalog notes baby greens are
possible in as little as 20 days and a full leaf harvest at 40-50
days — well before the root itself matures [1][2]. This is modeled as
two harvest paths sharing a single planting (a root harvest and a
baby-greens harvest), rather than as a repeat-cut leafy crop, since the
leaf pick is a bonus from thinning rather than a true cut-and-come-
again regime.

**Finding 2 — pickling is the usual preparation, and a well-known
Chinese pickled-stem product is confirmed to be a different crop
entirely.** The general plant reference describes the root as usually
sliced and made into pickles; the seed catalog describes it as
typically pickled or stir-fried [1][2]. No salt ratio or fermentation
time was found for this specific cultivar from any Western-language
source. A well-known Chinese pickled vegetable, made from the swollen
stem of a different cultivar group of this same species — described
in an encyclopedia source as a "knobbly, fist-sized, swollen green
stem" — is confirmed to be a different plant part and cultivar group
entirely, not this root crop [3]. A separate wrinkle: a Chinese food-
science paper on vacuum-impregnation pickling of a root product
referred to by a Chinese common name literally meaning "big-head
vegetable" reports that a 20%-brine, vacuum-impregnated batch
stabilized fastest, with its chemical and sensory properties settling
by about 90 days, and with a specific count of volatile flavor
compounds identified at that point compared with a traditionally
(non-vacuum) pickled comparison sample [4]. This is recorded only as
an unconfirmed analogy, not a sourced fact for this specific cultivar,
because that Chinese common name is itself ambiguous: one dictionary
source gives one sense of the same name as a different named variety
of the same species entirely, distinct from the variety covered in
this entry [5]. The two names may refer to the same plant under
different regional classification systems, or to genuinely different
root-type mustards; this entry does not guess which.

**Finding 3 — seed and pollination.** The general plant-reference
source describes this cultivar as self-fertile and bee-pollinated,
flowering in summer with seed ripening in late summer to early
autumn, at a documented seed count translating to roughly 0.57-0.6 g
per 1,000 seeds — notably smaller than the seed of the leafy-greens
cultivar group of the same species [1]. No isolation distance or
outcrossing percentage was found for this cultivar specifically.

**Finding 4 — cultivation conditions.** The general plant-reference
source, read in full, documents hardiness to a fairly cold zone,
surviving to at least about -4°C; a height of roughly 0.8 m; full sun
or partial shade tolerance (with one of that source's own cited
sub-sources noting the plant "prefers some shade"); adaptability to
light, medium, or heavy soil with a general preference for well-
drained ground (though that same source's own sub-sources
inconsistently also note a preference for heavy soil — an internal
inconsistency in the source itself, not resolved here); a mildly acid
to mildly alkaline pH preference; a rooting depth of roughly 90-120
cm; a preference for moist, moisture-retentive soil; tolerance of high
rainfall; and limited drought resistance [1]. That same source's own
discussion of the plant's geographic origin is internally
contradictory, describing it in one place as a cultivated-garden
origin plant and in another as native to a specific temperate region —
a conflict within that single source that is not resolved here
either. The seed catalog source adds that the plant thrives around
65-70°F, is described as tolerant of both heat and frost (a mild
tension with the plant-reference source's note that the plant
"dislikes very hot weather," not resolved here), prefers a pH of
roughly 6.0-7.5, is spaced about 12 inches apart, is sown about a
quarter-inch deep, germinates in 5-8 days, and benefits from 6-12
hours of sun, with partial shade making plants more tender and less
prone to bolting in warm conditions [2]. The plant-reference source
further notes that spring-sown plants "tend to bolt quickly, though
they can be eaten young," and recommends sowing later in the year
rather than in spring specifically to avoid this, while also advising
against sowing in very hot weather [1].

**Finding 5 — composition, yield, and storage remain a confirmed real
data gap.** No nutrition table, yield figure, or storage-life figure
was found for this specific cultivar in any source consulted,
including a dedicated search pass using non-English search terms. The
closest material found concerned unrelated crops (other turnip-like
roots) or the ambiguous pickling literature described in Finding 2,
which supplies processing chemistry but no composition or field-yield
numbers. This entry borrows a related leafy cultivar group's energy
and protein composition figures only as a weak analogy (root versus
leaf composition genuinely differs), and a related root crop's storage
regime as an analogy based on similar root shape and season; yield is
an outright estimate anchored only on documented plant spacing and a
related root crop's own yield range.

**Finding 6 — secondary uses beyond root and leaf.** The general
plant-reference source, read in full, lists edible flowers and young
flowering stems — described as "sweet and succulent," raw or cooked —
as a further harvestable product beyond root and leaf [1]. The seed
yields an edible, semi-drying oil used as a mustard flavoring and as a
milder "brown mustard" source; whole seed is used in curries and
pickles, often heated in oil to reduce pungency, and sprouted seed is
used in salads. The same source also lists folk-medicinal uses, rated
at a modest confidence level: documented regional folk uses include
seed used for tumors and leaves used in soup for bladder complaints in
one tradition, seed used for abscesses, colds, lumbago, rheumatism,
and stomach disorders in another, root used as a lactation aid in a
third, and an antisyphilitic/emmenagogue use in a fourth, along with a
folk claim that ingestion gives the body a mosquito-repelling odor. A
separate search result surfaced an English common name associated
with this variety that could not be independently confirmed on the
specific database record it pointed to; this is recorded at low
confidence only, as an unreconciled naming oddity. The same plant-
reference source also reports evidence that the plant, decaying in
soil, reduces soil-borne root rot in a following pea crop — a green-
manure effect [1]. None of these secondary uses (flowers, seed oil,
medicinal use, or the green-manure effect) is modeled in this
project's catalogue data; they are recorded here as watch items for
possible future research.

**Finding 7 — a genuinely unresolved taxonomic dispute between two
authoritative plant-reference databases.** One major international
plant-taxonomy database lists this variety's scientific name as a
synonym not of the species covered in this entry, but of an
intergeneric hybrid name entirely, citing a 2003 taxonomic reference
[7]. A separate major national plant-genetic-resources database
instead treats the same name as a genuine subspecies-rank name within
the species covered in this entry, with the variety-rank name used as
a synonym [8]. These two major reference databases thus disagree not
just on taxonomic rank, but on the parent taxon itself. This is not
resolved here and is recorded as an open question.

**Finding 8 — economic context at the species level only.** A general
search result describes a substantial cultivation area and production
volume for this species as a whole in a major East Asian producing
country in a recent year, but that figure covers leaf, oil, stem, and
root types together across many named varieties, and cannot be
attributed to this specific root-type cultivar alone [9]. This is
recorded only as a species-level economic-importance signal, not as a
per-cultivar yield source.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A cool-season biennial plant grown as an
  annual root crop; dislikes very hot weather and is frost-hardy to a
  fairly cold threshold (Finding 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** A single root dig per planting, with an
  optional separate young-leaf harvest from the same planting as a
  secondary product (Finding 1).
- **Climate:** Cool-temperate and temperate zones, consistent with a
  fairly cold hardiness threshold and documented dislike of very hot
  weather.
- **Food status:** Raw-edible (root and leaf).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root or leaf production; the seed
  crop is self-fertile and bee-visited (Finding 3).
- **Yield / composition:** No sourced yield or composition figure
  exists for this cultivar; all figures used are estimates or
  analogies from related root crops (Finding 5; see also Estimates
  below).
- **Water use:** Prefers steady, moisture-retentive conditions and
  tolerates high rainfall, but has limited drought resistance
  (Finding 4).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded in place; no vegetative propagation
  route was found for this cultivar (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures exist for this cultivar;
  all labor estimates are analogies from related root crops.
- **Soil:** Light, medium, or heavy soil tolerated, generally
  preferring well-drained ground, at a mildly acid to mildly alkaline
  pH; rooting depth is notably deep for this crop type, roughly 90-120
  cm (Finding 4).
- **Stand life:** Annual (grown as an annual root crop despite being
  biologically biennial).
- **Disease/pest profile:** No cultivar-specific disorder or pest
  profile was found; general mustard-family pest and disease concerns
  are assumed to apply but were not independently sourced for this
  cultivar.
- **Cultivars:** No distinct named breeding cultivars of this variety
  were found beyond a single commercial seed line sold under its own
  trade name by one seed company, which is a marketing name rather
  than a formally distinct breeding cultivar [2].
- **Harvest:** The root is dug once, typically 70-90 days from
  sowing; the young-leaf pick (when taken) happens earlier, at 20-50
  days (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** No sourced storage-life figure exists for this
  cultivar; storage is modeled conservatively as similar to, but
  somewhat shorter than, a related root crop's cool, humid storage
  regime (Finding 5).

Establishment detail: direct-seeded in place, roughly a quarter-inch
deep, at about 12-inch spacing, germinating in 5-8 days; no vegetative
or transplant-based propagation route was found documented for this
cultivar.

## Hazards

Like other mustard-family crops, this plant contains natural pungent
plant-defense compounds (glucosinolates and their breakdown products)
that are released on cutting or chewing, concentrated especially in
the seed; ordinary cooked or pickled root and leaf at normal food
portions are considered safe, with large raw amounts or dried seed in
animal feed representing a higher-risk form. **TO BE ADDRESSED** —
general mustard-family pungent-irritant and livestock-feed-poisoning
concerns apply to this crop and are deferred until this project has a
place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed → root, grown 70-90 days → pickled or stir-fried.
  Alternatively: seed → thinnings/baby greens, 20-50 days → eaten raw
  or cooked, root crop continues to maturity on the same planting.
  Seed → pressed → semi-drying mustard oil, used as flavoring.
  Whole seed → used directly in curries and pickles, often heated in
  oil to reduce pungency, or sprouted for salads.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** edible flowers and
  flowering stems, folk-medicinal preparations, and the plant's green-
  manure soil effect, all recorded as secondary uses not modeled in
  this project's data; the vacuum-impregnation pickling process
  described in Finding 2, since its identity match to this specific
  cultivar is unconfirmed; a separate well-known Chinese pickled-stem
  vegetable product, confirmed to come from a different cultivar group
  entirely.

No sect-specific religious production or ritual-use rule was found for
this specific cultivar after checking general ethnobotany and
mustard-family ritual-use sources.

## Open research questions

- No sourced yield, storage-life, nutrient-uptake, or nutrition figure
  exists for this cultivar in any source found, in English or any
  other language searched; every such figure in this entry is an
  estimate or analogy from related root crops. A more specialized
  search of non-English agricultural literature could potentially
  close this gap.
- Whether a particular Chinese pickling study's "big-head vegetable"
  subject is actually this cultivar, a different named variety of the
  same species, or something else entirely, is unresolved, since the
  common name involved is itself shown to be ambiguous across more
  than one named variety.
- A rooting depth of roughly 90-120 cm for this cultivar is notably
  deeper than the committed figures for related root crops — an honest
  divergence, not treated as an error.
- Two major plant-taxonomy reference databases disagree sharply on
  this variety's correct taxonomic placement, with one treating it as
  a synonym of an intergeneric hybrid name and the other keeping it
  within the species covered in this entry at subspecies rank; not
  adjudicated here.
- A search result associating an alternate English common name with
  this variety could not be confirmed on the specific database record
  it pointed to, and conflicts with every other source's description
  of this cultivar as a root (not stem) type; flagged as a possible
  database error or cross-contamination with the unrelated stem-
  vegetable cultivar group's naming.
- Whether this cultivar's seed is produced as open-pollinated or
  hybrid stock was not sourced; a low-confidence assumption of open-
  pollinated seed is carried by analogy to the related leafy cultivar
  group.
- Secondary uses (edible flowers and flowering stems, seed oil, folk-
  medicinal uses, and the green-manure soil effect) are recorded but
  not modeled in this project's catalogue data; flagged as candidates
  for a future research pass.

## Estimates and derivations

Energy and protein composition figures (320 kcal/kg, 12 g protein/kg)
are a weak analogy, estimated between two related root crops'
committed composition figures, since no sourced composition figure
exists for this specific cultivar's root.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 15,000 kg/ha, good 25,000 kg/ha, exceptional
40,000 kg/ha, poor 5,000 kg/ha) are an unsourced estimate, anchored on
a related root crop's committed yield range and this cultivar's
documented 12-inch planting spacing, since no yield figure was found
for this cultivar in any source consulted.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve and seasonal water
totals) are an analogy to a related root crop's committed crop-
coefficient shape, set somewhat below that crop's committed seasonal
water totals as a conservative estimate. A days-without-water
tolerance and yield-penalty-per-dry-day figure are likewise estimates.

A seeding rate of roughly 3 kg/ha is derived from the documented
12-inch planting spacing by analogy to a related root crop's committed
seeding-rate figure.

A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio is kept close to related root
crops' committed figures, pending a sourced seed-crop yield figure for
this cultivar specifically; the derivation uses this cultivar's own
documented seed count (translating to roughly 0.57-0.6 g per 1,000
seeds, notably smaller than the leafy cultivar group's seed).

Seed viability and germination-rate figures, labor-hour figures by
operation, and soil nutrient-uptake figures are all unsourced
analogies carried from related root crops, pending cultivar-specific
data.

Storage life (roughly 60 days cool and dry) is an unsourced,
conservative estimate, set below a related root crop's committed
storage life pending real data for this cultivar.
