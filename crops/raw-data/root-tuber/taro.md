# Taro (*Colocasia esculenta*, dasheen and eddoe)

**Researched:** 2026-10-19

## Naming and scope

*Colocasia esculenta* (L.) Schott, family Araceae (a historical
synonym places it under a different species name within the same
genus). Also called kalo, dasheen, eddoe, and in some markets tannier,
malanga, or cocoyam. A separate aroid root crop in the genus
*Xanthosoma* (sometimes also called cocoyam or tannia in trade) is a
distinct sibling entry this project has already researched, not
covered here.

This entry covers one species with two distinct cultivated plant
types, treated as cultivation paths/cultivar groups rather than
separate entries: dasheen (the default type, grown mainly for one
large corm) and eddoe (a cultivar group grown mainly for many smaller
cormels, with its own harvest and storage profile). Flooded (wetland)
and dryland growing are both covered as cultivation paths. Two further
aroid root crops in different genera entirely — a giant swamp taro and
a giant taro — are related but botanically distinct crops this project
has not yet researched as their own entries.

This entry is grounded against this project's own prior research into
cassava (a root crop that likewise needs processing to remove a raw
toxin) and into a closely related but not-yet-researched vegetatively
propagated tropical staple root crop with a chilling-sensitive storage
profile.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — every part of this crop must be cooked before eating.**
Corms, leaf stalks, and leaves all contain microscopic calcium-oxalate
crystal structures; raw tissue is acrid, caused by a compound
associated with these crystals. Cooking removes the irritation.
Boiling also reduces soluble oxalate content by roughly 36% in leaves
and 43% in corms, though it does not remove the insoluble calcium-
oxalate crystals themselves [2][3]. Acridity varies considerably by
type and cultivar: eddoe leaves are documented as more acrid than
dasheen leaves, and one specific Hawaiian cultivar is documented as
notably less acrid than other Hawaiian varieties [2][3].

**Finding 2 — two plant types with different products, growth cycles,
and climate tolerance.** Dasheen is usually diploid, producing one
large market corm (typically 1-3 kg, up to roughly 35 cm) with few
smaller cormels, mealy in texture, maturing in roughly 8-10 months,
with no dormancy in large corms; it grows best on heavy, moisture-
holding soil and is less drought- and cold-tolerant. Eddoe is usually
triploid, producing many large cormels (up to roughly 14 cm) that are
firmer and nuttier in texture, maturing in roughly 5-7 months, with
some dormancy; it grows best on well-drained loams and is considerably
more cold- and drought-tolerant, documented being grown as far north
as Korea and Japan [2]. Eddoe is well suited to storage and survival
in seasonally dry places, while dasheen needs to be kept growing more
or less continuously.

**Finding 3 — both flooded (wetland) and dryland growing are
practiced.** In flooded (wetland) culture, planting goes into roughly
2-5 cm of standing water, kept no deeper than about 5-8 cm; corm
formation starts at roughly 3-5 months in flooded culture, against
roughly 2 months on dryland, and the crop matures in roughly 9-12
months (or 12-15 months in one documented Hawaiian context) [2][5].
Water temperature matters considerably in flooded culture: a specific
fungal pathogen causing corm soft rot is the main lowland disease,
with documented losses of 50-100%, and the main mitigation is cool,
flowing water rather than stagnant, warm water; roots grew best at a
specific moderate water-temperature range and poorly at a
considerably warmer range [12]. Flood depth itself affects yield in
documented but somewhat conflicting ways: one lowland trial found
notably higher yield unflooded than at a 20 cm flood depth, while
another trial found more corm yield under flooding than without it; a
Hawaiian cultivar irrigated at up to 250% of standard reference
evapotranspiration still did not match flooded yields [5]. Dryland
production runs roughly 8-10 months rainfed (up to 12 months in one
Pacific region); a humid upland Indian growing trial found a specific
seasonal water requirement (including effective rainfall) with drip
irrigation at 100% of crop evapotranspiration giving optimal results
[6]. This crop generally wants more than 2,000 mm of rain per year and
a specific moderate temperature range; no standard international crop-
evapotranspiration reference work documents a dedicated row for this
crop [1][2].

**Finding 4 — storage is notably chilling-sensitive.** Best practice
is to room-cool corms to a specific moderate-cool temperature range as
soon as possible after harvest, then store at a somewhat cooler
temperature range and very high humidity for up to roughly 18 weeks.
Chilling injury (pitting and disease) occurs if corms are kept below
that cooler range for a few weeks, and internal browning can start
after roughly 10 days at a colder specific temperature. Once removed
from cold storage, corms should be eaten within about two days. At
ambient temperature, corms can lose up to 20% of their weight within
four weeks under low humidity and warm conditions; one source
documents ambient storage life of up to six weeks (often only two),
while dasheen specifically is documented keeping up to six months at a
specific cool storage temperature [2]. Cut corms are cured at a
specific warmer temperature range to heal wounds before storage [3].
This crop has very low ethylene production and no known ethylene
response [3]. Several specific storage-temperature figures conflict
across sources and are left unresolved in this entry (see Open
research questions).

**Finding 5 — planting material comes directly off the harvested
crop.** The apical few centimeters of the corm together with a length
of leaf stalk (a planting piece commonly called a huli in Hawaiian
practice) is cut at harvest and replanted; smaller cormels, side
suckers, and corm pieces also serve as planting material [2]. Handling
temperature affects later growth: storing planting material at a
specific moderate-cool temperature and high humidity improves later
growth, while storing it at a colder specific temperature delays
growth by roughly 40-60 days. A hot-water treatment at a specific
temperature for a specific duration clears root-knot nematodes from
planting material; tissue culture is used in at least one documented
region to produce virus-free planting stock [2]. There is effectively
no true-seed crop: most clones rarely flower and many never do, though
a specific plant hormone treatment can induce flowering for breeding
purposes, and hand pollination is documented being used in at least
one African breeding program [2].

**Finding 6 — a specific plant disease wiped out a national crop
within about a year.** A fungal leaf-blight pathogen reached one
Pacific island territory in 1993 and caused losses approaching 100%,
with all locally grown cultivars found susceptible. Documented
regional production fell from roughly 357,000 kg per year to under
5,000 kg by the end of 1994. Fungicide treatment failed to control the
disease; a recurrent-selection breeding program began in 1996 using
cultivars sourced from several other Pacific and Southeast Asian
regions, and had identified roughly 30 resistant clones by 1998-1999
[9]. Other documented diseases and pests include a viral complex
involving two bacilliform viruses together, a generally mild mosaic
virus, a beetle pest absent from Africa, leafhoppers, and thrips [2].

**Finding 7 — moderately salt-tolerant, but tolerance varies
considerably by cultivar.** In one Pacific island trial, this crop
maintained good yields under irrigation at salinity levels of roughly
3-7 dS/m. In potted trials, growth fell significantly at a specific
higher salinity threshold and above; this crop may partly exclude
sodium from its leaves, a possible tolerance mechanism. One specific
cultivar from a different Pacific island was documented suffering
damage at a considerably lower salinity threshold. No standard
international salinity-threshold reference table entry was found for
this crop [11].

**Finding 8 — products, history, and culture.** Global production
figures (combining this crop with a related aroid root crop) are
documented at roughly 9.4 million tonnes from roughly 1.6 million
hectares in a past reporting year, with this crop alone accounting for
roughly 5.6 million tonnes and several African and Asian countries
documented as leading producers [2]. This crop was domesticated very
early, possibly in northern India, and had spread to the Pacific,
Egypt, and East Africa more than 2,000 years ago [2]. A traditional
Hawaiian food made by cooking the corm and pounding it with water is
documented tasting sweet when fresh and turning pleasantly sour after
about three days through natural fermentation by lactic-acid bacteria,
yeasts, and a specific mold genus [8]. Other documented uses include a
West African fermented starch dish, chips, flour, and leaves and leaf
stalks eaten as a cooked green vegetable [2]. Peelings, leaves, and
cull corms are documented being fed to pigs, with ensiling or cooking
roughly halving foliage oxalate content [10].

A Hawaiian traditional-religion account holds that this crop is the
embodiment of a stillborn first child of two deities, from whose grave
the first corm grew; that child's younger sibling became the ancestor
of the Hawaiian people, making this crop and people siblings in this
tradition, with an associated duty of care extending to the flooded
taro field [13]. In Japan, a specific night of the traditional lunar
calendar (the 15th night of the 8th lunar month) is marked as a "taro
harvest moon" observance; in one Japanese region, the first corm of
this crop's harvest is traditionally offered to the moon [14].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season vegetatively propagated
  perennial grown as an annual-cycle clone; wants a specific moderate-
  warm temperature range and over 2,000 mm of annual rainfall.
- **Harvest pattern:** A single harvest of the corm per planting
  cycle; leaves can be picked from roughly 2-3 months onward, though
  repeat leaf-picking yield was not researched in detail.
- **Climate:** Tropical and subtropical zones broadly; the eddoe
  cultivar group specifically is documented grown considerably farther
  north, including into temperate East Asia.
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible — every part must be cooked (Finding
  1).
- **Pollination:** Not required for corm production, since this is a
  vegetatively propagated crop that rarely flowers.
- **Yield / composition:** World average yield is documented at
  roughly 5,000-6,000 kg/ha; a good fertile crop can reach at least
  12,000 kg/ha, and exceptional documented yields (one specific
  Pacific-region context) exceed 40,000 kg/ha (Finding 2; see also
  Estimates below).
- **Water use:** No standard international crop-water-use reference
  work documents this crop directly; a specific humid-region upland
  trial documents a seasonal water requirement in the range of roughly
  610-628 mm including effective rainfall (water detail below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from planting pieces cut
  from the harvested crop, planted at a documented in-row and between-
  row spacing, or into shallow standing water for flooded culture
  (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures were found for this crop;
  labor figures carried in internal planning material are unsourced
  estimates only.
- **Soil:** Dasheen favors heavy, moisture-holding soil; eddoe favors
  well-drained loams; a documented moderately acidic pH optimum applies
  to both (soil detail below).
- **Stand life:** An annual-cycle vegetatively propagated clone; not
  a perennial stand in the conventional sense.
- **Disease/pest profile:** A specific fungal leaf-blight pathogen
  capable of destroying an entire regional crop, a serious lowland
  corm-rot pathogen favored by stagnant warm water, a viral disease
  complex, a generally mild mosaic virus, root-knot nematodes, a
  beetle pest, leafhoppers, thrips, and hawk-moth larvae are all
  documented (hazard detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Up to roughly 600 named varieties exist across flesh
  colors from white through yellow, orange, red, and purple, including
  specific named Chinese, Hawaiian, Vietnamese, and Japanese cultivars
  and a breeding program's disease-resistant lines (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** Leaves, immature corms, and mature corms are all
  harvestable stages, with mature-corm harvest as the primary single
  harvest of the crop cycle.
- **Storage:** A notably chilling-sensitive cold-storage regime (up to
  roughly 18 weeks at a specific moderate-cool temperature and very
  high humidity), with a short two-day eating window once removed from
  cold storage (Finding 4).

Water detail: this crop is documented wanting water throughout the
growing season; in flooded fields, water should be kept shallow, cool,
and moving, since warm, still water promotes serious corm rot
(Finding 3).

Establishment detail: planting pieces or cormels are set at a
documented in-row spacing of roughly 50-80 cm and between-row spacing
of roughly 70-120 cm; one documented Southeast Asian upland practice
uses furrows roughly 30 cm deep and 80 cm apart; flooded fields are
planted directly into roughly 2-5 cm of standing water. Roots and
leaves emerge within about two weeks of planting; side suckers appear
at roughly two months; corm growth begins from roughly two months
onward (or 3-5 months in flooded culture); leaf area peaks at roughly
4-5 months after planting.

Soil detail: documented general fertilizer guidance recommends a
specific nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium range per hectare; a
documented optimal pH range of roughly 5.5-6.5 applies, with tolerance
beyond that range not researched in detail.

Cultivar detail: documented cultivars include a Chinese dasheen type
with white flesh and purple fibers, lower acridity, and documented
status as the most common retail taro in one Pacific US state; a
Hawaiian wetland dasheen type with purplish flesh and dark purple
fibers, the usual type used for the traditional pounded-corm dish
described in Finding 8; a group of disease-resistant bred dasheen
clones developed through the recurrent-selection breeding program
described in Finding 6; a Japanese eddoe type grown for its cormels,
maturing in roughly 5-7 months, and traditionally offered at the
Japanese moon-viewing observance described in Finding 8; and a
Vietnamese cultivar studied specifically for its oxalate content in
both food and feed contexts.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — raw corms, leaves, and leaf stalks contain
microscopic calcium-oxalate crystal structures that cause acrid
irritation if eaten uncooked; cooking removes the irritation, though
boiling only partially reduces the insoluble crystal content itself.
Deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data (Finding 1).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — foliage and processing waste fed to livestock
(typically pigs) after cooking or ensiling, which roughly halves
foliage oxalate content; deferred until this project has a place for
this kind of health/safety data.

A specific fungal leaf-blight pathogen is documented capable of
destroying an entire regional crop (Finding 6); a serious lowland corm-
rot pathogen favored by stagnant warm water is documented causing
losses of 50-100% in flooded culture (Finding 3); a viral disease
complex, a generally mild mosaic virus, root-knot nematodes, a rot
disease affecting stored and growing corms, leaf spots, a beetle pest,
leafhoppers, thrips, and hawk-moth larvae are all documented pests and
diseases. Skin irritation from raw sap is noted in older internal
planning material but was not independently resourced in this
research pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** planting piece (cut from the harvested crop) → wetland
  (flooded) or dryland cultivation → corms; planting piece cut off at
  harvest and replanted for the next cycle. Eddoe cultivar group:
  cormels planted → eddoes harvested at roughly 5-7 months. Corms →
  washed, trimmed, and cured → cool storage. Corms → steamed or
  boiled → pounded with water → a traditional pounded-corm dish,
  soured over two to four days through natural fermentation. Corms →
  sliced, dried, and milled → flour. Young leaves and leaf stalks →
  cooked as a green vegetable, or ensiled for livestock feed. Peelings
  and culls → livestock feed.
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** canned and frozen
  product (no sourced data found); specific regional cooked dishes
  beyond the pounded-corm dish already described; breeding-seed
  production in detail; the two related but botanically distinct giant
  aroid root crops mentioned in Naming and scope.

## Open research questions

- Documented chilling-storage temperature figures conflict
  significantly across sources: one detailed postharvest guideline
  documents both a specific moderate-cool storage range and, within
  the same source, an alternate figure attaching the same multi-week
  storage duration to a considerably colder temperature range; a
  separate regional trial found a specific cold temperature caused no
  chilling damage at all; and a Japanese-type trial found good storage
  results across two different moderate-cool temperatures. These
  conflicts are left unresolved in this entry.
- Documented flood-depth-versus-yield findings conflict between two
  trials: one found notably higher yield without flooding, while
  another found flooding itself raised corm yield.
- Documented maturity timelines conflict moderately between a general
  tropical-crop reference (roughly 9-12 months for flooded culture) and
  a specific Hawaiian documented range (12-15 months).
- Ploidy assignments for the two cultivar groups come from a single
  primary source and a secondary internal scoping note, and were not
  independently cross-verified against a third source.
- Weakest areas overall: water-use crop-coefficient parameters and
  effective rooting depth (no standard reference-table row exists for
  this crop, so these are carried over from older, unsourced internal
  estimates); labor-hour figures and soil nutrient-uptake figures
  (both unsourced, carried over from older internal planning material);
  and the lower yield tier (unsourced).
- Frost-tolerance thresholds were not researched.
- Repeat leaf-picking yield and flour-yield figures were not
  researched.
- Detailed Hawaiian flooded-taro-field ritual practice beyond the
  general cultural account in Finding 8 was not researched.
- This crop's history beyond a brief general-reference summary was not
  researched in further depth.
- Whether this crop has been tested in any space-agency or controlled-
  environment life-support research was checked and no such trial was
  found.

## Estimates and derivations

World-average yield (roughly 5,500 kg/ha) is derived as the midpoint of
a documented world range of roughly 5,000-6,000 kg/ha from a tropical-
crop reference source; a documented statewide Hawaiian yield figure
from a specific recent year converts to roughly 10,800 kg/ha, used here
only as a cross-check rather than as a tier value.

Water-use parameters (seasonal water total and days-of-supply slots)
are derived directly from the documented humid-region upland seasonal
water-requirement figure, since no standard crop-coefficient reference-
table row exists for this crop; this derivation reflects upland,
rainfall-dependent taro specifically, and does not represent flooded
culture, which is not limited by irrigation supply in the same way. A
soil-moisture-depletion allowance and a short days-without-water
tolerance of roughly 3 days are estimated by analogy to other shallow-
rooted, water-sensitive vegetable crops, reflecting this crop's
documented low drought tolerance (for the dasheen type specifically).

Planting density (roughly 10,400-28,600 plants/ha) is derived from
documented in-row and between-row spacing ranges.

Storage life (roughly 90 days cool and humid, used as a conservative
working figure) is derived as a cautious value within the documented
up-to-18-week maximum at the cooler documented storage-temperature
range, since ambient storage life is considerably shorter and some
storage trials found rot rates rising between four and eight weeks.

Edible yield per unit area per day (roughly 4-10 g/m²/day) is derived
by dividing documented yield figures by typical and extended growing-
cycle lengths for a good crop and for an extended Hawaiian cycle,
respectively.

Genome size (roughly 2.46 pg, 1C) is derived by converting a documented
genome-assembly size in megabases to picograms using a standard
conversion factor, cross-checked against a second independent genome-
assembly estimate.

Water-use crop-coefficient curve, effective rooting depth, labor-hour
figures by tier, and soil nutrient-uptake figures are all carried over
unchanged from older, unsourced internal planning estimates, since no
dedicated sourced study was found for any of these parameters in this
research pass.
