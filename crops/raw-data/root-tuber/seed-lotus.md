# Seed Lotus (*Nelumbo nucifera*, seed cultivar group)

**Researched:** 2026-10-26

## Naming and scope

The seed-lotus cultivar group of sacred lotus, *Nelumbo nucifera*
Gaertn., grown specifically for its edible seeds rather than its
rhizome. This is the same species as lotus root, which this project
has already researched as its own separate entry covering the
rhizome-producing cultivar group; this entry gives only the
seed-focused deltas, while family-level traits shared across the
species (photoperiod response of the rhizome, genome size, a parasite
hazard, heavy-metal uptake, thermogenic flowers, and seed longevity)
are documented in that companion entry rather than repeated here. A
third cultivar group, grown purely as an ornamental flower, rarely
sets seed and has a small, inedible rhizome, and is not covered by
either entry. This crop should also not be confused with a different
aquatic plant grown for a similarly processed popped seed, sometimes
imitated by lotus-seed snack products.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a single planting is cropped for three years, picked
every few days across a four-month harvest window.** One planting
(a "stand") is harvested for three growing seasons before being dug
out and replanted; documented dried-seed yield per unit area rises
from a first-year figure, peaks in the second year, and falls off
somewhat in the third year before replanting [12]. Flowering begins
from roughly the second or third fully developed standing leaf on each
plant, with one flower produced per leaf thereafter, from early summer
through late summer or early autumn [8][12]. Seed pods are picked
every two to five days from midsummer through autumn, along fixed
paths cut through the planting, by hand or with a hooked bamboo pole;
after each pod is picked, the old leaf beside it is removed to help
keep the plant canopy open [12]. Pods are classified by the season in
which they ripen (early-summer, midsummer, and autumn classes), with
earlier-season pods ripening faster (roughly 30-40 days after
flowering) and the latest-season pods taking somewhat longer (over
40-45 days) [12]. Signs of maturity include a pod color shift from
green to greyish-brown, blackening at the edges of the internal seed
chambers, and seeds that rattle inside a hardened shell; picking too
early gives soft, underdeveloped seeds, while picking too late lets
seeds fall loose and the shell harden to the point of being difficult
to peel [12].

**Finding 2 — the same flower can produce two very different harvest
products: fresh green seeds for direct eating, or mature seeds for
processing.** Picked young (roughly two weeks after pollination), the
green seeds are eaten raw, sweet and crisp; documented fresh-eating
cultivars yield several tonnes per hectare of fresh pods or fresh
shelled seed [8][9]. Left to mature, pods instead yield seeds destined
either for a cored, white processed product or for whole seeds dried
in the shell [7][13]. A detailed regional product standard for fresh
lotus seed documents specific minimum energy, protein, starch, and
moisture content requirements, along with a strict cold-chain
requirement: fresh shelled seed keeps no more than five days at a
specific refrigerated temperature and humidity, and must be
transported even colder [14].

**Finding 3 — documented yields vary enormously by product form,
cultivar, and region.** Cored white processed seed yields are
documented in the range of roughly 900-1,350 kg/ha depending on
cultivar [7][21]. Whole dried seed (seed dried in the shell) is
documented at a notably higher figure of roughly 2,850 kg/ha for one
specific bred cultivar, with national figures for dried seed in China
spanning roughly 1,050-1,900 kg/ha, and one specific well-known
production region documented achieving over 2,000 kg/ha using a
particular widely grown cultivar [1][6][7]. Documented pod counts run
tens of thousands of effective pods per hectare, with each pod
containing on the order of 25-35 potential seed chambers and a
documented seed-set rate of roughly 80-85% [7][8][9]. Vietnamese
production data document somewhat lower regional yields, generally in
the range of roughly 1.8-3 t/ha of seed, with one provincial-level
figure considerably lower than district-level or trial figures — an
unresolved conflict likely reflecting different product bases (fresh
versus dried, or whole-province averages versus best-practice
district figures) [10][11].

**Finding 4 — pollination is a genuine yield input, not incidental.**
Lotus flowers mature their female parts before their male parts
(protogynous), are bowl-shaped, and produce no nectar, with each
flower containing 20-35 potential seed chambers [16]. In wild
populations, bees and flies are documented as the most frequent flower
visitors and together account for roughly half to nearly 90% of
successful seed set, with beetles also contributing; a detailed
photographic study found that bee visits occurring in a specific early
-morning window on the flower's second day of opening, under calm, dry
conditions, are what principally drive seed set, and a separate
Chinese agricultural study concluded that high seed yield specifically
depends on adequate bee pollination [16][17][18]. By contrast, hand
pollination used in breeding programs gives markedly lower and more
variable seed set than natural insect pollination [16].

**Finding 5 — detailed field practice covers site selection, water
depth, and a precise fertilizer schedule.** Sites are chosen to be
sheltered, sunny, and easy to flood and drain, with deep, loose,
fertile soil prepared by plowing and sun-exposure over winter and
puddling in spring [12]. Planting happens once soil temperature at a
shallow depth exceeds roughly 10°C and air temperature exceeds
roughly 12°C, with rhizome pieces optionally pre-sprouted indoors
beforehand; planting density is documented at a specific close spacing
intended to bring flowering forward [12]. Water depth is carefully
managed through the season, starting shallow at planting and rising
through a mid-season peak before tapering slightly late in the season,
with the field never allowed to dry out but also never flooded over
the tops of the standing leaves [12]. Fertilizer practice follows an
"early, often, steady" principle: an initial dose roughly a month
after planting, followed by regular smaller top-dressings, with the
single largest application timed to coincide with the start of
flowering, and nitrogen application halted if leaf growth becomes
lush with comparatively few flower buds [12]. Canopy care includes
pulling out the original spent seed rhizome once the plant reaches a
specific leaf-count stage, pressing yellowed or overcrowded leaves
into the mud, and removing old leaves after each pod pick — except
once autumn air temperature falls to roughly 25°C, at which point leaf
removal stops so the plant can begin forming next year's overwintering
rhizomes [12]. Breeding selection favors plants that flower early,
have short stem segments between leaves, and set at least 18-20 seeds
in their very first pod [12].

**Finding 6 — processing mature seed into white lotus seed follows a
documented, standardized procedure.** One detailed regional processing
standard documents a specific flow: pods are picked in the morning or
evening and kept pod-face-up in shade before processing; seeds are
removed from the pod by hand or machine, shelled, hand-peeled from the
tip to remove the seed skin, washed, and have their bitter green
embryo pushed out, before being oven-dried at roughly 70°C down to no
more than 11% moisture and then cooled; the standard specifically
prohibits lead or aluminum processing tools, recommending stainless
steel or bamboo instead [13]. A separate standard for fresh (non-
dried) seed describes hand or machine shelling (machine settings tuned
to the specific cultivar), coring with a bamboo pin or automatic
corer, and vacuum or nitrogen packaging to extend shelf life [14]. A
dedicated fresh-seed husking-and-peeling machine is documented
achieving over 90% good-seed output with low damage at a throughput
of roughly one seed per second, though its settings must be adjusted
by growing region, cultivar, and maturity stage [15]. A separate study
found vacuum pre-cooling with added water reduces weight loss and
browning in stored fresh seed [22]. The bitter green embryo removed
during processing is itself used to make a bitter herbal tea as a
byproduct [1]. Composition figures document dried seed as notably
calorie- and protein-dense, with one specific cultivar's seed protein
content documented rising substantially from an immature to a mature
stage [7][19][24].

**Finding 7 — a range of named regional and modern bred cultivars.**
Several geographically branded, protected regional seed-lotus types
are documented, each tied to a specific production region with
official geographic-indication status or standards, alongside several
older named regional landrace types [3][4][5][12]. One notable modern
cultivar originated from seed flown aboard a spacecraft in 1994 as
part of a space-mutation breeding program, and is documented as
having unusually many flowers and pods, making it one of the most
widely grown processing cultivars in its home region [6][14]. Several
further modern bred cultivars from agricultural research institutions
are documented with detailed parentage and performance figures,
including both fresh-eating and processing-oriented types, one of
which has leaves documented as notably rich in alkaloids and
flavonoids [7][8][9]. In Vietnam, a high-yielding regional lotus type
is documented replacing older, degenerated local types in at least one
growing region [11].

**Finding 8 — climate requirements.** This crop needs a growing season
of more than 180 days from planting to the end of harvest and is best
suited to regions with long, warm summers; bred cultivars are
specifically recommended for one major river-valley growing region
[2]. One well-documented production region reports a specific mean
annual temperature, substantial annual rainfall, a long frost-free
period, and considerable annual sunshine hours, growing on paddy and
red soils [14]. Planting itself requires soil temperature above a
specific threshold at a shallow depth [12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season: A warm-season aquatic perennial grown in
  flooded fields, planted in spring, flowering from early to late
  summer, with pods picked from midsummer through autumn (Finding 1,
  5, 8).
- **Harvest pattern:** A continuously picked crop (pods harvested
  every 2-5 days across the harvest window) rather than a single
  harvest event, with two possible harvest stages per pod — a fresh
  stage (roughly 15-20 days after pollination) and a mature stage
  (roughly 30-45 days after pollination) — selected per pod depending
  on intended market (Finding 1, 2).
- **Climate:** Subtropical, tropical, and warm-temperate zones with a
  long, frost-free growing season exceeding 180 days (Finding 8).
- **Food status:** Raw-edible at the fresh stage; dried seed is
  cooked.
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated, mainly by bees, with
  flies and beetles also contributing (Finding 4).
- **Yield / composition:** Documented yields vary enormously by
  product form, ranging roughly 450-2,850 kg/ha depending on whether
  measured as cored white seed, whole dried seed, or by specific stand
  year (Finding 1, 3; see also Estimates below).
- **Water use:** Continuous shallow flooding at a water depth that
  rises through the season and never allows the field to dry out, but
  also never submerges the standing leaves (Finding 5; water detail
  below).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from whole seed rhizomes
  dug from an old stand just before replanting, optionally pre-
  sprouted indoors (establishment detail below).
- **Labor:** No dedicated labor-time study was found; labor estimates
  are built up from documented pod counts, processing throughput
  rates, and machine-processing speeds, with hand-processing (shelling,
  peeling, and coring individual seeds) dominating total labor time
  (labor detail below).
- **Soil:** Deep, loose, fertile, puddled soil, easy to flood and
  drain; documented production on paddy and red soils in one major
  growing region (Finding 5, 8).
- **Stand life:** A single planting is cropped for three growing
  seasons, peaking in the second year, before being dug out and
  replanted (Finding 1).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Not separately researched for this
  cultivar group beyond the family-level pest and disease concerns
  documented for lotus root; a specific disorder of over-vigorous leaf
  growth with few flower buds, driven by excess nitrogen in late
  spring, and a toxic rot of the spent seed rhizome that can harm new
  growth if not removed, are both documented (disorder detail below).
- **Cultivars:** Several geographically branded regional landraces, a
  notable space-mutation-bred cultivar, and several further modern
  bred cultivars spanning fresh-eating and processing use (Finding 7).
- **Harvest:** Continuous picking every 2-5 days across a roughly
  four-month window, by hand or with a hooked pole, along fixed paths
  cut through the planting (Finding 1).
- **Storage:** Fresh seed keeps only a few days even refrigerated;
  dried seed, properly dried to a specific low moisture content, keeps
  roughly a year in cool, dry, dark storage (storage detail below).

Water detail: water-use parameters are carried over by analogy from
this project's lotus-root entry, since both are the same species grown
in the same flooded system; this crop runs somewhat later into autumn
and is kept at a shallower water depth than the rhizome-focused
cultivar group.

Establishment detail: whole rhizome pieces with a terminal bud and one
or two side rhizomes are planted at a specific close spacing, sourced
by digging up an old third-year stand immediately before replanting,
with optional indoor pre-sprouting under straw until the shoot reaches
roughly 10 cm.

Labor detail: picking alone is estimated to take several hundred
hours per hectare given documented pod counts and a reasonable picking
rate; hand-processing (shelling, peeling, and coring) each individual
seed is estimated to dominate total labor at well over a thousand
hours per hectare, though machine processing at a documented
throughput rate substantially reduces this; no dedicated cost-of-
production labor survey was found to verify these estimates directly.

Disorder detail: over-vigorous leaf growth with comparatively few
flower buds is driven by excessive nitrogen fertilizer applied too
late in spring; a toxic rot of the spent seed rhizome, if left in
place rather than removed, can harm the new season's growth.

Storage detail: fresh shelled seed is documented keeping no more than
five days at a specific refrigerated temperature and humidity, shipped
even colder; dried seed, brought down to roughly 11% moisture, is
documented keeping in cool, dry, dark storage, though the source
standard sets a moisture limit without specifying an exact maximum
storage duration.

## Hazards

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — fresh seeds eaten raw from pods grown in open
water carry a documented parasite risk associated with aquatic plants
generally; deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data.

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — like other aquatic vegetables, this crop is
documented taking up heavy metals from its growing environment, a
family-level trait also noted in this project's lotus-root entry;
deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data. A detailed regional processing standard separately prohibits
lead or aluminum tools specifically to avoid metal contamination
during processing.

The seed's bitter green embryo, and the leaves of at least one bred
cultivar documented as notably alkaloid-rich, contain natural
bioactive compounds; no formal hazard note beyond this observation was
found. General pest and disease concerns are assumed consistent with
the family-level profile documented for lotus root, and were not
separately researched for this cultivar group specifically.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed rhizomes → three-year stand → pods picked every 2-5
  days. Green pods → fresh seeds eaten raw, or shelled and cored as a
  refrigerated fresh product (keeping up to five days). Mature pods →
  threshed → seeds in the shell → shelled, peeled, and cored (removing
  the bitter embryo) → oven-dried white lotus seed. Alternatively:
  mature pods → sun-dried whole as a traditional whole-dried-seed
  product. Cored seed → lotus seed paste (a family-level process
  shared with lotus root). Leaves → wrappers and tea; flowers →
  ceremonial offerings (both family-level uses shared with lotus
  root).
- **Not covered by this research in detail:** vacuum- or nitrogen-
  packed fresh-seed retail packaging; popped seed products and lotus-
  seed tea and drink products; managed beehive placement specifically
  for pollination; agricultural tourism tied to lotus flowering.

A tradition in both Buddhism and Hinduism uses lotus flowers broadly as
ceremonial offerings, a family-level religious use this project has
also recorded for lotus root. An unverified note suggests lotus seeds
feature in Chinese Buddhist vegetarian cooking, but this was not
independently confirmed in this research pass.

## Open research questions

- A major cultivar's claimed cultivation area (cited as covering over
  80% of a specific regional product's growing area in one source)
  conflicts with a separate source's much smaller total area figure
  for seed lotus as a whole in China in the mid-2000s; likely reflects
  either a cumulative-area claim or a misstated figure, not resolved
  here.
- One regional yield figure, expressed in a traditional local unit,
  conflicts with dried-seed yield figures from elsewhere by roughly a
  factor of three to four — most likely because the local figure
  describes fresh seed or whole pods rather than dried seed, not
  confirmed.
- A specific Vietnamese province's total documented output implies a
  notably lower per-hectare yield than separately documented district-
  level or trial-level target figures for the same country — not
  reconciled here.
- One specific high-yield trial figure from Vietnam is reported
  without a clearly stated product basis (fresh versus dried, whole
  pod versus seed).
- Documented seeding/planting-stock rates conflict depending on
  whether measured by piece count or by total weight per unit area.
- Weakest areas overall: labor-hour estimates (built up from indirect
  figures rather than a direct time-and-motion study); world-average
  yield (estimated between a stand-averaged historical figure and
  modern regional averages); phosphorus and potassium nutrient-
  removal figures (estimated from typical seed mineral content rather
  than directly sourced for this species); and dried-seed shelf life
  (no explicit maximum storage duration was found in the source
  processing standard).
- Pests and diseases specific to this cultivar group (as opposed to
  the lotus family generally) were not separately researched in this
  pass.
- Flowering photoperiod response (as distinct from the rhizome's
  already-documented photoperiod response) was not researched.

## Estimates and derivations

A stand-year yield profile (year 1 at roughly 0.4 of peak, year 2 at
peak, year 3 at roughly 0.55 of peak) is derived from documented
midpoint yield figures for each of the three stand years, with the
first-year figure rounded down somewhat to reflect that a newly
established stand's low end is a better representative figure than a
simple midpoint.

Yield tiers (worldAvg 1,000 kg/ha, good 1,350 kg/ha, exceptional 2,250
kg/ha, poor 450 kg/ha, all as cored white seed) are derived by setting
the world-average figure between a stand-averaged historical yield
figure and more modern regional average figures, since available
sources span a wide range of production methods and intensities; the
poor tier is set at the documented first-year-stand yield figure.

A fresh-to-dried weight ratio of roughly 2.6:1 is derived from one
cultivar's documented dry-matter percentage at a specific maturity
stage, adjusted for the fact that the dried product standard requires
a notably lower final moisture content than that raw dry-matter figure
alone would suggest, and cross-checked against documented fresh-seed
water-content figures.

Water-use parameters (a crop-coefficient curve, stage lengths, and
total seasonal water slots) are carried over by analogy from this
project's lotus-root entry, treating this as the same species in the
same flooded production system, with an estimated stage-length
breakdown reflecting this crop's documented planting, flowering, and
picking timeline running later into autumn.

A seed-rhizome planting-stock rate of roughly 1,500 kg/ha per year is
derived by dividing a documented per-planting rhizome weight
requirement by the three-year stand life, since the same planting
stock is reused across the whole stand rather than applied annually.

Labor-hour estimates are built up from documented pod-count figures
combined with an assumed picking rate, and from documented seed-per-
pod and hand-processing-rate figures for shelling, peeling, and coring,
cross-checked against a documented machine-processing throughput
figure; no direct time-and-motion labor study was found for this crop.

Nutrient-removal figures (nitrogen per tonne of dried seed) are
derived from documented seed protein content using a standard protein-
to-nitrogen conversion factor; phosphorus and potassium figures are
estimated from typical seed mineral-content assumptions, since no
sourced figures specific to this species were found. Rooting depth is
estimated from a qualitative description of this crop's weak, shallow
rhizome system.

Shelf-life figures for mature pods and in-shell seed (roughly 1-2
days) are estimated on the basis that both are typically processed
fresh on the day of picking, per the documented processing standards.

Edible yield per unit area per day is derived separately for cored
dried seed and for fresh seed, by dividing representative yield
figures for each product by their respective growing/picking season
lengths.
