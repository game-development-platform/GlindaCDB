# Amazonian Yam Bean, Ashipa type (*Pachyrhizus tuberosus*)

**Category:** Root/Tuber · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/root-tuber/amazonian-yam-bean.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-26

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

**Data quality note:** this entry is thin to moderate. Agronomy comes
from Brazilian trials at Manaus (mostly breeding progenies), a Benin
accession trial, a Brazilian extension note, and a PhD thesis that
reviews the Ecuadorian and Peruvian literature. There are no national
statistics, and the main reference monographs on the species were not
opened directly.

## Naming and scope

*Pachyrhizus tuberosus*, family Fabaceae, tribe Phaseoleae, 2n = 22
[1][13]. The species is a complex of four cultivar groups [1][3][12]:

- **Ashipa** — the widespread western-Amazon type, grown in slash-and-
  burn fields on terra firme (never-flooded land). This document covers
  the Ashipa type.
- **Chuin** — Peruvian river floodplains, a high-dry-matter root cooked
  like cassava [1][8]. This is a distinct crop with its own entry in
  this project's research.
- **Yushpe** — very local in Peru, on the várzea (floodplain) [1][12].
  Covered here only as a cultivar-group note, not a separate entry.
- **Jíquima** — the dry forest of coastal Ecuador, a determinate bush
  like ahipa [1][3]. Covered here only as a cultivar-group note.

Common names: ashipa, jíquima (Peru, Ecuador); several indigenous
Ecuadorian names varying by region and language group; *jacatupé*,
*feijão-macuco* (Brazil) [1][3][7]. Note: "jíquima" (this species'
coastal Ecuadorian group) is easily confused with "jícama" (a different
species this project has also researched, and in the Andes also with
yacón) [9].

Wild *P. tuberosus* is known (wild accessions in one genetic study came
from Ecuador), and genetic diversity is highest in the wild material
[1]. Yam beans were probably domesticated in the dry forests of western
Ecuador [1].

This species shares its general rotenone hazard and broader
*Pachyrhizus* biology with jicama and with this project's own ahipa
research [9][10].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the wet-tropics yam bean, grown on terra firme, eaten
raw.** Ashipa-growing areas get 1,500-5,700 mm of rain, mean temperature
20.7-25.5°C (mean minimum 11.0-13.2°C, mean maximum 29.7-35.4°C), and
84-92% RH [3]. The native habitat is lowland or premontane forest with
640-4,100 mm and 21-27°C [7]. By contrast, jicama is a seasonally dry
lowland crop, ahipa a cool Andean valley crop, and the Jíquima group
grows where only 450-500 mm of rain falls [3] — Ashipa is the only
cultivated yam bean of the permanently humid tropics [12]. It is grown
mostly on terra firme, often on poor, acid, high-aluminum soils, after
slash-and-burn clearing [1][12]; native soils are sandy, well-drained,
and fertile, but the plant is vigorous even on poor acid soils with
organic matter (pH 4.3-6.8) [7]. Management is low-intensity, with the
main field task being early hand weeding [7][12]. The roots are
normally eaten raw [12]: thin slices go in salads, and lightly cooked
they stay crunchy and take up sauces [3].

**Finding 2 — a climbing vine with one to several roots.** It is a
perennial herb, prostrate or vigorously twining, 0.3-3.5 m [7]. Ashipa
is semi-climbing to climbing with entire leaflets, against the shrubby,
deeply three-lobed Jíquima and the climbing, shallowly lobed Chuin [3].
One to several roots occur per plant in Ashipa (versus just one in
Jíquima and Chuin) [3]; all genotypes in a Manaus trial were
multituberous, with roots up to 23 cm long [5]; the skin is light brown
and the flesh white [7]. Staking is not needed per one source [7], but
on the várzea, staking plus flower removal gave 4.6 times the root mass
in another study [7] — these two findings are not reconciled.

**Finding 3 — temperature, not day length, controls the root.** One
study on Ashipa material found flowering occurred at 9-16 hour day
lengths, but a day/night regime of 30/25°C delayed flowering and
completely inhibited tuberization, while 25/20°C and 20/15°C were
suitable [14]. Day length is generally called short-day, but
photothermally insensitive genotypes exist; short days (9-12 h) raised
root dry matter and protein in one study [7]. Breeders use *P.
tuberosus* as the photothermally neutral breeding partner for jicama
[9]. This is a genuinely unusual finding: a heat-driven (not
photoperiod-driven) block on root formation itself, distinct from most
other root crops this project has researched.

**Finding 4 — sowing, pruning, and cycle.** Standard practice is
loosened soil, 2-3 seeds per hill thinned later, ridges about 20 cm, at
1.0 × 0.5 m spacing [7]. Sowing is year-round in the Amazon, or in the
rainy season elsewhere in Brazil [7]. Manaus trials used 1 × 0.5 m
spacing, 2 kg compost per pit or per m², on terra firme Ultisol at pH
6.0 [4][5]. Flower removal practice varies: one 2016 trial pruned
flowers weekly for 4 months starting from day 100, when 50% of plants
were flowering [5]; a Brazilian extension guide recommends removing
flowers early [7]; one secondary citation reports more than ten times
the root mass with pruning [3], while a different study judges pruning
"unfeasible on a large scale" [4] — this discrepancy on the magnitude
of pruning's effect is not reconciled. The cycle runs 5-7 months to
harvest in Brazil, with a recommendation not to delay lifting [7];
trials harvested at month 7 [5]. A Peruvian review instead gives a
growing season of about 12 months for Ashipa [12] — possibly referring
to the full cycle through to seed rather than root harvest, not
resolved.

**Finding 5 — yields span a huge range depending on management and
plant material.** Reported figures vary enormously: a Benin trial of 6
accessions gave 20.6 t/ha pruned and 13.9 t/ha unpruned (plus 2.2 t/ha
seed), with a combined-use range of 5-29 t/ha roots and 1.5-2.9 t/ha
seed, and a pruned range of 6-45 t/ha [2]. Manaus trials in 2014
(pruned, composted) gave 27-108 t/ha; earlier Manaus trials gave up to
50 t/ha in one study and 3.1-44 t/ha in another [5]. A 2016 trial across
64 genotypes gave a grand mean of only 7.24 t/ha at month 7 (best
individual results 33.2 t/ha on terra firme, 12.8 t/ha on várzea) [3].
Unpruned erosus × tuberosus progenies at Manaus gave only 1.6-3.5 t/ha
over 6 months [4]. A rising-water-season study gave 19.6-33.2 t/ha [4].
A Brazilian extension guide cites over 30 t/ha, and separately 27-108
t/ha [7]. The top trial figures are from pruned, composted plots of
selected breeding progenies; unpruned farm crops are far lower. Harvest
index was measured at 45.6% pruned and 23.7% unpruned in the Benin
trial [2]. Critically, the Manaus trial material (the "64 genotypes")
derives from natural outcrossing between *P. erosus* and *P. tuberosus*
[4] — these are not pure Ashipa landraces, so their yield and dry-matter
figures are only indicative for this crop, not a clean representative
sample.

**Finding 6 — composition: dry-matter figures conflict sharply.** Ashipa
is described qualitatively as "medium" dry matter, between jicama (low)
and chuin (high) [3], but measured values disagree substantially: Benin
*P. tuberosus* accessions measured 31.9% pruned and 30.0% unpruned (with
two named accessions at 32.0% and 27.4%) [2] — these values actually
match the chuin range rather than Ashipa's expected "medium" position,
so the accessions' cultivar-group identity is uncertain and they may
actually be chuin types [2][3][8]. By contrast, Manaus erosus ×
tuberosus progenies measured only 6.5-9.2% dry matter by specific
gravity [4], and that same study states only chuin reaches about 30%
[4] — a genuine, unresolved conflict. Protein content is reported
anywhere from 4.35% to 18% of dry matter depending on study and
material [2][3][4][6][7]. Carbohydrate is reported at 58-80% of dry
matter, with starch specifically up to 52.7-55.0% dry basis [2][4][7].
Fiber is reported at 10-27% of dry matter [2][4]. Minerals measured in
one secondary citation: iron 37 mg/kg, zinc 7.4 mg/kg [3]. A dedicated
starch study of five Peruvian phenotypes (including a yushpe type)
found easy extraction with rapid sedimentation, 10-13% yield, amylose
8.7-16.1% (the yushpe sample specifically at 9.1%), gelatinization onset
63.2-65.1°C, A-type starch crystallinity, low retrogradation, and
granules 10-21 µm [6].

**Finding 7 — partial harvest makes Ashipa a near-perennial.** In
Ashipa, farmers lift one or two lateral roots and leave the root next
to the crown; the plant then forms new roots in about 8 months [3].
Propagation is by seed or by roots [3]. This gives the crop a genuinely
dual harvest pattern — a single full lift, or a repeated partial-lift
pattern with regrowth — distinct from most single-harvest root crops
this project has researched.

**Finding 8 — pests and diseases.** Named problems include *Diabrotica*
beetles, anthracnose (*Colletotrichum*), and mosaic virus [3]; root-knot
(*Meloidogyne*) and lesion (*Pratylenchus*) nematodes make galled roots
taste bitter [3][7]; *Fusarium* is also named [7]. On terra firme sites,
anthracnose, rust, and *Meloidogyne* dominate; on várzea sites, rust
(*Phakopsora pachyrhizi*) was found on 100% of plants in one survey,
alongside anthracnose and *Sclerotium* wilt [3]. In the Benin trial,
nematode and insect damage scores were lower than those measured for
ahipa grown alongside it [2][10].

**Finding 9 — seed is toxic and has pesticidal use.** In the Benin
trial, 1,000 seeds weighed 180-230 g, with protein 26-32%, oil 22-26%,
and about 1% rotenone; oil was extracted after 30 minutes in 80°C water
[2]. Five rotenoids, neotenone, and a furanocoumarin have been
identified in *P. tuberosus* seed, cytotoxic in laboratory assays [16].
Farmers use the seed as a pesticide [4], and it can serve as a
pesticide or fish poison, or as food only after heat or chemical
treatment [5]. One Brazilian extension source claims rotenone and
pachyrhizin occur in seeds, leaves, and roots [7] — no other source
puts these toxins in the root, and the root is eaten raw everywhere
[3][12], so this specific claim is treated as unverified. Foliage is
usable as green manure or feed only from plants without pods [7]; in
jicama, leaves were found higher in rotenone than seeds [17], which is
at least consistent with foliage carrying some risk. Outcrossing rate
is reported at 2-4% [5].

**Finding 10 — history, people, and uses.** A 1556 colonial-era
account from São Vicente, Brazil, records that indigenous people grew
the crop for its starchy roots and that its seeds were poisonous; it
also fed enslaved people in colonial Brazil [3]. Today it is grown by
indigenous peoples in Brazil and is marginal commercially [1][7]. Uses
in Brazil include flour or starch for bread, biscuits, and cakes;
purée; grated root in cakes; and raw salads [3][7]. In the Pastaza
region of Ecuador, seed and root harvest in December coincides with
religious festivals and typical dishes [3]. Present distribution and
abundance have fallen sharply, and some landraces are considered
threatened [12].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial vine grown as an annual (or
  near-perennial under partial harvest); sown year-round in the Amazon,
  or in the rainy season elsewhere in Brazil (Finding 1, 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** Either a single full harvest, or a partial
  lateral-root lift leaving the crown root to regrow (new roots in
  about 8 months) — a genuine dual-pattern crop (Finding 7).
- **Climate:** Tropical rainforest, tropical monsoon, and premontane
  zones; 1,500-5,700 mm annual rain, mean temperature 20.7-25.5°C
  (Finding 1). Flowering occurs at 9-16 hour day lengths; tuberization
  is blocked by warm nights (30/25°C day/night), not by day length
  itself (Finding 3).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — the root only; seed, pods, and foliage
  contain rotenone and are not eaten (Finding 9).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the root crop, since flowers are
  typically removed; mainly self-pollinating when seed is wanted, with
  2-4% outcrossing (Finding 9).
- **Yield / composition:** Highly variable by management and plant
  material — farm-scale unpruned yields as low as 1.6-13.9 t/ha,
  research-trial pruned yields as high as 27-108 t/ha, with a 64-
  genotype trial mean of only 7.24 t/ha; the highest figures come from
  pruned, composted plots of selected breeding progenies, not
  representative farm conditions (Finding 5).
- **Water use:** Tolerates short dry spells but fills roots best with
  steady moisture; irrigation is recommended where rainfall is
  seasonal, despite general drought tolerance. In very wet fields,
  ridges are used to manage excess water, and várzea (floodplain)
  sites carry heavy rust pressure (Finding 1, 8).
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding, 2-3 seeds per hill thinned later,
  at roughly 1.0 × 0.5 m spacing (about 20,000 hills/ha); roots can
  also be used as planting material. Ridges about 20 cm are used in
  wet or flood-irrigated fields (Finding 4).
- **Labor:** No reliable sourced labor-hour figures exist specifically
  for this crop; flower removal is a recurring hand operation (weekly
  in at least one trial) that materially affects yield (Finding 4).
- **Soil:** Native habitat pH 4.3-6.8; trial soil pH around 6.0. Native
  soils are sandy, well-drained, and fertile, but the plant tolerates
  poor, acid, high-aluminum terra firme soils with organic matter
  (Finding 1).
- **Stand life:** Annual for a single lift; near-perennial under
  partial-harvest management, with new roots forming about 8 months
  after each partial lift (Finding 7).
- **Disease/pest profile:** *Diabrotica* beetles, anthracnose, mosaic
  virus, root-knot and lesion nematodes (causing bitter galled roots),
  *Fusarium*, rust, and *Sclerotium* wilt are all named problems, with
  disease profile varying meaningfully between terra firme and várzea
  sites (Finding 8).
- **Cultivars:** No named commercial cultivars exist in trade; sourced
  material consists of cultivar-group subtypes (Ashipa subgroups,
  Yushpe, Jíquima) and specific research trial accessions/progenies
  with measured composition or yield deltas (Finding 2, 6).
- **Harvest:** Either single-stage (one full lift at 5-7 months) or a
  repeated partial-lift pattern (lifting one or two lateral roots,
  leaving the crown root to regrow over about 8 months) (Finding 4, 7).
- **Storage:** No dedicated storage regime specific to this cultivar
  group was found; roots are generally washed and shade-dried, then
  used relatively quickly, since delayed lifting and prolonged storage
  both degrade quality.

## Hazards

*Diabrotica* beetles, anthracnose, mosaic virus, root-knot and lesion
nematodes (which cause bitter, galled roots), *Fusarium*, rust, and
*Sclerotium* wilt are all named field problems, with disease profile
differing between terra firme and várzea growing sites (Finding 8).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed, pods, and foliage contain rotenone
(about 1% in seed) along with several related rotenoids and a
furanocoumarin, cytotoxic in laboratory assays; only the root is eaten.
Farmers use the toxic seed deliberately as a pesticide or fish poison.
This is a genuine hazard to humans, livestock, and fish if seed or
pod/foliage material is ingested or enters feed or water, deferred
until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data
(Finding 9). A separate, single-source claim that rotenone and a
related compound also occur in the root itself was not confirmed by any
other source and is treated as unverified, since the root is reportedly
eaten raw everywhere without issue (Finding 9).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** seed sown in hills on terra firme → flowers removed →
  roots and tops harvested; or left unpruned, yielding smaller roots
  plus dry pods for seed; or one or two lateral roots lifted per pass,
  crown root left to regrow over about 8 months, lifted again later.
  Harvested roots are washed and shade-dried for raw salads, purée, or
  cakes; or grated for starch used in bread, biscuits, and cakes; or
  sliced and dried into flour. Seed can be ground into a farm-made
  insecticide/fish poison, or (proposed, not established commercially)
  heat-treated and pressed for oil. Tops provide green manure or feed,
  but only from plants that never set pods.
- A December harvest of seed and roots coinciding with religious
  festivals and traditional dishes is documented in the Pastaza region
  of Ecuador (Finding 10).
- Not covered by this research: canned, pickled, or frozen roots (no
  sources found); seed detoxification methods; root-piece propagation
  in detail; the erosus × tuberosus breeding program itself.

## Open research questions

- Dry-matter content is sharply disputed: 6.5-9.2% in one set of Manaus
  breeding-progeny trials vs. 27-32% in a Benin accession trial whose
  cultivar-group identity is itself uncertain (the values resemble
  chuin, not the expected "medium" Ashipa position).
- Growing-cycle length is disputed: 5-7 months per Brazilian sources vs.
  about 12 months per a Peruvian review (possibly referring to a
  different endpoint, e.g. full cycle to seed).
- A single Brazilian source's claim that rotenone occurs in the root
  itself (not just seed, pods, and foliage) is not supported by any
  other source and conflicts with the root being eaten raw everywhere
  without reported issue.
- Protein content conflicts across studies: as low as 4.35% of dry
  matter in one study of Peruvian phenotypes vs. 10-18% in a different
  secondary citation.
- The magnitude of the pruning effect on root yield is disputed:
  "more than ten times" in one secondary citation vs. 4.6 times (with
  staking) in a different várzea study.
- The Manaus trial genotypes used for several yield and composition
  figures are natural outcross derivatives between this species and
  jicama, not pure Ashipa landraces — their figures are only indicative
  for this entry, not a clean representative sample.
- The weakest-sourced figures in this entry are world-average yield (a
  roughly 7-108 t/ha spread across very different conditions), caloric/
  protein content (dry matter itself is disputed), the partial-harvest
  yield profile, labor requirements, and storage life.
- This crop's dual harvest pattern (single full lift vs. repeated
  partial lateral-root lift with regrowth) does not map cleanly onto a
  simple single-harvest-pattern model.
- Yushpe and Jíquima carry distinct site and growth-habit
  characteristics substantial enough that either could warrant separate
  treatment as its own crop entry in future research.
- The heat-driven (not photoperiod-driven) block on tuberization at
  warm night temperatures is a genuinely unusual finding worth further
  investigation, distinct from the more common photoperiod-sensitivity
  findings in related crops.
- The practice of partial lateral-root harvest leaving the crown root
  to regrow (a near-perennial root-cropping pattern in a legume) is
  sourced only narratively, not quantified in detail.
- Seed use as a farm-made pesticide and fish poison is a documented
  by-product use with agronomic significance, not explored in depth
  here.
- Nematode-induced bitterness in the edible root is sourced but not
  quantified.
- No hybrid-seed commercial system exists; only landraces and research
  breeding progenies are available.
- Several referenced primary sources (including two reference
  monographs on the species, one agricultural technical datasheet, and
  one full-text study on temperature/photoperiod effects) were not
  opened directly this pass and would strengthen this entry if
  consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 10,000 kg/ha fresh roots is estimated: inputs
include a 64-genotype trial mean of 7.24 t/ha (mixed terra firme and
várzea sites), a Benin unpruned mean of 13.9 t/ha, unpruned breeding-
progeny yields of 1.6-3.5 t/ha, and pruned trial yields of 20-108 t/ha.
Since farm crops are mostly unpruned, unfertilized swidden plantings, a
value between the 64-genotype mean and the Benin unpruned mean is used:
about 10 t/ha. Good yield is estimated at 30,000 kg/ha; exceptional at
50,000 kg/ha (from an older trial citation); a single selected,
composted breeding progeny reaching 108 t/ha is recorded as a trial
outlier, not adopted as the exceptional figure. Poor yield is estimated
at 2,500 kg/ha (middle of the 1.6-3.5 t/ha unpruned breeding-progeny
range). Plausible range: 5,000-15,000 kg/ha. On-farm production surveys
would replace this estimate.

Caloric and protein content are derived from composition figures, with
substantial uncertainty given the dry-matter conflict noted above. Dry
matter is assumed at 15%, between jicama's roughly 13% and chuin's
roughly 30%, consistent with Ashipa's qualitative "medium" position.
Carbohydrate contribution of roughly 70% of dry matter gives about 420
kcal/kg fresh weight; protein contribution adds about 48 kcal/kg; total
rounded to about 500 kcal/kg. Protein is estimated at 12 g/kg fresh
weight. Plausible range: 300-1,200 kcal/kg depending on which dry-matter
figure is actually representative of pure Ashipa material. A fresh-
weight proximate analysis of a confirmed Ashipa landrace would replace
this estimate.

A seed rate of about 10 kg/ha is derived from standard spacing (20,000
hills/ha at 2-3 seeds per hill) and seed weight (180-230 g per 1,000
seeds). A seed-to-yield multiplication ratio of about 200 is derived
from unpruned seed-crop yield divided by seed rate (plausible range
roughly 50-360 depending on source). Days to seed from sowing is
estimated at 180-270 days, based on flowering starting around day 100
plus roughly 3 months for pod maturation.

A partial-harvest yield profile is estimated: the practice and its
roughly 8-month regrowth interval are sourced, but the fraction of a
full lift taken per pass is not directly measured. A first lift around
6-8 months, taking roughly 0.3-0.5 of a full harvest per pass (since
typically only one or two of the roots per multituberous plant are
lifted), with 2-3 passes per stand before the crown root is finally
lifted, is a reasonable estimate pending a dedicated ethnographic yield
study of Ashipa swidden plots.
