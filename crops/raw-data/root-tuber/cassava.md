# Cassava (*Manihot esculenta*)

## Naming and scope

*Manihot esculenta* (Euphorbiaceae), also called manioc or yuca, 2n =
36 [14]. Native to South America; the wild progenitor is centered in
west-central Brazil, where domestication occurred no more than about
10,000 years ago [1]. The harvested organ is a true storage root: some
fibrous roots begin swelling with starch from about month 3, with most
root starch forming after month 6 [2].

Sweet and bitter cassava are treated here as cultivar groups of one
species along a continuum of cyanogen (cyanide-precursor) content,
rather than as separate crops — international food-safety guidance
draws the sweet/bitter line at a specific cyanide-equivalent threshold
[4]. They share climate, propagation, and cultural use, differing
mainly in hazard level and processing requirements.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — roots keep in the ground for up to two years but spoil
within two days once lifted.** Roots can be left in the ground and
harvested at any time from about 6 months up to 2 years after planting;
in food-shortage conditions they are sometimes dug piecemeal, a single
plant or even a single root at a time, as needed [2]. For direct food
use, harvest is typically done at 8-10 months; for industrial
processing, a longer growing season yields more total root and starch
[2]. Once lifted, however, a rapid oxidative process called postharvest
physiological deterioration (PPD) begins within roughly 24-48 hours and
quickly renders roots unmarketable; some varieties deteriorate within
as little as 3-4 days [7]. This deterioration is attributed to reactive
oxygen species forming in the wounded root tissue; one genetically
modified experimental line engineered to resist this process extended
shelf life to roughly three weeks [1]. The practical consequence is
that the field itself effectively functions as cassava's main storage
system — a standing crop that can be dug piecemeal as needed — while
processing into gari, flour, chips, or starch functions as the real
post-harvest storage step, since fresh roots simply cannot be kept.

**Finding 2 — a cyanogenic staple crop, where processing is also
detoxification.** Both roots and leaves contain cyanogenic glycosides
(linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide when the
plant tissue is damaged [1]. Sweet cultivars contain very low levels
of these compounds, while bitter cultivars can contain levels tens of
times higher; drought conditions raise cyanogen levels further, and the
outer root layers hold the highest concentration [1][2]. Critically,
the traditional processing chains used to prepare cassava for eating
are also detoxification chains: a fermented, granulated product (gari)
involves peeling, grating, fermenting for 2-3 days, pressing, sieving,
and roasting, a sequence that reduces cyanogenic potential very
substantially (by somewhere in the 85-99% range) [1][9]; other
traditional products involve pressing out juice and roasting, or
soaking (retting) for 2-3 days followed by drying and milling, or
simply slicing and sun-drying; leaves are pounded and boiled for
30-60 minutes before eating [1][2][9]. International food-safety
standards set specific maximum cyanide-equivalent limits for different
finished cassava products [4]. A serious, irreversible paralytic
condition called konzo is specifically linked to weeks of consuming
short-cut-processed bitter cassava combined with an otherwise low-
protein diet; documented historical epidemics followed periods of
drought, conflict, or disrupted food-cash economies, with one 1981
outbreak in a documented food-shortage context affecting over 1,000
people [5].

**Finding 3 — drought-hardy once established, but fragile in the first
three months.** Planting stakes sprout reliably only above a specific
minimum soil temperature and with soil moisture at a specific minimum
fraction of field capacity; critically, the crop is described as "very
sensitive" to soil-water deficit during its first three months, and
early-stage water stress impairs later storage-root development even
if adequate moisture returns afterward [2]. After this establishment
period, the picture changes substantially: there is no flowering or
seed stage that drives yield sensitivity, roots grow deep enough to
reach subsoil moisture, and under drought the plant simply reduces leaf
production until rain returns rather than suffering lasting damage [2].
The crop can survive on remarkably little rainfall once established
(as little as 400 mm annually in one documented semi-arid growing
region), while a different well-documented tropical region's maximum
yields corresponded to substantially higher seasonal rainfall [2].
Supplemental irrigation can roughly double yields in some contexts, and
one documented comparison found drip irrigation achieving equivalent
yield to surface irrigation while using half the water [2]. A standard
international crop-water-use reference does include a dedicated row
for this crop (contrary to an earlier, since-corrected internal
assumption that it did not), with separate growth-stage breakdowns for
a first-year versus a second-year standing crop, reflecting the
species' capacity to be grown as a longer-cycle, semi-perennial crop
[3].

**Finding 4 — stem cuttings are a distinct planting material with a
short shelf life.** Planting material ("stakes") comes from the stems
of vigorous 8-12-month-old plants; stems are stored upright in shade
with their bases resting on moist, loosened soil, and sprout fastest
when stored no more than about 5 days, with most guidance capping
storage at roughly two weeks, though some documented research has
successfully stored stems considerably longer under specific conditions
[2][8]. Stakes themselves are typically cut to a specific short length
with several nodes, at a specific minimum thickness, just before
planting, and are often dipped briefly in hot water as a treatment step
[2]. Standard monocrop planting density is documented at a specific
plants-per-hectare figure for 1×1 m spacing; planting the stake
vertically or at a steep angle generally outperforms horizontal
planting in both yield and starch content [2]. Multiplication rate from
planting material is relatively low under normal practice (one stake
yields material for roughly ten new stakes per year), though specialized
rapid-multiplication techniques using small multi-node cuttings and
multiple stem harvests per year can multiply stem material far more
quickly [2]. Infected planting material is a documented route for
spreading a major viral disease [2].

**Finding 5 — leaves offer a second, repeatable harvest, but trade off
against root yield.** Cassava leaves are notably high in protein
(reported up to roughly a quarter of dry weight) [1][2]. Tops can be
cut roughly every 2.5-3 months and will regrow within a similar
timeframe [2]. A documented Thai trial over roughly a year found that
taking five separate leaf cuts over the season substantially increased
total dry leaf and leaf-protein yield compared to a single harvest, but
at a real cost: final root yield fell substantially (from roughly 40
t/ha down to under 25 t/ha) under the repeated-leaf-cutting regime [2].
Leaves are used as a cooked vegetable in parts of Africa, as livestock
feed (cattle, pigs, poultry), dried into leaf meal, ensiled as silage
(a process that also reduces cyanogen content somewhat), and even fed
to silkworms in several Asian countries [2].

**Finding 6 — yields, soils, and nutrient needs vary enormously by
region and management.** World cassava production is enormous, with a
small number of countries (notably in West/Central Africa and Southeast
Asia) dominating output; documented world-average yield figures from
different sources and years range from roughly 10-13 t/ha, with a
well-managed, achievable average considerably higher, and up to around
80 t/ha reported as attainable under truly optimal conditions
[1][2][16]. National averages vary dramatically by country and growing
system, with some countries' averages several times the world average
[16]. Detailed commercial reference budgets from a South American
growing region document both a shorter-cycle industrial/starch type and
a longer-cycle sweet-table type, each with distinct spacing, cycle
length, and yield [6]. The crop is notably tolerant of acid soils:
yields are typically unaffected until soil acidity and aluminum
saturation reach levels that would already severely damage maize or
rice [2]. Nutrient removal is relatively modest when crop tops are
returned to the field (a documented 15 t/ha root harvest removes only
roughly 30 kg nitrogen, 20 kg potassium, and 3.5 kg phosphorus per
hectare), though sustained high yields over the long term require
considerably more fertilizer input, with potassium identified as the
main long-term limiting nutrient in a majority of long-term trials
reviewed [2]. The crop is rated moderately salt-sensitive, with the
storage root specifically identified as the most vulnerable organ —
one study found a dramatic reduction in root dry matter at a moderate
salinity level, though established plants tolerate somewhat higher
salinity than young plants [12]. Optimal mean temperature is warm
(documented figures cluster around 20-29°C depending on source), growth
stops below about 10°C, and frost kills above-ground growth entirely —
a meaningful constraint, since a substantial share of one major
growing region's cassava area lies in subtropical, frost-risk zones [1]
[10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A warm-season, frost-tender perennial grown
  as either a single-year or extended multi-year root crop; also
  managed as a dual-purpose root-and-leaf crop (Finding 1, 3, 5).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of roots (or flexible piecemeal
  digging over a 6-month to 2-year window) combined with a repeatable
  cut-and-come-again leaf harvest every 2.5-3 months — a genuine dual
  harvest-pattern crop, with leaf harvesting trading off against final
  root yield (Finding 1, 5).
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical (frost-risk), and semi-arid (once
  established) zones; optimal mean temperature roughly 20-29°C, growth
  stops below about 10°C, frost kills above-ground growth (Finding 3,
  6).
- **Food status:** Not raw-edible; sweet roots are peeled and cooked,
  while bitter roots require dedicated detoxifying processing before
  consumption (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not required for root production; the plant is
  insect-pollinated when grown for true seed, which is used only in
  breeding programs, since cassava is propagated clonally in practice.
- **Yield / composition:** Extremely variable by region and management
  — documented world averages cluster around 10-13 t/ha, well-managed
  yields reach the low-mid 20s t/ha, and up to roughly 80 t/ha is
  documented as achievable under optimal conditions (Finding 6).
- **Water use:** Highly drought-tolerant once established (surviving on
  as little as 400 mm annual rainfall in some regions), but "very
  sensitive" to water deficit during the first three months after
  planting, with early stress causing lasting damage to later storage-
  root development even if moisture conditions later improve (Finding
  3).
- **Establishment:** Propagated vegetatively from stem cuttings
  ("stakes"), typically planted vertically or at a steep angle; stakes
  require a minimum soil temperature and moisture level to sprout
  reliably (Finding 3, 4).
- **Labor:** Documented commercial reference budgets show planting-
  stage work (land preparation and planting) and harvest/post-harvest
  work as the two largest labor components, with weeding historically
  described as the single most time-consuming task for some
  smallholder farmers (Finding 6; see also Estimates below).
- **Soil:** Notably tolerant of acid, aluminum-rich soils well beyond
  what most other staple crops can handle; potassium is the most
  common long-term soil-fertility constraint under sustained
  cultivation (Finding 6).
- **Stand life:** Can be managed as either a single-cycle root crop (6
  months to 2 years per cycle) or, when leaves are repeatedly
  harvested, effectively as a longer-standing crop that regrows from
  the stems after each leaf cut (Finding 1, 5).
- **Disease/pest profile:** A major whitefly-transmitted viral disease
  has caused historical famines and continues to spread in some
  regions; mealybug and a mite pest can both cause very severe yield
  loss, though at least one mite has a documented effective biological
  control agent; weed competition alone can cause substantial yield
  loss if not managed (Finding 6; see also Hazards below).
- **Cultivars:** Distinguished primarily by the sweet/bitter cyanogen
  continuum, by intended use (table/sweet type vs. industrial/starch
  type), and by stem-branching habit (late-branching types producing
  the best straight stems for planting material) (cultivar detail
  below).
- **Harvest:** Roots can be lifted at any point from 6 months to 2
  years after planting, including piecemeal as a flexible food
  reserve; leaves, where harvested, are cut on a roughly 2.5-3 month
  cycle starting around month 3 (Finding 1, 5).
- **Storage:** Essentially no post-harvest storage window for fresh
  roots — deterioration begins within 24-48 hours of lifting, making
  prompt processing (or leaving roots in the ground until needed) the
  only practical storage strategy; planting-material stems have their
  own separate, short storage window of roughly 1-2 weeks (Finding 1,
  4).

## Hazards

A major whitefly-transmitted viral disease has caused historical
famines and continues to spread in parts of at least one major growing
region; a mealybug and a mite pest can each cause very severe yield
loss (up to a large fraction of the crop), though biological control
exists for at least one of these pests; weed competition alone can cut
yield substantially if not controlled (Finding 6).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — cassava roots, leaves, and processing by-products
(notably the liquid pressed out during gari-type processing) contain
cyanogenic glycosides that release hydrogen cyanide; bitter cultivars
can contain levels high enough to pose a genuine poisoning risk if
eaten raw or inadequately processed, and drought conditions raise
cyanogen levels further. A serious, irreversible paralytic condition
(konzo) is specifically linked to sustained consumption of
inadequately processed bitter cassava combined with a low-protein
diet, with documented historical outbreaks following disruptions to
normal food processing and diet. This is deferred until this project
has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** stems stored upright in shade → cut into stakes → hot-
  water dipped → planted. Roots lifted between 8 and 24 months are
  peeled within 1-2 days (peels can be dried and fed to livestock) and
  then either: grated, fermented, pressed, sieved, and roasted into a
  granulated detoxified product (gari); grated, pressed, and roasted
  into a different traditional flour product (farinha); soaked
  (retted), dried, and milled into flour; sliced and sun-dried into
  chips (which can themselves be milled into flour); or grated, washed,
  and settled into starch, with the leftover pulp as a by-product. The
  liquid pressed out during processing can itself be boiled down into
  a flavoring product. Sweet-type roots are more simply boiled or
  fried. Leaves, where harvested, are pounded and boiled as a cooked
  vegetable, dried into leaf meal, or ensiled into silage. True seed is
  used only in breeding programs.
- Not covered by this research: frozen cassava and fermented cassava-
  derived liquid products; detailed cooking methods for traditional
  dishes; cassava-based alcoholic beverages or industrial ethanol
  production; detailed pest and disease management practice.

A historical religious tradition among Indigenous Caribbean peoples
venerated a deity specifically associated with cassava cultivation and
fertility, with small symbolic stone objects sometimes buried in
cassava mound fields as part of this practice.

## Open research questions

- World-average yield figures differ meaningfully between sources and
  years (roughly 10-13 t/ha) — likely reflecting genuinely different
  data years and survey methodologies rather than a true conflict, but
  not reconciled into one figure here.
- Optimal mean growing temperature is given somewhat differently across
  sources (roughly 20-29°C in one source vs. a narrower 25-29°C range
  in another).
- Nutrient-removal figures per tonne of root conflict between a
  detailed, sourced calculation from one reference and a different,
  unreconciled figure for potassium removal specifically from a
  separate reference describing fertilizer recommendations under
  sustained high-yield management — the gap likely reflects different
  management intensity (tops returned vs. removed, or low vs. high
  cumulative yield) rather than a true contradiction, but is not
  resolved here.
- Whether cassava's photosynthesis is better classified as standard C3
  or as a C3-C4 intermediate pathway is genuinely disputed in the
  scientific literature, with evidence on both sides (elevated enzyme
  activity and an intermediate CO2 compensation point, but no
  specialized leaf anatomy typically associated with true C4 plants).
- Caloric content figures differ somewhat between two cited composition
  sources (roughly 140 vs. 160 kcal per 100 g raw).
- Drought-tolerance parameters (days without water before yield
  penalty, and the penalty rate itself) are derived largely by estimate
  and analogy to a different root crop's documented establishment-
  stage sensitivity, rather than from a dedicated cassava-specific
  water-stress model.
- Labor-hour estimates outside of one well-documented commercial
  reference-budget region are built substantially by estimate and
  analogy, and should be treated as rough, particularly for
  less-mechanized hand-labor tiers.
- No named commercial cultivars were sourced in this pass — cultivar-
  level detail here is limited to the sweet/bitter distinction, an
  industrial/starch type, and a general note on branching habit; a
  fuller pass through named breeding-program releases would add useful
  detail on disease resistance and specific cyanogen/shelf-life traits.
- Whether sweet and bitter cassava, which differ meaningfully in hazard
  profile and processing requirement despite being a single species, is
  better modeled as one crop entry with distinct harvest items (the
  approach taken here) or as two separate crop entries is a genuine
  open design question.
- Several topics were not researched in this pass at all: photoperiod
  sensitivity, mycorrhizal dependence, detailed waterlogging tolerance,
  a specific brown-streak viral disease, and fermented cassava
  beverages or industrial ethanol production.

## Estimates and derivations

A poor-yield figure of 5,000 kg/ha is estimated at roughly half the
documented world average, representing unfertilized smallholder fields
affected by viral disease pressure or heavy weed competition — both of
which are independently documented as capable of cutting yield very
substantially on their own. Plausible range: 2,000-7,000 kg/ha.
National-level low-yield statistics from a particularly affected
growing region would replace this estimate.

Water-use parameters (crop coefficient curve, stage lengths, seasonal
water requirement) are taken directly from a standard international
crop-water-use reference's first-year-crop growth-stage breakdown,
producing a seasonal water-use index of about 133 (days-equivalent);
a longer second-year crop (grown where a multi-year cycle is used)
produces a notably higher seasonal index, consistent with a longer
total growing period. A drought-buffer estimate of about 10 days before
yield penalty begins during the vulnerable first-year establishment
period is calculated from standard soil-water-holding and root-depth
assumptions; after establishment, the plant tolerates much longer
rainless periods by simply reducing leaf growth rather than suffering
lasting yield damage, so the per-day yield-penalty rate used for the
established phase is set considerably lower than the rate used during
the vulnerable establishment period.

Planting density for a dedicated leaf (foliage) harvest system is
derived from standard foliage-crop spacing, giving a considerably
higher plants-per-hectare figure than standard 1×1 m root-crop spacing.

Labor-hour estimates across different mechanization tiers are built
partly from a detailed, sourced commercial reference budget for a
moderately mechanized production system (where land preparation and
planting together make up the majority of labor, and harvest/post-
harvest work most of the remainder), and partly by estimate and
analogy for less- and more-mechanized tiers, since no single study
spans the full range of practice; a plausible range of roughly ±50%
around these estimates is assumed.

Nutrient removal per tonne of fresh root (roughly 2.0 kg nitrogen, 0.23
kg phosphorus, and 1.3 kg potassium) is derived from a documented
15 t/ha harvest's total nutrient removal when crop tops are returned to
the field; this is presented alongside, but not reconciled with, a
separate, higher potassium-removal implication from fertilizer
recommendations for sustained high-yield management, as noted in Open
research questions above.

Dry-leaf yield per individual cut (roughly 0.52 t/ha) is derived by
dividing a documented five-cut total dry-leaf yield evenly across the
five cuts, acknowledging that individual cuts were likely unequal in
the original study.

Edible fresh-root yield per unit area per day (roughly 7.7 g/m²/day) is
derived from a representative good-yield figure divided by a typical
roughly 300-day growing cycle.

Genome size (roughly 0.77-0.79 pg, 1C) is derived from published
genome-size estimates for this species, converted using the standard
base-pairs-per-picogram conversion factor; published estimates and
genome assemblies for different reference materials vary somewhat.
