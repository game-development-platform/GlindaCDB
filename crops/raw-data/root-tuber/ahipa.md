# Ahipa (*Pachyrhizus ahipa*)

**Category:** Root/Tuber · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/root-tuber/ahipa.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-26

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

**Data quality note:** this entry is thin to moderate. Agronomy comes from
a handful of trials (Bolivia, Brazil, Benin, Spain) and one grower's
guide. No yields from national statistics, no labor figures, and the main
reference monograph on the species was not opened directly — only cited
through secondary sources.

## Naming and scope

*Pachyrhizus ahipa* (synonym *Dolichos ahipa*), family Fabaceae, the
Andean yam bean [1][3]. Common names: ahipa, ajipa, ajipo; in Portuguese,
*jacatupé* [1][2][4]. Range: a few villages in Bolivia (greatest
diversity) and north-western Argentina (Salta, Jujuy); Peru in some lists
[1][2][3]. No wild form is known; it may exist only in cultivation [2][3].
Archaeological finds go back to the 1st century CE [1].

Ahipa is treated as its own species, separate from jicama (*P. erosus*,
a different crop this project has researched). Interspecific hybrids
among the yam beans are fertile and vigorous [5], though some show
reduced seed set [1]. The general rotenone hazard shared across the
yam-bean group, and general *Pachyrhizus* biology, are discussed more
fully in this project's own jicama research [11].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a cool-valley Andean crop, day-neutral for its root.**
Grown in Bolivian valleys at 1,800-3,000 m with a mean of 16-18°C and
400-700 mm of rain in 4-6 months (semiarid) [1]. Another figure gives a
mean of 15-21°C, with near-freezing nights to 32-35°C days in the
growing season [2]. At altitude, nights can fall to 0°C and days reach
35°C [3]. One cultivation-database profile gives an optimum mean
temperature of 12-22°C (tolerates 10-30°C); rain 800-1,200 mm (750-1,250)
with a dry season up to 8 months; sea level to 3,000 m [3]. It wilts
above 32°C and grows very slowly below 10°C [2]. Frost kills the tops,
but an established plant resprouts from the root [2]. Cool nights cut
yield in Spanish trials [6]. Tuberization does not depend on day length
[2][3], so the crop can be grown outside the tropics where the season is
long enough [3] — the main contrast with jicama, which needs short days
[11]; breeders value ahipa for this trait [2][11]. Sources conflict on
flowering photoperiod specifically: one encyclopedia-level source calls
ahipa short-day for flowering [1], while a cultivation database calls it
day-neutral [3], and a US grower reports flowering is "supposedly"
day-neutral, but several varieties never flowered before late September
[2] — not resolved.

**Finding 2 — a dense bush crop, not a vine.** Three growth forms are
reported [1][2]: an erect, determinate bush (15-40 cm); a semi-erect
sprawling bush (30-60 cm); and a twining, indeterminate form (60-200
cm). Only bush forms are widely available outside the Andes [2]; the
dwarf habit suits field-scale growing [1]. Most plants form one spindle-
shaped root about 15 cm long, weighing 500-800 g [1]. Roots reach about
1 kg in the Andes and up to 680 g in trial plantings in Oregon [2], or
500-1,500 g per a cultivation database [3]. A few multituberous forms
add small side tubers [2]. The skin is yellowish and the flesh white
[1]. Spacing is rows 20-60 cm apart, plants 6-25 cm apart in the row,
giving 6-83 plants/m² [1][2]. Closer planting gives smaller roots but
usually more total yield [2][6].

**Finding 3 — reproductive pruning sets the root yield.** Roots were
2-5 times larger with pruning in all but one tested variety [2], and
"reproductive pruning greatly increased root yield" per one agronomic
study [6]. In Benin, 14 ahipa accessions gave 20.0 t/ha pruned and 12.4
t/ha unpruned (a 1.6x difference), and the root harvest index rose from
29% to 62% [5]. The practice is to remove all flowers at least every 30
days [2][4]; Bolivian farmers prune once or twice a season by hand,
which takes substantial labor [1]. Two Andean strategies are reported
[2]: leaving the first few pods on each plant then pruning all later
flowers, or letting only the most promising plants flower fully and set
seed. In Benin, plants that also set seed gave 6-27 t/ha of roots plus
2.6-2.7 t/ha of seed [5] — a combined root-and-seed production path. A
breeding goal noted by multiple sources is a shorter cycle and fewer
pods, so pruning could eventually be dropped [1][2].

**Finding 4 — cycle and timing.** In Bolivia, sowing is August-October
with the rains [1]. First flowers appear at 87-140 days [1], or about
75 days per one source [3]. Pods mature April-June [1]. Roots are
lifted once they reach market size, at least 0.4 kg [1] — about 150-180
days [3], or about 6 months to full size in a warm climate [2]. Lifting
lasts 10-30 days [1]; roots can be left in the ground until needed,
with the tops cut off [1][2]. The full cycle runs 5-10 months [1]; in
warm climates the plant finishes in 7-9 months with a dry top [2][4]. A
Brazilian trial (Botucatu, 20.6°C mean, 1,517 mm rain) [4] found that
an October planting flowered from month 2 with no roots yet at 3
months, root weight leveling off after 7 months and the top drying by 9
months when flowers and pods were removed every 30 days from month 3; a
February planting instead gave a 7-month cycle with roots rotting by 9
months. The trial's recommendation was to plant in October, harvest by
9 months, and cut flowers from month 3. In Spain, late sowing shortened
the season and cut yield [6].

**Finding 5 — yields vary widely by region, pruning, and inoculation.**
In Bolivia at moderate elevations, 8-30 t/ha of roots is reported [3].
A separate, likely experimental figure gives roots at 28-53 t/ha,
"fruits" (pods) at 18-36 t/ha, and dry seed at 1.3-2.7 t/ha, probably
from Spanish or experimental plots [1]. In Benin, across 14 accessions
with no fertilizer or inoculant, pruned yield averaged 20.0 t/ha (range
10-38) and unpruned 12.4 t/ha with 2.07 t/ha of seed; combined roots-
and-seed production gave 6-27 t/ha of roots and 2.6-2.7 t/ha of seed
[5]. In the same Benin trial, ahipa roots had far more nematode and
insect damage (scores 3.3 and 4.3 of 6) than jicama roots grown
alongside them (0.8 and 1.9) [5]. Effective rhizobial inoculation
"greatly increased root and seed production" [6], nearly doubling root
yield per a secondary citation [2].

**Finding 6 — roots are drier and starchier than jicama, eaten raw like
fruit.** Dry matter is reported at 15-30% [1]; one study's samples were
81-84% moisture (i.e., 16-19% dry matter) [4]; Benin samples were
17.6-20.9% dry matter, averaging 23% across accessions [5] — jicama is
about 13% dry matter by comparison [5]. Composition on a dry basis
varies by source: starch 44-65% across different studies, sugars
17-28%, protein 7.9-18%, and fiber 2-26% depending on measurement
method and source [1][4][5][14]. At 7 months in the Botucatu trial,
starch was 57.2% for an October planting versus 42.6% for a February
planting, with theoretical starch at 89.6 vs 68.5 kg per tonne of root
[4]. Amylose content is genuinely disputed: one secondary citation gives
96-99.9% amylopectin (implying very low amylose) [1], while another
study measured 13-16% amylose, and a third gives 11.6-16.8% [4] — not
reconciled. Starch granules measured about 16.5 µm at 9 months [4]. A
dedicated laboratory study found starch extraction yield of 8-9% w/w,
gelatinization at 66°C (vs. maize's 72°C), high digestibility, and
proposed the starch for gluten-free foods [9]. The root is eaten fresh,
"almost like a fruit," sweet and crisp like an apple, sometimes made
into juice, and stays crunchy when boiled [1]. It is usually eaten raw;
baked, boiled, or fried it turns starchier [2]. It discolors slowly, so
it suits fruit salads [3]. In folk medicine it is considered cleansing
and used for throat and airway infections [1]. In a Benin trial, roots
of two ahipa accessions were processed into gari (a fermented, roasted
cassava-style product) after starch extraction, yielding a product
about 91% dry matter and 6.2-6.3% protein [5].

**Finding 7 — storage sources conflict on temperature.** One secondary
citation of a reference monograph gives an optimum storage temperature
of 12.5-17.5°C, with roots shriveling in store and damaged roots
rotting (from several named fungi) at low temperature and above 80% RH
[1]. A US grower instead reports about 10°C and 90% RH keeping roots up
to 2 months without loss of quality, after which texture changes and
roots may sprout, with much warmer or cooler storage shortening life
sharply [2] — the two temperature figures are not reconciled. Storage
shifts starch to sugar, which most buyers prefer; some farmers leave
roots in the sun before market to sweeten them further [1][2]. Leaving
roots in the ground is a viable store where the soil does not freeze
[2].

**Finding 8 — seed is toxic and short-lived in humid climates.** Seed is
round to kidney-shaped, 0.8-1 cm, in colors from black to lilac, maroon,
or black-and-white mottled, 20-100 seeds per plant, with 1,000 seeds
weighing about 300 g [1]; pods are 13-17 cm long [1]. Pollination is
mainly self-pollinating, with pollen fertility 45-100% [1], almost
always over 50% [2]; bumblebees visit the flowers [2]. Seed germination
falls fast in humid storage [1]; seed life is reported at 3-4 years in
the tropics [2] or more than 5 years at 10°C [2]. Propagation is by
seed, since cloning from roots multiplies too slowly [1]; stem cuttings
can root in 6-8 weeks for breeding or rescue purposes [2]. Yam-bean
seeds hold about 1% rotenone [5]; the seed and green parts contain an
insecticide, probably rotenone [3]; foliage, seeds, and pods contain
rotenone, and only the root is eaten [2]. One development-organization
source states the seeds are inedible because of rotenone despite their
oil and protein content, which may explain why ahipa never became a
major crop [10]. A separate, uncited claim states the seeds also
accumulate canavanine, described as toxic to some insects and a risk to
grazing livestock [1] — this claim is not confirmed by any other source
found and is treated as unverified; canavanine is better known from a
different legume (jack bean), raising the possibility this is a
confused attribution. Seed oil is reported high in palmitic acid and
γ-tocopherol, low in linolenic acid [1]; yam-bean seed generally is
26-32% protein and 22-26% oil, with oil extracted after heating seed for
30 minutes in 80°C water in one study [5]. One research group proposed
ahipa for "simultaneous production of starch, feed protein, and
industrial oil" [6].

**Finding 9 — nitrogen fixation and nutrient uptake.** Nitrogen
fixation is reported at 58-80 kg N/ha; roots and seeds took up 67 kg
N/ha in one season; tops returned 12-80 kg N/ha if left on the field,
and no nitrogen fertilizer was needed with effective nodulation [1].
Phosphorus helps nodulation and yield; whole-crop removal was 7-9.6 kg
P/ha and 20.2 kg K/ha, most of it in pod shells, seed, and roots [1].
Rhizobial strain matters: a US grower found no nodules with generic
bean inoculants, and seedlings stayed yellow without extra nitrogen
[2]. The nitrogen-rich straw is used for feed or plowed back into the
field [1].

**Finding 10 — pests and diseases.** The crop is not very susceptible
overall. Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne*) can be serious; bean
weevils (bruchids) attack seed; bean common mosaic virus can cut yield
by up to 30%; and roots can rot [1]. A grower reports slug and vole
damage [2]. In Benin's lowland trial conditions, root damage from
nematodes and insects was heavy [5].

**Finding 11 — status, culture, and market.** Ahipa is grown in small
native communities in Bolivia and northern Argentina [1], mostly for
home use, with no commercial crop in the Andes [2], though roots are
sold in local and national markets [3]. The crop has become scarce in
Bolivia: between 1994/96 and 2012, farmers shifted to maize, and prices
had not risen in 70% of urban markets studied; growers tend to select
and keep the largest seeds [7]. A few Argentine varieties may be
critically endangered [2]. One grower's guide calls it "among the least
cultivated domesticated crops in the world" [2]. One source suggests
local sale at the Corpus Christi festival hints at older religious uses
[1]. In Tarija, Bolivia, on Corpus Christi Thursday, an ajipa root is
carved into a cup and decorated with seasonal flowers and a sprig of
basil; wine or non-alcoholic *chicha de uva* is drunk from it. Sellers
in municipal markets supply both; one vendor reported having sold them
for 30 years, describing the ajipa as representing the chalice and the
grape chicha as the blood of Christ [8]. A 2010-era development-
organization project worked on yam beans for Africa, including gari
production from the roots and the search for rotenone-free genotypes
[10]. A university research group in Argentina has developed flour,
starch, and fiber products from ahipa [9][14].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Cool-to-warm season, frost-tender legume grown
  for its tuberous root; in Bolivia sown August-October, harvested from
  about 5 months, with pods maturing April-June (Finding 1, 4).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest of roots (Finding 4); seed
  production is a separate or combined path depending on pruning
  strategy (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Tropical highland, subtropical, semiarid, and warm
  temperate (long season) zones have all been used in trials — valleys
  at 1,800-3,000 m in Bolivia, sea level to 3,000 m per a cultivation
  database, with trials also run in Spain and Brazil (Finding 1).
  Tuberization is day-neutral; flowering photoperiod is disputed
  (short-day vs. day-neutral across sources) (Finding 1).
- **Food status:** Raw edible — the root only; seed, pods, and foliage
  contain rotenone and are not eaten (Finding 8).
- **Pollination:** Not required for the root crop, since flowers are
  typically removed; the species is mainly self-pollinating when seed
  is wanted (Finding 8).
- **Yield / composition:** Reported root yields range roughly 6-53 t/ha
  depending on region, pruning, and inputs — Bolivian farm yields 8-30
  t/ha; one likely-experimental figure of 28-53 t/ha; Benin trial means
  of 20.0 t/ha pruned vs. 12.4 t/ha unpruned with no inputs; combined
  root-and-seed paths 6-27 t/ha of roots plus 2.6-2.7 t/ha of seed
  (Finding 5). Dry matter is roughly 15-30% of fresh root weight
  (Finding 6).
- **Water use:** Tolerates long dry spells (400-700 mm rain over 4-6
  months reported for Bolivian valleys, plus flood irrigation); roots
  fill best with steady moisture, and heavy watering in the final month
  before harvest is reported to crack roots (Finding 1, 4). Waterlogging
  tolerance is low; poor root quality is reported in wet soil, and roots
  rotted when left too long in a wet season in the Brazilian trial's
  February planting (Finding 4, 6).
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding is the standard method; transplants
  are possible but the crop is sensitive to transplant shock. Seeding
  rate is reported around 40-65 kg/ha, with spacing of 6-83 plants/m²
  depending on density chosen (Finding 2). Sowing in Bolivia follows the
  rains, August-October; a Brazilian trial used October planting with
  better results than February (Finding 4).
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour figures were found for this crop.
  Reproductive pruning (flower removal) is a recurring hand operation,
  done at least every 30 days from first bloom, and is described as
  taking substantial labor when done by hand (Finding 3).
- **Soil:** Loamy riverbanks and hillsides, well-drained, are the
  preferred soil type; a fertile sandy-to-loamy profile is also reported
  workable. pH guidance disagrees across sources (ranges spanning
  roughly 5.0-8.0 depending on source). Crop removal of phosphorus and
  potassium is modest (around 7-9.6 kg P/ha and 20.2 kg K/ha across
  roots, pods, and seed) (Finding 9). Nitrogen fixation of 58-80 kg N/ha
  generally eliminates the need for nitrogen fertilizer when the correct
  rhizobial strain is present (Finding 9).
- **Stand life:** Grown as an annual; a second year is sometimes kept
  only for seed production, since root eating quality declines after
  the first year (Finding 4, 8).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Root-knot nematodes, bean weevils (attacking
  seed), bean common mosaic virus (up to 30% yield loss), and general
  root rot are the main named problems; slugs and voles also cause
  damage; nematode and insect root damage was notably heavy in a
  lowland Benin trial (Finding 10).
- **Cultivars:** About 20 landraces are known, but no named commercial
  cultivars exist in trade; sourced entries are habit types (erect bush,
  sprawling bush, twining, multituberous) and specific research trial
  accessions with measured composition deltas (Finding 2, 6).
- **Harvest:** Single stage — roots lifted once they reach market size
  (at least 0.4 kg), over a 10-30 day lifting window, or left in the
  ground until needed (Finding 4).
- **Storage:** Roots store about 2 months cool and humid (around 10°C,
  90% RH) before texture changes and sprouting begin, per one source;
  a different secondary citation gives 12.5-17.5°C as optimum — not
  reconciled. Storage shifts starch to sugar, sweetening the root, and
  some farmers deliberately sun roots before market to sweeten them
  further (Finding 7).

## Hazards

Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne*), bean weevils (attacking seed), bean
common mosaic virus (up to 30% yield loss), and general root rot are the
main named field problems; slugs and voles also cause damage (Finding
10). Several named fungi (including *Rhizopus stolonifer*,
*Cladosporium*, and *Penicillium*) can rot damaged roots in storage,
with internal browning, at low temperature and above 80% RH (Finding
7).

**TO BE ADDRESSED** — the seed, pods, and foliage of ahipa contain
rotenone (about 1% in yam-bean seed generally), a plant-derived
insecticide; only the root is eaten. This is a genuine hazard to humans,
livestock, and fish if seed or seed waste is ingested or enters feed or
water, deferred until this project has a place for this kind of
health/safety data (Finding 8). A separate, uncited claim that the seed
also accumulates canavanine (toxic to some insects, a possible livestock
risk) was not confirmed by any other source and is flagged as
unverified rather than adopted (Finding 8).

## Uses and processing

- **Chains:** direct-sown seed → flowers removed every 30 days → roots
  and tops harvested; or first pods kept for seed, then later flowers
  pruned, yielding roots plus dry pods; or unpruned seed plants (or
  selected roots replanted a second year) yielding dry pods and seed.
  Harvested roots are cut of tops, sorted, then either sun-sweetened or
  stored cool for fresh market, or juiced. Roots can also be grated and
  washed for starch, with residue roasted into gari in trial settings;
  or peeled, sliced, and dried into flour in laboratory settings. Seed
  can be heat-treated and pressed for oil and cake, as a proposed
  (not yet commercial) use. Tops provide nitrogen-rich straw for feed or
  are plowed back into the field.
- A fresh root carved into a ceremonial drinking cup is a documented
  ritual use tied to the Catholic Corpus Christi festival in Tarija,
  Bolivia (Finding 11).
- Not covered by this research: canned, pickled, or frozen ahipa
  (no sources found); the ritual cup-carving process itself; seed
  detoxification methods; propagation from cuttings and side tubers;
  interspecific breeding programs.

## Open research questions

- Storage temperature conflicts across sources: about 10°C per one
  grower source vs. 12.5-17.5°C per a secondary citation of a reference
  monograph.
- Flowering photoperiod conflicts: short-day per one encyclopedia-level
  source vs. day-neutral per a cultivation database.
- Amylose content conflicts sharply: a secondary citation gives
  96-99.9% amylopectin (implying very low amylose), while two other
  studies measured 11.6-16.8% and 13-16% amylose.
- Root yield figures conflict: 8-30 t/ha per one source vs. 28-53 t/ha
  per another, likely reflecting different field conditions
  (experimental vs. farm-scale) not fully reconciled.
- Soil pH guidance disagrees across sources (ranges spanning roughly
  5.0-8.0 depending on source).
- The claimed presence of canavanine in the seed comes from a single
  uncited source and was not confirmed elsewhere — treated as
  unverified rather than adopted.
- The weakest-sourced figures in this entry are world-average yield,
  labor requirements, and caloric/nutrient content (no fresh-weight
  proximate analysis was found).
- Partial reproductive pruning (keeping the first pods, then pruning
  later flowers) as a strategy distinct from fully-pruned or fully-
  unpruned paths is documented but not quantified in detail.
- Biennial seed production from replanted roots in cool climates is
  mentioned by one grower source but not independently verified.
- The planting-density tradeoff (6-83 plants/m², smaller roots but more
  total yield at higher density) is sourced only qualitatively.
- Sun-sweetening of roots before market (a post-harvest light treatment
  that raises sugar content) is documented from only one practical
  example.
- Day-neutral tuberization within a genus of otherwise short-day
  relatives is noted as a potentially valuable breeding-donor trait, not
  independently studied in depth here.
- Heavy pest/pathogen load (nematodes, insects) was specifically noted
  outside the crop's native range (in a lowland West African trial) —
  not reconciled against its generally low-susceptibility reputation in
  its home range.
- No hybrid-seed commercial system exists; only landraces are grown.
- Several referenced primary sources (including the main reference
  monograph on the species, and a few other full-text studies cited
  only via abstract or secondary mention) were not opened directly this
  pass and would strengthen this entry if consulted.

## Estimates and derivations

A world-average yield of 15,000 kg/ha fresh roots is estimated: inputs
include a Bolivian farm range of 8-30 t/ha, a Benin 14-accession trial
mean of 20.0 t/ha pruned and 12.4 t/ha unpruned with no inputs, and a
likely-experimental figure of 28-53 t/ha. Since farm crops in Bolivia
are typically pruned only once or twice, are often seed-and-root
combined crops, and are not deliberately inoculated, a typical crop
likely sits between the unpruned and pruned Benin means, near the lower
middle of the Bolivian range — about 15 t/ha. Good yield is estimated at
30,000 kg/ha (top of the Bolivian range); exceptional at 53,000 kg/ha
(the likely-experimental figure); poor at 6,000 kg/ha (low end of
Benin's combined-use range). Plausible range: 10,000-25,000 kg/ha. A
dedicated national production survey would replace this estimate.

Caloric and protein content are derived from composition figures: dry
matter about 17% (consistent across two cited studies), with starch and
sugars together about 75% of dry matter, giving roughly 12.8% available
carbohydrate by fresh weight (×4 kcal/g = ~510 kcal/kg); adding protein
contribution (~54 kcal/kg) and a partial fiber contribution (~10
kcal/kg) gives roughly 575 kcal/kg, rounded up to 650 kcal/kg to account
for drier roots at full maturity. Protein is estimated at 14 g/kg fresh
weight (17% dry matter × 80 g/kg dry-basis protein). The fresh-to-dry
ratio is estimated at about 5:1 (range 3.3:1 to 6.7:1 depending on dry-
matter percentage). A full fresh-weight proximate analysis would replace
this estimate; plausible range 500-900 kcal/kg.

A starch-extraction yield fraction of 0.06-0.09 is derived: laboratory
extraction gave 8-9% w/w of fresh root in one study, while theoretical
starch content from a separate study implies 6.9-9.0%; farm-scale
recovery is assumed lower than laboratory extraction, so the practical
floor is set at 6%.

A dried-slices yield fraction of 0.14-0.30 is derived from dry matter
content (15-30% of fresh weight across sources), less peel losses (a
few percent), with slices dried to about 10% residual moisture.
