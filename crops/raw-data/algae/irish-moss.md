## Naming and scope

This covers *Chondrus crispus* Stackhouse, a small red alga (family
Gigartinaceae) of North Atlantic rocky shores [1]. Common names are Irish
moss, carrageen, carrageen moss and carraigín, Irish for "little rock" [1].
It is the original commercial source of carrageenan [1][2]. It covers the
single cropID `irish_moss`.

**Mostly a wild-managed resource.** Almost all supply has come from wild
beds, raked from boats or gathered after storms (Finding 3). Tank culture
works but has cost more than wild harvest (Finding 4). It is a candidate
for the wild-managed template (GitHub issue #2); notes are in
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/irish-moss.md`.

**Name conflicts.**

- **"Sea moss".** In today's supplement market, "sea moss" and even
  "Irish moss" labels are often *Gracilaria* (an agar seaweed) or
  eucheumatoids, not *Chondrus* [3]. That is a labelling issue for the
  `gracilaria` and `cottonii` rows, not part of this doc. (Low weight: a
  testing-laboratory guide.)
- **Not the plant.** *Sagina subulata*, a flowering plant, is also called
  Irish moss [1].
- **Hana-tsunomata.** Acadian Seaplants sells its tank-grown edible
  strains as Hana Tsunomata® [4]; one older account calls the product
  Hana-nori™ [2]. These are cultivars of this species (see Cultivars).

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`false_irish_moss`** (*Mastocarpus stellatus*, formerly *Gigartina
  stellata*; proposed). In Ireland it is gathered more or less
  interchangeably with Irish moss and sold mixed as "carrageen" [1]. But
  it differs in ways that matter:
  - Its fronds are channelled, not flat [1].
  - Its life cycle is heteromorphic. The sporophyte is a crust once named
    as a separate species, *Petrocelis cruenta*; some populations skip it
    altogether [5].
  - It yields hybrid carrageenans [5].
  - It tolerates freezing better, so it grows higher on northern shores
    [5].
  - Canadian managers advise against harvesting it, because it is less
    common than *Chondrus* [2].
- **`tsunomata`** (*Chondrus ocellatus*, proposed). One Japanese source
  lists it among traditionally eaten seaweeds [1]. *C. crispus* is the
  only Atlantic *Chondrus*; eight other species are in the Pacific [1].
  Low weight: found in one snippet only.
- ***Chondracanthus chamissoi*** (Chile). Sold by one retailer as "Pacific
  Irish moss" [1]. Not proposed; it is a different genus and came up only
  in a shop listing.
- **`cottonii`** and **`spinosum`**. The tropical eucheumatoids that
  replaced Irish moss as the main carrageenan source (Finding 3) [2].
  `cottonii` is cross-linked.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the two generations look identical but make different
carrageenans.**

- **Life cycle.** Male and female gametophytes (haploid) alternate with
  tetrasporophytes (diploid), and all look the same [6]. A third stage,
  the carposporophyte, grows on the fertilised female [1].
- **The chemical split.**
  - Gametophytes make mainly kappa and iota carrageenans, which gel [6].
  - Tetrasporophytes make mainly lambda carrageenan, which thickens but
    doesn't gel [6].
  - Industry uses kappa and iota as gels and lambda as a thickener [6].
- **How to tell them apart.** Infertile plants are hard to tell apart.
  Gametophytes often show a blue iridescence at the tips; fertile
  sporophytes look spotty [1].
- **Content differs too.** In Galicia, gametophytes held 37.4% carrageenan
  by dry weight against 29.1% for tetrasporophytes [6]. Content peaked in
  spring and autumn, at over 40% in gametophytes [6].
- **Why it matters.** The harvested crop is a mixture, so its carrageenan
  depends on which phase dominates a bed. Gametophytes dominate many
  wave-swept shores [6], including Nova Scotia's Atlantic coast [6].

**Finding 2 — the giant Basin Head strain: no holdfast, no sex, and now
almost gone.**

- **What it is.** A strain found only in Basin Head lagoon, Prince Edward
  Island [7]:
  - It has no holdfast. Mussels' byssal threads anchor it instead [7].
  - It is almost all in the non-sexual stage and multiplies only by
    fragmentation [7].
  - A plant weighs about 800 g, against about 50 g for the common form
    [7]. It keeps its red-brown colour all year; the common form shifts
    from red to yellow to green in summer [7].
- **Protection.** Its rarity was the main reason Basin Head became a
  Marine Protected Area in 2005 [7].
- **Collapse.**
  - Bed area, cover and biomass declined 1980–2008 [7]. One DFO report
    puts the loss at about 99% [7].
  - Since about 2000 its bed area has shrunk by more than 99.9%, coinciding
    with the arrival of the invasive green crab [7].
  - The crabs eat the mussels that anchor it, so it floats away and dies
    [7]. Nutrient inputs and green-algal blooms also contribute [7].
  - A crab-removal programme ran in 2016–2018 [7]. Fragments are being
    kept in mussel socks and cages [7].
- **Why it matters.** It is a striking example of one species having a
  local form with a different life history, and a cultivation candidate:
  in the 1980s one isolate from Basin Head produced about 300 wet tonnes in
  tanks [2], and a sock-culture method was developed for it [4].

**Finding 3 — a raked and storm-gathered wild crop that rose and fell with
world carrageenan markets.**

- **Early history.**
  - Irish moss has been a folk remedy in Ireland since the early 19th
    century [1].
  - In Scituate, Massachusetts, an Irish immigrant began gathering it
    around 1847, drawing famine emigrants into "mossing" [8]. Mossing
    there lasted until about 1997 [8]. An 1890 gazetteer counted 72 people
    gathering it in Scituate (low weight: a reprint) [8].
- **Second World War boost.** Canada's harvest only became prominent when
  Japanese agar was cut off and French beds became inaccessible [1].
- **Canada's peak.**
  - Canada was the world's top producer from about 1950 to 1970 [1][2],
    supplying 65–70% of world production until the early 1970s [2].
  - Landings peaked at about 50,000 wet tonnes in 1974 [2][1].
  - Around 1990, about 2,000 harvesters from 47 communities took part, and
    on PEI it was second only to lobster in value [2].
- **Decline.**
  - Cheaper farmed *Kappaphycus* and *Eucheuma* from the Philippines and
    Indonesia took over the market [2].
  - Overharvesting was also documented [2].
  - On PEI another red alga, *Furcellaria lumbricalis*, became dominant
    after the mid-1980s. Buyers rejected moss with too much *Furcellaria*
    mixed in [2].
  - Landings fell to about 20,000 wet tonnes a year in 1976–1990, and
    PEI landed 4,830 wet tonnes in 1992 [2]. By 1992 *Chondrus* was 3.8%
    of harvested carrageenophytes worldwide [2].
- **Today.** A 2015 article reported the PEI industry all but dead, with
  the beds overtaken by other seaweeds [2]. Storm-cast
  weed is now gathered for organic seaweed meal instead [2].

**Finding 4 — tank culture worked at scale, but couldn't beat the wild
price for carrageenan; it found a niche as a coloured salad seaweed.**

- **Scale.** Tank culture was developed in Nova Scotia from the 1970s
  [2][4]. Full production in 1987 used 17 tanks of 2,000 m² plus nine
  smaller ones over 3.4 ha [2]. By 2004 the operation covered 8 ha [2].
- **Yield.** Net annual production was 6.27 kg dry weight per m² [2]
  (about 63 t/ha a year; see Estimates, below).
- **Why it stopped for carrageenan.** Production cost exceeded wild
  harvest, and the purer carrageenan didn't make up for it [2]. The main
  client withdrew once lambda carrageenan was available elsewhere [2].
- **The edible niche.**
  - The tanks were turned over to edible strains, sold to Japan for kaiso
    salads, sashimi garnishes and soups [2].
  - The strain used is an "oddball". Wild Irish moss is too tough to chew,
    according to the company's scientist [4].
  - It is grown free of epiphytes, which the company calls a first in
    seaweed cultivation [4].
  - The product comes in pink, green and yellow [4]. A green mutant was
    derived vegetatively from a red strain kept in culture for over 20
    years [4].
- **Why tanks suit it.** Irish moss can grow free-floating, without a
  holdfast [1].

**Finding 5 — warming and invaders squeeze it from the south.**

- **Thermal envelope.** Its range spans about 40–60 °N, at 0–20 m depth,
  in waters of −1.8 to 21.8 °C [5].
- **Projections.**
  - Under high emissions, its southern edge in the Northwest Atlantic is
    projected to move north by up to 406 km by 2100 [5].
  - Northward gains still outweigh southern losses, giving an overall range
    expansion in that study [5].
  - A 2017 study projected that its presence is threatened by warming in
    the Gulf of St. Lawrence and on Nova Scotia's Atlantic coast [5].
  - These two projections differ in emphasis; both are kept here.
- **Heat and freezing.** Intertidal plants can swing 10–20 °C in one tide
  and can acclimate to growth temperature [5]. Sporelings died just after
  germination at 30 °C [5]. Fronds frozen daily acclimated to freezing
  [5].
- **Competitor.** The Asian red alga *Grateloupia turuturu* has invaded
  its low-shore habitat from Rhode Island (1994) into the Gulf of Maine
  [5]. Its sporelings grow faster and tolerate a wider temperature range
  [5]. Where it dominates, macrophyte biomass and invertebrate richness
  drop [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, living 2–6 years [8]. A long-lived
  basal crust can regrow fronds after damage [8]. In Canada it grows from
  May to November [8]. In the UK it reproduces from autumn to spring [8].
- **Harvest pattern:** Wild harvest of attached beds by hand rake or drag
  rake, plus gathering of storm-cast weed [2]. Repeated cropping of the
  same beds each season (see Labor).
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable for wild beds. Summer-
  harvested plants recovered in 5–6 months; recovery after winter harvest
  was much slower [8].
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate North Atlantic. Iceland and the Faroes
  south to southern Spain, and Atlantic Canada [1]. One source gives
  Portugal and New Jersey as the southern limits (low weight) [1]. Thermal
  envelope −1.8 to 21.8 °C (Finding 5) [5]. Pacific records need
  verification [1].
- **Composition:**
  - Raw (USDA FoodData Central 168456, via aggregators): 49 kcal, 1.5 g
    protein, 12.3 g carbohydrate, 1.3 g fibre and 8.9 mg iron per 100 g
    [3].
  - Dry basis: about 55% carrageenan, nearly 10% protein and about 15%
    minerals [1]. Galician carrageenan content was 29–37% (Finding 1)
    [6].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as a milk pudding (blancmange) [2], or boiled to a jelly [1]. It holds 20 to 100 times its weight of water when
  boiled [1]. Wild plants are very tough; the edible tank strains are
  chosen to be tender [4].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Gametophytes are separate male and
  female plants [6]. Spores have low viability (under 30%) and settle
  within about 10 m of the parent [8].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:**
  - Tank culture: 6.27 kg dry weight per m² a year (about 63 t/ha) [2].
  - Wild landings: about 35,000 wet tonnes a year in the Maritimes over
    1967–1979 [2], peaking at 50,000 in 1974 [2].
  - In commercial beds in the southern Gulf of St. Lawrence it made up
    about 80% of plant biomass [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:** Wild beds regrow from basal crusts and spores [8].
  Tank culture is vegetative, from selected strains [2][4]. Tank juveniles
  start indoors, then move to greenhouses and outdoor tanks [4].
- **Labor:**
  - Winching boats hauling drag rakes cropped 20.1 ± 9.8 kg an hour in PEI
    trials [2].
  - In western PEI, about 100 licensees covered about 875 ha, averaging
    22.8 drag-rake passes per hectare per season [2].
  - In Nova Scotia, 4–5 m hand rakes are worked from small boats, from two
    hours before to two hours after low tide [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. It grows on rock, from the mid-intertidal into
  the subtidal [1]. Strong wave exposure favours it [8].
- **Storage (postharvest):** It must be dried at once to prevent spoilage
  [2]. It was sun-dried, often after bleaching [2]. An oil-fired drum
  drier was introduced in 1966–1967, after which buyers bought wet crop at
  the dock [2].
- **Establishment:** Tank stocking density kept at or below about
  10 kg/m³ [2].
- **Cultivars:**
  - Acadian Seaplants' proprietary edible colour strains (red, green
    mutant; pink, green and yellow product) [4].
  - The Basin Head giant strain (Finding 2) [7]. It is protected and not
    harvested, so it stays a note here rather than a proposed row.
- **Weather response:**
  - Storms drive the storm-cast harvest; poor weather cut the 1972 landing
    [2].
  - Ice scouring was among the suspected causes of Nova Scotia's decline
    [2].
  - Tanks were left to freeze up to half their water in winter [2].
  - Frost as a quality event: Not sourced.
- **Water regime:** Marine, mid-intertidal to subtidal [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:**
  - PEI mossing ran mainly May–July [2].
  - A reprinted 19th-century Irish account gives May to about 1
    September (low weight) [8].
  - A summer harvest may allow faster recovery with less effect on
    reproduction [8].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Epiphytes such as *Ulva* were kept in
  check in tanks by grazing amphipods and isopods [2]. Endophytic algae
  infect it [4].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to the North Atlantic [1]. No invasive record was found in this
    pass.
  - It is itself displaced by invaders: *Grateloupia turuturu* (Finding 5)
    [5] and, at Basin Head, the green crab (Finding 2) [7].
  - **Harvest regulation** (rules on wild collection, not biosecurity):
    - Canada's Atlantic Coast Marine Plant Regulations date from 1978,
      under the Fisheries Act [2].
    - Harvest is organised by Marine Plant Harvesting Districts [2]. Fees rose from $45 to
      $200 a year in 1996, and licences became limited and
      non-transferable [2].
    - Nova Scotia: a 5 mm minimum rake-tine spacing, and seasonal closures
      in some districts [2]. Drag-raking was limited to those who used it
      before 1977 [2].
    - Suction harvesting was banned in Canada in the 1990s [8].
    - Québec guidance: take at most 33% of a species' biomass per site
      [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Proven at commercial scale in land-based
  tanks (Finding 4) [2][4]. Tumble (air-agitated) tank culture was first
  worked out in detail for this species [4].

## Hazards

- **Iodine.** Sources disagree on how much:
  - A 2020 Spanish study found 3.86 ± 1.49 mg/kg dry weight, higher than dulse in the same study [3].
  - A review of bioavailability gave 24 mg/kg of bioavailable iodine for
    *Chondrus*, the lowest of the species it covered. On that basis it
    allowed about 26 g dry a day within the EFSA limit of 600 µg [3].
  - US supplement guidance warns that more than 1 mg a day could cause
    iodine poisoning or affect the thyroid [3].
  - See Open questions.
- **Heavy metals.** Supplement guidance says to choose products tested for
  heavy metals [3]. No species-specific figures were found in this pass.
- **Carrageenan.** EFSA's 2018 re-evaluation kept the group ADI of
  75 mg/kg body weight a day as temporary, pending more data [3].
  Degraded carrageenan (poligeenan), made by acid hydrolysis, is linked to
  harm and is not authorised as a food additive [3].
- **Bycatch.** Drag-raking kills lobsters living in the beds [2]. Traditional
  rakes also pulled up 24–35% of fronds with their holdfasts [2]. Rake
  designs were changed to reduce both [1][2].
- **Mixed species.** *Mastocarpus* is hard for non-specialists to tell
  apart, so harvests may contain more of it than intended [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (Maritimes, 20th century):**
  - drag rake or hand rake from boats, or storm-cast gathering (on PEI
    with horse-drawn rakes) [2][1] →
  - separate sand and stones from storm-cast weed [2] →
  - dry at once, by sun or drum drier [2] →
  - sell to buyers and extraction plants for carrageenan [2].
- **Chain (Irish carrageen):** pick → bleach and dry on sand hills under
  rain, dew and sun [8].
- **Uses:**
  - Carrageenan for dairy products such as ice cream, and processed foods
    (E407) [1].
  - Beer fining (bleached moss) [1][2].
  - Thickener in calico printing and paper marbling [1].
  - Puddings and jellies [2][1].
  - A WWII substitute for agar [1].
  - Edible tank-grown fronds for kaiso salads, sashimi garnish and soups
    [2].
  - Organic seaweed meal for farms, from storm-cast weed [2].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** Carrageenan extraction methods (covered
  in `cottonii.md`); folk remedies for respiratory ailments [1].

## Open research questions

- **Iodine level.** 3.86 mg/kg total [3] vs 24 mg/kg bioavailable [3]
  can't both describe typical plants. Origin, season, method or
  processing?
- **Why PEI beds turned to *Furcellaria*.** The source lists five
  hypotheses without resolving them [2].
- **Gametophyte vs tetrasporophyte ratio.** How it shifts with harvesting,
  and what that does to carrageenan type in a game model.
- **Modern landings.** No figures after the early 2000s were found [2].
- **Wild-managed template** (issue #2): districts, rake specs, licence
  limits and storm-cast rights.
- **Decisions:** whether to keep `false_irish_moss` and `tsunomata` as
  proposed rows.

## Estimates and derivations

**Tank yield per hectare, about 63 t dry a year.** Input: 6.27 kg dry
weight per m² a year [2]. 6.27 × 10,000 / 1,000 = 62.7 t/ha. This is net
production in managed tanks at one site; a range wasn't given.

**Iodine allowance from the review's figure, about 25 g dry a day.**
Inputs: 24 mg/kg bioavailable iodine; EFSA limit 600 µg a day [3].
600 / 24 = 25 g dry a day. The review itself gives 26 g [3]; the small
difference is presumably rounding in its inputs.
