# Hijiki (*Sargassum fusiforme*)

## Naming and scope

This covers *Sargassum fusiforme* (Harvey) Setchell, a brown alga (order
Fucales, family Sargassaceae), called hijiki in Japan [1]. It covers the
single cropID `hijiki`.

**Name history.** It was long known as *Hizikia fusiformis* (or *Hizikia
fusiforme*), and US labels may say "hiziki" [1]. *Hizikia* is now treated
as a synonym of *Sargassum*, after a phylogenetic analysis [1]. Food-safety
papers still use the old name [2].

**Product names.** The leafy buds are sold as me-hijiki and the stems as
naga-hijiki [3].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`akamoku`** (*Sargassum horneri*, proposed). An edible seaweed
  harvested in spring in Japan, boiled until it turns green and sticky,
  and eaten chopped [4]. It has become invasive in southern California
  [4]. Different species, use and biosecurity profile.
- **`mojaban`** (*Sargassum fulvellum*, proposed). Cultivated in Korea
  alongside hijiki [1]. Name taken from its Korean common name; no other
  detail was found in this pass.
- ***Sargassum muticum*** (wireweed). A close relative and a major
  invader in Europe and North America, first found in British Columbia in
  1944, probably carried with Japanese oysters [4]. Not proposed: it isn't
  a food crop, and US guidance says it shouldn't be farmed [1].
- **`arame`** (*Eisenia bicyclis*). A LISTED row; another dried Japanese
  brown seaweed, but one where the arsenic surveys found no inorganic
  arsenic (Finding 1) [2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the arsenic seaweed: several governments advise not eating
it at all.**

- **The measurements.** A UK survey of 31 samples of five seaweeds found
  inorganic arsenic only in the nine hijiki samples, at 67–96 mg/kg [2].
  The other seaweeds (arame, kombu, nori, wakame) were all below the
  0.3 mg/kg detection limit [2].
- **Why.** Brown seaweeds including hijiki accumulate arsenic in the
  inorganic form, which is more toxic than the organic forms [2].
- **The advice.**
  - Canada's food inspection agency advised avoiding it in 2001 [2].
  - The UK Food Standards Agency did the same in 2004, and repeated it in
    2010 after an EU alert on one brand [2].
  - Hong Kong and Food Standards Australia New Zealand issued similar
    advice in 2004 [2].
  - Australia now tests all imported hijiki at the border [2]. Its limit
    for inorganic arsenic in seaweed is 1 mg/kg, measured at 85%
    hydration [2].
- **Preparation helps, a lot but not enough.**
  - Heating at 90 °C for 5 minutes cut arsenic by about 33–80%, and a 2%
    salt soak removed a further 5–20% [2].
  - Parboiling twice for 5 minutes cut inorganic arsenic by 79% [2].
  - One report gives inorganic arsenic of 68.8–96.2 mg/kg as sold and
    7.9–30.9 mg/kg prepared [2].
  - Even prepared, that is many times the Australian limit (see
    Estimates, below).
- **Two views kept.** Australia's regulator notes that countries advised
  avoidance despite evidence that preparation greatly reduces the arsenic
  [2]. A seller of hijiki cited the Japanese health ministry's view that
  it can be eaten safely in small amounts when prepared properly [2] (low
  weight: company statement, quoted in a trade article).

**Finding 2 — the "iron-rich" reputation came from the iron cooking pots.**

- **The revision.** Japan's 2015 food composition tables split dried
  hijiki by how it was processed [5]:
  - boiled in an iron pot: 58.2 mg iron per 100 g;
  - boiled in a stainless-steel pot: 6.2 mg per 100 g [5].
  - The 2010 tables had listed 55.0 mg [5].
- **Why.** The iron was taken to come from long heating in iron pots;
  stainless steel is now common [5].
- **Products vary.** A Tokyo survey found a mean of 30.1 mg/100 g with a
  coefficient of variation of 89.7%; the lowest products were about 15% of
  the table value [5].
- **Dispute.** Japan's hijiki industry council said it doubted that pot
  material makes a difference (low weight: reported second-hand) [5].
- **Why it matters.** A real case where a processing step, not the plant,
  set a nutrient value in the standard tables.

**Finding 3 — farmed at scale in China and Korea, still picked wild in
Japan.**

- **Farmed supply.** Hijiki has been cultivated in South Korea and China
  since the 1980s [1]. World production rose from 109,597 t in 2003 to
  303,797 t in 2020 [1]. In China, farming is concentrated in Zhejiang,
  especially Dongtou [1].
- **Japan.** Japan eats about 12,000–15,000 t dry a year [1]. Over 90% is
  imported from China and Korea, according to one 2016 paper [1]; other
  sources give over 80% [1] or nearly 90% [3].
  - Japanese hijiki grows wild on rocks from southern Hokkaido to Kyushu
    [3]. In Ise-Shima it is harvested from April to May on low-tide days,
    by fishers and divers [3].
  - Japanese farming stays limited by seedling supply and high labour
    cost [1]. Domestic hijiki has gained value with origin labelling [1].
  - Trials in Oita grew both wild and hatchery seedlings to over 1 m and
    over 10 kg wet per metre of rope [1].
- **Why it matters.** The same species is a farmed crop in one country
  and a wild-picked one in another; Japan's domestic product commands a
  premium.

**Finding 4 — three seeding routes, including regrowth from last year's
holdfasts.**

- **Wild seedlings.** Groups of three or four 5–10 cm seedlings were
  pushed into rope every 5–10 cm, hung at 2–3 m, and grown from November
  to May [1].
- **Holdfast regrowth.** The perennial holdfast is left in the sea over
  summer and regrows the next season [1]. A line can produce for two to
  four years this way, but yield falls each year [1].
- **Zygote seedlings.** Male and female plants (separate sexes) are mixed
  1:1 in tanks at 18–20 °C in May–June [1]. Gametes are released together
  within about a week, and zygotes stick to seed string within about 24
  hours [1]. Seedlings spend one to five months in tanks, then one to five
  months in a sea nursery [1]. This route gives more biomass [1].
- **Tissue culture.** A 2024 study built an asexual seedling method from
  tissue culture, aiming to keep superior traits stable [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial holdfast, with fronds grown in winter
  and spring [1]. Japanese wild harvest runs March–May [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single spring harvest of fronds (wild in Japan [3];
  rope culture November–May in trials [1]). Holdfasts can regrow fronds
  for two to four seasons [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About six months from November
  outplanting to May harvest (Oita trials) [1].
- **Climate zones:** Warm-temperate to subtropical [1]. Japan from southern
  Hokkaido to Kyushu [3]; China from Liaoning to Guangdong [1]; Korea [1].
  Intertidal rocky shores [3].
- **Composition (dried):**
  - Calcium about 1,200 mg/100 g (Tokyo survey mean) [5].
  - Iron 6.2 or 58.2 mg/100 g depending on pot (Finding 2) [5].
  - High in fibre, calcium, iron and magnesium [2]; minerals,
    polyunsaturated fats, phlorotannins and fucoidan [1].
- **Edibility:** Eaten only after processing (boiling or steaming, then
  drying) and soaking [3]. Arsenic makes it a special case (Finding 1)
  [2].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Separate male and female plants;
  each oogonium releases one egg, each antheridium 64 sperm [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for hijiki.
- **Yield:**
  - Over 10 kg wet per metre of rope (Oita trials) [1].
  - World production about 304,000 t in 2020 [1] (dry or wet not stated).
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:** Wild seedlings, regrown holdfasts, zygote seedlings or
  tissue culture (Finding 4) [1].
- **Labor:** Not sourced as figures. High labour cost limits Japanese
  farming [1].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Grows on intertidal rock [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried; one source says it keeps for years
  [3], while one retail pack gives about 8 months [3].
- **Establishment:** Seed string wound onto longlines at 1–2 m depth [1].
  Lowering lines to 3 m late in the season delays reproduction and extends
  the season (Korean sargassum culture generally) [1].
- **Cultivars:** Chinese researchers compared two strains (SF-1, SF-2)
  with different vesicle shapes [1]. No named commercial cultivars found.
- **Weather response:** Fouling organisms increased as water warmed in the
  Oita trials [1]. Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine, intertidal [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Spring in Japan [3]. Korean sargassum
  culture harvests in December–January in the first year and
  October–January in the second (all species, not hijiki specifically)
  [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Fouling includes *Ulva*,
  *Ectocarpus*, *Gracilaria*, diatoms and amphipods; controls include acid
  and alkaline dips [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to the Northwest Pacific (China, Korea, Japan) [1]. No invasive
    record was found in this pass.
  - Two close relatives are notable invaders: *S. muticum* in Europe and
    North America, and *S. horneri* in California [4].
  - Australia tests every imported consignment for inorganic arsenic [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Tank nurseries are standard for seedlings
  [1]. Full land-based grow-out: Not sourced.

## Hazards

- **Inorganic arsenic.** The defining hazard (Finding 1) [2]. Inorganic
  arsenic is linked to cancers of the liver, bladder, lung and skin [2].
  The UK agency said occasional past eating was unlikely to have raised
  cancer risk much, but advised stopping [2].
- **Iron content is unreliable** as a nutrition claim (Finding 2) [5].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (Japan):**
  - cut from rocks at low tide [3] →
  - sun-dried [3] →
  - washed, then steamed (Ise method) or boiled raw (Boshu method) [3] →
  - dried again; buds and stems separated into me-hijiki and naga-hijiki
    [3].
- **Kitchen:** Soak 15–20 minutes (buds) or 25–30 minutes (stems) before
  cooking [3]. Repeated parboiling further lowers arsenic (Finding 1) [2].
- **Uses:**
  - Food: simmered side dishes, salads, furikake [3]; in China also
    jelly, yoghurt and vegetable paper [1].
  - Traditional Chinese medicine (*Compendium of Materia Medica*) [1].
  - Raw material for textiles, dyeing and pharmaceuticals, and for
    fucoidan [1].

## Open research questions

- **Safe preparation vs avoidance.** Does a standard preparation bring
  hijiki under a regulatory limit? On the figures found, no (see
  Estimates). Which preparation would the game model?
- **Production basis.** Is the 303,797 t figure wet or dry weight?
- **Japanese wild harvest rules.** Harvest days are set locally (low-tide
  days in Ise-Shima) [3]; who sets them and how wasn't found.
- **Decisions:** whether to keep `akamoku` and `mojaban` as proposed rows.

## Estimates and derivations

**Inorganic arsenic at the Australian 85%-hydration basis.** Inputs: 67–96
mg/kg as sold, 7.9–30.9 mg/kg prepared [2]; limit 1 mg/kg at 85% water
[2]. Assumption: the as-sold figures are close to dry weight. Dry matter at
85% hydration is 15%, so multiply by 0.15. As sold: 67 × 0.15 = 10.1 to
96 × 0.15 = 14.4 mg/kg, about 10–14 times the limit. (A NSW survey used the
same factor: 38 mg/kg dry became 5.7 at 85% hydration [2].) Prepared
figures are probably already hydrated, so they aren't converted; even the
lowest, 7.9 mg/kg, is about 8 times the limit. A measurement of prepared
hijiki at a stated moisture would replace this.

**Iron ratio, about 9 times.** Inputs: 58.2 vs 6.2 mg/100 g [5].
58.2 / 6.2 = 9.4.
