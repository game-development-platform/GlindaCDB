## Naming and scope

This covers *Neochloris oleoabundans*, a single-celled green microalga
studied as a source of oil (triacylglycerol, TAG) [1][2]. It covers the
single cropID `neochloris`. It is a research and pilot-scale organism, not
an established commercial crop (Finding 3).

**Names.**

- **Origin of the name.** *oleoabundans* means "abounding in oil". It was
  named by Chantanachat and Bold in 1962 [1].
- ***Ettlia oleoabundans*.** It is often treated as a synonym, and some
  papers use *Ettlia* as the current name, calling *Neochloris* the former
  one [1]. AlgaeBase-derived references still list it under *Neochloris*
  [1]. Both names are in use.
- **Status-table name.** Updated to *Neochloris oleoabundans* (syn.
  *Ettlia oleoabundans*).

**Relatives flagged:** none proposed.

**Not wild-managed.** It is known from one desert-soil isolate (Finding 1);
all supply is cultured.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an alga from a desert sand dune.**

- **Where it came from.** It was isolated between 1958 and 1962 from the
  top of a sand dune in the Rub' al Khali, Saudi Arabia [1]. That is a
  hyper-arid desert where water comes only as dew, fog and humidity [1].
- **Collection.** Part of a study of algae from desert soils in Saudi
  Arabia, Arizona, Utah, Israel and Mexico [1]. Seawater was one of the
  test media, because some of the soils were old marine deposits [1].
- **Type culture.** The original strain is UTEX 1185, held at the
  University of Texas [1].
- **Cells.** Spherical, about 3–6 µm, with a thin smooth wall [1]. It
  reproduces asexually by zoospores or aplanospores; sex hasn't been seen
  [1].
- **Why it matters.** A soil alga from desert sand, which could fit a
  desert or salt-water setting (inference).

**Finding 2 — makes oil when starved of nitrogen, in fresh or salt
water.**

- **Oil content.** Under nitrogen deprivation it makes 35–55% of dry
  weight as total lipids [2], and up to 80% of those lipids can be TAG [2].
  TAG reaches up to 44% of dry weight [2].
- **The trade-off.** Long nitrogen starvation gives high oil content but
  low biomass growth; more nitrogen gives faster growth and less oil [3].
  The best industrial point lies between [3].
- **Composition shift.** Protein was about 38% of dry weight when nitrogen
  was plentiful, and about 19% when limited [2].
- **Salt tolerant.** It grows in fresh water and in salt water, which can
  cut production costs [2]. It copes with salt using proline and an
  antioxidant (ascorbate-glutathione) pathway [2].
- **Genetic engineering.** One modified strain raised total lipid to
  about 79% of dry weight [2].
- **Why it matters.** Its oil is the kind that becomes biodiesel or food
  oil. That differs from *Botryococcus*, whose hydrocarbons can't be
  turned into biodiesel (inference, from `botryococcus.md`).

**Finding 3 — pilot-scale oil production only.**

- **Spanish pilot.** A 6 m³ horizontal tubular reactor at Matalascañas,
  southern Spain, ran from 22 October to 7 November, funded by Neste [3].
  - Biomass productivity was 7.4 g dry weight/m² a day while nitrogen was
    supplied [3].
  - Lipid reached 39% of biomass, and lipid productivity averaged 2.0
    g/m² a day after nitrogen was cut [3].
- **Lab maximum.** TAG productivity up to 164 mg/L a day [2].
- **Other uses.** Feed for rearing freshwater mussels [4], and a patented
  cosmetic slimming extract [4].
- **Why it matters.** Like most oil algae, the biology works but the
  economics haven't (inference).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Single spherical cells [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Two-stage batch: grow with nitrogen, then starve to
  build oil [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable.
- **Climate zones:** Isolated from hot desert [1]. The Spanish pilot ran in
  autumn at a site with summer highs rarely above 35 °C [3].
- **Composition:** Lipid 35–55% of dry weight when starved; protein about
  38% when not [2].
- **Edibility:** Not sourced as human food.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Outdoor oil yield is hard to optimise
  because light varies with day and cloud [3].
- **Yield:** 7.4 g/m² a day biomass; 2.0 g/m² a day lipid (pilot) [3].
- **Water use:** Fresh or salt water [2].
- **Propagation:** From culture stock (UTEX 1185) [1].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable; originally a soil alga [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Starting a reactor at low density risks photoinhibition
  [3].
- **Cultivars:** UTEX 1185 is the standard strain [1].
- **Weather response:** Photoinhibition risk outdoors [3].
- **Water regime:** Fresh to salt water [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Harvest after the nitrogen-starvation
  phase [3].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** No record found.
- **Controlled environment:** Tubular photobioreactors [3].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** reactor culture with nitrogen → nitrogen starvation → harvest
  → lipid extraction → biodiesel (fatty acid methyl esters) or oil [2][3].
- **Uses:** Biofuel research [2][3]; mussel feed [4]; cosmetics [4].

## Open research questions

- **Accepted name** (*Neochloris* vs *Ettlia*) [1].
- **Food or feed safety:** Not sourced.
- **Year-round outdoor yield:** The pilot ran only about two weeks [3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Pilot yields scaled to a year.** Inputs: 7.4 g/m² a day biomass and 2.0
g/m² a day lipid [3]. 7.4 × 10,000 × 365 ≈ 27 t biomass/ha a year; 2.0 ×
10,000 × 365 ≈ 7.3 t lipid/ha a year. Both assume the autumn rates hold all
year, and the two phases can't both run at once, so real yields would be
lower (inference).
