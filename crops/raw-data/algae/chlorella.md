## Naming and scope

This covers chlorella, the single-celled freshwater green algae sold
dried as a health food, chiefly *Chlorella vulgaris* and products
labelled *C. pyrenoidosa* [1]. It covers the single cropID `chlorella`.

**The name on the label often isn't the organism.**

- **A grab-bag genus.** Since *C. vulgaris* was described in 1890, many
  small round green algae were put in *Chlorella* on looks alone [1].
  DNA work shows the old genus is polyphyletic, spread across two classes
  [1]. One analysis kept only four species in *Chlorella* proper: *C.
  vulgaris*, *C. sorokiniana*, *C. kessleri* and *C. lobophora* [1].
- ***C. pyrenoidosa*** is listed by AlgaeBase only as a synonym, yet most
  products that give a full species name say *C. pyrenoidosa* [1]. Culture
  strains once labelled *C. pyrenoidosa* have been renamed after
  sequencing, for example to *Auxenochlorella protothecoides* [1].
- **EU food status.** *C. pyrenoidosa*, *C. vulgaris* and *C. luteoviridis*
  are listed as eaten in Europe before 1997, so they aren't "novel foods"
  [1]. But *C. luteoviridis* is now *Heterochlorella luteoviridis*, and
  some strains were misidentified [1].
- **Consequence.** The row stays at genus level, as queued. Figures below
  come from mixed or uncertain strains.

**Relatives flagged:** `spirulina` (RESEARCHED) — the other farmed
"microalga" in this category, though a cyanobacterium; already
cross-linked. No new rows proposed.

**Supply is farmed** (Finding 1), so this is not a wild-managed resource.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — Japan and Taiwan built the industry, first in round ponds,
then in fermenters.**

- **Origins.** After the Second World War, chlorella was promoted as a
  cheap protein to end world hunger [2]. The first outdoor pilot plant ran
  in 1951 at Arthur D. Little in Cambridge, Massachusetts [2]. But growing
  it at food scale proved too costly, and it settled into a niche
  supplement market [2].
- **Commercial start.** The first commercial plant opened in Japan around
  1960, and Taiwan followed in 1964 [2]. By 1980 there were 46 large
  factories in Asia each producing over 1 t a month, mainly of chlorella
  [2].
- **Ponds.** Classic culture uses round, shallow concrete ponds 36–50 m
  across, stirred by a rotating arm [2]. Yields rise with added CO₂,
  acetic acid or glucose [2]. A Japanese company's plant in East Java has
  16 such ponds [2].
- **Fermenters.** From the late 1970s, Japanese and Taiwanese producers
  grew chlorella in the dark in stainless-steel tanks, fed acetate or
  glucose [2]. Combining fermenters with lit tubular reactors can give
  yields about ten times higher than ponds [2].
- **Scale.** About 2,000 t dry of chlorella powder traded in Japan in 1996,
  including 900 t from Taiwan; Taiwan's total was about 1,600 t [2]. Over
  70 Japanese companies sold chlorella health food [2].
- **Why it matters.** Unlike most crops, it can be grown without light, on
  sugar, in a factory.

**Finding 2 — the wall has to be broken, or the nutrients pass through.**

- **The wall.** Chlorella has a tough cell wall that people can't digest,
  so most supplements break the cells mechanically [3]. The wall includes
  sporopollenin, a resistant polymer, and sugars [2].
- **Effect.** One retailer cites digestibility of about 35% with intact
  walls against 70–85% after mechanical breaking [3] (low weight:
  retailer's summary of a study).
- **Why it matters.** Processing, not just growing, sets the food value;
  a model could add a "cell-wall cracking" step.

**Finding 3 — a 1977 skin-rash outbreak led to a legal limit on a
pigment breakdown product.**

- **Outbreak.** In 1977 at least 23 people around Tokyo developed
  photosensitivity dermatitis (a sunlight-triggered rash) after taking one
  brand of chlorella tablets [4].
- **Cause.** The tablets held large amounts of pheophorbide, a
  light-sensitising breakdown product of chlorophyll [4]. Ethanol used to
  granulate the powder had activated the enzyme chlorophyllase, which made
  the pheophorbide [4].
- **Limit.** Japan set limits of 100 mg pheophorbide per 100 g, or 160 mg
  "total" pheophorbide (including what the enzyme can still make in three
  hours at 37 °C) [4]. The EU had set no limit at the time of the source
  [4].
- **Storage matters.** In tablets kept 30 days at 30 °C and 75% humidity,
  pheophorbide rose 2–10 times, in proportion to leftover enzyme
  activity; light had little effect [4].
- **Why it matters.** A safety hazard created by processing and storage,
  not by the alga itself.

**Finding 4 — unlike spirulina, chlorella products carry B12 and vitamin
D2.**

- **Protein.** About 59% of dry weight in commercial products [5]; 43–58%
  for *C. vulgaris* depending on growing conditions [5].
- **Vitamins.** A 2020 review found commercial chlorella products contain
  substantial vitamin B12 and vitamin D2, both absent from plants, and
  about 2.5 mg folate per 100 g dry [5].
- **Conflict.** Some secondary sources say chlorella's B12 is mostly an
  inactive form [5] (low weight); the review measured it as B12. Kept as an
  open question.
- **Vitamin K.** High vitamin K content may interfere with warfarin and
  similar anticoagulants [5] (low weight: retailer sources; no clinical
  study found).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Single cells that reproduce asexually by
  splitting into 2–32 daughter cells (autospores) [5]. Pond production in
  Japan ran summer and winter using different strains [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous or batch culture, harvesting the cells
  from ponds or tanks [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable; culture is continuous.
  Days per batch: Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Ponds outdoors in Japan, Taiwan and Indonesia [2];
  fermenters anywhere (climate-independent) [2].
- **Composition:** Protein 43–65%; fat about 11–22%; carbohydrate about
  17%, more than 65% of it fibre from the cell wall [5].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as tablets or powder, usually with the cell walls
  broken [3].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable (asexual) [5].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not applicable in fermenters (grows in the
  dark on sugar) [2]. Pond light response: Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Early Japanese ponds gave about 240–500 kg dry per acre a
  month in 1959 [2] (see Estimates). Modern pond yields: Not sourced.
- **Water use:** Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Inoculation from a starter culture [2].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dry, cool and low-humidity, because heat and
  humidity raise pheophorbide (Finding 3) [4].
- **Establishment:** Ponds supplied with CO₂ (about 5% in air through the
  rotating arms in early Japanese ponds, of which only about a third was
  absorbed) [2].
- **Cultivars:** Proprietary strains; species identity often uncertain
  (Naming) [1].
- **Weather response:** Not sourced for ponds.
- **Water regime:** Freshwater culture [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** A common freshwater alga; marine-looking
  strains of *C. vulgaris* also occur [1]. No invasive record was found in
  this pass.
- **Controlled environment:** Fully proven: heterotrophic fermenters and
  photobioreactors [2].

## Hazards

- **Pheophorbide photosensitivity** (Finding 3) [4].
- **Vitamin K and anticoagulants** (Finding 4) [5] (low weight).
- **Species identity.** Products may not contain the species named [1].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (pond):** culture in round ponds with CO₂ → harvest cells →
  break cell walls → dry → tablets or powder [2][3].
- **Chain (fermenter):** dark culture on glucose or acetate in steel tanks
  → harvest → process as above [2].
- **Processing hazard:** Avoid ethanol granulation without enzyme
  inactivation (Finding 3) [4].
- **Uses:** Health-food tablets and powder [2]; research model (Melvin
  Calvin's Nobel-winning photosynthesis work used chlorella) [2].

## Estimates and derivations

**1959 Japanese pond yield, about 6–12 t dry/ha a year.** Inputs: 240–500
kg dry per acre a month [2]. 1 acre = 0.4047 ha. 240 / 0.4047 × 12 = 7.1
t/ha; 500 / 0.4047 × 12 = 14.8 t/ha. The monthly figures were for
summer, so a full-year average would be lower; call it about 6–12 t/ha
(rough).
