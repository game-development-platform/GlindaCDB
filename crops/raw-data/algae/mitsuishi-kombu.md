## Naming and scope

This covers *Saccharina angustata* (Kjellman) C.E. Lane, C. Mayes, Druehl &
G.W. Saunders, a brown kelp of Hokkaido's Pacific coast [1]. In Japanese it
is ミツイシコンブ (Mitsuishi kombu), sold as 日高昆布 (Hidaka kombu) [1][2].
It was formerly *Laminaria angustata* [1]. It covers the single cropID
`mitsuishi_kombu`, promoted from PROPOSED (flagged in `kelp-kombu.md`).

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-15).**

- **A separate species, not a kombu variety.** A study using 13 nuclear
  microsatellites and a mitochondrial marker found strong genetic
  differentiation between *S. angustata* and *S. japonica* (FST > 0.25),
  with little hybridization even where they overlap [1]. A genome
  resequencing study places *S. angustata* and *S. longissima* (naga kombu)
  in a sister lineage to *S. japonica* [1].
- **The 2026 merger doesn't touch it.** The 2026 Hokkaido University study
  that folded Rausu, Rishiri and Hosome kombu into *S. japonica* covered
  only those varieties [1].
- **Grows and matures differently.** In a side-by-side culture trial, it
  formed sori later and in a different part of the blade than ma-kombu. It
  also stored carbon and nitrogen more evenly along the blade [1]. The
  authors judged it more distantly related than the *S. japonica*
  varieties [1].
- **Used differently** (Finding 2) and **harvested only from the wild**
  (Finding 1).

**Relatives flagged:**

- `kelp_kombu` (*S. japonica*): the main farmed kombu; cross-linked.
- `naga_kombu` (*S. longissima*): its sister species [1]; already PROPOSED,
  now cross-linked.

**Supply is wild-managed** (Finding 1). Saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/mitsuishi-kombu.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — wild-harvested only, and declining.**

- **No farming in Hidaka.** Hidaka sells only wild kombu; no aquaculture is
  practised there [2].
- **Where.** The main producing areas are Hidaka and Tokachi [2]. The
  Erimo fishery co-op alone produces about 60% of Hidaka kombu [2].
- **Two harvests** [2]:
  - **Tori-kombu (採りコンブ):** cut from the sea bed in July to October.
  - **Hiroi-kombu (拾いコンブ):** beach-cast kombu picked up year-round.
  - The harvest goes out only on days with good weather and sea [2].
- **Decline.** Over 20 years, boats fell 31% and production 45%, to 1,979
  t in 2022 [2]. The main cause given is too few people to dry the harvest
  [2].
- **Management.** Clearing other seaweeds from the rock helps zoospores
  settle, so it is best done before the kombu releases them [2]. The
  research station has monitored stocks at Hiroo monthly since 2014 [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop of the commons: the sea bed is not planted,
  but it is weeded, watched and harvested in seasons.

**Finding 2 — the soft, everyday kombu.**

- **Texture.** Soft, quick-cooking, and tender when simmered [3]. Thinner
  and softer than Rishiri or ma-kombu [3].
- **Uses.** Simmered dishes (nimono), kombu rolls (kobumaki), tsukudani
  (kombu cooked in soy and sugar) and dashi [2][3]. Kombu rolls are a New
  Year dish [3].
- **Shape.** A long, narrow ribbon, typically 7–8 m [3]. Its blade and
  midrib are narrower than ma-kombu's [1].
- **Price.** The cheapest of the four main Hokkaido kombu [3].
- **Umami.** One retailer, citing the Umami Information Center, gives 1,344
  mg/100 g of umami content for Hidaka, against 1,985 for Rishiri and 2,286
  for Rausu [3]. Treat this as low weight.
- **Why it matters.** Kombu you eat, not only steep. It's a different
  in-game product from dashi kombu (inference).

**Finding 3 — the beach-drying economy.**

- **Sun-dried on the shore.** In summer, the harvest is laid out on the
  Hidaka beaches to dry in sun and sea wind [2]. Drying draws on family,
  relatives and other local people [2].
- **Labour helpers.** Shore workers (陸廻り, *okamawari*) lay out each
  blade so none overlap [3]. Since 2022, prefectural staff can take side
  jobs helping with drying [2].
- **Graded and sold jointly.** Kombu is sold through a joint-sales system
  run by the Hokkaido fisheries federation since 1954 [2]. Drying and
  sorting are labour-heavy and margins are low [2].
- **Beach grades.** One Urakawa beach, Ikantai, is said to be the only one
  in Hidaka graded "special top beach" (特上浜) [3]. This comes from a
  seller listing.
- **Why it matters.** Turns harvest into a village labour event, with
  quality tied to beach and weather.

**Finding 4 — vulnerable to marine heat.**

- **2023 heatwave.** In late August 2023 water at Urakawa reached 25.9 °C,
  5.5 °C above average [2]. In September there were reports of large-scale
  loss, with kombu stripped off the rock [2].
  - Feeding damage from a root-boring isopod (コンブノネクイムシ) was
    found, but not confirmed as the cause [2].
  - Maturation was delayed, and the next generation's juveniles appeared
    about two months late, in May 2024 [2].
- **Temperature tolerances.** Net photosynthesis peaked at 16 °C, and
  maximum quantum yield at about 7–8.4 °C [4]. Gross photosynthesis peaked
  at 21.2 °C [4]. Hokkaido is near its southern limit [4].
- **Range shift.** Niche models project both it and *S. japonica* moving
  north under climate change [1].
- **Why it matters.** A climate-sensitive wild stock.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelp. First- and second-year plants
  dominate the fishing grounds [2]. Third-year plants are possible [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Seasonal cut harvest (July to October), plus
  year-round beach-cast collection [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced. Age at harvest not stated.
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate Pacific coast of Hokkaido [1][2].
- **Composition:** One label gives 7.7 g protein, 1.9 g fat and 64.7 g
  carbohydrate per 100 g dried, with 7.6 g salt equivalent [3]. Low weight.
- **Edibility:** Edible; a cooking kombu (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Releases zoospores, mainly September to
  November [2].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** 1,979 t in 2022 [2]; per area not sourced.
- **Water use:** Marine.
- **Propagation:** Natural zoospore settlement; cleared rock helps [2].
- **Labor:** High for drying and sorting (Finding 3) [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Rocky shore [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Sun-dried; stored away from light in a dry,
  cool place [3].
- **Establishment:** Juveniles normally appear in winter [2].
- **Cultivars:** None; wild stock. Local beaches graded by quality [3].
- **Weather response:** Harvests only in calm, fine weather; heat waves
  cause losses (Findings 1, 4) [2].
- **Water regime:** Marine, rocky subtidal.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Sori form around July on second-year plants
  and October on first-year plants [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Root-boring isopods (Finding 4) [2].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native; no record found.
- **Controlled environment:** None. Experimental culture only, in Miyagi
  [1].

## Hazards

- **Iodine.** Not sourced for this species; see `kelp-kombu.md` for kombu
  iodine generally.
- **Grit.** Sun-dried kombu may carry sand or small stones; wipe before use
  [3].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** cut from boats or picked from the beach → sun-dried on the
  shore → sorted and graded → joint sale through the co-ops [2][3].
- **Products:** Whole dried kombu, root kombu (ne-kombu) [3], tsukudani,
  kombu rolls, dashi [2][3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Erimo co-op output, about 1,200 t a year.** Inputs: Hidaka kombu output
1,979 t in 2022 [2]; Erimo co-op produces about 60% [2]. 1,979 × 0.6 ≈
1,190 t. The two figures may be from different years.
