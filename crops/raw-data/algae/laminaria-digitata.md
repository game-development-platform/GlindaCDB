# Oarweed (*Laminaria digitata*)

## Naming and scope

This covers *Laminaria digitata* (Hudson) J.V. Lamouroux, a brown alga
(order Laminariales, family Laminariaceae) known in English as oarweed or
tangle [3][7]. An older synonym is *L. flexicaulis* [4]. This doc covers
the single cropID `laminaria_digitata`.

Unlike the two *Saccharina* kelps already researched, this is essentially
a **wild-harvested crop**. The commercial supply is mechanically harvested
from natural beds, mainly in Brittany. Cultivation exists only at trial and
pilot scale [1][2].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **Tangle / cuvie (*Laminaria hyperborea*).** Proposed as
  `laminaria_hyperborea`. It is the other mechanically harvested
  *Laminaria* (trawled in Norway, combed in Brittany), taking about 30% of
  Brittany's kelp landings [1]. It yields a gelling alginate rather than
  *L. digitata*'s thickening/emulsifying one [1]. An older FAO account places
  it in more open water than *L. digitata* [1]. In Norway it is managed on a 5-year
  harvest-rotation cycle [2].
- ***Laminaria ochroleuca*.** Proposed as `laminaria_ochroleuca`. A
  warmer-water southern relative sold as "kombu" in Europe; one iodine
  survey analyses "European kombu" under that name [4].
- **Sugar kelp (`sugar_kelp`).** Already researched. It is co-cultivated
  with *L. digitata* in Irish and Shetland trials [2] and cross-linked.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the raw material of the French alginate industry, harvested
wild with a hydraulic hook.**

- **Landings.** Brittany's mechanised kelp harvest is capped at 35 vessels
  and lands 60,000–70,000 t a year, 70% of it *L. digitata* [1]. That is
  roughly 42,000–49,000 t of this species (see Estimates). One source cites
  total landings dictated by the processing industries at about 47,000 t
  [1]. A global figure of about 45,000 t wild harvest in 2016 [1] fits
  Brittany being almost all of it.
- **Gear.** Since the 1970s, the harvest uses 7–12 m boats fitted with a
  *scoubidou*, a curved iron hook on a hydraulic arm that twists the kelp
  and tears it from the rock [1]. Harvest runs mainly in spring and summer,
  May to September [1].
- **Buyers.** Two processing plants near Brest, owned by foreign industrial
  groups, buy most of France's *Laminaria* [1].
- **Landing trends.** Brittany landings fell from about 60,000 to about
  50,000 t a year over one decade. Analysis attributed this to reduced
  harvesting effort, not a smaller stock [1]. More recently, a trade-press
  report described a "disastrous" season: boats from Lanildut (normally
  40,000–55,000 t, May–September) returned with half their usual loads
  after yields fell for 3–4 years [1]. That report was read only at
  snippet depth, without a firm date or cause.

**Finding 2 — an extreme iodine accumulator, and the organism behind a
discovery in atmospheric chemistry.**

- **Content.** *L. digitata* concentrates iodine more than 30,000 times
  above seawater, often averaging about 1% of dry weight [3]. A survey of
  eight European populations found [3]:
  - Highest levels in late autumn and winter, at 0.75–1.20% dry weight.
  - Lowest levels in summer, at 0.25–0.60%.
  - Young plantlets near 5%.

  Another study ranged from 0.4% in adults to 4.7% in young plantlets
  [3]. The outer cortex of the stipe reached 57 mg/g dry weight [3]. Those
  figures are 2,500–12,000 mg/kg in adults, above sugar kelp's
  1,556–7,208 mg/kg (see `sugar_kelp`).
- **Function.** In 2008, researchers showed the iodide works as an
  *inorganic antioxidant* protecting the frond surface. This was the first
  such antioxidant described in a living system [3]. When exposed at low
  tide, especially with ozone, the plant releases molecular iodine. That
  iodine forms iodine-oxide aerosol particles, so oarweed beds measurably
  affect coastal atmospheric chemistry [3].
- **Other roles.** Iodine also helps the plant acclimatise to salinity
  changes [3]. Bromide complements iodide as an antioxidant [3].

**Finding 3 — historically the iodine and soda crop.** Brittany's
*L. digitata* was gathered in the mid-19th century for the iodine industry
[1]. Earlier, burned brown seaweed ("kelp" or *varech* ash) was a main
source of soda for glass, soap, pottery and textiles, before the Leblanc
process [5]. In 1811 Bernard Courtois discovered iodine in the mother
liquor of seaweed ash, from brown algae including *Fucus* and *Laminaria*
[5]. An 1874 account of French production describes a supply chain of
540,000 t of green seaweed yielding 40 t of pure iodine, alongside potash
and other salts [5]. That is about 0.007% of fresh weight recovered as
iodine (see Estimates). The source covers mixed seaweed, not *L. digitata*
alone.

On Orkney, sheep were confined to the shore once kelp burning became
uneconomical, giving rise to the seaweed-eating North Ronaldsay sheep
(Finding 5) [6].

**Finding 4 — a cold-water kelp at its southern limit in Brittany, with
little heat-tolerance variation to fall back on.**

- **Range.** It occurs from Spitsbergen (79°N) and Greenland south to
  Brittany (Quiberon, 47°N) [7].
- **Upper limits.** Juvenile sporophytes survive up to about 23 °C for
  seven days, or 21 °C for 14 days [7]. A range-wide common-garden
  experiment found 23 °C was a growth limit for every population [7].
- **Local variation.** It was modest: southern-edge Quiberon plants grew
  better at 19 °C and recovered better from 21 °C. Researchers concluded
  that this local differentiation is "likely too weak" to protect the
  species against warming and marine heatwaves at its southern edge [7].
- **Genetics.** Diversity was highest at the southern limit and lowest on
  isolated Helgoland. All populations were genetically distinct, splitting
  into southern and northern clades [7].
- **Market expectation.** A trade report gives its comfortable range as
  roughly 10–15 °C [1].

**Finding 5 — a livestock feed with an arsenic story attached.** The North
Ronaldsay sheep of Orkney live year-round on the shore below a
1.8 m dyke and subsist almost entirely on seaweed, mainly *L. digitata* and
*L. hyperborea* [6]. They eat up to about 3 kg a day and graze with the
tides [6].

- **Arsenic intake.** These two kelps had the highest arsenic of all the
  seaweeds the sheep ate, at 74 ± 4 mg/kg dry mass, mostly arsenosugars.
  Ewes took in about 35 mg of arsenic a day [6]. Urinary excretion was
  dominated by dimethylarsinic acid (DMA) [6].
- **Ordinary sheep.** Blackface sheep given free choice ate on average
  20 ± 9% *L. digitata*, and metabolised the arsenic the same way [6].
- **Copper.** The breed's adaptation to low-copper seaweed means North
  Ronaldsay sheep moved onto grass have died of copper poisoning [6].

**Finding 6 — farmable, but cultivation has stalled at pilot scale.**
Ireland's Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM) grew *L. digitata* "very successfully"
on longlines in Roaring Water Bay. It described that winter's harvest as
the first pilot-scale one in Europe, and published a full hatchery manual
[2]. A Dutch model based on that manual describes the cycle as 3–5 months
in the hatchery, deployment at sea in late autumn, and 5–6 months of
grow-out to a spring harvest [2].

Economics were marginal. An Irish business plan for a 100 t/year longline
unit found the lowest break-even price was €1.12/kg wet weight, using an
existing mussel site, and called even that "on the high side" [2].

In a Shetland comparison on longlines, *L. digitata* grew less than sugar
kelp and was more heavily fouled. The authors judged sugar kelp the better
candidate for partial-cut harvesting [2]. Denmark was reported as the only
European country cultivating *Laminaria* at one point [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial marine kelp with a heteromorphic life
  cycle (large diploid sporophyte, microscopic gametophytes) [2][8].
  Growth follows a seasonal pattern: rapid length growth in spring and
  early summer, then decay in late summer and autumn [2]. On Helgoland,
  that seasonality is reported to be governed by a circannual rhythm. This
  was seen only as a cited paper title (Schaffelke & Lüning 1994), not
  read [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Wild: repeated mechanised harvest of natural beds in
  a May–September season, under licence and quota [1]. Cultivated:
  single spring harvest [2]. Partial cutting above the meristem was trialled
  but suited sugar kelp better [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Cultivated: about 8–11 months from
  zoospore to spring harvest (3–5 months hatchery plus 5–6 months at sea)
  [2]. Sporogenesis can be induced in plants as young as 7.5 months, with
  first sori after 5 weeks [8]. Natural populations take about 18 months to
  first sori, against 8.6 months in lab mass culture [8]. Wild regrowth
  interval after harvest: not sourced for *L. digitata*.
- **Climate zones:** Arctic to cold-temperate NE Atlantic, from Spitsbergen
  and Greenland to Brittany [7]. Temperature limits are in Finding 4 [7].
  Chill-hours/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
  Depth plays that role: it grows from the low-tide level into the upper
  sublittoral [1][3], and an older FAO account says it prefers calmer zones
  and gives way to *L. hyperborea* in more open areas [1].
- **Composition:**
  - **By season.** It swings strongly with the season [4]. In April
    (Danish Baltic), alginate and ash were each about 30% of dry weight,
    with 10% protein. In August (North Sea), glucose, mostly from
    laminarin, made up 51% of dry weight and alginate only 16%.
  - **Family ranges.** Reported ranges for Laminariaceae: alginate 17–45%,
    fucoidan 3–10%, laminarin 0–33%, mannitol 2–20% (*L. digitata* and
    *S. latissima*), ash 15–39%, protein 3–16%, lipids under 2% [4].
  - **Protein.** Swedish *L. digitata* had 5–12% protein, with mannitol and
    protein both peaking in autumn [4]. Scottish *L. digitata* ranged
    6.8–14.3% crude protein over the season [4].
  - **Iodine.** 0.25–1.2% dry weight in adults (Finding 2) [3].

  No primary food-database entry was located.
- **Edibility:** Edible (sold dried as a food and "functional" ingredient)
  [2]. Its iodine content is higher than sugar kelp's, so the iodine-excess
  concern (see `sugar_kelp` and `kelp_kombu`) applies even more strongly.
  No species-specific consumer guidance or iodine-reduction study was found
  in this pass.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sexual reproduction is via microscopic
  gametophytes [8].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Unlike sugar kelp, *L. digitata* formed sori
  under various light regimes and temperatures. It did not need short days
  [8]. Lab conditions brought sporogenesis forward by about 5 months [8].
  No photoperiod effect on yield was found.
- **Yield:** Wild: about 42,000–49,000 t a year from Brittany (see
  Estimates) [1]. Old hand-harvest productivity was at least about 2 t per
  harvester per day by boat on the mainland [1]. Cultivated: no per-metre
  or per-hectare yield was found in this pass. The Irish model assumes a
  100 t/year longline unit [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine crop).
- **Propagation:** Sexual, from sori collected on the shore. Hatcheries use
  either flask cultivation of gametophytes or direct spore settlement onto
  nylon seed string (35 m of 1.3 mm string per spool in the Shetland trial)
  [2]. Wild beds regenerate naturally after harvest [1]. No clonal or viral
  degeneration risk applies.
- **Labor:** Wild mechanised harvest is licensed per vessel (maximum 35
  boats in Brittany, with a €100 fee and statistical declarations required)
  [1]. Historical hand harvest: about 2 t per harvester-day [1]. The Dutch
  economic model includes hatchery and at-sea labour sheets, but the
  figures weren't extracted [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable (attaches to rock). Salinity: no tolerance range
  was sourced. Iodine contributes to osmotic acclimatisation [3]. One
  composition sample came from the Danish Baltic Sea [4], so it does occur
  in reduced-salinity water.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced specifically for food use. Wild
  harvest goes mainly to alginate plants [1]. Shetland trial material was
  kept cold in seawater and processed immediately [2]. Ensiling has been
  tested as a pre-treatment for pyrolysis [9].
- **Establishment:** Cultivated: seeded string deployed on longlines in late
  autumn [2]. Wild: natural recruitment.
- **Cultivars:** None. Populations are genetically distinct, with
  southern and northern clades (Finding 4) [7].
- **Weather response:** Heat is the limiting stress (Finding 4) [7]. Low-tide
  emersion triggers iodine release (Finding 2) [3]. Frost: not sourced.
- **Water regime:** Marine. Grows from the low intertidal to the upper
  subtidal, so it is exposed at extreme low tides [1][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Wild harvest May–September [1]. Cultivated
  spring harvest [2]. Iodine is lowest in summer, so the harvest season
  coincides with the lowest-iodine period [3] (an observation, not a
  sourced management rationale).
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. It was more heavily fouled than sugar
  kelp in culture [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Hatchery stage only, 3–5 months [2]. Lab
  sporogenesis acceleration is described in Photoperiod [8]. No full
  closed-system cultivation found.

## Hazards

- **Iodine excess.** Higher than sugar kelp at 0.25–1.2% dry weight in
  adults and up to about 5% in plantlets [3]. No species-specific consumer
  limits or processing guidance were found. **TO BE ADDRESSED** alongside
  the other kelps.
- **Arsenic.** 74 ± 4 mg/kg dry mass (combined with *L. hyperborea*),
  mostly as organic arsenosugars [6]. These are metabolised by ruminants
  to DMA and other methylated species [6]. Inorganic arsenic was not
  quantified in this pass.
- **Livestock copper interaction.** Not a toxin in the kelp itself. Animals
  adapted to a low-copper seaweed diet are at risk of copper poisoning when
  switched to grass [6]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — this project has no place
  yet for livestock-safety data.
- **Pyrolysis char.** Chars from ensiled kelp, including cultivated
  *L. digitata*, harmed lettuce germination and growth as soil amendments
  [9].
- **Harvest pressure and climate.** Wild-stock sustainability is regulated.
  The recent landing decline is attributed to effort, not stock [1], but
  heat at the southern edge is a long-term risk (Finding 4) [7].
- No fungal, ergot-type or disease outbreak with quantified severity was
  found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (alginate, main use):** wild kelp hooked by boat → landed at
  Breton ports (Lanildut) → processing plants near Brest → alginate
  extraction → thickeners and emulsifiers for cosmetics, pharmaceuticals
  and food [1]. *L. hyperborea* alginate is the gelling grade [1].
- **Chain (historical soda and iodine):** dried seaweed → burned in shore
  pits → kelp ash (soda, potash salts) → glass and soap. Mother liquor →
  sulfuric acid → iodine [5].
- **Food:** dried and packaged as a functional ingredient, marketed (by an
  Irish source) against cellulite and obesity, at €10–16/kg bulk [2].
- **Animal feed:** grazed directly by North Ronaldsay sheep, and taken
  voluntarily as about 20% of diet by other breeds [6].
- **Biomass and char:** ensiled and pyrolysed (thermo-catalytic reforming),
  with poor results as a soil amendment [9].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** biostimulant and fertiliser products,
  fucoidan and laminarin extraction, and bioethanol from the
  laminarin-rich August biomass [4].

## Open research questions

- **Cultivated yield.** No sourced per-metre or per-hectare yield figure for
  *L. digitata*.
- **Recent Brittany harvest collapse.** The trade report of half loads is
  undated and its cause is unconfirmed.
- **Food-use iodine processing.** No species-specific study of blanching or
  processing for iodine reduction was found.
- **Inorganic arsenic and cadmium.** Not quantified for this species.
- **Salinity tolerance.** No tolerance range was found.
- **Circannual rhythm.** The growth-rhythm claim (Schaffelke & Lüning 1994)
  was seen only as a cited title.
- **Wild-bed regrowth.** The recovery interval after scoubidou harvest
  wasn't sourced. Brittany uses some form of fallow ("jachère") system [1],
  not detailed.
- **Labour figures.** Not extracted from the Dutch model.
- **Proposed rows.** Whether `laminaria_hyperborea` and
  `laminaria_ochroleuca` should become full rows.

## Estimates and derivations

**Brittany *L. digitata* landings, about 42,000–49,000 t/year.** Inputs:
60,000–70,000 t/year of kelp landings, 70% *L. digitata* [1]. Arithmetic:
60,000 × 0.70 = 42,000 t; 70,000 × 0.70 = 49,000 t. Cross-checks:

- The cited industry-dictated total of about 47,000 t [1] falls in this
  range.
- So does the 2016 global wild harvest of about 45,000 t [1].
- One regional source gives a lower 40,000 t of *L. digitata* (with
  20,000 t *L. hyperborea*) [1].
- The decade figures of 60,000 to 50,000 t [1] look like *L. digitata*
  alone. They sit above this range, so the sources may be counting
  different years or definitions.

Plausible range: 40,000–60,000 t. Official annual landing statistics would
replace it.

**Historical iodine recovery, about 0.007% of fresh seaweed.** Inputs:
540,000 t green seaweed and 40 t pure iodine [5]. Arithmetic:
40 / 540,000 = 0.0000741 = 0.0074%. This is for mixed seaweeds, not
*L. digitata* alone. It is far below the plant's actual iodine content of
about 1% dry weight [3]. With a dry-matter fraction of about 10–20% of
fresh weight (not sourced for this species), that would be about
0.1–0.2% of fresh weight. The difference suggests most of the iodine was
lost, partly through volatilisation during burning [3]. That last point is
an inference, not a sourced claim.
