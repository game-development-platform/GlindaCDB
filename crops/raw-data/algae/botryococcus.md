## Naming and scope

This covers *Botryococcus braunii*, a colonial freshwater green alga that
makes liquid hydrocarbon oils [1][2]. It covers the single cropID
`botryococcus`. It is a research and pilot-scale organism, not an
established commercial crop (Finding 2).

**Names and the 2024 split.**

- **Three chemical races.** It has long been treated as one species with
  three races, told apart by the oils they make [1]:
  - **Race A:** fatty-acid-derived C23–C33 alkadienes and trienes [1].
  - **Race B:** triterpenoid botryococcenes (C30–C37) and methylated
    squalenes [1].
  - **Race L:** the tetraterpenoid lycopadiene (C40) [1].
- **Proposed split (2024).** A genomics study found roughly 20–30% genetic
  difference between the races and proposed three species [1]:
  - *Botryococcus alkenealis* (race A)
  - *Botryococcus braunii* (race B, keeps the name)
  - *Botryococcus lycopadienor* (race L)
- **Not yet settled.** The authors themselves say a species is really
  defined by general acceptance in the field [1].
- **Status-table name.** Kept as *Botryococcus braunii*, covering all
  three races for now. Whether to split the row is left as an open
  decision (Open research questions).

**Relatives flagged:** none proposed.

**Not wild-managed.** Wild densities are normally very low (10–100 colonies
per L) [1], and no wild harvest was found.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the oil alga, and the source of some oil shales.**

- **Hydrocarbon content.** Oils make up 25–75% of dry weight [1]. Race B
  reaches up to 86% (botryococcenes), race A up to 61%, and race L only up
  to 8% [1].
- **Stored outside the cells.** The oils sit mainly in the extracellular
  matrix of the colony [1].
- **Not biodiesel.** The oils are hydrocarbons, not fats, so they can't be
  made into biodiesel by transesterification [1]. Race B oil suits
  hydrocracking into gasoline-type fuel [1].
- **In the rocks.** Torbanite (boghead coal, "kerosene shale") is made of
  fossil colonies of an alga very like *B. braunii* [3]. Coorongite, a
  brown rubbery combustible deposit found in South Australia's Coorong,
  is its dried, partly decayed remains [3]. Botryococcane, its fossil
  marker, makes up as much as 90% of the organic carbon in some shale oils
  [3].
- **Why it matters.** A plant that makes something close to crude oil. In
  a setting, coorongite is a natural fuel you can pick up off a lake shore
  (inference).

**Finding 2 — slow growth is the main barrier.**

- **Doubling time.** About 3 days was long typical [2]. The fastest known
  wild strain, Showa (race B), doubles in 1.4 days [1].
- **Why slow.** Making hydrocarbons and polysaccharides is metabolically
  expensive [2].
- **Open ponds are hard.** Fast-growing algae outcompete it, so open-pond
  culture is hard to control [1].
- **Pilot results.**
  - Indian raceway and circular ponds reached up to 2 g/L biomass and 28%
    hydrocarbons, best in November–December [2].
  - A Thai strain in a 3,675 L raceway at 5 ppt salinity gave 58.8%
    hydrocarbon content and 2.5 g/L biomass [2].
  - Italian outdoor panel reactors up to 1,400 L reached about 140 mg/L a
    day [2]. The main problems were sensitivity to high light, low
    temperature and contamination [2].
- **Milking.** Oil can be extracted with solvent without killing the
  colonies, and then extracted again [2]. Modelled at pond scale, this
  gives about 13.4 g of lipid per m² a day [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop you could tap repeatedly, like a rubber tree,
  rather than harvest and replant (inference).

**Finding 3 — its blooms can kill fish.**

- **Malaysia.** A race-L bloom in a man-made lake in Sarawak was linked to
  the deaths of about 8,500 fish, mainly black tilapia [4]. Brick-red
  patches and an oil layer covered the water [4]. Algal cells and oily
  substances were found in the dead fishes' gills [4].
- **Taiwan.** Autumn blooms in Liyu Lake are linked to fish deaths [4].
  The toxic agents are free fatty acids (alpha-linolenic, oleic and
  others), which also suppress competing plankton [4].
- **Philippines.** A bloom in Paoay Lake lowered dissolved oxygen [4].
- **Why it matters.** Contained culture is advisable (inference).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Colonial single cells in a shared oily matrix
  [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Batch harvest, or repeated non-destructive milking
  [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable; doubling 1.4–3 days
  [1][2].
- **Climate zones:** Freshwater and brackish water in temperate and
  tropical regions [3]. Optimum 20–25 °C [2].
- **Composition:** Hydrocarbons up to 86% of dry weight (race B) [1].
- **Edibility:** Not a food. Not sourced as edible.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Optimum light about 60–100 W/m² [2];
  sensitive to high light [2].
- **Yield:** Lab and pilot only (Finding 2). Modelled milking about 13.4 g
  lipid/m² a day [2].
- **Water use:** Freshwater; one strain did best at 5 ppt salinity [2].
- **Propagation:** From culture stock; reactors used to inoculate ponds
  [2].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Inoculum built up in columns and panels, then ponds
  [2].
- **Cultivars:** Named strains (Showa, CCAP 807/2, KMITL 2 and others)
  [1][2].
- **Weather response:** Sensitive to low temperature and high light [2].
- **Water regime:** Freshwater to slightly brackish [2][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Hydrocarbons peak in the stationary phase
  [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native worldwide in freshwater [4];
  blooms can kill fish (Finding 3).
- **Controlled environment:** Photobioreactors and panels; open ponds are
  prone to contamination [1][2].

## Hazards

- **Fish kills from blooms** (Finding 3) [4].
- **No food-safety data found.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** culture → solvent extraction (heptane) or harvest and dry →
  hydrocracking to fuel [1][2].
- **Uses:** Fuel feedstock (research); fossil deposits as oil shale and
  coal [3].

## Open research questions

- **Split the row?** Into `botryococcus` (*B. braunii*, race B),
  `botryococcus_alkenealis` (race A) and `botryococcus_lycopadienor`
  (race L), following the 2024 proposal [1]. The races differ sharply in
  oil type and amount.
- **Commercial production:** None found.

## Estimates and derivations

**Modelled milking yield, about 49 t of lipid per hectare a year.** Inputs:
13.4 g/m² a day [2]. 13.4 × 10,000 × 365 ≈ 48,900,000 g ≈ 49 t/ha a year.
As a cross-check, the same study's 15,900 m³ of oil a year from 3.53 km²
(353 ha) is about 45 m³/ha a year [2]. This is a model, not a measured
farm yield.
