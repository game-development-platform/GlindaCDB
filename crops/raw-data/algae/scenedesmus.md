## Naming and scope

This covers the freshwater green microalgae long grouped as *Scenedesmus*,
which often grow as small flat or bundled colonies (coenobia) [1]. It covers
the single cropID `scenedesmus`. They were the first algae mass-cultured
for protein food (Finding 1) and are now mainly used to clean wastewater
(Finding 2).

**Names.**

- **The old genus was split.** In the 2000s, DNA showed *Scenedesmus* was
  not one natural group, and it was split into genera matching its old
  subgenera: *Scenedesmus*, *Desmodesmus* and *Acutodesmus* [1].
- ***Tetradesmus*.** *Acutodesmus* was later merged into the older name
  *Tetradesmus* (2015), which has priority [1]. So the workhorse "*S.
  obliquus*" is now *Tetradesmus obliquus*, and "*S. dimorphus*" is *T.
  dimorphus* [1]. The old names are still common in biotechnology papers
  [1].
- ***Coelastrella vacuolata*** was formerly *S. vacuolatus* [4].
- **Status-table name.** Updated to *Scenedesmus* sensu lato (incl.
  *Tetradesmus obliquus*, formerly *S. obliquus*), so the row still covers
  the whole working group.

**Relatives flagged:** none proposed. `chlorella` is the closest comparison
crop (already RESEARCHED).

**Not wild-managed.** All supply found was cultured.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the first algal protein crop.**

- **Germany.** Mass culture began in Essen and moved to Dortmund, where
  C. J. Soeder's group developed the paddle-wheel raceway with an endless
  channel, still the standard design [2].
- **Czechoslovakia.** The Třeboň institute built a cascade culture system
  and began producing *Scenedesmus* in 1960 [2]. By the late 1970s a 1 ha
  plant was built in Bulgaria [2].
- **Developing-country projects.** In the early 1970s the German
  government funded *Scenedesmus* culture in Thailand, India and Peru [2].
  They were reviewed at a 1977 workshop [2].
- **Nutrition.** Drum-dried *S. acutus* protein scored a biological value
  of 75–78 and digestibility of 88–89 in bioassays [2].
- **Limit on eating it.** Thai nutrition tests found people would hardly
  eat more than 15–20 g of dry algae a day [2]. Uric acid in blood was
  monitored [2], since algae are rich in nucleic acids (inference).
- **Why it matters.** A Cold War "protein from ponds" crop that was
  dropped as uneconomic. Recovering the algae and turning it into food was
  the main problem [2].

**Finding 2 — today it cleans sewage.**

- **Nutrient removal.** *Scenedesmus* strains are among the most used
  algae for removing nitrogen and phosphorus from municipal wastewater [3].
- **Productivity.** Raceways on wastewater typically give 9–25 g/m² a day
  [3]. A year-long Spanish pilot peaked at 25.1 g/m² a day, depending on
  season [3]. One South African raceway averaged 7.25 g/m² a day at day 7
  [3].
- **Hardy.** It grew on 60% centrate (the liquid left after sludge
  digestion) [3].
- **Mixed cultures.** Open wastewater ponds become mixtures of
  *Scenedesmus*, *Chlorella* and other algae [3].
- **Why it matters.** A crop that turns a waste stream into biomass for
  feed, fertiliser or fuel.

**Finding 3 — EU food status only for one relative.**

- *Coelastrella vacuolata* (formerly *S. vacuolatus*) was confirmed "not
  novel in food" in July 2024, so it can be sold as food in the EU without
  pre-market authorisation [4].
- No EU status was found for *Tetradesmus obliquus* (Not sourced).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Single cells or 2–8-celled colonies [1].
  Year-round in warm climates; seasonal outdoors [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous or semi-continuous; daily dilution of
  10–30% of the culture [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable.
- **Climate zones:** Freshwater worldwide, including soil and rock [1].
- **Composition:** Protein of good quality (Finding 1) [2]. Lipid about
  12.6% of dry weight on centrate [3].
- **Edibility:** Edible as dried powder, at moderate intakes [2].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Productivity follows season and light [3].
- **Yield:** 7–25 g/m² a day in pilot raceways [3] (see Estimates).
- **Water use:** Can use wastewater and centrate [3].
- **Propagation:** From culture stock.
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Drum-dried powder [2].
- **Establishment:** Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** Lab strains (e.g. *S. acutus* 276-3a, Göttingen) [2].
- **Weather response:** Productivity drops with cold and low light [3].
- **Water regime:** Freshwater, shallow raceways about 0.1–0.3 m deep [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Grows with bacteria in wastewater consortia [3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native and common in freshwater [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Raceways and tubular photobioreactors [3].

## Hazards

- **Nucleic acids.** Uric acid was tracked in Thai feeding tests [2];
  intake was kept to about 15–20 g a day [2].
- **Wastewater-grown biomass** may carry contaminants (inference); not
  sourced.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** raceway culture → harvest → drum-dry → protein powder or
  feed [2]. For sewage: wastewater raceway → harvest biomass → fertiliser,
  feed or fuel [3].
- **Uses:** Food protein (historical) [2]; wastewater treatment [3];
  biofuel research [3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Yield, about 26–91 t dry biomass per hectare a year.** Inputs: 7.25 g/m²
a day average [3] and 25.1 g/m² a day peak [3]. 7.25 × 10,000 × 365 ≈ 26
t/ha; 25.1 × 10,000 × 365 ≈ 92 t/ha. The peak was seasonal, so the true
yearly figure is well below the top of the range.
