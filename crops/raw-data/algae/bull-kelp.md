## Naming and scope

This covers the genus *Durvillaea* ("southern bull kelp") as the single
cropID `bull_kelp`. Despite the name, it is **not a true kelp**: it belongs
to the order Fucales, family Durvillaeaceae, while the other algae in this
project's kelp docs are Laminariales [1]. Its English, Spanish and Māori
names include cochayuyo (Chile) and rimurapa (New Zealand) [1][4].

**Name clash.** In North American sources, "bull kelp" means *Nereocystis
luetkeana*, a true kelp. That is proposed separately as `nereocystis`
(see `giant_kelp`). This row is the Southern Hemisphere genus only.

**Species.** A 2020 review lists six accepted species [1]:

- *D. potatorum* (south-east Australia)
- *D. antarctica*
- *D. willana* (New Zealand)
- *D. chathamensis* (Chatham Islands)
- *D. poha* (New Zealand, split from *D. antarctica* in 2012)
- *D. amatheiae* (split from *D. potatorum* in 2017)

The same group then described *D. incurvata* (Chile) and *D. fenestrata*
(Antipodes Islands) [1]. So older "*D. antarctica*" literature from Chile
and New Zealand may include *D. incurvata* or *D. poha*, and older
*D. potatorum* work may include *D. amatheiae* [1].

Most species don't float and have small ranges, some limited to single
oceanic islands. The buoyant *D. antarctica* ranges across the
subantarctic, Chile and New Zealand [1]. Buoyancy has apparently been
gained and lost more than once. It helps the plants disperse by rafting,
but it restricts them to shallow water [1].

**Relatives flagged:** none proposed. All the commercially used taxa found
are within this genus. Cross-linked rows:

- `giant_kelp`: shares the Chilean seaweed fishery and the iodine survey
  [3][6].
- `lessonia`: co-harvested in Chile [3].
- `nereocystis`: name clash.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — no free-living gametophyte, which is why it has never been
farmed commercially.** *Durvillaea* has a diplontic life cycle with no
free-living gametophyte and oogamous reproduction [1]. Kelp hatcheries rely
on banking and seeding microscopic gametophytes (see `kelp_kombu`,
`sugar_kelp`). That route doesn't exist here.

A 2023 Tasmanian project stated that little is known about *Durvillaea*
reproduction and that successful cultivation has never been achieved [2].
In 2021 a Chilean project grew the first lab-produced *D. incurvata*
plantlets on suspended longlines at sea. It fertilised gametes from wild
fertile plants, then raised the embryos free-floating or on substrates
[3]. A 2024 Chilean restocking trial found transplanted *D. incurvata*
juveniles re-attached and grew on artificial substrates, but with high
juvenile mortality [3].

**Finding 2 — cochayuyo, Chile's most-eaten seaweed, under overharvest
pressure.**

- **Food.** It is Chile's most-consumed seaweed [3]. Plants reach about
  15 m [5]. The dried fronds are cochayuyo, and the stem (hulte) is
  usually eaten fresh [5]. Stems and holdfasts of *D. antarctica* and
  *D. incurvata* go into salads and stews [1].
- **Landings.** Official landings rose from 6,048 t (2010) to a peak of
  11,458 t (2018), then 7,709 t (2020) [3]. The landings basis (wet or dry)
  wasn't stated.
- **Closures.** Heavy extraction led to the first harvest closures in 2015
  and 2017 [3]. In Maule, three closures and quotas in 2018–2020 limited
  both collecting stranded kelp and prying attached plants off the rock
  [3].
- **Exports.** About 2,000 t of cochayuyo were exported a year between 2010
  and mid-2015, peaking at US$5.26 million FOB in 2014 [5].
- **EU market.** A Chilean government project filed an EU novel-food
  application for dried, chopped *D. antarctica* in December 2021 [3].

**Finding 3 — Australia's version is a beach-cast-only alginate fishery
that supplies about 5% of world alginate.**

- **Rules.** In Tasmania, live seaweed may not be cut. Only cast seaweed
  can be taken [2].
- **King Island.** Storm-cast *D. potatorum* has been collected on King
  Island since the early 1970s, dried, milled and shipped to Kelco in
  Girvan, Scotland [2]. About 25,000 t of dried milled kelp was exported
  from 1976 to 1986 [2]. Collectors gathered 15,000–23,000 t wet a year in
  the 1990s, about 50% of what washes ashore [2].
- **Trend.** Collection peaked at 4,000 t DW in 1990 and has declined
  significantly since 2000 [2]. A 2025 University of Tasmania statement
  notes signs of change in the kelp population's health [2].
- **Today.** The industry supplies about 5% of world alginate [2] and is
  worth over US$1.5 million GVP, including liquid plant biostimulants [2].
- **Management.** No new licences are issued at King Island or Granville
  Harbour [2]. Only 50% of the beach-cast may be taken at each site, and
  sandy-beach harvest is barred while hooded plovers nest [2].
- **Product.** Collectors claim *Durvillaea* alginate is of higher quality
  than other algae's [3] (an unverified claim).

**Finding 4 — earthquake and heatwave together wiped out New Zealand
stands.**

- **Earthquake.** In November 2016 the magnitude 7.8 Kaikōura earthquake
  uplifted about 130 km of coast by 0.5–6 m [6]. Bull kelp lost 75% of its
  canopy to the uplift [6].
- **Heatwave.** The 2017/18 summer brought the strongest marine heatwaves
  in 38 years of satellite records [6]. It removed a further 35% of the
  surviving canopy [6]. Four years later, there was no recovery, and turf
  and foliose seaweeds dominated [6].
- **Lyttelton Harbour.** Near Christchurch, sea temperature passed 23 °C
  and air temperature passed 30 °C while the kelp was exposed up to 3 hours
  a day at low tide [6]. All bull kelp was eliminated from Pile Bay and
  nearby reefs, including 100% of the endemic *D. poha* there. The invasive
  kelp *Undaria* then recruited at about 120 plants/m² [6].

**Finding 5 — pōhā: kelp bags that preserve food for years.**

- **Construction.** The blades have an airtight outer skin and a honeycomb
  interior, so they can be split, hollowed and inflated into bags [4].
- **Use.** Ngāi Tahu, who hold legally recognised harvest rights, store
  muttonbirds (tītī) in pōhā. Contents keep for up to 2–3 years [4].
- **Timing.** Blades are gathered in January or February. They are
  inflated and softened in dew in March, ready for the April muttonbird
  season [4].
- **Size.** Average bags hold 40–50 birds and up to 110 [4]. Bags are
  wrapped in tōtara bark and set in flax baskets [4].
- **Other uses.** Pōhā also carried fresh water, served as floats, and
  wrapped food cooking in earth ovens [4]. *D. poha* is named after the
  bags [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Large perennial fucoid of the low intertidal on
  wave-exposed rocky coasts [1][6]. The lifecycle is diplontic, with no
  free-living gametophyte (Finding 1) [1]. Plants reach about 15 m [5].
- **Harvest pattern:**
  - Chile: wild collection of attached plants pried off the rock, plus
    stranded material, under seasonal closures and quotas (Finding 2) [3].
  - Tasmania: beach-cast collection only (Finding 3) [2].
  - New Zealand: customary Māori harvest of blades for pōhā (Finding 5)
    [4].

  The 2020 review covers harvest timing, but its recommendations weren't
  extracted [1].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced. Cultivation is
  experimental, and wild age to harvestable size wasn't found.
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate to subantarctic Southern Hemisphere [1].
  *D. potatorum* in Australia runs from Robe (South Australia) to Bermagui
  (New South Wales) [2]. In Chile, cochayuyo runs from Coquimbo to Cape
  Horn [3].
  - Heat tolerance: stands died at sea temperatures over 23 °C combined
    with low-tide air temperatures over 30 °C (Finding 4) [6].
  - Chill-hours/vernalization: not applicable.
  - Elevation: not applicable. Shore height plays that role: it is
    restricted to the low intertidal and shallow subtidal, and uplift of
    0.5–6 m was lethal (Finding 4) [6].
- **Composition:**
  - **Fibre.** *D. antarctica* frond is 71.4% total dietary fibre and stem
    56.4% (dry basis) [5]. Drying at 40–80 °C didn't significantly change
    fibre or essential amino acids [5].
  - **Vitamin E.** Fronds contain γ-tocotrienol (651.7 mg/kg),
    δ-tocopherol (245.9 mg/kg) and α-tocopherol (179.4 mg/kg) [5].
  - **Other (consumer source).** One Spanish consumer source gives
    11.26 g protein, 8.67 g carbohydrate, 3,460 mg sodium and 490 µg
    iodine per 100 g [5]. This is a low-weight source, and its basis (dry
    or as-sold) is unstated.
  - **Iodine (study).** A 2025 study measured 0.46–4 mmol I/kg dry weight
    (DW), about 58–508 mg/kg (see Estimates). That is much lower than
    *Macrocystis*, *Lessonia* or *Laminaria* [6].

  No primary food-database entry was located.
- **Edibility:** Edible. Fronds are eaten dried and rehydrated, and stems
  fresh [5]. It is a traditional food of Indigenous South American
  peoples [5]. Among the brown seaweeds researched here, it has the lowest
  iodine (see Hazards).
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Reproduction is by eggs and sperm from
  the sporophyte itself (oogamous) [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. A Tasmanian project is
  establishing its reproductive period [2].
- **Yield:** No farmed yield exists. Wild/beach-cast output:
  - Chile landings 6,000–11,500 t a year [3].
  - King Island 15,000–23,000 t wet a year in the 1990s, peaking at
    4,000 t DW in 1990 [2].
  - A 1994–95 survey of one Tasmanian west-coast stretch projected about
    1,103 t wet of cast kelp a year [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable.
- **Propagation:** Sexual. Gametes are released from wild fertile plants
  and fertilised in the lab, then embryos are raised free-floating or on
  substrates [3]. There is no gametophyte stage to bank (Finding 1) [1].
  No clonal or viral degeneration risk applies.
- **Labor:** Chilean collectors describe beach-cast collection as slow,
  perhaps a week to gather one tonne of stranded kelp [3]. Tasmanian
  collection is by licensed independent collectors who dry the kelp
  before sale [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable (rocky substrate) [1]. Salinity: not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried fronds are the main traded form. Dried
  samples kept vacuum-sealed at 5 °C away from light for analysis [5].
  Tasmanian kelp is air-dried on racks, then milled [2]. Pōhā show the dried
  blade's durability, keeping food 2–3 years (Finding 5) [4].
- **Establishment:** Experimental only. Longline grow-out of lab plantlets,
  and juvenile transplants on artificial substrates (Finding 1) [3].
- **Cultivars:** None. Chilean researchers are mapping *D. incurvata*
  ecotypes from Valparaíso to Los Lagos for differences in bioactive
  compounds [3].
- **Weather response:** Storms drive beach-cast supply. Cast kelp arrives
  mainly in autumn and winter, under westerly weather [2]. Heatwaves
  combined with low-tide exposure are lethal (Finding 4) [6]. Frost: not
  sourced.
- **Water regime:** Marine, low intertidal. Exposed at low tide, which
  makes air temperature a stressor [6].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Pōhā blades in January–February [4]. Chile
  harvests are "markedly seasonal" in O'Higgins because of closures
  requested by fishers [3]. Tasmanian cast supply peaks in autumn and
  winter [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Controlled environment:** Lab culture of embryos and plantlets under
  controlled temperature, salinity, light and nutrients (Chile) [3]. No
  full closed-system cultivation was found.

## Hazards

- **Iodine.** About 58–508 mg/kg DW per the 2025 study [6]. That is
  roughly a quarter to a tenth of giant kelp's and far below oarweed's 2,500–12,000
  mg/kg or sugar kelp's 1,556–7,208 mg/kg. Chilean sources still call
  cochayuyo iodine-rich [3], so it is not negligible. The consumer figure
  (490 µg/100 g) is much lower still, and its basis is unclear [5].
- **Sodium.** About 3,460 mg/100 g in a consumer source [5].
- **Arsenic.** Levels are typical of brown algae [6]. No figure was
  extracted.
- **Over-harvest.** Chilean beds needed closures (Finding 2) [3]. King
  Island beach-cast volumes have declined since 2000 [2].
- **Climate and seismic loss.** Local extinctions after heatwaves and
  uplift, with no recovery after 4 years (Finding 4) [6]. Endemic,
  small-range species such as *D. poha* are especially exposed [6].
- **Ecosystem knock-on.** Bull-kelp loss let the invasive *Undaria*
  establish at high density [6].
- No fungal, ergot-type or disease hazard was found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (food, Chile):** attached plants pried off or stranded plants
  gathered → fronds sun-dried (cochayuyo) or stems sold fresh (hulte) →
  rehydrated and cooked in salads and stews. Also exported dried [1][3][5].
- **Chain (alginate, Tasmania):** storm-cast *D. potatorum* collected
  (50% per site) → air-dried on racks → milled → exported to Scotland for
  alginate extraction [2].
- **Chain (biostimulant and fertiliser):** cast kelp → liquid plant
  biostimulants and fertilisers (Tasmania; e.g. a dairy farmer's "Marrawah
  Gold") [2].
- **Chain (pōhā):** blades cut and hollowed (January–February) → inflated
  and softened → rolled for transport → filled with cooked muttonbird and
  fat → sealed, wrapped in tōtara bark and placed in a flax basket [4].
- **Other:** pōhā as water carriers, floats and earth-oven wrappers [4].
  Chilean work is identifying bioactive compounds for added value [3].

## Open research questions

- **Cultivation.** Whether the 2021 Chilean longline plantlets reached
  harvest, and with what yield. "Never achieved" (Tasmania 2023) vs "first
  cultivated plants" (Chile 2021) reflects different stages, not a
  contradiction.
- **Iodine.** Reconcile the 2025 study (58–508 mg/kg DW) with the consumer
  figure and with Chilean "iodine-rich" descriptions.
- **Chilean landings basis.** Wet or dry wasn't stated.
- **Harvest-timing recommendations.** In the 2020 review, not extracted.
- **Photoperiod and reproductive season.** Not sourced.
- **Split the row?** Whether `bull_kelp` should split into species rows
  (`cochayuyo` for *D. incurvata* / *D. antarctica*, and *D. potatorum*),
  and whether it should be renamed to avoid the *Nereocystis* clash.

## Estimates and derivations

**Iodine, about 58–508 mg/kg DW.** Input: 0.46–4 mmol I/kg DW [6].
Arithmetic: 0.46 × 126.9 = 58.4 mg/kg; 4 × 126.9 = 507.6 mg/kg.

**Tasmanian cast kelp dry matter, about 20%.** Input: about 1,000 t DW of
drift kelp corresponds to about 5,000 t wet [2]. Arithmetic:
1,000 / 5,000 = 0.20, so about 20% dry matter (a 5:1 wet-to-dry ratio).
On that basis, the 1990s collection of 15,000–23,000 t wet [2] ≈
3,000–4,600 t DW, consistent with the 4,000 t DW peak in 1990 [2].
