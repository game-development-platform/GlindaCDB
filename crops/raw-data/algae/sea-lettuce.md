## Naming and scope

This covers the foliose ("sheet") green seaweeds of the genus *Ulva*
(Chlorophyta, Ulvaceae), sold and studied as sea lettuce: chiefly *U.
lactuca*, *U. fenestrata*, *U. ohnoi*, *U. australis* (= *U. pertusa*),
*U. rigida* and *U. fasciata* [1][2]. It covers the single cropID
`sea_lettuce`.

**One genus, many look-alikes.**

- **Enteromorpha merged in.** The tube-shaped seaweeds once called
  *Enteromorpha* were folded into *Ulva* in 2003, because the two weren't
  separate lineages [1]. So *Ulva* now holds both sheets and tubes [1].
- **Species can't be told by eye.** Sea lettuce species are often
  misidentified, even in herbaria and gene banks; DNA barcoding is needed
  to name even common species [1]. Over 550 names exist, but only about 25
  species are reported often [1].
- **Consequence.** Yields, composition and growth figures here come from
  different species and strains, and vary widely (Agronomic data). The
  row stays genus-level, matching the original queue.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`aonori`** (*Ulva prolifera*, suji-aonori; proposed). A tubular,
  branched *Ulva* farmed on nets in brackish estuaries in Japan and dried
  into a green powder [3]. It differs in habitat (brackish), form,
  product and culture method, and is the main Yellow Sea green-tide
  species (Finding 2) [4].
- **`hitoegusa`** (*Monostroma* spp., proposed). Sold in Japan as "aosa"
  or "aosa-nori", but a different genus, with blades one cell thick [1].
  Farmed in Mie (*M. nitidum*) and elsewhere in East Asia (*M.
  kuroshiense*) [1].
- **`nori`**. Cross-link: Yellow Sea green tides start partly on nori
  (*Pyropia*) farm rafts (Finding 2) [4].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — among the fastest-growing crops measured, and a standard
partner for fish farms.**

- **Yield range.**
  - *U. ohnoi* in 10,000-litre land tanks in Australia averaged 38 g dry
    weight per m² a day, or 138 t dry/ha a year, over six months [2]. Daily
    output swung widely, from 16 to 77 g [2].
  - A review gives 25–40 t dry/ha a year for *Ulva*, against 2.1–5.1 t for
    soybean, wheat and maize [2].
  - Offshore cages in Israel reached 33.7 g dry per m² a day with
    intensified mixing [2]; a single layer at a shallow Israeli site
    managed only 5.8 g [2].
- **Why so variable.** Reported growth rates in temperate land systems
  range from 1.6% to 18.7% a day, reflecting species, strain and method
  [2].
- **Cleaning fish-farm water.** In Israeli designs, 1 ha of fish ponds is
  paired with 2.5 ha of *Ulva* to strip dissolved nitrogen. A farm using
  500 t of feed a year on 3.4 ha would yield about 220 t of fish and
  1,600 t of *Ulva* [2].
- **Abalone feed.** South African abalone farms grew over 1,000 t fresh
  weight of *Ulva* in 2007 [2].
- **Why it matters.** Sea lettuce is the textbook "grows anywhere, fast"
  seaweed, and a natural fit for an integrated fish–seaweed–shellfish
  system.

**Finding 2 — the same growth makes it the world's biggest nuisance
bloom: the Yellow Sea green tide.**

- **Scale.** Floating *U. prolifera* has formed green tides in the Yellow
  Sea every summer since 2007 [4]. In June 2008 it covered about 400 km²
  off Qingdao, threatening the Olympic sailing [4]. That year's bloom
  reached a maximum mat cover of 3,489 km², spread across about
  84,000 km² of sea [4].
- **Cost.** Qingdao spent an estimated US$30 million clearing more than a
  million tonnes before the Olympics [4].
- **Origin (contested).**
  - Many studies trace the start to nori farm rafts off Jiangsu: farmers
    scrape *Ulva* off the rafts at the end of the nori season, releasing
    about 5,000 wet tonnes into the sea [4].
  - One 2009 study instead ruled out nori rafts as the principal source
    [4]. Both views are kept.
- **Bloom season.** It typically forms about 300 km south of Qingdao in
  mid-April and reaches the city between mid-June and late July [4].
- **Why it matters.** A farmed crop's weed can become a regional
  disaster; a model could tie farm hygiene to downstream blooms.

**Finding 3 — rotting sea lettuce gives off a deadly gas: Brittany's
green tides.**

- **What happens.** Sea lettuce piles up on Brittany beaches, sometimes a
  metre deep [5]. As it rots, it releases hydrogen sulfide, which smells
  of rotten eggs [5]. At 100 ppm the gas deadens the sense of smell within
  two to five minutes [5].
- **Deaths.**
  - In 2009 a truck driver died after hauling a load of algae; a court
    later ruled his death a workplace accident caused by hydrogen sulfide
    [5].
  - A horse (2009) and more than 30 wild boars (2011) were officially
    attributed to algae fumes [5].
  - Other deaths, including two in 2019, remain disputed [5].
- **Cause.**
  - Nitrogen from fertiliser and animal waste feeds the blooms [5].
  - A 2021 report by France's public audit court attributed 90% of the
    problem to intensive agriculture, according to one columnist [5] (low
    weight: second-hand).
  - Farm groups dispute the blame, pointing to coastal wastewater [5].
- **Scale.** About 6,000 t was removed from Saint-Brieuc Bay in 2018 [5].
- **Why it matters.** A hazard from the crop's waste, not from eating it;
  any stockpile of decaying *Ulva* is dangerous.

**Finding 4 — protein is high but swings with season and nutrients.**

- **Range.** *Ulva* protein is often 10–20% of dry weight [6]; reported
  values run from 5% to 44% [6][2].
- **Nutrients raise it.** *U. fenestrata* grown in herring-processing
  water had about 30% protein against 10% in seawater, with four to six
  times the biomass [6].
- **Season lowers it.** At a Swedish sea farm, harvesting late in the
  season maximises yield but gives low protein [6].
- **Ash.** Israeli cage-grown *Ulva* was 38.5% ash and 5.3% protein [2].
- **Why it matters.** Nutrient-rich water makes a better food crop, so
  sea lettuce grown on fish-farm waste is both cleaner water and more
  protein.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Fast-growing thalli; in culture it is kept
  growing vegetatively in short cycles [2]. Growth
  peaks in summer in Israeli tanks [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous partial harvests from tanks or ponds
  [2]; seasonal harvests from sea farms [6].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable in the usual sense;
  biomass can be harvested within weeks [2]. Aonori nets in Japan are set
  in winter [3].
- **Climate zones:** Worldwide, from polar to tropical seas, and brackish
  water [1][3]. Species differ: *U. ohnoi* grows best at about 28 °C, with
  a lower limit of 10 °C; *U. australis* best at 20–25 °C [7].
- **Composition:** Protein 5–44% dry weight (Finding 4) [6][2]. *U.
  ohnoi* tank biomass held about 12% ulvan (a soluble fibre) and 13%
  amino acids [2]. A good source of calcium, magnesium, iron, selenium
  and vitamin B12 per 5 g dry portion [6].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as food; chefs in the North Atlantic are adopting
  it [6].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Reproduces by spores and gametes, and
  vegetatively [2]. Cutting thalli into small pieces triggers reproduction
  [3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for foliose species.
- **Yield:** 25–40 t dry/ha a year typical; up to 138 t in intensive tanks
  (Finding 1) [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine). Drip-irrigated greenhouse
  culture of *U. compressa* would use about 20% of the seawater needed for
  tumble tanks [2].
- **Propagation:** Vegetative fragments in tanks [2]; spores settle on
  nets (aonori) [3].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Attached on rock or floating free [1][4].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for sea lettuce; aonori is sold
  dried as a powder [3].
- **Establishment:** Tumble culture (air-mixed tanks) is the standard
  land method [2].
- **Cultivars:** None named for sea lettuce. A heat-tolerant aonori strain
  was in field trials in Tokushima [3].
- **Weather response:** Heavy rain lowers estuary salinity and cuts aonori
  yields [3]. Warming is cutting aonori production [3].
- **Water regime:** Marine to brackish [1][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Swedish sea farms: earlier harvests for
  protein, later for yield [6].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Pests in culture are diseases
  (bacteria, fungi, parasitic algae), grazers and epiphytic biofilms [2].
  A perforation disease hit *Ulva* farms in Israel [2].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - *U. australis* (= *U. pertusa*) is native to north-east Asia and has
    been introduced to Australasia, the Americas and Europe [7]. In Europe
    it turned up in the 1990s and 2000s, often near oyster farms [7].
  - *U. ohnoi* is an invasive green-tide species, recorded in the eastern
    Mediterranean [7].
  - Sea lettuce is a major bloom former (Findings 2 and 3) [4][5].
  - Implication: farming should use local strains, confirmed by DNA,
    rather than moving stock between regions (inference from [1][7]).
- **Controlled environment:** Proven in land tanks, ponds and greenhouses
  (Finding 1) [2]. A closed recirculating system on artificial seawater
  is under EU study [2].

## Hazards

- **Hydrogen sulfide from rotting biomass** (Finding 3) [5]. The main
  hazard.
- **Metals.** For a 5 g dry portion of selected *Ulva* species, inorganic
  arsenic reached 9–97% of the tolerable daily intake, lead 21–46% and
  cadmium 6–15% [6]. So inorganic arsenic in some samples approaches the
  limit. Herring-water-grown *U. fenestrata* was well within EU limits
  [6].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (land tanks):** seed fragments → tumble culture with aeration
  and nutrients (often fish-farm effluent) → partial harvest → wash, dry
  or use fresh [2].
- **Uses:**
  - Food [6].
  - Abalone and sea-urchin feed [2].
  - Biofilter for fish-farm effluent [2].
  - Biorefinery and bioethanol feedstock (Israeli studies) [2].
  - Ulvan and protein extraction [2].
- **Green-tide biomass:** collected from beaches as waste [4][5]; uses for
  it weren't covered here.

## Open research questions

- **Species to model.** Which *Ulva* species should the game's
  `sea_lettuce` represent (temperate *U. lactuca*/*U. fenestrata*, or
  warm-water *U. ohnoi*)?
- **Bloom link.** How to model farm-hygiene choices causing blooms
  (Finding 2).
- **Labour and storage** for sea lettuce weren't sourced.
- **Decisions:** whether to keep `aonori` and `hitoegusa` as proposed
  rows.

## Estimates and derivations

**Tank yield check.** Input: 38 g dry per m² a day [2]. 38 × 365 × 10,000
/ 1,000,000 = 138.7 t/ha a year, matching the source's 138 t.

**IMTA ratio check.** Inputs: 3.4 ha at 1 ha fish : 2.5 ha *Ulva* [2].
3.4 / 3.5 × 2.5 ≈ 2.4 ha of *Ulva*, giving about 1,600 / 2.4 ≈ 660 t of
*Ulva* per hectare a year (fresh weight presumably; the source doesn't
say).
