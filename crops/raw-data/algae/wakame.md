## Naming and scope

This covers *Undaria pinnatifida* (Harvey) Suringar, a brown alga (order
Laminariales, family Alariaceae) [1][2]. Its names are wakame (Japanese),
miyeok (Korean) and sea mustard (English) [1]. Synonyms are *Alaria
pinnatifida* Harvey 1860 (the basionym) and *Ulopteryx pinnatifida* [2].
This doc covers the single cropID `wakame`.

It is native to the Sea of Japan coasts of Japan, Korea and parts of
China, from the intertidal down to 15–20 m [2].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`winged_kelp`** (*Alaria esculenta*, proposed). Same family
  (Alariaceae) [1].
- ***Undaria undarioides*.** A warm-water Japanese congener used in
  temperature comparisons [3]. Not a crop in the sources found, so not
  proposed.
- **`bull_kelp`.** Wakame invaded New Zealand reefs after bull kelp loss
  [6]. Cross-linked.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one of the world's five big seaweed crops, but the official
Chinese numbers may be inflated three- to fourfold.**

- **Share of world output.** Wakame is one of five species groups making
  up 95% of world seaweed production [1].
- **FAO wet weights.** China rose from 1,511,135 t (2000) to 2,256,040 t
  (2020), then 2,061,290 t (2022). South Korea rose from 212,429 t to
  501,501 t, then 585,955 t [1].
- **The catch.** China's FAO figure comes from its fishery yearbook's dry
  weight, 225,604 t in 2020, converted at a 10% dry-matter factor. Industry
  estimates put 2022 Chinese output at only 500,000–750,000 t wet [1]. So
  the true Chinese figure may be 3–4.5 times lower than FAO's (see
  Estimates).
- **Abalone feed.** About 60% of China's wakame is sold as abalone feed, so
  Chinese production tracks the abalone industry [1].
- **Japan.** Japanese output peaked at about 174,000 t in 1974 and has
  fallen to about 50,000 t a year [1]. It ran 52,000–61,000 t in
  2006–2010, then about 10,000 t lower (45,000–51,000 t) after the 2011
  Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami [3]. Japan now imports about 70% of its
  wakame from China and 10% from Korea [1].
- **History.** Japan has cultivated it industrially since the 1950s–60s,
  and Korea since the 1960s [1][3]. China's industry formed in the 1980s
  with Japanese guidance [1].

**Finding 2 — a winter-only crop, set by a 20 °C optimum and a hard
ceiling near 27–28 °C.**

- **Season.** In China, Japan and South Korea it is farmed only in winter
  [1].
- **Gametophytes.** Growth is best at 20 °C, with an upper critical
  temperature of 28 °C [3]. They mature in October–December [3].
- **Young sporophytes.** Growth is also best at 20 °C, with an upper
  critical temperature of 27 °C [3]. In Ise Bay the growing season runs at
  about 12.7–13.1 °C, with a February minimum of 7.9 °C [3].
- **Southern edge.** At Japan's southernmost population, photosynthesis
  peaked at about 21–22 °C. Most gametophytes couldn't survive above 28 °C,
  and sporophytes were gone by July [3]. The authors warn the population
  could go locally extinct with further warming [3].
- **Sex differences.** Male gametophytes have higher temperature optima
  (20.7–20.9 °C) than females (16.5–19.8 °C). This may restrict
  fertilisation to cooler autumn water [3].
- **Korea.** Recent winter warming has threatened Korean productivity and
  seedling survival [3].

**Finding 3 — bred into regional cultivars, with heat tolerance traded
against them.** A Naruto (Tokushima) cultivar was crossed with a wild
warm-water strain from Tsubaki [3]:

- All three strains grew best at 15–20 °C.
- The cultivar's growth stopped at 26 °C and the wild strain's at 27 °C.
- At 27 °C, cultivar blades "melted" within 6 days.
- The hybrid survived twice as long, with intermediate heat tolerance.

Quality also varies by strain and growing conditions. Sanriku wakame is
said to have more dietary fibre and a firmer bite [1]. Regional lines from
Iwate, Tokushima and Kagoshima kept the same sex-specific gametophyte
traits [3].

**Finding 4 — one of the world's "100 worst" invaders, yet farmed in
parts of its invaded range.**

- **Listing.** IUCN lists it among the world's 100 worst invasive alien
  species [6]. In Europe it is counted among the 10 worst [6], and ranked
  third most invasive of 113 introduced seaweeds [6].
- **Spread.** The first recorded introduction was accidental, in 1971, in
  the Thau lagoon in the Mediterranean [2][6]. It was then deliberately
  introduced for farming on the Brittany coast [2]. It has since reached
  New Zealand (1987), south-eastern Australia, Patagonia in Argentina,
  California and Mexico [6].
- **Impacts vary.** In Argentina, invaded areas showed a sharp drop in
  native seaweed diversity, while New Zealand studies found little impact
  [6]. A review of three decades of Australasian research calls it a case
  of "decoupling between the success and impact" of an invader [6].
- **Farming in the invaded range.** New Zealand allows active mariculture.
  Some European areas allow it only where infrastructure exists and wild
  wakame is already abundant, under strict controls [6]. One low-weight
  web source claims all New Zealand harvest is banned [6], which conflicts
  with this.

**Finding 5 — far less iodine than kombu, but the highest arsenic of the
common edible seaweeds.**

- **Iodine.** A Taiwanese survey found wakame at 93.9–185.1 mg/kg (mean
  139.7), against kombu's 2,523.5 mg/kg mean [4]. A 2024 US feeding study's
  wakame had only 27 µg/g [4]. Spanish work found European-origin seaweeds
  higher in iodine than Asian ones, with significant differences between
  wakame origins [4].
- **Arsenic.** In that US feeding study, wakame had the highest total
  arsenic (45.91 µg/g) of three seaweeds. After eating it, arsenic was the
  dominant element in volunteers' urine [4].
- **Arsenic form.** Most is arsenosugar. It's hard to extract with
  standard methods, which recover only 4–49%, but an enzyme method recovered
  88.8% [4].
- **Processing.** Commercial dried wakame from Sanriku goes through
  parboiling, salting, desalting and drying [4]. An Alaskan trial found
  hot-water blanching before drying cut iodine by 58–73% and arsenic by
  42–46% [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual kelp with a heteromorphic life cycle:
  macroscopic sporophyte, microscopic sexual gametophytes [3]. Sporophytes
  grow through winter and early summer, and disappear by July at the
  southern limit [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single winter–spring harvest of rope-grown
  sporophytes [1][3]. Japan's historical wild harvest was 40,000–60,000 t
  wet a year in the 1960s. By 1976, about 20,000 t came from the wild
  against 127,000 t cultivated [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Gametophytes mature in October–December,
  sporophytes grow through winter, and the crop is harvested before summer
  [1][3]. That is roughly 5–7 months at sea (inferred from the season; an
  exact figure wasn't sourced).
- **Climate zones:** Native cold- to warm-temperate NW Pacific [2]. Invaded
  temperate coasts worldwide (Finding 4) [6]. Temperature limits are in
  Finding 2 [3]. Chill-hours/vernalization: not applicable, though
  maturation needs autumn temperatures [3]. Elevation: not applicable.
  Depth: intertidal to 15–20 m [2].
- **Composition:** Iodine 93.9–185.1 mg/kg in dried products (Taiwan) [4],
  and 27 mg/kg in a US sample [4]. Total arsenic 45.91 mg/kg [4]. Sanriku
  wakame is reportedly higher in dietary fibre [1]. A full proximate
  composition wasn't extracted. No primary food-database entry was located.
- **Edibility:** Widely eaten: in soups (miso soup in Japan, miyeok-guk in
  Korea), salads and as dried or salted product [1][5][6].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Male and female microscopic
  gametophytes produce sperm and eggs [3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Gametophyte growth and maturation are best
  under blue light (λmax 453 nm) and green light (λmax 525 nm), the same for
  both sexes [3]. One crossbreeding experiment used 11 h light : 13 h dark
  [3]. No yield photoperiod effect was found.
- **Yield:** No per-metre yield was extracted. A Japanese density study
  varied stocking from 10 to 200 plants per metre of rope. Sanriku farms
  thin to about 100 plants/m in January [3]. National output figures are in
  Finding 1 [1].
- **Water use:** Not applicable.
- **Propagation:** Sexual, via zoospores and gametophytes. Japanese and
  Chinese methods are taught in FAO-sponsored workshops [2]. Cultivars are
  propagated as regional lines (Finding 3) [3]. No clonal or viral
  degeneration risk was found.
- **Labor:** No labour-hours figure. Production cost was once about
  US$0.70/kg, but has been rising sharply with wages, seed and salt costs.
  Dalian wakame hit a record price in 2022 [1]. Japan's ageing farmers are
  expected to keep driving production down [1].
- **Soil:** Not applicable (rocky substrate, ropes, artificial surfaces)
  [2][6]. Salinity tolerance was compared with native New Zealand kelps in
  a 2016 study, but values weren't extracted [6].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Three main routes:
  - Blanched and salted, sold through auction in Japan's Sanriku region
    [1].
  - Blanched and frozen in bags (Korea) [1].
  - Dried after parboiling, salting and desalting [4].

  Drying ratio (wet ÷ dry): 11–15 unblanched, 19–24 blanched [4].
- **Establishment:** Seeded rope deployed in autumn [1][3]. Density adjusted
  in winter (about 100 plants/m) [3].
- **Cultivars:** Regional cultivars such as Naruto, plus crosses with wild
  warm-water strains for heat tolerance (Finding 3) [3]. Hybrids with
  higher growth and nutritional value are mentioned by FAO [2].
- **Weather response:** Heat (above about 20 °C stresses it; over 27–28 °C
  is lethal) and warm winters (Finding 2) [3]. The 2011 tsunami cut
  Japanese output by about 10,000 t a year [3]. Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine. Grown on ropes, and naturally from the
  intertidal to the shallow subtidal [2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Winter–spring. Wet-to-dry ratio changes
  month to month, and harvest timing sets quality and price [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Controlled environment:** Gametophyte lab culture under controlled
  temperature and light (Finding 2–3) [3]. A Japanese tank system was used
  for common-garden comparisons of five cultivation-site lines [3]. No full
  closed-system production was found.

## Hazards

- **Arsenic.** The highest total arsenic of the common edible seaweeds in
  one comparison (45.91 mg/kg), mostly arsenosugars [4]. Blanching removes
  about 42–46% [4].
- **Iodine.** Moderate: 27–185 mg/kg in the samples above [4]. Thresholds
  for comparison [4]:
  - WHO's provisional maximum tolerable intake is 1.0 mg/day from all
    sources.
  - Germany's risk agency warns about dried algae above 20 mg/kg.
  - Australia caps dried algal food at 1,000 mg/kg.
  - The US FDA limit for algal products is 5,000 ppm on a dry basis.

  So wakame passes the Australian and US limits but exceeds the German
  warning level. The miyeok-guk tradition has prompted thyroid-safety
  concerns [5].
- **Ecological hazard.** A listed invasive species (Finding 4) [6].
  Cultivation outside its native range is restricted or prohibited in many
  jurisdictions [6]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — this project has no field for
  invasiveness or biosecurity regulation.
- No fungal, ergot-type or disease hazard was sourced. Diseases of farmed
  wakame weren't researched in this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (Japan, blanched-salted):** rope harvest → blanched → salted →
  auctioned (JF Zengyoren, Sanriku) → desalted and cut by processors →
  sold salted or dried ("cut wakame") [1][4].
- **Chain (Korea, frozen):** harvest → blanched in bulk → bagged → frozen →
  consumer packs [1].
- **Chain (abalone feed):** fresh wakame fed directly to caged abalone. This
  is about 60% of Chinese output [1].
- **Food:**
  - Japan: miso soup and salads [6].
  - Korea: miyeok-guk, eaten by new mothers for postpartum recovery for
    over a thousand years, and by everyone on their birthday [5]. A
    research group is studying its link to breastfeeding outcomes [5].
- **Market structure:** Japanese consumption was over 200,000 t wet a year
  as of 1991 [2]. Japan's domestic supply is now about 20% of demand [1].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** mekabu (the sporophyll, sold separately),
  and fucoidan or fucoxanthin extraction. Not sourced in this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

**FAO's Chinese figure is the yearbook dry weight × 10.** Inputs:
225,604 t dry (2020) at a 10% dry-matter factor [1]; FAO 2020 = 2,256,040 t
wet [1]. Arithmetic: 225,604 / 0.10 = 2,256,040 t, an exact match, so FAO's
number is purely derived. Against the industry's 500,000–750,000 t [1]:
2,256,040 / 750,000 = 3.0 and 2,256,040 / 500,000 = 4.5, so FAO is 3–4.5
times higher. A survey-based Chinese wet-weight figure would resolve this.

**Dry matter, about 7–9% (unblanched).** Input: wet-to-dry ratio 11–15 [4].
Arithmetic: 1 / 15 = 6.7%; 1 / 11 = 9.1%. That is close to the 10% factor
used for China [1]. Blanched wakame (ratio 19–24) is 4.2–5.3% dry matter.

**Japan's total demand, about 250,000 t wet.** Inputs: domestic output about
50,000 t, which is about 20% of supply [1]. Arithmetic: 50,000 / 0.20 =
250,000 t, consistent with over 200,000 t consumption in 1991 [2].

**Dried wakame per iodine reference.** Inputs: mean 139.7 mg/kg = 139.7 µg/g
[4]; RDI 150 µg/day [4]; WHO maximum 1.0 mg/day [4]. Arithmetic: RDI:
150 / 139.7 = 1.07 g. Maximum: 1,000 / 139.7 = 7.2 g (5.4 g at the 185.1
mg/kg top of the range). That compares with about 0.03 g for raw dry sugar
kelp (see `sugar_kelp`).
