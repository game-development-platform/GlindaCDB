## Naming and scope

This covers *Asparagopsis taxiformis*, a red seaweed of tropical to warm
temperate seas [1]. It is grown on land as a cattle feed additive that cuts
methane [2][3], and it is gathered wild in Hawaii as the food seaweed limu
kohu [4]. It covers the single cropID `asparagopsis`.

**Names.**

- **Limu kohu.** Hawaiian, meaning "pleasing seaweed" [1]. Other Pacific
  names include *kohrmwek* (Kosrae) and *char* (Palau) [4].
- **Synonym.** Formerly *A. sanfordiana* [1].
- **Two-phase life cycle.** The erect plant is the gametophyte. The
  filamentous tetrasporophyte was first described as a separate species,
  *Falkenbergia hillebrandii* [1].
- **Cryptic species.** *A. taxiformis* holds four genetically distinct
  lineages that look alike [1]. Hawaii is in the native range of lineage 2
  [1].

**Relatives flagged:** `asparagopsis_armata` (*A. armata*), the genus's
other recognised species [1]. It is also used in methane trials [2], and
it gets its own PROPOSED row.

**Supply is mixed.** Feed supply is tank- and pond-farmed (Finding 2). The
Hawaiian food supply is wild-gathered and is now being restored (Finding
4). The Hawaiian side is saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/asparagopsis.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a feed additive that removes most cattle methane.**

- **Active compound.** Bromoform. The seaweed's essential oil is about 80%
  bromoform by weight [1].
- **Dose and effect.**
  - Beef steers given 0.25% and 0.5% of organic-matter intake for 21 weeks
    cut methane by over 80% at the high dose, with no loss of effect over
    time [2]. Feed intake fell while weight gain held, so feed efficiency
    rose [2].
  - Steers on a high-grain diet at 0.05–0.20% of organic matter (90 days)
    reached a 98% reduction [2].
  - Bromoform extracted into canola oil, at 17, 34 and 51 mg per kg of
    dry-matter intake, cut methane yield by 64%, 98% and 99% [2].
- **Rule of thumb.** A review averaged about 0.33 g less methane per mg of
  bromoform eaten, across 16 animal groups [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop grown for what the animal doesn't make. Its
  value depends on bromoform content, which is volatile, so the product is
  freeze-dried or extracted into oil [2].

**Finding 2 — farmed on land, not at sea.**

- **Ocean farming has been dropped.** CH4 Global's CEO says sea growing is
  not commercially feasible, so most firms have moved to land tanks [3].
  Its New Zealand arm started on mussel-farm space at sea, then moved to
  land because it lowered risk and regulatory hurdles [3].
- **Scale.**
  - CH4 Global's EcoPark at Louth Bay, South Australia, opened in January
    2025 with ten 200,000 L ponds, producing more than 80 t a year [3]. It
    planned 100 ponds, enough for 45,000 cattle a day [3].
  - Provectus Algae (Queensland) opened a 30,000 L closed demonstration
    plant [3].
  - Asparagopsis Technologies (Bluff, New Zealand) makes about 20,000 doses
    a month for research trials [3].
- **Light limits density.** As tank cultures thicken, cells shade each
  other, which caps density [3].
- **The tetrasporophyte trick.** Dense tank cultures of the filamentous
  *Falkenbergia* phase resist invasion by microorganisms, which makes that
  phase suited to tank propagation [3].
- **Cost.** Asparagopsis companies have struggled to get below US$1 per
  daily dose, against a perceived adoption barrier of 50 cents [3].
- **Why it matters.** A seaweed crop that is really a tank or pond crop.
  It is grown for a chemical, so the farm controls light and nutrients
  rather than relying on the sea.

**Finding 3 — an invasive species in the Mediterranean.**

- **High invasive potential.** It has a broad worldwide spread [1].
- **Two taxa there.**
  - One native-looking taxon described from Alexandria in 1813, confined
    to the eastern basin [1].
  - A more recent Indo-Pacific introduction, invasive and a nuisance in the
    western basin (Algeria, Balearics, France, Italy, Sicily, Tunisia) [1].
  - A further lineage (L4), known from the western Indo-Pacific and Hawaii,
    was reported off Israel in 2022 and probably arrived via the Suez Canal
    [1]. It has formed large seasonal blooms there [1].
- **Why it matters.** Biosecurity rules are the reason land tanks with
  local strains are preferred (inference).

**Finding 4 — a gathered food in Hawaii, now being restored.**

- **Food use.** A traditional ingredient in poke and a condiment [1]. It
  was mixed with raw fish, and it was said the wealthy served limu kohu
  with raw fish while those less well off used *Gracilaria* [4].
- **Gathering.** Whole families gather at favoured "limu places" [4]. Pick
  the branchlets without pulling up the base [4]. It grows off reef edges
  in clean, well-flushed water [4].
- **Decline.** It and many other limu became less common over the last 50
  years [4]. Traditional limu knowledge, passed down orally, has dwindled
  [4].
- **Restoration.** Hawaii Sea Grant, the start-up Symbrosia and Kuaʻāina
  Ulu ʻAuamo run a supply and outplanting programme to restore limu kohu
  [4].
- **Why it matters.** The same species is a factory crop in Australia and
  a managed wild food in Hawaii.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Seaweed with alternating erect gametophyte and
  filamentous tetrasporophyte phases [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous tank or pond harvest [3]. Hawaiian
  gathering picks branchlets and leaves the base [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Tropical to warm temperate seas [1]. The two
  Mediterranean taxa differ in lower survival temperature (17 °C and
  9–11 °C) [1].
- **Composition:** Bromoform-rich (Finding 1); bromoform per dry gram
  ranged 6.6–7.8 mg in trial biomass [2]. Iodine-rich [4].
- **Edibility:** Edible but bitter and strongly iodine-flavoured [4].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** More than 80 t a year from 2 million L of ponds (CH4 Global)
  [3]; dry or wet basis not stated.
- **Water use:** Seawater tanks and ponds [3].
- **Propagation:** Vegetative propagation of the *Falkenbergia* phase in
  tanks; inducing meiosis and seeding gametophytes on nets has been trialled
  [3]. Hatchery production of juveniles [3].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Freeze-dried, or extracted into oil, to keep
  the volatile bromoform [2]. As food, kept in shade after picking, then
  salted and refrigerated or frozen [4].
- **Establishment:** Hatchery and nursery stage, then grow-out [3].
- **Cultivars:** Not sourced. Lineage matters (Finding 3).
- **Weather response:** Land systems reduce weather disruption [3].
- **Water regime:** Marine.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Invasive in the western Mediterranean
  (Finding 3) [1].
- **Controlled environment:** Yes — tanks, ponds and closed systems
  (Finding 2) [3].

## Hazards

- **Bromoform residues.** Bromoform is chemically similar to chloroform, a
  known carcinogen. In animal studies it has enlarged large-intestine
  tumours and caused some developmental toxicity [5].
  - A Wageningen dairy trial found bromoform in milk (6.2–35 µg/L when
    detected) and urine [5]. FutureFeed, which licenses the technology,
    disputes how those data were handled [5].
  - The UC Davis beef trial found no measurable bromoform residues and no
    change in meat quality [2].
- **As food:** Bitter and iodine-rich; soaking overnight in fresh water
  reduces the bitterness [4].
- **Iodine load in cattle** was a measured trial variable, with no adverse
  residues reported [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Feed chain:** tank or pond culture → harvest → freeze-dry, or extract
  bromoform into canola oil → mix into cattle feed [2][3].
- **Food chain (Hawaii):** pick branchlets → rinse in seawater → soak
  overnight → chop into poke or use as a relish [4].
- **Markets:** CH4 Global partnered with Lotte to supply up to 4 million
  cattle in South Korea, pending approval [3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Dose implied by CH4 Global's plan, about 49 g per head a day.** Inputs:
10 ponds give more than 80 t a year [3]; 100 ponds are meant to serve
45,000 cattle a day [3]. Assuming output scales with pond count (an
inference), 100 ponds give about 800 t a year. 800,000,000 g ÷ (45,000 ×
365 = 16,425,000 head-days) ≈ 49 g per head a day. That is in line with
0.25–0.5% of a feedlot animal's intake (inference), but the basis (dry or
wet) is not stated.
