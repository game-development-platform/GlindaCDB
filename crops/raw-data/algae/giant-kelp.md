## Naming and scope

This covers *Macrocystis pyrifera* (Linnaeus) C. Agardh, a brown alga (order
Laminariales) known as giant kelp or bladder kelp [1]. It forms floating
surface canopies in cold-temperate waters of both hemispheres [1][7]. This
doc covers the single cropID `giant_kelp`.

**Taxonomy is unsettled again.**

- **Merged in 2009–2010.** The genus once held four species, *M. pyrifera*,
  *M. integrifolia*, *M. angustifolia* and *M. laevis*, separated by
  holdfast and blade shape. In 2009 these were merged into one
  morphologically plastic species. A 2010 DNA-barcoding study of 19 sites
  worldwide supported that merger [1]. The four forms crossbreed to give
  fertile offspring [1].
- **Partly reversed in 2023.** A whole-genome study found *integrifolia*
  and *pyrifera* genetically distinguishable after all. It also found
  northern (California) and southern (Chile) populations of each distinct,
  so new names may be needed for the northern taxa [1].

This doc treats the whole complex as one crop, as the cultivation
literature does. The former *M. integrifolia* is the smaller, rhizomatous
intertidal and shallow-subtidal form of British Columbia, Peru and northern
Chile [1]. Chilean breeders have crossed it with *M. pyrifera* [5].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **Bull kelp (*Nereocystis luetkeana*).** Proposed as `nereocystis`. It is
  the NE Pacific canopy kelp that replaces giant kelp in northern
  California, and it is harvested there for food [2]. **Naming
  collision:** this project's existing `bull_kelp` row is *Durvillaea*
  (southern bull kelp), a different alga in a different order (Fucales)
  [8]. "Bull kelp" in North American sources means *Nereocystis*.
- ***Lessonia* spp. (Chilean "huiro").** Proposed as `lessonia`. A
  co-occurring Chilean kelp studied alongside *Macrocystis* for its iodine
  and arsenic [8]. Its harvest volume was not sourced in this pass.
- **Oarweed (`laminaria_digitata`).** Already researched. It is the
  European alginate counterpart, and a 2021 review compares the two [3].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — among the fastest-growing organisms on Earth, but with almost
no nutrient reserve.**

- **Growth.** Under good conditions it grows up to about 50 cm a day [2][7].
  Individuals exceed 50 m [7]. It grows about 2% of its dry mass per day and
  turns over its biomass 6–7 times a year [4].
- **Nitrogen.** Growth saturates at only 1–2 µmol/L nitrate, but the plant
  can't build nitrogen reserves lasting more than about 3 weeks [4].
  Southern California losses during El Niño years were driven mainly by
  nitrate starvation. In 1983, fronds grew so slowly that terminal blades
  formed before reaching the surface [4].
- **Light vs nutrients.** In normal years, juvenile growth is limited by
  light. In El Niño years nutrients take over, and fertilising juveniles
  restored growth [4]. Fertiliser trials on adults under warm, low-nutrient
  conditions also boosted growth [4].
- **Depth.** It grows from the low intertidal to about 25 m, and at most
  about 30 m in California [2]. It thrives on rocky reefs at 5–30 m [2].
- **Reproduction.** Plants start releasing spores from special basal blades
  (sporophylls) once about 5 m tall [7].

**Finding 2 — the original alginate crop, harvested like a lawn.**

- **History.** Commercial harvest in California began in 1911. In 1929
  Kelco of San Diego became the world's first producer of algin products
  [2].
- **Volume.** California's harvest rose from about 10,000 t in the early
  1930s to about 170,000 t in the late 1970s, and settled around 100,000
  wet t/year, about 95% of it by Kelco [2]. Kelco's ships, fitted with
  mechanical blades, could cut up to 600 t a day [2].
- **The rule.** Harvesters may not cut attached kelp more than 4 ft
  (1.22 m) below the surface [2]. Because the growing tips keep elongating,
  the canopy can be cut several times a year "without damaging kelp beds",
  which NASA likens to a haircut [2]. A Monterey study found no detectable
  harvest effect on canopies, though with low statistical power [2].
- **Today.** The harvest is managed in 87 administrative kelp beds [2].
  California adopted revised harvest regulations effective 1 January 2026,
  but their details weren't read [2]. The industry largely ended when ISP
  Alginates (ex-Kelco) closed its San Diego operation. That plant had
  harvested about 25,000 t a year from Point Loma's beds alone [2]. The
  closure year wasn't captured from the source.
- **Global supply.** By 2016, global *M. pyrifera* output was about
  31,835 t, with only about 1 t from aquaculture [3]. Chile is the largest
  wild producer, and one review gives Chile's *Macrocystis* harvest as
  23,587 t [3].

**Finding 3 — cultivation works and responds strongly to breeding, but
remains a pilot-scale crop.**

- **Food trial.** In southern Chile, ropes seeded with 10,000–40,000
  spores/mL gave over 14.4 kg fresh weight (FW) per m of rope in about 8
  months. Over 70% was food-grade, and the rest was suitable for abalone
  feed or fertiliser [5]. Ropes had to go to sea by April at the latest
  for a December harvest [5].
- **Abalone-feed pilot.** This gave 25 kg/m over a 9-month spring–summer
  period and 16.2 kg/m in autumn–winter. The total was 41.3 kg wet per m
  of line per year, with lines 4 m apart [5]. That's roughly 100 t/ha a
  year (see Estimates). Return on investment came after the first year on
  30–50 ha at US$78/t [5].
- **Hybrid vigour.** Crosses of selected gametophyte parents from different
  Chilean coasts reached 11 kg FW per frond, equal to 66 kg/m of line,
  after 10 weeks in the lab and 5 months at sea. One run reached 80 kg/m
  in 12 months [5]. That's about 3–5 times the traditional 14.4–22 kg/m
  [5].
- **Australia.** Trials in south-east Australia (Tasmania) found that
  for an April deployment, the best compromise of yield, fouling and
  composition was a July–August (mid–late winter) harvest [5].

**Finding 4 — "depth cycling" as an open-ocean farming idea.** A US
Department of Energy ARPA-E project (MARINER, US$22 million) tested
cultivation away from coastal reefs. A solar-winched "kelp elevator" off
Catalina Island lowered kelp to about 80 m at night for nutrients and
raised it to about 9 m by day for light, for 90 days [6]. Depth-cycled
kelp grew 5% a day and produced four times the wet biomass of kelp
transplanted to a natural bed. Its tissue showed it was not
nitrogen-deficient [6]. This is a single 90-day trial on 35 juveniles. Its
purpose was biofuel (bio-crude) feedstock, not food [6].

**Finding 5 — heat and nutrient-poor water have erased whole regional
forests.**

- **Tasmania.** More than 95% of Tasmania's surface-canopy giant kelp
  forests have been lost since the 1970s [7]. The cause is the warm,
  nutrient-poor East Australian Current pushing south, plus invading sea
  urchins [7]. East-coast waters there are warming at 3–4 times the global
  average [7]. Canopy that was extensive in the late 1990s collapsed to
  under 10 ha by 2015 [7].
- **Endangered listing.** In 2012 the forests became the first marine
  community listed as endangered under Australian federal law [7].
  Restoration now breeds warm-tolerant strains from surviving individuals
  [7].
- **Heat ceiling.** One researcher puts the global maximum survivable
  temperature at about 24 °C [7].
- **El Niño.** In the NE Pacific, the 1982–83 and 1997–98 events combined
  warm, nutrient-poor water with large waves. After 1982–83, forests at
  the southern Baja California limit disappeared and never recovered in
  the southernmost area [4]. Recovery after 1997–98 took from 6 months to
  several years [4].
- **2014–16 marine heatwave.** Canopy loss tracked the absolute
  temperature of the warmest month more than the size of the anomaly [4].
- **New Zealand.** Heatwave losses were worse where water clarity was poor
  [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial marine kelp with a heteromorphic life
  cycle. The large diploid sporophyte, fixed by a holdfast, carries fronds
  grown from apical meristems. It alternates with microscopic gametophytes
  [5]. Sporophylls form at the base once plants are about 5 m tall [7].
- **Harvest pattern:** Wild California canopy: repeated top-cutting, several
  times a year, to ≤1.22 m depth (Finding 2) [2]. Farmed: single harvest per
  rope cycle (December in southern Chile [5], July–August in south-east
  Australia [5]). Spring–summer and autumn–winter cycles are both possible
  in Chile [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Farmed: about 5–9 months at sea after
  about 10 weeks of hatchery culture (Finding 3) [5]. Wild sporophytes
  become reproductive at about 5 m tall [7]. No age to reproduction was
  sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate coasts of both hemispheres: California
  and Baja California, British Columbia, Peru, Chile, New Zealand, Tasmania
  and sub-Antarctic islands [1][7]. Upper limit: about 24 °C survivable
  [7], with production collapsing far below that when warm water is
  nutrient-poor (Finding 5) [4][7]. Chill-hours/vernalization: not
  applicable. Elevation: not applicable. Depth plays that role: low
  intertidal to about 25–30 m [2].
- **Composition:**
  - Dried giant kelp (feed database), as % or units of dry matter (DM):
    crude protein 10.1% (range 6.6–13.2%), gross energy 9.0 MJ/kg, calcium
    14.1 g/kg, potassium 67.5 g/kg, sodium 36.9 g/kg, magnesium 39.0 g/kg,
    iron 117 mg/kg [3].
  - Protein of about 9% dry weight and lipid of 1.3% (the lowest of three
    kelps compared) [3].
  - Farmed Chilean abalone-feed kelp at about 9% protein [5].
  - Depth-cycled kelp had higher protein than bed-grown kelp [6].
  - Iodine: 6.1–17 mmol/kg DW, about 770–2,160 mg/kg (see Estimates) [8].
    That is "much less" than *Laminaria* species [8].

  No primary food-database entry was located.
- **Edibility:** Edible. Chilean trials developed food products for Asian
  markets [5], and New Zealand composition work rates it comparable to
  other edible seaweeds [3]. Its lower iodine than *Laminaria* or
  *Saccharina* is a relative advantage [8]. No consumer iodine guidance
  specific to this species was found.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable (gametophyte sexual reproduction) [5].
  Wild-type forms are interfertile (Naming and scope) [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for reproduction in this pass.
  For growth, light limits juveniles in normal years and nutrients limit
  them in El Niño years (Finding 1) [4].
- **Yield:** Farmed lines: 14.4–25 kg FW/m per cycle, 41.3 kg/m a year
  (about 100 t/ha a year at 4 m line spacing), and up to 66–80 kg/m with
  hybrids (Finding 3) [5]. Wild California: about 100,000 t a year
  statewide historically [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:** Sexual. Fertile sporophylls are collected, and spores
  seeded onto rope [5]. Breeders keep gametophyte clones and make F1
  crosses, giving heterosis (Finding 3) [5]. Parent-site origin strongly
  affects cultivated performance [5]. Restoration seeds juveniles onto
  substrates (ceramic plates, clam shells) [5] or attaches lab-grown
  juveniles to the seafloor after about 6 weeks [7]. No clonal or viral
  degeneration risk was found.
- **Labor:** No labour-hours figure. Historical mechanised harvest was up to
  600 t per ship per day [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable (holdfast on rock). Salinity: kelp is said to
  tolerate seawater diluted by almost one-third [4] (a general kelp
  statement cited in a *Macrocystis* paper).
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for food use. Feed-database entries
  are for dried kelp at 92.5% dry matter [3]. An alginate-extraction
  residual pulp exists as a by-product [3].
- **Establishment:** Seeded ropes deployed on longlines in autumn (Chile:
  by April) [5]. Submersible longline designs have been modelled for
  high-energy sites [5].
- **Cultivars:** None commercial. The Chilean heterosis lines are research
  material [5]. Tasmania is selecting warm-tolerant strains while trying
  to keep genetic diversity [7]. Its southern populations are more diverse
  than north-eastern ones [7].
- **Weather response:** Strongly negative to warm, nutrient-poor water
  (El Niño and heatwaves) and to large storm waves, which tear plants off
  the seafloor [4]. Poor water clarity worsens heat losses [4]. Frost: not
  applicable.
- **Water regime:** Fully marine, forming a floating canopy kept up by gas
  bladders [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Repeated canopy cuts for alginate [2];
  single harvest at about 5 m+ length for feed or food [5]. Winter harvest
  in Australia balances yield against fouling [5]. Fouling by spores
  arriving naturally from the wild is a "serious" problem in Chilean
  plantations [5].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Urchin grazing is the main biotic
  pressure in the wild (Finding 5) [7].
- **Controlled environment:** Hatchery stage about 10 weeks [5]. Lab
  gametophyte culture for restoration [7]. The depth-cycling elevator is an
  engineered open-ocean system (Finding 4) [6]. No closed-system trial was
  found.

## Hazards

- **Iodine.** About 770–2,160 mg/kg DW: lower than *Laminaria* or
  *Saccharina*, but still very high for food (see `sugar_kelp`,
  `laminaria_digitata`) [8]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** with the other kelps.
- **Arsenic.** Present at levels "typical of brown algae" [8]. In a New
  Zealand comparison of four seaweeds including this one, total arsenic was
  up to 97 mg/kg in some species, but mainly organic. Inorganic arsenic was
  well under New Zealand's limit [3]. A species-specific value wasn't
  extracted.
- **Ecosystem harm from harvest.** Mechanical harvest in California showed
  no detectable canopy effect [2]. Rules still restrict harvest near
  sea-otter pup-rearing rafts and in mixed giant/bull kelp beds [2]. In
  Chile, strong harvest pressure has severely reduced natural beds [5].
- **Climate collapse.** Not a consumer hazard, but a supply one: regional
  forests can vanish (Tasmania, over 95%; southern Baja) (Finding 5)
  [4][7].
- **Biofouling.** Epiflora and epifauna introduced with wild spores cause
  serious fouling on farms [5].
- No fungal, ergot-type or disease hazard was found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (alginate):** surface canopy cut to ≤1.22 m by harvester ships →
  landed → alginate extraction (the Kelco process, 1929 onward) →
  thickeners and gels. A residual pulp by-product is studied for further
  products [2][3].
- **Chain (abalone feed):** farmed or wild kelp → fed fresh to farmed
  abalone. Kelp supply was a "critical issue" for Chile's abalone industry
  [5].
- **Chain (food):** farmed rope kelp → over 70% food-grade → novel food
  products for Asian markets [5].
- **Livestock feed:** dried giant kelp is catalogued as feed (see Composition)
  [3]. California's first kelp harvests (Park Inc., San
  Pedro) were for livestock and poultry feed [2].
- **Cosmetics, biostimulants, fertiliser:** skin products, feed enrichment
  and agricultural biostimulant [3]; organic fertiliser in Chile [5].
- **Biofuel:** a target for bio-crude via hydrothermal liquefaction under
  ARPA-E MARINER (Finding 4) [6]. This was never commercialised. A 1970s–80s
  methane-farming programme was also never realised [5].
- **Herring roe-on-kelp:** a San Francisco Bay fishery uses kelp as the egg
  substrate (December–March) [2]. The kelp species used there wasn't
  confirmed.

  northern vs southern) should be reflected, and whether northern taxa get

  closure, and the 2026 regulation details, weren't read. The CDFW

- **Chilean wild-harvest series.** Not sourced beyond one review figure.

- **Species-specific contaminants.** Arsenic and cadmium values specific to

  be clarified as southern bull kelp (*Durvillaea*), given the North
  American usage.

## Estimates and derivations

**Farmed areal yield, about 100 t FW/ha/year (southern Chile).** Inputs:
41.3 kg wet per m of line a year, with lines 4 m apart [5]. Arithmetic:
10,000 m² / 4 m = 2,500 m of line per ha; 2,500 × 41.3 kg = 103,250 kg ≈
103 t FW/ha/year. This assumes lines fill the whole hectare at 4 m spacing
with no access lanes, so it is an upper bound for that system. Hybrid
yields of 66 kg/m in 5 months [5] at the same spacing would give 165 t/ha
per cycle. That figure was never measured at farm scale and is shown only
for scale. A farm-scale areal measurement would replace this.

**Iodine, about 770–2,160 mg/kg DW.** Input: 6.1–17 mmol I/kg DW [8].
Arithmetic: iodine molar mass 126.9 g/mol; 6.1 × 126.9 = 774 mg/kg;
17 × 126.9 = 2,157 mg/kg. That is roughly a tenth to a third of oarweed's
adult range (2,500–12,000 mg/kg) and below sugar kelp's mean of
4,605 mg/kg (see those docs).

**Harvest-depth rule in metric.** 4 ft × 0.3048 m/ft = 1.22 m [2].
