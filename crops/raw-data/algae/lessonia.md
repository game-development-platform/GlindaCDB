## Naming and scope

This covers the *Lessonia* kelps harvested in Chile and Peru for alginate
[1][2]. It covers the single cropID `lessonia`, promoted from PROPOSED
(flagged in `giant-kelp.md`).

**Species and trade names.**

- **Huiro negro, or chascón** [1]:
  - The intertidal kelp once called *L. nigrescens* [1].
  - Molecular work showed two cryptic species [1]. They were named in
    2012: *L. berteroana* in southern Peru and northern Chile (about
    16–30 °S) and *L. spicata* in central Chile (about 29–42 °S) [1].
  - Fisheries treat them as one resource, "*L. berteroana/spicata*" [2].
- **Huiro palo, or rubio** [1]:
  - *L. trabeculata*, the subtidal kelp [1].
- **Related trade kelps.** Huiro flotador is *Macrocystis* (`giant_kelp`)
  [2], and cochayuyo is *Durvillaea* (`bull_kelp`) [2]. They are managed
  under the same plans.
- **Status-table name.** Kept as *Lessonia* spp., covering both trade
  products (see Open research questions).

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-16).**

- **A distinct genus** from `giant_kelp` and `bull_kelp`.
- **The most exploited seaweed in South America** (*L. berteroana*) [3].
  Chile accounts for about 40% of the world's kelp harvest [3].
- **Its own management system:** Chilean regional management plans with
  quotas, seasonal bans and a beach-cast-only mode (Finding 2) [2].

**Relatives flagged:**

- `giant_kelp` (huiro flotador, co-managed).
- `bull_kelp` (cochayuyo, co-managed).
- No new rows proposed. A split into `huiro_negro` and `huiro_palo` is
  left open.

**Supply is wild-managed** (Finding 2). Saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/lessonia.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — Chile's biggest seaweed harvest, for alginate.**

- **Peak.** Chile's brown seaweed landings hit a record 382,000 t in 2013,
  of which 81.9% was huiro negro [2].
- **Huiro palo.** Over 72,000 t of wet *L. trabeculata* is harvested each
  year for the alginate industry [3].
- **Who.** In the northern zone (Arica and Parinacota to Coquimbo), 6,372
  artisanal fishers are authorised to work these kelps [2].
- **Where it goes.** Exported mostly to Europe and Asia [2].
- **Standing stock example.** A survey of open-access areas in Valparaíso
  found about 115,000 t wet of huiro palo and 40,077 t of huiro negro [2].
- **Why it matters.** A bulk raw material for a global industry, gathered
  by thousands of small-scale fishers.

**Finding 2 — beach-cast, pull or cut: managed by mode.**

- **History.** Until 2013 the fishery was open to all [2]. Law 20.657
  brought regional management plans with management committees [2].
- **Harvest modes** [2]:
  - **Varado:** picking up beach-cast kelp.
  - **Barreteo:** prying whole plants off the rock with a bar.
  - **Segado:** cutting (used for huiro flotador).
  - Quotas are now set separately for each mode [2].
- **Seasonal bans.**
  - In Atacama, the ban covers January, February, April–August, October
    and November for huiro negro [2]. It forbids barreteo and cutting, but
    beach-cast collection continues under a quota [2].
  - Coquimbo has a nine-month ban for huiro negro, a total ban for huiro
    flotador and two months for huiro palo [2].
  - Huiro palo was banned in Atacama in October–December 2020 and 2021 [2].
- **Quotas.** The 2026 Atacama quota for huiro negro is 61,551 t [2].
  Quotas are split by province and by quarter, with a list of
  participating fishers [2].
- **The direction of policy.** The Atacama committee aims to cut active
  removal of huiro negro gradually, in favour of beach-cast collection [2].
- **The catch with beach-cast.**
  - Officials note that beach-cast and pulled kelp come from the same beds;
    one is natural mortality and the other active removal [2].
  - In 2025 one province used 483% of its beach-cast huiro palo quota but
    only 9% of its pulled quota [2]. This suggests pulled kelp may be
    declared as beach-cast (inference).
- **Illegal harvest.** In 2022, companies were investigated for trafficking
  huiro negro and huiro palo taken illegally from a fishers' management
  area [2].
- **Why it matters.** The rules depend on how the kelp is taken, not just
  how much.

**Finding 3 — recovery and harvest strategy.**

- **Size changes by season.** In an unharvested *L. berteroana* bed, median
  size swung from 150 cm in early spring to 40 cm in summer [3].
- **Recovery.** Recruits grew most after total harvest in autumn [3].
- **Huiro palo.** Long-term studies in Atacama support periodic removal of
  older, senescent plants to keep the industry going [3].
- **Harvest shapes the forest.** Harvest intensity changes the population
  structure of *L. trabeculata* along the Chilean coast [3].
- **Indicator.** A limpet (*Scurria*) association with huiro negro is
  proposed as a field sign of bed health [3]. El Niño is a disturbance to
  these beds [3].

**Finding 4 — composition.**

- *L. trabeculata* alginate and proximate composition differ between
  holdfast and stipe and over the season [4]. Its protein suggests uses
  beyond alginate [4].
- The two huiro negro species differ in lipids, phlorotannins and pigments
  [1].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelps (lifespan not sourced).
- **Harvest pattern:** Year-round beach-cast collection; active harvest in
  open months under quota [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Humboldt Current coast of Peru and Chile, about 15–42
  °S [1].
- **Composition:** Alginate, varying by organ and season [4].
- **Edibility:** Not sourced as food.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Standing stocks up to 115,000 t wet per region surveyed [2].
- **Water use:** Marine.
- **Propagation:** Natural recruitment [3]; experimental transplants and
  mariculture studies [3].
- **Labor:** Artisanal; on foot and diving [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Intertidal (huiro negro) and subtidal (huiro
  palo) rock [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced in detail; dried before export
  (Not sourced).
- **Establishment:** Not applicable (wild).
- **Cultivars:** None.
- **Weather response:** El Niño disturbs beds [3].
- **Water regime:** Marine; wave-exposed intertidal and shallow subtidal
  [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Set by ban months [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native.
- **Controlled environment:** None.

## Hazards

- **Food:** Not a food crop here; iodine and arsenic are covered in
  `giant-kelp.md`.
- **Ecological:** Over-harvest and illegal harvest (Finding 2) [2].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** beach-cast collection or barreteo → landing declared by mode
  → drying and export → alginate extraction abroad [2].

## Open research questions

- **Split the row?** Into `huiro_negro` (*L. berteroana/spicata*,
  intertidal) and `huiro_palo` (*L. trabeculata*, subtidal). They are
  harvested and banned differently [2].
- **Drying and price:** Not sourced.
- **Peru's fishery:** Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Huiro negro landings in 2013, about 313,000 t.** Inputs: 382,000 t brown
seaweed landings [2] × 81.9% huiro negro [2] = 312,858 t.
