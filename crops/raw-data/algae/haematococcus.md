## Naming and scope

This covers the single-celled freshwater green alga grown commercially for
the red pigment astaxanthin [1][2]. It covers the single cropID
`haematococcus`.

**Names.**

- **Correct name *H. lacustris*.** A 2016 study showed that *H.
  pluvialis* and *H. lacustris* are the same species and that *H.
  lacustris* is the correct name [1]. Applied and industry papers still
  mostly say *H. pluvialis*, and strains stay registered under it [1].
  Status-table name updated to *Haematococcus lacustris* (formerly *H.
  pluvialis*).
- **More than one species in the old name.** DNA work found several
  lineages; some are now separate species (*H. rubicundus*, *H. rubens*,
  *H. privus*) [1]. So a strain sold as *H. pluvialis* may not be *H.
  lacustris* [1].
- **Wild life.** A pioneer of shallow, temporary pools (rain pools, rock
  hollows, birdbaths) worldwide, spread by air [1]. In rock pools it forms
  a blood-red crust [1].

**Relatives flagged:** `chlorella`, `nannochloropsis` and `dunaliella`
(other farmed microalgae). `dunaliella` (next in the queue) is the other
stress-induced carotenoid crop (beta-carotene). No new rows proposed.

**Supply is farmed** (Finding 2), so this is not a wild-managed resource.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — grown green, then stressed red: a two-stage crop.**

- **Two stages.** A green, swimming stage where cells divide, and a red,
  non-motile resting stage (cyst) where division stops and astaxanthin
  and dry weight keep rising [2].
- **Commercial method.** Production grows green biomass in good
  conditions, then switches the culture to stress (high light, no
  nitrate) to make it turn red [2].
- **Timing.** In outdoor two-stage production, a 10-day cycle (4 days
  green, 6 days red) can reach 8–10 mg astaxanthin per litre a day, with
  astaxanthin up to 4% of dry weight [2].
- **Ceiling.** Under strong stress astaxanthin can reach about 5% of dry
  weight, and up to 7.7% with 5% CO₂ [2].
- **Die-off.** Many cells die in the first days after the switch to the
  red stage, which keeps overall productivity low [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop with a deliberate "stress to ripen" step;
  a model would have two phases with different inputs.

**Finding 2 — a niche, high-value pigment crop, competing with cheap
synthetic astaxanthin.**

- **Producers.** Algatech (Kibbutz Ketura, Israel; closed tubes), Cyanotech
  (Kona, Hawaii; open ponds) and Fuji Chemical (Toyama, Japan) founded a
  natural-astaxanthin trade association [3]. Others include Algalif
  (Iceland, closed facility), Beijing Gingko Group, Atacama Bio (Chile) and
  Piveg (Mexico) [3].
- **Scale.** Algalif's new 12,500 m² facility opened in late 2023, raising
  capacity from 1.5 to 5 t of pure astaxanthin a year [3].
- **Synthetic rival.** Synthetic astaxanthin, made from petrochemicals,
  has long been the main salmon-feed colourant [3]. Algal astaxanthin
  dominates human supplements [3].
- **Market.** Feed is the largest use by volume, about 62% in 2024 by one
  market report [3] (low weight: commercial market report).
- **Why it matters.** The same pigment can come from a farm or a factory;
  natural sells at a premium for supplements.

**Finding 3 — a fungal parasite can wipe out a whole culture.**

- **Pathogen.** The blastocladialean fungus *Paraphysoderma
  sedebokerense* often attacks commercial *Haematococcus*, causing culture
  crashes and economic loss [4].
- **Both stages.** Its spores caused epidemics in both green and red
  cells; in red cells it spread faster when nitrogen was present [4].
- **Resistance.** A sensitive strain gained resistance over several
  generations of infection by shifting to a mostly swimming (flagellated)
  phenotype, which stayed stable for months [4].
- **Why it matters.** A crop disease with no field-crop analogue; a
  natural "plague" event for the game.

**Finding 4 — tough cysts, which need cracking and extraction.**

- **Cyst wall.** The red cysts build thick, rigid walls containing
  algaenan [2]. Breaking them is hard even with harsh treatments, and
  losses of astaxanthin follow [2].
- **Product.** The EU-authorised food ingredient is an oleoresin extracted
  with supercritical CO₂ from spray-dried algal meal [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Single cells, 8–20 µm in the swimming stage [1];
  year-round culture in controlled systems [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Batch: grow green, induce red, harvest the red
  cysts [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 10 days per outdoor cycle (4 green
  + 6 red) [2].
- **Climate zones:** Wild in temperate regions worldwide [1]; farmed from
  the Negev desert (Israel) to Hawaii and Iceland [3].
- **Composition:** Astaxanthin 4% of dry weight typical, up to about 5–7.7%
  under stress [2].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as astaxanthin supplements (oleoresin, softgels) [5].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sexual reproduction is rarely observed;
  reproduction is mostly vegetative [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** High light triggers the red stage [2].
- **Yield:** 8–10 mg astaxanthin per litre a day in outdoor two-stage
  culture [2]. Area yields: Not sourced.
- **Water use:** Freshwater culture; volumes Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** From a starter culture of green cells; red cysts
  germinate again when conditions improve [1].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for biomass; supplements are
  oleoresin [5].
- **Establishment:** Green stage at low light; red stage at high light,
  often with 5–15% CO₂ [2].
- **Cultivars:** Proprietary strains; one *Paraphysoderma*-resistant line
  selected in research [4].
- **Weather response:** Cysts form above about 30 °C, under high light,
  salt or nutrient stress [1]. Desiccated red resting cells survived −80 °C
  and 55 °C in lab tests [1].
- **Water regime:** Freshwater [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Harvest when cells are red cysts, at the
  end of the red stage [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. *Paraphysoderma* fungus (Finding 3) [4].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Already cosmopolitan and air-dispersed
  [1]; no invasive record found. Biosecurity concern runs inward: keeping
  the fungal parasite out of cultures [4].
- **Controlled environment:** Closed tubes (Israel), closed indoor
  facilities (Iceland) and open ponds (Hawaii) all in commercial use [3].

## Hazards

- **Dose limit.** EFSA's acceptable daily intake for astaxanthin is
  0.2 mg/kg body weight [5]. With normal diet, 8 mg a day from supplements
  is safe for adults and teenagers over 14; children aged 10–14 would
  exceed the ADI by 28% [5]. EU supplements must be labelled for adults
  [5].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** green culture → red stress stage → harvest cysts → dry
  (spray-dried meal) → supercritical CO₂ extraction → oleoresin →
  supplements [2][5].
- **Uses:** Human supplements [3][5]; fish feed colourant (mostly
  synthetic, some algal) [3]; cosmetics [3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Algalif biomass, at least about 125 t a year.** Inputs: 5 t pure
astaxanthin capacity [3]; about 4% astaxanthin in dry biomass [2].
5 / 0.04 = 125 t dry biomass, assuming full capacity and 4% content (more
if content is lower or losses occur in extraction).

**Per-litre output of one outdoor cycle, about 80–100 mg.** Inputs: 8–10 mg
per litre a day over 10 days [2]. 8 × 10 = 80; 10 × 10 = 100 mg/L. At 4%
astaxanthin that is 2–2.5 g dry biomass per litre.
