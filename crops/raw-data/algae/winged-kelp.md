## Naming and scope

This covers *Alaria esculenta* (Linnaeus) Greville, a brown kelp of cold,
wave-exposed North Atlantic and Arctic shores [1]. It covers the single
cropID `winged_kelp`, promoted from PROPOSED (flagged in `sugar-kelp.md`
and `wakame.md`).

**Names.**

- **Meaning.** *Alaria esculenta* means "edible wings" [1]. It was first
  described as *Fucus esculentus* (Linnaeus, 1767) [1].
- **English and Scots.** Winged kelp, dabberlocks, badderlocks [1].
  Honeyware or henware [1]. "Atlantic wakame" in trade [1].
- **Irish.** Láir, láracha, murlins [1].
- **Synonym.** *Alaria platyrhiza* [1].

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-16).**

- **A different genus and family from `sugar_kelp`.** It is in Alariaceae,
  with wakame (`wakame.md`). Sugar kelp is in Laminariaceae (`sugar-kelp.md`).
- **One of the two main farmed kelps in Europe.** It and sugar kelp are
  the most cultivated marine algae in Europe [2].
- **Its own food profile.** It has a midrib, eats like wakame (Finding 3)
  [1], and has less iodine than sugar kelp (Finding 4) [3].
- **Not wakame.** It is the Atlantic counterpart of Japanese *Undaria*
  [1][2], but a different genus with its own range and farming season.

**Relatives flagged:**

- `sugar_kelp` (co-farmed).
- `wakame` (same family).
- No new rows proposed. The genus has twelve species [1], but only *A.
  esculenta* is in the Atlantic [1].

**Not wild-managed.** It is mainly farmed in Europe [2]. Wild harvest also
occurs, for example in Maine [2], but no managed-harvest system was found.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a fast winter crop on ropes.**

- **Season.** Deploy seeded rope in autumn and harvest in spring or early
  summer [2]. In Bantry Bay, Ireland, rope deployed on 15 October gave more
  than 20 kg/m by June [2]. A line deployed six weeks later never caught up
  (about 14 kg/m) [2].
- **Size.** Farmed fronds reached about 3.6 m by early April [2]. Wild
  plants grow to about 2 m [1].
- **Strain matters.** In an Irish hybrid trial [2]:
  - A Canadian self-cross gave over 45 kg/m.
  - An Iceland × Ireland cross gave 13.75 kg/m.
  - An Aran Islands strain gave 7.4 kg/m.
  - Crosses with Norwegian female gametophytes grew fastest.
- **Fouling sets the end of the season.** Epiphytes start settling in May,
  and by June they compromise quality [2]. Late harvests also suffer frond
  erosion [2].
- **Offshore.** In the Faroe Islands, an offshore longline rig built for
  exposed sites has been tested since 2010 [2]. Kelp there can be cut
  several times without re-seeding [2].
- **Scale.** Yearly production was 24 t fresh weight in Ireland and 44 t
  in Norway, the largest European producers [2]. These are small figures,
  from the cited source's own date.
- **Why it matters.** A crop for exposed northern coasts, planted in
  autumn and gathered in spring.

**Finding 2 — the hatchery cycle.**

- **Spores.** Fertile sporophylls (the small "wings" at the stipe base)
  were collected from a wild bed in spring and kept dark at 10 °C [2].
- **Gametophytes.** Spores were warmed to 14 °C and kept under constant
  dim light until autumn [2].
- **Seeding.** In October, cooling to 10 °C on a 12:12 light cycle
  triggered fertilisation [2]. Eight days later the culture was blended,
  sprayed onto string, and the string was wound onto ropes at sea [2].
- **Why it matters.** Seed is held over summer as microscopic
  gametophytes, which suits a hatchery-and-sea farming loop (inference).

**Finding 3 — a traditional northern food.**

- **Eaten in.** Greenland, Iceland, Scotland and Ireland, fresh or cooked
  [1]. In Ireland, Scotland and Iceland it was served as a vegetable or
  salad leaf [1].
- **Preparation.** In parts of the north Atlantic, the midrib was removed
  and the blade and sometimes the sporophylls were eaten [1].
- **Modern use.** Sold as "Atlantic wakame" for wakame-style dishes [1].
  Young plants can substitute for *Undaria* [2].
- **Feed.** It can replace about 5–10% of conventional protein in poultry
  feed [1]. Low weight (retailer source).
- **Why it matters.** A North Atlantic sea vegetable with a long food
  history.

**Finding 4 — less iodine than sugar kelp, but watch cadmium.**

- **Iodine.** Iodine runs sugar kelp > winged kelp > dulse [3]. Only about
  283 mg of dry winged kelp meets the daily iodine recommendation, against
  32 mg of sugar kelp [3]. It is described as low in iodine for a kelp [3].
- **Cadmium.**
  - Norwegian farmed samples reached 2.01 mg/kg dry weight [3]. That
    exceeds the French food-safety limit [3], but its contribution to
    dietary cadmium was not judged a threat [3].
  - Soaking in strong brine (2.0 M NaCl) reduced cadmium [3].
  - Irish samples averaged 1.03 mg/kg [3].
- **Arsenic.** Total arsenic averaged 57.9 mg/kg dry weight in Irish
  samples [3]. Inorganic arsenic was low in Norwegian samples [3].
- **Bantry Bay farmed product.** Cadmium and lead were below the EU limit
  for dried seaweed supplements (3 mg/kg); mercury was below 0.011 mg/kg
  [2].
- **Why it matters.** A safer everyday kelp than sugar kelp for iodine.

**Finding 5 — a cold-water kelp at its southern edge.**

- **Range.** North Atlantic from Novaya Zemlya to Iceland, and south to
  Brittany [1]. West Atlantic from Greenland to the Bering Strait [1]. In
  Britain it is absent from the south and east of England [1].
- **Habitat.** Forms the main canopy on exposed rocky shores [1]. Storms
  and winter ice can strip whole beds [1].
- **Temperature.** In the north-west Atlantic it lives in water from −2 °C
  to about 24 °C [4]. Warming may already have pushed its range north up
  the US East Coast over 50 years [4].
- **Heat-hardy strains.** Gametophytes from southern Maine showed signs of
  heat adaptation [4]. Some survived far more warming than predicted for
  the Gulf of Maine [4].
- **Why it matters.** Strain choice is a climate lever.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelp in the wild [1]; farmed as an
  autumn-to-spring crop [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Single spring–early summer harvest, or repeated
  partial cuts offshore [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 5–7 months from autumn deployment
  to spring harvest (inference from the dates in [2]).
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate to Arctic; −2 to about 24 °C [4].
- **Composition (dried, farmed):** Protein 11.2–11.8%; ash about 32%;
  lipid 1.8–2.3% [2].
- **Edibility:** Edible; blade and sporophylls, midrib often removed [1].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Zoospores from basal sporophylls [1][4].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Phenolic content falls as spring light
  rises [2].
- **Yield:** 7.4 to over 45 kg/m of rope, by strain [2]; about 20 kg/m in
  Bantry Bay [2].
- **Water use:** Marine.
- **Propagation:** Hatchery gametophytes sprayed onto seed string [2].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Exposed rock in the wild [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried, oven or freeze [2].
- **Establishment:** Seeded string wound onto longlines in autumn [2].
- **Cultivars:** Regional strains and hybrids (Finding 1) [2]; heat-tolerant
  Maine isolates [4].
- **Weather response:** Wave-exposed in the wild [1]; storms and ice can
  remove beds [1].
- **Water regime:** Marine, subtidal, wave-exposed.
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Early harvest gives best quality; late
  harvest brings fouling [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Epiphyte fouling from May [2].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native; no record found.
- **Controlled environment:** Hatchery for gametophytes only [2].

## Hazards

- **Cadmium** can exceed some national limits (Finding 4) [3].
- **Iodine:** Lower than sugar kelp, but still high (Finding 4) [3].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** hatchery → seeded rope in autumn → spring harvest → dry or
  blanch [2].
- **Uses:** Sea vegetable and wakame substitute [1][2]; poultry feed [1];
  extracts with antioxidant phenolics [2].

## Estimates and derivations

**Iodine content, about 530 mg/kg dry weight.** Inputs: 283 mg of dry
winged kelp meets the recommended daily intake [3]. Assuming an adult
recommendation of 150 µg (an assumption, not from the source): 150 µg ÷
0.283 g ≈ 530 µg/g ≈ 530 mg/kg. The same calculation gives about 4,700
mg/kg for sugar kelp (150 ÷ 0.032).
