## Naming and scope

This covers *Nereocystis luetkeana* (K. Mertens) Postels & Ruprecht, the
canopy kelp of North America's Pacific coast [1]. It covers the single
cropID `nereocystis`, promoted from PROPOSED (flagged in `giant-kelp.md`
and `bull-kelp.md`).

**Names.**

- **Meaning.** *Nereocystis* is Greek for "mermaid's bladder" [1]. The
  species honours the explorer Fyodor Litke [1]. It is the only species in
  its genus [1].
- **English.** Bull kelp, bullwhip kelp, edible kelp, ribbon kelp [1].
- **Name clash.** "Bull kelp" also means the southern bull kelps
  (*Durvillaea*) [1]. In this project that is `bull_kelp` (`bull-kelp.md`).
  The cropID `nereocystis` avoids the clash.

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-16).**

- **Not a duplicate of `bull_kelp`.** *Durvillaea* is in a different order
  (Fucales; see `bull-kelp.md`). *Nereocystis* is a true kelp
  (Laminariales) [1].
- **A different growth form from `giant_kelp`.** It has a single float on
  a long hollow stipe, where giant kelp has many floats [1]. It is usually
  annual [1].
- **Its own fishery and rules:** licensed wild harvest in British Columbia
  and US states (Finding 3) [2].
- **Its own food product:** bullwhip kelp pickles (Finding 4) [3].

**Relatives flagged:**

- `giant_kelp` (cross-linked).
- `bull_kelp` (name clash; cross-linked).
- `winged_kelp` (BC licence groups pair bull kelp with *Alaria marginata*
  [2]). Cross-linked.
- No new rows proposed.

**Supply is wild-managed** (Finding 3). Saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/nereocystis.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — an annual giant with one float.**

- **Size.** Up to 36 m long [1]. The holdfast is about 40 cm across [1].
- **Shape.** A single hollow stipe ends in one large float (pneumatocyst),
  which holds carbon monoxide [1]. About 30–64 blades grow from the float,
  each up to 4 m long [1].
- **Strength.** The stipe can stretch more than 38% of its length before
  breaking [1].
- **Lifespan.** Usually annual, sometimes persisting up to 18 months [1].
  Forests largely die back in winter and reappear in spring [1].
- **Growth.** Blades grow up to 15 cm a day [1]. Including fronds, growth
  of at least 25 cm a day has been recorded [1]. Low weight.
- **Spores.** Spore patches (sori) form on the blades and drop to the sea
  floor, so spores land near the parent [1]. Sori sometimes release young
  sporophytes directly [1].
- **Range.** From the Aleutian Islands, Alaska, to central California [1].
  It is the most abundant kelp in Oregon, Washington, British Columbia and
  Alaska [2]. It is the dominant kelp in northern California, at the
  southern end of its range [4].
- **Habitat.** Rock from the low intertidal to subtidal, in semi-exposed or
  high-current sites [1]. Forests grow in shallow rocky habitat under 30 m
  [2].
- **Why it matters.** A forest that regrows from spores every year.

**Finding 2 — northern California lost over 90% of it.**

- **What happened.** In 2014, bull kelp canopy fell by more than 90% along
  more than 350 km of northern California coast [4].
- **Causes** [4]:
  - Sea star wasting disease from 2013 wiped out the sunflower star, a
    major urchin predator.
  - The 2014–2017 marine heatwave.
  - Purple sea urchins rose about 60-fold between 2014 and 2015.
- **Knock-on effects.** About 80% of red abalone died in 2017 [4]. The
  recreational abalone fishery (worth about US$44 million) closed in 2018
  [4]. The commercial red urchin fishery (about US$3 million) collapsed
  [4].
- **Restoration.** Projects in the Salish Sea test nursery seeding and
  outplanting [4].
- **Why it matters.** A crash that wiped out a dependent fishery: harvest
  the kelp, lose the abalone.

**Finding 3 — licensed hand harvest, cut above the float.**

- **The cutting rule.** Cutting near the float kills the plant and can stop
  it releasing spores [2]. Because the plant is annual, cutting off the top
  before spores drop can stop reproduction [2]. In British Columbia it has
  been recommended to harvest after spore release [2].
- **British Columbia.**
  - Commercial harvest needs a licence that sets species, quota, method
    and area [2].
  - All harvest is by hand [2]. Harvesters must prune, cutting a set
    distance from the holdfast or stipe [2].
  - Applicants submit a harvest plan per species group and management area
    [2]. Bull kelp is Group 1 [2].
- **Other jurisdictions (older report).** Across Alaska, Washington,
  Oregon, California and BC there were about 20 permits and under 350 t
  harvested a year [2]. Oregon set no limits [2]. These figures date from
  a report of about the mid-2000s.
- **Sustainability rating.** Seafood Watch assessed BC hand-harvested bull
  and giant kelp in February 2025 [2].
- **Uses of the harvest.** Food, liquid fertiliser and feed for farmed
  abalone [1].
- **Why it matters.** Harvest rules turn on where you cut and when.

**Finding 4 — food: bullwhip kelp pickles.**

- **Pickles.** The University of Alaska Fairbanks extension service
  publishes dill and sweet pickle recipes using kelp rings or strips,
  canned in a boiling-water bath [3].
- **Prized for eating.** Valued for its unique palatability [3].
- **Why it matters.** A "sea vegetable" from the hollow stipe.

**Finding 5 — farming is just starting in Alaska.**

- **Hard to farm.** Its hollow, very buoyant stipe means standard kelp
  arrays haven't given a good product [5]. A Bull Kelp Research Squad of
  farmers and researchers formed in Alaska to work on this [5].
- **Co-culture trial (Kodiak).** Lines seeded in early February 2023 were
  harvested in late June 2023 [5]. Combined arrays of bull kelp and sugar
  kelp yielded most, then bull kelp alone, then sugar kelp alone [5].
- **Northern edge.** A Prince William Sound trial notes that its growing
  conditions there, at the north of its range, are not well understood
  [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual kelp, sometimes up to 18 months [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Hand-cut canopy, ideally after spore release [2].
  Farmed: spring to early summer [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Within one season [1].
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate north-east Pacific, Alaska to central
  California [1].
- **Composition:** Not sourced.
- **Edibility:** Edible; stipe pickled [3].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sori drop near the parent [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Not sourced per area (see Estimates).
- **Water use:** Marine; avoids low-salinity water [1].
- **Propagation:** Wild recruitment from spores [1]; nursery-seeded line
  for farms and restoration [4][5].
- **Labor:** Hand harvest [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Rock [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Pickled and canned [3].
- **Establishment:** Seeded lines set out in winter [5].
- **Cultivars:** None.
- **Weather response:** Marine heatwaves cause collapse (Finding 2) [4].
- **Water regime:** Marine, low intertidal to under 30 m [1][2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** After spore release [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Grazed by purple urchins [4].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native.
- **Controlled environment:** Nursery seeding only [4][5].

## Hazards

- **Ecological:** Over-cutting before spore release stops reproduction
  (Finding 3) [2].
- **Food:** Not sourced (iodine not found for this species).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** licensed hand harvest → cut and rinse → pickle or can, or
  process for fertiliser or abalone feed [1][2][3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Average harvest per permit, under about 17.5 t a year (older data).**
Inputs: under 350 t a year [2] ÷ about 20 permits [2] ≈ 17.5 t. This is an
average ceiling, and the data are from about the mid-2000s.
