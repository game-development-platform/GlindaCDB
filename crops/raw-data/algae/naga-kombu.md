## Naming and scope

This covers *Saccharina longissima* (Miyabe) C.E. Lane, C. Mayes, Druehl &
G.W. Saunders, formerly *Laminaria longissima* [1]. In Japanese it is
ナガコンブ (naga kombu, "long kombu"), and the young-season harvest is
sold as サオマエコンブ (saomae kombu) [2][3]. An English name is
"ribbon weed" [1]. It covers the single cropID `naga_kombu`, promoted from
PROPOSED (flagged in `kelp-kombu.md`).

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-15).**

- **A separate species.** A genome resequencing study treats it as a
  sister species to *S. japonica*, in the same lineage as *S. angustata*
  [1]. One deposited "*S. longissima*" sequence matched an *S. japonica*
  haplotype exactly. Its authors called that a misidentification, not
  evidence against the species [1].
- **It is a parent of farmed kombu.** Chinese hybrid cultivars used it as a
  parent, as noted in `kelp-kombu.md`.
- **Its own range.** Shallow Pacific coasts from eastern Hokkaido (Kushiro,
  Nemuro) to the Kuril (Chishima) Islands [1].
- **Its own uses and harvests.** It is an eating kombu, not a dashi kombu
  (Finding 2). It also has a distinct young-plant harvest and a fishery in
  Russian-held waters (Finding 3).

**Relatives flagged:**

- `kelp_kombu` (*S. japonica*).
- `mitsuishi_kombu` (*S. angustata*), its sister species [1].
- No new rows proposed. Other eastern-Hokkaido kombu (*S. coriacea*,
  gaggara kombu, and others) [1] could be proposed later if wanted.

**Supply is wild-managed** (Finding 1). Saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/naga-kombu.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the bulk wild kombu of eastern Hokkaido.**

- **Volume.** Kushiro and Nemuro produce about 10,000 t of dry kombu a
  year, slowly declining [1]. Naga kombu is assumed to be about 70% of that
  [1].
- **Wild-harvested.** Plants in their second year (age 1+) and older are
  cut with long-handled gear from June to October [1].
- **Weeding the sea bed.** Clearing other seaweeds is described as an
  indispensable part of managing kombu grounds, but costly [2]. One method
  uses rotating chains to scrape weeds off the rock [2].
  - Researchers mapped growth potential along more than 50 km of the
    Habomai–Ochiishi coast [2]. Clearing is prioritised where kombu should
    grow well but other seaweeds have taken over [2].
  - The best grounds are steep, wave-exposed places with fast bottom
    currents [2].
- **Long-term records.** The Habomai co-op weighed blades every May and
  June from 2000 to 2014, along more than 15 km of coast [1].
- **Why it matters.** A managed commons, like `mitsuishi_kombu`, but
  larger.

**Finding 2 — the long "kombu for eating".**

- **Size.** Blades grow to more than 10 m long [1]. A newspaper gives up
  to 15 m [3].
- **Low glutamate.** It has less glutamic and aspartic acid than other
  kombu, so it's poor for dashi [2]. It cooks soft, so it's used in kombu
  rolls, tsukudani and oden [2].
- **Okinawa.** Kūbu irichī, shredded naga kombu stir-simmered with pork,
  is an Okinawan dish needed at weddings and celebrations [3]. In the Edo
  period, Hokkaido kombu travelled by ship to western Japan, Ryukyu and on
  to China [3].
- **Why it matters.** A trade good with a long sea route, and the kombu of
  southern feasts (inference).

**Finding 3 — saomae and the Kaigara Island fishery.**

- **Saomae kombu.** This is naga kombu taken while still growing, before
  about early July [3]. Its flesh is soft and it's valued for simmered
  dishes [3]. In Kushiro, the saomae harvest is described as thinning naga
  kombu partway through growth [3].
- **Kaigara Island (貝殻島).** A saomae fishery runs around this islet in
  the Russian-held Habomai group, 3.7 km off Cape Nosappu [3]. It operates
  under a private Japan–Russia agreement [3].
  - **History.** It began in 1963, and 2026 was its 60th season after four
    interruptions [3].
  - **Fee and catch.** In 2026, 181 Nemuro boats paid Russia about ¥76.67
    million for a 3,024 t catch of kombu and other seaweed [3]. The season
    runs from 1 June to the end of September [3].
  - **Start.** Boats leave together at a siren and fireworks signal [3].
  - **Disruption.** In 2022 the Ukraine war delayed the start by three weeks
    [3].
  - **Branding.** Kaigara-jima saomae is a premium brand [3].
- **Why it matters.** An early-harvest stage with its own product, and a
  fishery that depends on diplomacy.

**Finding 4 — growth tied to the Oyashio.**

- **Growth curve.** Regrowth starts about November [1]. It grows 5–10 mm a
  day until March, peaking at 68 mm a day in May–June [1].
- **Temperature.** Warm water the previous autumn and very cold water the
  previous winter–spring both reduce summer blade weight [1]. Autumn sea
  temperatures off Kushiro have risen since 2000 [1].
- **Nutrients.** Growth rises further east, which the authors put down to
  nutrients from the Oyashio current [1].
- **Disaster.** The 2011 tsunami probably explains that year's shortfall
  [1].
- **Range.** Water in its range runs from below 0 °C to 20 °C [1].
- **Why it matters.** Yield swings with last year's weather.

**Finding 5 — drying is the bottleneck; freezing is being tested.**

- **Fishers do everything.** They harvest, dry, cut, sort and pack it
  themselves, and drying takes the most time and labour [2]. Whole
  families take part in drying [2].
- **Frozen kombu trial.** Strips were frozen and stored at −10 °C or
  −30 °C for up to 12 months [2].
  - Thawing gave 83–84% yield, against 92% for soaked dried kombu [2].
  - Frozen kombu kept more minerals and mannitol [2] but cooked softer [2].
  - A taste panel could tell frozen from dried kombu rolls but showed no
    overall preference [2].
- **Why it matters.** Skipping the drying step would cut the labour that
  keeps young people out of kombu fishing [3].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelp; harvested from age 1+ [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Saomae from 1 June to early July [3]; main harvest
  June–October [1]. Harvest in the freezing study was July–August [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Second year (age 1+) [1].
- **Climate zones:** Cold Pacific coast of eastern Hokkaido and the Kurils;
  water below 0 °C to 20 °C [1].
- **Composition (raw, wet weight):** 11.4% solids; ash 3.8%; alginic acid
  3.3%; mannitol 2.2%; crude protein 0.9% [2]. Low in glutamate [2].
- **Edibility:** Edible; eating kombu (Finding 2).
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Blade wet weight averaged 703 g in May and 1,038 g in June at
  Habomai [1]. About 7,000 t dry a year regionally (Estimates).
- **Water use:** Marine.
- **Propagation:** Natural recruitment on cleared rock [2].
- **Labor:** High, especially drying [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Rock in the lower intertidal to upper subtidal
  [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried; frozen storage trialled up to 12
  months [2].
- **Establishment:** Not sourced.
- **Cultivars:** None; wild. Kaigara-jima saomae is a brand [3].
- **Weather response:** Fishing starts are delayed by storms, drift ice and
  cold springs [3]. Temperature effects (Finding 4) [1].
- **Water regime:** Marine, wave-exposed rocky shore [1][2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Saomae (growing, soft) vs mature naga
  kombu (Finding 3).
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native; no record found.
- **Controlled environment:** None found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** boat harvest with long poles → beach drying → cutting →
  sorting → packing [1][2].
- **Products:** Dried kombu for rolls, tsukudani and oden [2]; saomae
  kombu [3]; kūbu irichī in Okinawa [3]; frozen kombu trial [2].

## Estimates and derivations

- **Regional naga kombu output, about 7,000 t dry a year.** Inputs: about
  10,000 t of kombu from Kushiro and Nemuro [1] × 70% naga kombu [1] ≈
  7,000 t.
- **Kaigara-jima fee, about ¥25,000 per tonne (2026).** Inputs: ¥76.67
  million [3] ÷ 3,024 t [3] ≈ ¥25,354/t. The tonnage includes other
  seaweed.
