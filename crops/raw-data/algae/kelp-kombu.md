# Kombu / Japanese kelp (*Saccharina japonica*)

## Naming and scope

This covers *Saccharina japonica* (J.E. Areschoug) C.E. Lane, C. Mayes,
Druehl & G.W. Saunders, a brown alga (class Phaeophyceae, order
Laminariales, family Laminariaceae), known in English as Japanese kelp or
sweet kelp, in Japanese as kombu/konbu or ma-kombu, in Chinese as haidai
and in Korean as dashima/dasima [1]. It was moved from *Laminaria* to
*Saccharina* in 2006, so most cultivation and food-safety literature
(including FAO's own material) still calls it *Laminaria japonica* [1][2].
This doc covers the single cropID `kelp_kombu`.

**Taxonomy is still moving.** A 2008 re-examination merged four
northern-Japanese "species" (*L. japonica*, *L. religiosa*, *L. ochotensis*,
*L. diabolica*) into varieties of one species [1]. A 2026 *Journal of
Phycology* study of 475 wild individuals then proposed merging those
varieties into *S. japonica* itself, i.e. var. *diabolica* (Oni/Rausu
kombu), var. *ochotensis* (Rishiri kombu) and var. *religiosa* (Hosome
kombu) [1]. That study was only read through a news summary and an
encyclopedia citation, not opened. All four are treated here as one crop,
and their real differences go in the Cultivars line. Their differences are
mostly culinary and in price, but they do come from distinct coasts.

**Relatives flagged, not covered here:**

- **Mitsuishi / Hidaka kombu (*Saccharina angustata*).** A separate, closely related
  species [1] sold as kombu. Sources disagree on whether it is mainly a
  dashi kombu or an everyday cooking kombu [1][15]. Proposed as `mitsuishi_kombu`.
- **Naga kombu (*Saccharina longissima*).** A separate species sold as
  kombu [1]. It also matters genetically: it was a parent of Chinese
  interspecific hybrid cultivars (Dongfang no. 2, variety 90-1), and the
  genome study found Chinese cultivars carry limited *S. longissima*
  admixture [10]. Proposed as `naga_kombu`.
- **Sugar kelp (*S. latissima*).** Already its own listed row. It is
  cross-linked because the female parent of the Chinese cultivar
  'Ailunwan' was itself selected from an *S. japonica* × *S. latissima*
  cross [10].

Several other species are also sold as "kombu" (*S. coriacea*,
*S. sculpera*, *S. longipedalis*, *S. gyrata*, *S. cichorioides*,
*Arthrothamnus bifidus*) [1]. They are only named here; none was researched
in this pass.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — two very different generations, and farming squeezes a
two-winter wild life into one winter.** The harvested plant is the large
diploid sporophyte. It alternates with microscopic haploid male and female
gametophytes of one to a few cells [2]. In the wild a plant lives about two
years across two winters (fronds up to three years). Farmed kombu is raised
through only one winter [2][3]. In Japan, one-year farmed kombu is called
*sokusei* ("forced") kombu, harvested in about 10 months against two years
for wild kombu [5]. Japanese cooks treat the two as different products:
one-year farmed kombu is softer and suited to dishes where the kombu is
eaten, while two-year kombu is closer to wild for dashi. Wild kombu sells
for more than three times the price of farmed [5].

**Finding 2 — a cold-water crop whose key stage now runs in chilled
indoor hatcheries.** Chinese farming moved from "autumn seedlings" to
"summer seedlings" in the late 1950s, which FAO credits with the sector's
rapid growth [2].

- **Old method (autumn seedlings).** Zoospores were collected in
  mid-October, once the sea fell below 20 °C [3].
- **New method (summer seedlings).** Zoospores are collected in mid-July.
  Gametophytes and young sporophytes are then grown for about three months
  in greenhouse tanks of seawater cooled to 8–10 °C. Seedlings go to sea at
  about 2–5 cm in October–November, when the sea cools to about 20 °C
  [2][3].
- **Effect.** The growing season stretches to about nine months (mid-October
  to mid-July), and yield rose by about 40% [3].

The hatchery scale is industrial. One FAO figure is about 800 million
seedlings a year. Each 50 m sporeling curtain carries 40,000–50,000
seedlings and costs about US$0.50–0.60 [2].

**Finding 3 — the open sea itself gets fertilised.** In the nutrient-poor
outer Yellow Sea (about 2–3 mg/m³ dissolved nitrogen), growth is visibly
impaired below 5 mg/m³, and rapid growth needs more than 20 mg/m³ [3].
Chinese farms therefore sprayed or soaked kelp with nitrogen fertiliser
such as ammonium nitrate, typically once total nitrogen fell below
100 mg/m³ [2]. Plants were soaked for about 15 minutes every 3–5 days,
since they take up several days' nitrogen in a few hours [3].

Early fertiliser trials took output from 2,350 t fresh weight (1956) to
31,604 t fresh / 5,267 t dry (1958), which FAO's manual reports as a 1,345%
increase in two years [3]. The southern farming waters off Zhejiang and
Fujian carry 6–11 times as much nitrogen as Qingdao and need no fertiliser
[3]. In contrast, a Hokkaido source describes Japanese kombu farming as
needing no feeding or fertilising [5].

**Finding 4 — farmed hundreds of kilometres south of its native range, and
losing its native range to warming.** Native waters reach south to about
36°N, but Chinese sea-farming reaches 25°N (Fujian) [3]. The species is
not native to China at all. It was introduced from Hokkaido in the late
1920s [3] and farmed at scale from the 1950s [4].

Thermal limits, from the FAO sources [2][3]:

- Fast growth occurs at about 1–13 °C (the same manual also says 1–15 °C).
- Growth nearly stops above 18 °C.
- Blades rot once the sea passes 23 °C, so kombu must be harvested by about
  21 °C.
- Prolonged water above 27 °C kills sporophytes.

A lab study saw no young sporophytes form at all at 20 °C [7].

Southern Chinese blades grow only to 1–2 m, against 4–6 m in Shandong and
Dalian [5]. Heat tolerance is a standing Chinese breeding goal. One study
found gametophyte heat tolerance rose steadily from the 1980s to the 2020s
across 100 samples [7].

Meanwhile, Hokkaido output has collapsed. It fell from about 30,000 t in
1993 to under 15,000 t by 2013 [6]. In fiscal 2024 it was 8,213 t (dry),
the first year below 10,000 t since records began in 1981, and in fiscal
2025 it was 9,907 t [6]. Japanese sources attribute this mainly to rising
sea temperatures [6]. A Hokkaido University projection reported by one
outlet expects 11 kombu types to disappear from Hokkaido's coasts between
2040 and 2090 [6] (secondary report only).

**Finding 5 — an extreme iodine accumulator: both a public-health tool and
the crop's main hazard.** FAO reports kombu concentrating iodine up to
100,000 times above seawater [2]. Measured contents:

- Japan's official food tables give about 200 mg iodine per 100 g of dried
  kombu [14].
- A 2020–2024 Korean survey of 348 samples found sea tangle (*S. japonica*)
  the highest of five seaweeds, averaging 2,432 mg/kg dry weight [12].

At those levels, about 0.06–0.08 g of dried kombu supplies a full adult
daily iodine reference intake (see Estimates, below).

Human trials and cases:

- **Controlled trial.** Normal Japanese adults eating 15 or 30 g of kombu a
  day (35 or 70 mg iodine) for 7–10 days had raised TSH, in some above
  normal limits. Effects reversed 7–40 days after stopping [12].
- **Thyrotoxicosis cases.** In two Japanese case reports, diets supplying
  28–140 mg/day of iodine from kombu caused iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis.
  The same study found 99% of kombu's iodine passed into the water after
  15 minutes of boiling [12].
- **Soy milk.** An Australian soy milk made with kombu caused thyroid
  dysfunction in adults and hypothyroidism in a breastfed newborn, until
  kombu was removed from the recipe [12].

Spain's food-safety agency advises people with thyroid conditions, and
pregnant or breastfeeding women and children, to avoid kombu-containing
foods [12]. In the other direction, FAO reports that about 400 million
people in China's iodine-deficient areas rely on kelp as an iodine source
[2].

**Finding 6 — the crop behind the discovery of umami.** In 1908 the chemist
Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamic acid from kombu dashi as the source of the
taste he named umami. Commercial MSG followed in 1909 [15]. Kombu holds about
2–3 g of free glutamate per 100 g dry weight [15]. Standard professional
practice extracts it slowly at about 60 °C for about an hour, with some
testers preferring 65 °C [15]. The white bloom on good dried kombu is
mannitol, not mould [15][14]. Dried kombu is also deliberately aged:
*kuragakoi* cellar-aging for one to three years, sometimes 10+ years, is
marketed as removing bitterness and deepening umami [16]. This claim was
found only in merchant sources.

**Finding 7 — bred like a hybrid crop from banked gametophyte clones, on a
narrow genetic base.** Kelp gametophytes can be cloned asexually and crossed
on demand [10]. Chinese breeders cross banked male and female clones to make
F1 hybrids, then raise seed from bulked sporophytes. 'Dongfang no. 7' gave
an average 43.2% more air-dry yield than two widely farmed controls [10].

The narrow base is documented in several ways:

- The genome study found more genetic diversity within wild populations
  than among all cultivars, which descend from a single wild accession with
  limited *S. longissima* admixture [10].
- A 2025 survey found genetic homogeneity in five of six cultivars from
  seven hatcheries. Those hatcheries seed about 50,795 ha producing about
  2.7 Mt fresh biomass a year [10].
- A 2021 review lists declining germplasm diversity and degraded
  agronomic traits as major threats to the Chinese industry [10].

**Finding 8 — production and yield figures conflict sharply, partly
because of wet-versus-dry bookkeeping.** The crop is sold by dry weight, and
the wet-to-dry ratio changes month to month, so farmers time harvest for the
best combination of area yield and wet-to-dry ratio [4]. China's fishery
yearbook reports dry weight (1,651,573 t in 2020). FAO converts that at 15%
to about 11 Mt wet [4].

Conflicting production figures:

- An industry overview gives China 12 Mt in 2023 [4].
- A 2022 paper gives about 1.4 Mt dry weight [4].
- The 2015 genome paper's 7.9 Mt "dry weight" for 2012 [10] is
  irreconcilable with these and is probably a wet-weight figure mislabelled.

Per-hectare yields also disagree, at roughly 25–53 t/ha fresh in China and
26 t/ha in Russia (see Estimates, below).

Japan's figures mix wild and farmed, and fresh and dry, across sources. One
overview gives about 30,000 t farmed plus a garbled wild figure (printed as
"45,0000 t"), while Hokkaido's own dried-weight total is under 10,000 t
[4][6]. These aren't reconciled here.

**Finding 9 — disease is bacterial and hatchery-stage-heavy, and warming
and light changes appear to trigger it.**

- **Nursery stage.** Green rot (seedling tips lose pigment, then the whole
  frond rots) is the most destructive and can cause complete failure of a
  hatchery's seedling production [11].
- **Late nursery stage.** A newer bleaching disease is attributed to
  *Pseudoalteromonas piscicida* X-8 and, in Fujian, to *P. agarivorans* [11].
- **Mature crop.** Alginate-degrading *Pseudoalteromonas* and *Vibrio* cause
  red spot, rot and rotten-hole disease [11].
- **Sanggou Bay outbreak.** A severe outbreak with extensive kelp mortality
  in Sanggou Bay, one of the world's densest kelp-farming areas, was linked
  to a sharp increase in coastal light penetration that disrupted the kelp's
  microbiome [11].

FAO also lists non-infectious triggers: excessive or poor light, and
freshwater dilution after heavy rain [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Marine macroalga with a heteromorphic life
  cycle: large diploid sporophyte (harvested) and microscopic haploid
  gametophytes [2]. Naturally a 2-year (two-winter) sporophyte; farmed as a
  1-year winter crop [2][3] (Finding 1).
- **Harvest pattern:** Single harvest per rope per season. In China most
  kombu is harvested June–July, with some cut in April for instant-food
  processing [2]. In northern Japan harvest runs mid-June to mid-August,
  and Japanese yield is counted in pieces, not weight [5]. An "interval
  harvesting" method appears in FAO's Chinese training manual, but only its
  section title was read [3]. Wild Japanese kombu is harvested on short,
  tightly regulated openings [5].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Chinese summer-seedling cycle: about 3
  months indoors (mid-July to October), 2–4 weeks of intermediate culture at
  sea, then about 8 months of grow-out (mid-November to late June–July) [3].
  That makes roughly 11–12 months from zoospore to harvest. Japanese
  "forced" kombu takes about 10 months. Two-year farmed and wild kombu take
  about 2 years [5].
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate NW Pacific. Native south to about 36°N,
  farmed to 25°N in China [3]. Also farmed in Japan, Korea, Russia's Far
  East and France [1][2]. Thermal limits are in Finding 4 [2][3].
  Reproductive limits [3]:
  - Sori (spore patches) form best at 15–20 °C, few below 10 °C, and none
    above 20–22 °C.
  - Gametophytes grow up to 26 °C, but egg release happens at 5–15 °C and
    stops above 20 °C.

  Chill-hours/vernalization: not applicable in the land-crop sense. The
  closest equivalent is that sporophytes can't complete their life cycle
  where summer water stays above 20 °C for long periods [3]. Elevation: not
  applicable. Depth plays that role: natural depth is to about 10 m or more
  depending on turbidity, and Japanese grow-out ropes hang about 2 m below
  the surface to avoid scorching [2].
- **Composition:** Dried kombu per 100 g, from Japan's official food
  composition tables (read via an aggregator reproducing them, not the
  primary table) [14]:
  - Energy and macronutrients: about 170 kcal, 5.8 g protein, 1.3 g fat,
    32.1 g dietary fibre.
  - Minerals and vitamins: 780 mg calcium, 530 mg magnesium, 200,000 µg
    iodine, 1,600 µg β-carotene, 110 µg vitamin K.

  Retail labels for Hokkaido kombu give similar macronutrients and 232.7 mg
  iodine/100 g [14]. Main industrial fractions [14]:
  - Alginate: about 25% of dry weight (34% in another analysis). It is
    higher in the blade tip than the base.
  - Mannitol: about 15% of dry weight (26.7% in another analysis). It is
    much higher at the blade base, up to 25-fold in mature June plants, and
    rises in summer.

  Shandong kelp had higher mannitol and better colour than Liaoning or
  Fujian kelp, while Liaoning had the best overall quality [14].
- **Edibility:** Edible raw, dried, salted or boiled. In practice it is
  mostly eaten after drying, or extracted into dashi. Iodine load limits
  how much can safely be eaten whole (Finding 5) [12].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable (no flowers). Sexual reproduction is by
  motile sperm fertilising eggs on microscopic female gametophytes [2].
  Zoospores attach within about 2 hours, and the gametophyte phase takes
  12–15 days [3]. Wild zoospores disperse very little, usually no more than
  about 3.5 m [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Reproduction, not yield, is
  photoperiod-controlled.
  - Short days (9 h light : 15 h dark) induce sorus formation in mature
    sporophytes. Sori appear in about 3 weeks and cover up to about 41% of
    the blade underside after about 5 weeks [8].
  - Blue light promotes sorus formation, and red light inhibits it [8].
  - Short photoperiods favour gametogenesis [8].
  - Blue light gave better indoor seedling culture than white light [8].

  Not applicable to tuberization or bulbing.
- **Yield:** Fresh-weight yields, which conflict between sources:
  - China, about 25 t/ha (older pers.-comm. figure) [4].
  - China, about 53 t/ha (2023–2024 hatchery-region figure) [10].
  - Russia's Primorsky farms, 26 t/ha [2].

  See Estimates. Dry matter is roughly 12.5–17% of fresh weight (see
  Estimates). The FAO manual's fertiliser and summer-seedling history
  implies large management-driven swings [3]. Japanese farm yield is
  reported per line, as 40,000–100,000 pieces a year from a 3,000 m line
  [5].
- **Water use:** Not applicable. This is a fully marine crop (see Water
  regime).
- **Propagation:** Sexual, via zoospores released from sori. Seed is
  produced either from wild or selected parent sporophytes, or from banked,
  cloned gametophytes crossed in culture [2][10]. A fertile-parent shortage
  in Hokkaido led to work on inducing sori in captive plants under short
  days [8].

  Clonal/varietal degeneration risk is real but genetic, not viral. China
  reports declining germplasm diversity, degraded agronomic traits and
  near-uniform cultivars (Finding 7). The breeders' answer is a
  self-maintained broodstock and gametophyte-clone bank, rebuilding seed
  sporophytes from it every year [10]. No viral degeneration was found in
  this pass.
- **Labor:** Seedlings are transplanted by hand, at about 30 juveniles per
  2 m rope [4]. Chinese harvest is done by two-person boat teams and is
  physically demanding. A mechanical lifting arm in use since 2020 lets one
  worker cut about 1 t per hour, and boats harvest 600–3,000 m of line per
  hour [5]. Japanese harvests start at 3–5 am, landing 1,000–2,000 pieces
  per haul [5]. No labour-hours-per-hectare figure was sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable (no soil). The plant attaches by a holdfast to
  rock in the wild, or to ropes when farmed [2]. Nutrient needs are in
  Finding 3 [3]. Salinity is its own dimension. The species is described as
  stenohaline (it tolerates only a narrow salinity range), and low salinity
  harms it more than high salinity [9]:
  - Dropping from 30 to 20 psu cut juvenile growth by 48.3% (61.4% under
    elevated CO2).
  - Juvenile sporophytes tolerate salinity swings better than adults.
  - In adult blades, the base is the most tolerant part and the tip the
    least.

  Freshwater dilution after heavy rain is a recognised disease trigger [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Harvested blades are sun-dried on land the same
  day. Other routes are salting, or boiling in seawater followed by chilling
  and cold storage [2][4]. Dried kombu keeps for long periods. Merchant
  sources describe deliberate cellar-aging (*kuragakoi*) at controlled
  temperature and humidity for 1–3 years, sometimes over 10, said to
  improve dashi [16]. No sourced humidity or temperature specification and
  no formal shelf-life study was found. Consumer-level sources only warn
  that damp storage causes mould.
- **Establishment:** Seedlings are reared indoors on seed rope, then held at
  sea and transplanted onto culture ropes at 10–15 cm [2], at about 30
  plants per 2 m rope [4]. Two raft systems are used in China:
  hanging-rope ("single-raft") and horizontal-rope ("double-raft"). The
  horizontal system grows more evenly but resists water motion more [4].
- **Cultivars:** Chinese-approved varieties include '901', 'Dongfang
  No. 2', 'Rongfu', 'Dongfang No. 3', 'Dongfang No. 6', 'Ailunwan' and
  'Sanhai' [10]. Notable differences:
  - 'Rongfu' was bred for heat tolerance and yield [10].
  - 'Dongfang No. 6' (Korean ecotype × 'Lianza no. 1') is heat- and
    strong-sun-tolerant and matures late. Its air-dried product yield was
    88–109% higher than controls [10].
  - 'Dongfang No. 7' gave 43.2% more air-dry yield than controls [10].
  - 'Ailunwan' is high in yield and algin, and genetically stable [10].

  'Huangguan No. 2' is named in a 2025 temperature/light study [7].

  Japanese market types, all now within *S. japonica* (see Naming and
  scope) [1]:
  - Ma-kombu: thick, wide, light brown. Hakodate/southern Hokkaido;
    Osaka's preferred dashi kombu.
  - Rishiri kombu (var. *ochotensis*): firm. Rishiri/Rebun; Kyoto's
    preferred kombu.
  - Rausu/Oni kombu (var. *diabolica*): broad, dark, soft-fibred. Gives a
    darker, richer dashi.
  - Hosome kombu (var. *religiosa*): narrow. Suited to shredding into
    tororo kombu.

  The descriptions and 2025 retail prices (about ¥2,000–8,000/kg, Rishiri
  highest) come from merchant and news sources [1][15].
- **Weather response:** Heat is the dominant risk (Finding 4). Japanese
  annual production swings with winter–spring sea temperature and nutrient
  supply. Juveniles survived at 15–20 °C in enriched seawater but died in
  unenriched water, so warming and nutrient stress interact [7]. Heavy
  rain hurts through salinity dilution [2]. Frost: not applicable. Ice
  effects not sourced (one tourism source credits drift ice with mineral
  richness, not treated as evidence).
- **Water regime:** Fully submerged marine crop, grown on floating raft or
  longline ropes in the open sea [2][4]. Hatchery stage runs in cooled
  (8–10 °C), circulating seawater [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Two products from one species: one-year
  (forced) and two-year (biennial) kombu, which differ in texture, use and
  price [5]. Within a season, blades are harvested once they thicken
  ("thickening of the blades" as harvest cue), balancing area yield
  against wet-to-dry ratio [3][4]. China also cuts an early April crop
  for instant food [2]. Not asynchronous ripening in the usual sense.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. The closest analogue is the kelp's
  surface microbiome, whose disruption is implicated in bleaching and
  Enterobacterales disease outbreaks (Finding 9) [11].
- **Controlled environment:** The seedling stage is already a
  controlled-environment process. It runs about 3 months in chilled
  greenhouse tanks, with blue light shown better than white [3][8]. A
  Japanese tank system with controlled temperature, light and water speed
  grew *S. japonica* through its entire life cycle. The plants matched
  sea-grown ones in shape. It was built to speed breeding, not for
  production [17]. No closed-ecosystem or spaceflight data were found.

## Hazards

- **Iodine excess.** The main consumer hazard: reversible thyroid
  suppression at 15 g/day in healthy adults, and thyrotoxicosis case
  reports at diets supplying 28+ mg/day of iodine [12]. Regulators advise
  vulnerable groups to avoid it [12] (Finding 5). Boiling moves almost all
  the iodine into the water, so the risk follows the broth as well as the
  blade [12].
- **Arsenic: present, but mostly organic.** Total arsenic in Japanese
  ma-kombu is about 30–85 mg/kg dry, and Rishiri kombu about 50–60 mg/kg
  [13]. Arsenic in kombu is mostly bound in organic forms and excreted
  quickly [13]. The UK FSA found no inorganic arsenic above its 0.3 mg/kg
  detection limit in kombu, unlike hijiki at 67–96 mg/kg [13]. However, a
  Russian study found commercial *S. japonica* exceeding the Eurasian
  Customs Union's undifferentiated 5 mg/kg total-arsenic limit [13]. So
  regulatory status depends on whether a rule separates organic from
  inorganic arsenic.
- **Sodium.** Dried kombu labels show about 5–7 g salt per 100 g [14].
- **Crop diseases.** Green rot (nursery stage, can wipe out a hatchery's
  seedling crop), bacterial bleaching, rot and rotten-hole diseases, and
  malformation disease are all sourced (Finding 9) [2][11]. No fungal or
  ergot-type contamination hazard was found.
- **Climate loss of production areas.** Not a consumer hazard, but a
  supply one: Hokkaido output halved within roughly 20 years and fell below
  10,000 t (dry) in fiscal 2024 [6].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED.** Animal-feed iodine loading is not covered here. That
  matters because at least 70% of Korea's fresh kelp is fed to abalone [4],
  and Laminaria meal ("Lameal") has been fed to chickens and cattle [3].
  This project has no place yet for livestock-safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (dried food kombu):** fresh blades cut from ropes → ferried to
  shore → sun-dried the same day → trimmed/shaped and graded → sold dried
  or cellar-aged (*kuragakoi*) [2][3][4][16]. Yield is about 12.5–17% dry
  matter from fresh (see Estimates).
- **Chain (dashi):** dried kombu → cold soak and/or held at about 60 °C for
  about 1 hour → broth, often finished with katsuobushi. Spent kombu is
  reused for second dashi, simmered dishes or tsukudani [15][16]. Iodine
  and free glutamate both move into the broth [12][15].
- **Chain (industrial):** dried kelp → extraction → alginate, mannitol and
  iodine. About 50% of China's dried kelp went to these products [2]. China
  makes up to about 10,000 t of alginate a year from this species [1], and
  China's kelp has been said to supply about 90% of global alginate [11].
- **Chain (salted / boiled):** fresh kelp → salting, or boiling in seawater
  → chilled cold storage [2]. Cultivar trials report separate "salting
  processed product" and "air-drying processed product" yields [10].
- **Food uses:**
  - Japan: dashi; tororo kombu (vinegared and shaved) [5]; kombu rolls;
    curing raw fish by wrapping it in kombu (kobujime) [15].
  - China: sliced kelp as a cold starter [1].
  - Korea: broth, deep-fried bugak/twigak, soy-pickled jangajji, raw ssam
    wraps, kelp tea, and noodles made from its alginic acid [1].
- **Animal feed:** Fresh kelp feeds abalone, sea urchins and sea cucumbers.
  At least 70% of Korea's fresh kelp goes directly to abalone [4]. China
  also uses it in synthetic mariculture feed [4], and Laminaria meal has
  been fed to chickens and cattle [3].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** fucoidan, laminarin and fucoxanthin
  extraction; biofuel/biorefinery use (mannitol-rich feedstock) [14];
  integrated polyculture with scallops, mussels and *Undaria*, named in
  FAO's manual but not read in detail [3]; carbon-sink claims [11].

## Open research questions

- **Per-hectare yield.** The widest uncertainty: about 25 vs about 53 t/ha
  fresh for China, from two different eras and sources, and 26 t/ha in
  Russia. Neither Chinese figure states its management system or line
  density.
- **Chinese national production.** Reported variously as about 1.4 Mt dry,
  1.65 Mt dry, about 11–12 Mt wet, and 7.9 Mt "dry". The last is probably
  mislabelled but wasn't checked against FAOSTAT directly.
- **Japanese production.** Fresh vs dry and wild vs farmed figures across
  sources weren't reconciled.
- **The 2026 merger study.** Its proposal to merge var. *diabolica*,
  *ochotensis* and *religiosa* into *S. japonica* was read only via a news
  summary. Whether it's accepted affects how the Japanese market types
  should be modelled.
- **Thermal optimum.** The FAO manual gives 1–13 °C in one place and
  1–15 °C in another; not resolved.
- **Japanese composition values** were read only through an aggregator
  reproducing Japan's official food tables, not the primary table.
- **Aging and shelf life.** *Kuragakoi* aging claims and dried-kombu shelf
  life come from merchant sources only. No study measuring glutamate or
  quality change with aging was located.

## Estimates and derivations

**Fresh yield, China, about 24.7 t/ha (older figure).** Inputs: about
2,000,000 t wet (250,000 t dry) from about 200,000 acres of farms, a
personal-communication figure reproduced by a seaweed reference site [4].
Arithmetic: 200,000 acres × 0.4047 ha/acre = 80,940 ha; 2,000,000 t /
80,940 ha = 24.7 t/ha fresh. Dry: 250,000 t / 80,940 ha = 3.1 t/ha.

**Fresh yield, China, about 53.2 t/ha (2023–2024 figure).** Inputs: about
2.7 million t fresh a year from about 50,795 ha supplied by seven hatcheries
[10]. Arithmetic: 2,700,000 t / 50,795 ha = 53.2 t/ha.

The two Chinese figures differ by about 2×. Plausible range for intensive
Chinese longline culture: 25–55 t/ha fresh, with Russia's 26 t/ha [2] at
the low end. A FAOSTAT area-and-production series, or a farm-level trial
with stated rope density, would replace both.

**Dry matter, about 12.5–17% of fresh weight (fresh:dry about 6–8 : 1).**
Inputs [3][4]:

| Source | Fresh | Dry | Dry ÷ fresh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seaweed reference site [4] | 2,000,000 t | 250,000 t | 12.5% |
| 1980 Chinese figures [4] | 1,500,000 t | 200,000 t | 13.3% |
| FAO manual, 1958 [3] | 31,604 t | 5,267 t | 16.7% |
| China yearbook conversion [4] | — | — | 15% |

Range: 12.5–16.7%. Midpoint about 15%, matching the yearbook convention.
Since the ratio is known to shift month to month [4], any single value is a
seasonal average. A monthly dry-matter series would replace it.

**Dried kombu needed to supply 150 µg iodine (adult RDI): about
0.06–0.08 g.** Inputs: RDI 150 µg/day [12]; iodine content 200,000 µg/100 g
= 2,000 µg/g [14], or 2,432 mg/kg = 2,432 µg/g [12]. Arithmetic:
150 / 2,000 = 0.075 g; 150 / 2,432 = 0.062 g. Range 0.06–0.08 g, i.e.
about 60–80 mg of dried kombu. The Japanese trial's 35 mg iodine per 15 g
(= 2,333 µg/g) [12] falls inside the same band.

For comparison, the 28 mg/day level linked to thyrotoxicosis [12]
corresponds to 28,000 / 2,000 ≈ 14 g of dried kombu a day (about
11.5–14 g across the two iodine figures). Most of the iodine leaves in the
cooking water, so this applies to kombu plus its broth, not the blade
alone. Iodine content varies widely by product and processing. A
per-product iodine assay would replace this.
