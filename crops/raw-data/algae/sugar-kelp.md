## Naming and scope

This covers *Saccharina latissima* (Linnaeus) C.E. Lane, C. Mayes, Druehl &
G.W. Saunders, formerly *Laminaria saccharina*. It is a brown alga (order
Laminariales, family Laminariaceae) known as sugar kelp, sea-belt or sugar
tang, and sometimes sold as "sugar kombu" [1][4]. The name refers to the
whitish, sweet-tasting powder (mainly mannitol) that forms on the dried
frond [1]. This doc covers the single cropID `sugar_kelp`.

**It is a species complex, not one tidy species.** Genetic work splits
*S. latissima* sensu lato into divergent lineages in the NE Pacific, NW
Atlantic and NE Atlantic, which barely overlap or interbreed [1]. One
study calls the group "a complex of incipient species" [1]. The NE Pacific
*S. cichorioides* complex, and NW Pacific plants once classed as separate
species, fall inside it [1]. The North American "longicruris" form is
treated as a morphotype within it [1]. Cultivation literature treats the
whole complex as one crop, and this doc does too. Where a study's
population origin matters, the doc says so.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **Winged kelp (*Alaria esculenta*).** Proposed as `winged_kelp`. It is
  the other main cultivated kelp in Norway and Europe, often co-farmed with
  sugar kelp, and is also grown in Maine [7][9]. It is in a different
  family (Alariaceae), with a different processing profile (lower iodine,
  higher cadmium) [7].
- **Skinny kelp (*Saccharina angustissima*).** Proposed as `skinny_kelp`.
  It is a separately farmed species in Maine [9].
- **Kombu (*Saccharina japonica*).** Already researched as `kelp_kombu`.
  The two have been crossed in Chinese breeding [8].
- ***Saccharina nigripes*** (the North American form once attributed to
  *S. groenlandica*). Not proposed. It is a distinct, look-alike congener
  across the NE Pacific, Arctic and North Atlantic, while *S. groenlandica*
  itself is a synonym of *S. latissima* [1]. No cultivation or use was
  found for it.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — Europe's and North America's flagship kelp crop, but still
tiny next to Asia.**

- **Europe.** Sugar kelp is the most widely cultivated species in Europe,
  concentrated around the North Sea [2][3]. European production was almost
  1,000 t in 2018, with Norway contributing 174 t [3].
- **United States.** Farmed sugar kelp was about 3 million lb in 2022
  (about 1,360 t). Maine alone landed over 1 million lb (about 450 t) in
  2023 [9]. Alaska grew from about 16,500 wet lb in 2017 to 89,300 lb in
  2018 [9]. See Estimates.
- **Industry status.** Production is seasonal and winter-grown. It largely
  runs on existing fishing infrastructure, and one farmer is quoted saying
  lobster pays the bills while kelp reuses the gear [9].

**Finding 2 — areal yields vary by more than an order of magnitude, driven
by gear, site and year.**

- **Single lines.** Reported yields range from 0.4–3.2 kg fresh weight
  (FW) per m of line (a 1–5 m vertical system, after 5 and 18 months) to
  16.1 kg/m in northern Spain [2].
- **Areal yields.** These run from 1.6 t/ha (Denmark, vertical lines) and
  7.1 t/ha (the same 1–5 m system) to 40.2 t/ha (Spain, horizontal lines) [2].
- **Nets.** These raise yields to 10.9–30.4 kg FW per m² of net, and up to
  91.3 t FW/ha over a 1.5-year growth period [2]. A newer Danish
  assessment found nets gave 8.6–10.1 kg FW per m of pipe against 5.1 kg/m
  for longlines. When scaled to 18.75 ha, though, the longline layout gave
  up to 1.5 times more total yield [2].
- **Year to year.** Yield from the same system at the same site varied by a
  factor of four between years [2].
- **Potential estimates.** A commonly cited annual production potential of
  75–200 t wet weight per hectare at sea [2] comes from modelling. It sits
  well above any measured farm yield found in this pass.

**Finding 3 — biofouling, not a disease, sets the end of the season.** The
dominant fouler on Norwegian farms is the bryozoan *Membranipora
membranacea* [3]. Fouling starts later further north and at
freshwater-influenced sites [3]. Fouled biomass is less attractive as food
and worth less [3]. This forces a nutrition-versus-cleanliness trade-off.
One IMTA study found protein peaked in November at 10.8% of dry weight
(DW), but concluded that higher yield and cleaner food biomass come in May
[4]. Spring or early-summer harvest is standard in southern and central
Norway [8]. Biofouling has also been tied to sheltered sites, and is
described as a major issue for European cultivation [2].

**Finding 4 — an Arctic-to-temperate kelp whose southern edge is set by
summer heat, and which has collapsed along part of its range.**

- **Optimum.** Growth is best at about 10–15 °C (10–17 °C in some sources)
  and 30–35 psu [3][5].
- **Upper limit.** It doesn't generally persist where summer water exceeds
  20 °C for long [5]. Plants grown at 20 °C suffered substantial tissue
  deterioration [5]. A 3-week simulated heatwave at 24 °C killed over 90%
  of plants [5].
- **Cold limit.** Arctic material reached 100% germination of
  embryospores at 0 °C, and Arctic sporophytes may carry sori year-round
  [1].
- **Range.** In Europe it runs from Novaya Zemlya south to northern
  Portugal [1].
- **Skagerrak decline.** Around 2000, sugar kelp forests in southern Norway
  were replaced by sediment-laden turf algae. A survey of about 600
  stations found turf dominance at about 80% of Skagerrak stations and
  about 40% of North Sea coast stations [6]. Another estimate put the
  Skagerrak loss at 50.7% to 83% [6]. The suspected causes are extreme
  summer temperatures (1997, 2002, 2006) combined with eutrophication and
  epiphyte shading [6]. Norway holds about half of Europe's sugar kelp [6].

**Finding 5 — one of the highest-iodine edible seaweeds, so food use
depends on processing.**

- **Content.** Farmed sugar kelp is recorded at 1,556–7,208 mg iodine/kg
  DW depending on site [7]. One sample set averaged 4,605 mg/kg DW [7], and
  another reached up to 6,568 mg/kg DW [7]. A North Atlantic survey
  concluded that only about 32 mg of dry biomass meets the daily iodine
  intake for most adults [7]. EFSA's adult upper limit is 600 µg/day [7].
- **Iodine removal.** Industry now routinely blanches the kelp. Blanching
  at 80 °C for 2 minutes in seawater cut iodine to 5% of the original [7],
  and 80 °C for 120 seconds in fresh water reached 293 mg/kg DW [7].
  Plain boiling is effective but unpredictable, with reported reductions of
  38–94% [7].
- **Other elements.** Rinsing and boiling removed 85% of iodine and 43% of
  arsenic, but no significant cadmium, lead or mercury [7]. Blanched sugar
  kelp still held arsenic above the maximum allowed in animal feed [7].

So processing fixes the iodine problem, but not the arsenic or heavy-metal
problem.

**Finding 6 — highly perishable fresh, and it keeps living after harvest.**
Under refrigeration at 2.8 °C, the sensory shelf life was 7–9 days
whatever the post-harvest treatment [10]. Spoilage tracked counts of over
7 log CFU/g, and was dominated by Pseudoalteromonadaceae and
Psychromonadaceae [10]. Unblanched kelp went on respiring and consuming
carbohydrates for up to 5 days. It lost sweetness and umami as mannitol and
free glutamate fell [10].

Freezing has its own losses: thawed frozen sugar kelp shed over 40% of its
weight as a viscous brown drip [10]. This is why sources describe fresh
kelp as "quick to spoil", needing prompt processing [10]. Industry relies
on blanching, freezing, drying or fermentation. In one trial, blanched
kelp fermented with 2% glucose and 10⁶ CFU/g yeast reached pH below 4.6
with under 0.8% alcohol by volume (ABV) [10].

**Finding 7 — no cultivars: European farms grow wild, local genotypes on
purpose.** Seed is usually made from locally collected sori, to avoid
moving genes between regions [8]. In aquaculture, genetic introgression
from farmed into wild populations is a main concern for kelp farming in
Europe and the Americas [8]. Wild *S. latissima* shows strong population
structure, with gene flow between sampled locations described as
"virtually nonexistent" [1][8]. Norway's coast is an exception, with
little structure except across the Lofoten break [8]. Alaska has rules on
where sori may be sourced [9].

Breeding is just starting:

- A Norwegian three-generation selection experiment saw no gain in blade
  length despite selecting for it [8].
- That project is also working towards sterile triploid kelp that can't
  hybridise with wild stands [8].

This is the reverse of the Chinese kombu model of banked, near-uniform
hybrid cultivars (see `kelp_kombu`).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Short-lived perennial marine kelp with a
  heteromorphic life cycle. The large diploid sporophyte, which is
  harvested, alternates with microscopic haploid gametophytes [1]. It is
  farmed as a winter crop: deployed in autumn and harvested in spring or
  early summer [3][9].
- **Harvest pattern:** Usually a single full harvest in spring or early
  summer, before fouling [3][8]. Multiple partial harvests ("coppicing" the
  blades and leaving the base to regrow) have been trialled:
  - Faroe Islands: four partial harvests over two years from 10 m vertical
    lines, about 0.29 kg DW per m of line per harvest [2].
  - Denmark: coppicing tested alongside full harvest [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 5–9 months at sea to a spring
  harvest. Examples are a Danish 9-month crop [2], a Norwegian 7-month crop
  harvested in May [11], and yields measured as early as 5 months [2].
  Multi-season growth (18 months, 1.5 years) gives higher yield [2].
- **Climate zones:** Amphi-boreal and circumpolar in the northern
  hemisphere, from the Arctic to cold-temperate waters [1][3]. Farmed from
  northern Spain to northern Norway (69–70°N) [2][3][8].
  Further north, peak frond length and yield came 5–8 weeks later [3].
  Temperature limits are in Finding 4 [3][5]. Chill-hours/vernalization:
  not applicable, though the closest analogue is that reproduction is
  cued by winter-like short days (see Photoperiod). Elevation: not
  applicable. Depth plays that role: growth was greater at 1–2 m than at
  8–9 m [3], and wild forests grow at depths of less than 30 m [8].
- **Composition:** Cultivated Norwegian/Faroese sugar kelp, monthly sampling
  2015–2017, all as % of dry weight [4]:
  - Carbohydrate 42–57%, ash 36–42%, protein 11–14% (N × 6.25), lipid
    2.4%.
  - Dry matter and carbohydrate rise in winter, while ash and protein are
    lowest then; summer is the opposite.

  A species-specific nitrogen-to-protein factor of 3.8 gives a lower
  protein range of 2.2–10.9% DW [3]. Using the generic 6.25 factor likely
  overstates protein. In Sweden, total carbohydrate peaked at 360 mg/g DW
  in August, and mannitol peaked at 90 mg/g in June [4]. Iodine figures
  are in Finding 5 [7]. No USDA or other primary food-database entry
  specific to this species was located.
- **Edibility:** Edible raw, blanched, dried or fermented. Iodine load
  limits how much can safely be eaten unprocessed (Finding 5). One
  Norwegian thesis put the safe adult intake of its blanched product at
  about 4.3 g wet weight [7].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable (no flowers). Fertilisation happens
  between microscopic male and female gametophytes [1]. Zoospores are
  released from sori in the middle of the blade between autumn and winter
  [6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Reproduction is photoperiod-controlled.
  Hatcheries induce sori outside the natural season by cutting off the
  meristem and holding sporophytes under short days at about 10 °C [11].
  *S. latissima*, unlike *L. digitata*, needs short days for sorus
  formation, and induction brought sori at least two months early [11].
  Full darkness worked best of the light levels tested: over 90% of
  sporophytes developed sori within 49 days [11]. No photoperiod effect on
  yield was found. Light and season shape yield indirectly, through the
  latitude effect on timing [3].
- **Yield:** 0.4–16.1 kg FW per m of line, 1.6–40.2 t FW/ha on lines, and
  up to 91.3 t FW/ha on nets over 1.5 years (Finding 2) [2]. Norway's nine
  farms (58–69°N) gave maximum yields of 0.2–14 kg/m² [3]. Interannual
  variation is up to fourfold at one site [2]. Modelled potential is
  75–200 t/ha, unverified [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine crop; see Water regime).
- **Propagation:** Sexual, from zoospores off locally collected sori
  [8][11]. Three ways of getting the crop onto lines:
  - **Seed string.** Zoospores are settled onto seed string in a nursery,
    then wrapped onto culture lines. This is the US industry standard [9].
  - **Direct seeding.** Gametophytes or embryonic sporophytes are sprayed
    or painted straight onto the culture lines. This is widespread in
    Europe and still being trialled in the US [9]. One Norwegian hatchery
    targeted 400,000 gametophyte spores per mL on 1.2 mm twine [3].
  - **Free-living gametophyte cultures.** These are also used, allowing
    seed production outside the natural sorus season [11].

  No clonal or viral degeneration risk was found, since the crop is
  re-seeded from wild sori each year [8].
- **Labor:** No labour-hours figure was sourced. A Faroese offshore trial's
  cost per kg DW fell from €36.73 to €9.27 as the number of harvests
  without re-seeding increased [2]. A Danish life-cycle and techno-economic
  study includes labour in its inventory, but the labour figures weren't
  extracted [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. The plant attaches to rock, or to ropes when
  farmed, and can also grow unattached in loose-lying populations on sand
  in calm water [1]. Salinity is its own dimension:
  - Optimum is about 30–35 psu [3], or 23–31 absolute salinity in another
    source [5].
  - It still occurs in reduced-salinity waters (MarLIN lists full, reduced,
    variable and low salinity), including the brackish Baltic [1][5].
  - Hyposalinity (salinity 20) had a larger effect on gene expression than
    temperature did [5].
  - Large site-to-site growth differences in Norway were mainly due to
    local salinity [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Fresh, refrigerated at 2.8 °C: 7–9 days
  [10]. Frozen: viable, but thawing loses over 40% of weight as drip
  [10]. Other preservation routes are dried (sun, hot-air or freeze-dried)
  and fermented [10]. Hot-air drying at 70 °C degraded chlorophyll to
  pheophytin [10]. Blanching turns the kelp green but fucoxanthin persists
  [10].
- **Establishment:** Seeded lines or nets are deployed at sea in autumn.
  Gear options are longlines or suspended ropes at 2–5 m, or horizontal
  nets [2][3]. Higher line density per area (nets or multi-line systems)
  raises areal yield [2].
- **Cultivars:** None. Farms use wild local populations [8]. Sugar kelp
  from wave-exposed and sheltered sites differs markedly in form. Exposed
  plants have short solid stipes and narrow, thick fronds, while sheltered
  plants have broad, thin, wavy blades [1]. North American "longicruris"
  plants are a long-stiped morphotype [1]. Population origin affects
  temperature tolerance: Arctic and Brittany material behaves differently
  under identical conditions [5]. Northern Norwegian sori from sites 90 km
  apart were tested for growth and mineral differences [8].
- **Weather response:** Heat is the main risk (Finding 4). Gametophytes
  "primed" at 20 °C produced sporophytes with about 1 °C higher heat
  tolerance [5], which is suggested as a tool for farming or restoration.
  Freshwater runoff lowers salinity and suppresses performance [5]. Frost:
  not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Fully submerged marine crop on longline, rope or net
  systems [2][3]. Nursery stage runs in seawater tanks [9][11].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** One spring–early-summer window, balancing
  yield and cleanliness against protein (Finding 3) [3][4]. Peak timing
  shifts 5–8 weeks later in the north [3]. Partial-harvest systems spread
  harvests across two years [2]. Not asynchronous ripening.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. The closest analogue is the biofouling
  community (Finding 3). Epiphyte loading is also implicated in the lack of
  wild-forest recovery in Skagerrak [6].
- **Controlled environment:** Land-based tank cultivation has been studied
  as a way to finish sea-grown sporophytes. Plants grown 7 months at sea
  were transferred to light- and nutrient-controlled tanks for 20 days
  [11]. Nursery production is already tank-based [9][11]. No full
  closed-system or spaceflight trial was found.

## Hazards

- **Iodine excess.** Very high raw content, at 1,556–7,208 mg/kg DW (Finding
  5). Daily raw consumption leads to excessive iodine intake [7].
  Mitigated by blanching or warm-water treatment, which removes up to about
  90–95% [7]. No EU maximum level for iodine in seaweed foods exists [7].
- **Arsenic and cadmium.** Inorganic arsenic was low in Norwegian samples
  [7]. Arsenic and cadmium in northern Norwegian farmed sugar kelp were
  below EU maximum levels for food supplements [8]. However, blanched
  sugar kelp still held arsenic above the EU maximum for animal feed [7].
  Boiling removes some arsenic (43%) but no significant cadmium, lead or
  mercury [7].
- **Microbial spoilage.** Short refrigerated shelf life, with
  *Pseudoalteromonas*-type spoilage organisms dominating (Finding 6) [10].
  This is an ordinary food-safety cold-chain issue rather than a toxin.
- **Biofouling.** Mainly *Membranipora membranacea*. A quality and market
  hazard rather than a consumer-safety one (Finding 3) [3].
- **Wild-stock genetic risk.** Introgression of farmed genotypes into wild
  stands is a recognised regulatory concern, mitigated by using local
  seed (Finding 7) [8].
- **TO BE ADDRESSED.** Livestock and aquafeed iodine and arsenic limits.
  Sugar kelp is grown for animal feed (Norway's Breed4Kelp2Feed project; US
  nurseries list feed as an end use) [8][9]. Blanched kelp can still exceed
  feed arsenic limits [7]. This project has no place yet for
  livestock-safety data.
- No fungal or ergot-type contamination was found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (food, fresh):** harvested blades → washed in seawater or potable
  water → refrigerated → sold fresh within about a week [10].
- **Chain (food, iodine-reduced):** harvested blades → blanched (for example
  80 °C, about 2 minutes, seawater or fresh water) → frozen, dried or used
  directly. The blanching water carries away about 90–95% of the iodine,
  plus some minerals and flavour compounds [7][10].
- **Chain (fermented):** blanched kelp → glucose and yeast (or other
  starter) → acidified product, pH below 4.6 [10]. Fermentation is also
  used as an iodine-reduction step [7].
- **Chain (dried):** fresh kelp → sun, hot-air or freeze drying → whole
  leaf, flakes or powder [9][10].
- **Food products:** Maine products include seaweed salads, kimchi, teas,
  smoothie cubes, snack bars, beverages and spice blends, plus dried whole
  leaf, flakes and powders [9]. Also kelp beer [9] and plant-based
  "meatballs" with 33% sugar kelp [9]. Dashi-style broth has also been
  explored, with sugar kelp tested alongside dulse as a Nordic dashi
  source [12].
- **Non-food:** fertiliser and biostimulants, animal feed, nutraceuticals,
  cosmetics and skin care, and experimental biomaterials (rope, PFAS
  replacements) [9]. Biorefinery and biofuel feedstock is discussed but not
  detailed here [4][11].
- **Nutrient and carbon extraction:** Nets removed up to 110 kg nitrogen,
  13.1 kg phosphorus and 5.1 t carbon per hectare [2]. The Danish
  life-cycle study also models carbon capture [2]. Sugar kelp is
  co-cultured next to salmon farms (IMTA) [4].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** alginate extraction (sugar kelp is not
  a major alginate source in the sources read), and protein extraction by
  pH-shift [7].

## Open research questions

- **Real farm yield.** The widest uncertainty (Finding 2): measured values
  span about 1.6–91 t FW/ha, with fourfold year-to-year variation at one
  site, and the 75–200 t/ha potential is modelled, not measured.
  Commercial farm-level averages per hectare were not found.
- **Dry-matter fraction of fresh biomass.** Not sourced as a single figure,
  so fresh-to-dry conversions for yield and iodine dose (per gram fresh)
  couldn't be made.
- **Taxonomy.** Whether the NE Pacific, NW Atlantic and NE Atlantic
  lineages should become separate crops. Each has a different temperature
  and salinity response.
- **Primary food-database composition.** No primary food-database entry
  (USDA or Nordic) specific to *S. latissima* was located.
- **Labour.** No labour-hours or per-hectare cost figures.
- **Cadmium.** Precise ranges for *S. latissima* itself were not
  extracted.
- **Proposed rows.** Whether `winged_kelp` and `skinny_kelp` should become
  full rows.
- **Feed safety.** Livestock and aquafeed iodine and arsenic safety (see
  Hazards).

## Estimates and derivations

**US farmed sugar kelp, 2022, about 1,360 t.** Input: about 3 million lb
reported [9]. Arithmetic: 3,000,000 lb × 0.4536 kg/lb = 1,360,800 kg ≈
1,360 t. Maine 2023, 1,000,000+ lb × 0.4536 = 453.6 t, so "about 450 t,
at least" [9]. Alaska: 16,500 lb × 0.4536 = 7,484 kg ≈ 7.5 t (2017), and
89,300 lb × 0.4536 = 40,506 kg ≈ 40.5 t (2018) [9]. These are wet-weight
landings. One source says the whole US contributed only 263 t to the
global market [9], which conflicts with the 2022 figure (possibly a
different year or definition). A NOAA or state landings table would settle
it.

**Raw dry sugar kelp to reach iodine reference intakes.** Inputs: mean
4,605 mg/kg DW = 4,605 µg/g [7]; range 1,556–7,208 µg/g [7]; RDI
150 µg/day [7]; EFSA upper limit 600 µg/day [7]. Arithmetic: RDI 150 /
4,605 = 0.033 g (about 33 mg); upper limit 600 / 4,605 = 0.13 g. Across
the range, the upper limit is reached at 600 / 7,208 = 0.083 g to
600 / 1,556 = 0.39 g of dry kelp.

The 33 mg figure matches the survey's own "32 mg" statement [7]. After
blanching to 293 mg/kg DW [7], the upper limit rises to 600 / 293 ≈ 2.0 g
dry. A per-product assay would replace these.
