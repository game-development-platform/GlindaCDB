## Naming and scope

This covers *Caulerpa lentillifera* J. Agardh, a tropical green seaweed
(order Bryopsidales, family Caulerpaceae) eaten fresh for its small
grape-like branchlets [1]. Common names: sea grapes, green caviar; lato in
the Visayas (Philippines), lelato in Indonesia [1]; umibudo ("sea grapes"),
formally kubiretsuta, in Okinawa [2]. It covers the single cropID `sea_grapes`.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`nama`** (*Caulerpa racemosa* complex; proposed). Also sold as sea
  grapes, and the most commonly harvested *Caulerpa* in Fiji [1]. In
  local markets it comes mostly from the wild [1]. It can be pond-farmed,
  but *C. lentillifera* is the species best suited to ponds [1]. A
  possible wild-managed candidate for issue #2 once researched.
- ***Caulerpa taxifolia*** (aquarium strain). The "killer alga" of the
  Mediterranean (Finding 3) [3]. Not a food crop; not proposed.

**Supply is farmed.** Commercial sea grapes are pond- and tank-farmed
(Findings 1 and 2), so this is not a wild-managed resource.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — Philippine pond culture began by accident and runs like rice
paddies.**

- **Origin.** In the early 1950s a fish farmer on Mactan, Cebu, threw
  pieces of *Caulerpa* into his milkfish pond as fish feed [1]. It grew
  well, sold well, and became his main crop, with fish and prawns
  secondary [1].
- **Scale.** About 400 ha of ponds on Mactan, producing at least 4,000 t
  fresh weight a year, more than 90% of it in the dry months [1].
- **Method.**
  - Stock at 100 g/m² (1,000 kg/ha), burying one end of a handful of seed
    at about 1 m spacing along a string guide [1].
  - Change pond water every 3–4 days at first, more often from about the
    third week; a flow-through system raises output [1].
  - Lime acidic new ponds; old ponds need 0.5–1 t of agricultural lime per
    hectare a year [1].
  - Weed out competing seaweeds [1].
- **Harvest.** First partial harvest after about 45 days (another source
  says two months), keeping a fifth to a quarter of the crop as seed, then
  every 45 days [1].
- **Yield.** 12–15 t fresh/ha a year [1], up to 15 t or more with good
  management [1].
- **Why it matters.** A low-tech crop that fits existing fish ponds, with
  a ratoon-like partial harvest.

**Finding 2 — Okinawa grows it in tanks, but summer heat spoils the
"grapes".**

- **History.** Commercial culture began in Okinawa in 1986 [1]. A
  fisheries cooperative developed land-tank culture in 1989, and it spread
  fast [2].
- **Scale.** About 250 t a year, worth ¥700–800 million; Okinawa's
  third-largest aquaculture species by volume [2].
- **Growth.** Daily growth averages about 40%, about tenfold in a week
  [2]. Tanks reach market size in about 30 days in summer but about 60 in
  winter, when light is short [2].
- **The summer problem.** Demand peaks in summer, but high water
  temperature and strong sun cause poor formation of the grape-like
  branchlets that are sold [2]. Farms now use separate seed stock for the
  cool and hot seasons [2]. The suitable water temperature is about
  25–27 °C [2].
- **Fixes being tested.** Heat exchange with groundwater [2]; extra LED
  lighting in winter [2]; and a CO₂ and stirring system that more than
  doubled growth from 0.5 kg of seed (1.15 kg vs 0.64 kg) and lengthened
  each "berry" from about 45 to 65 mm [2].
- **Price.** About ¥2,000/kg, against ¥200–300/kg for farmed seaweed on
  average [2].
- **Why it matters.** A premium, fast crop whose quality (not just mass)
  depends on temperature and light.

**Finding 3 — the genus is a notorious invader; California bans all of
it.**

- ***C. taxifolia*.** An aquarium strain was released into the
  Mediterranean in 1984 and now covers thousands of hectares from France
  to Croatia [3]. It is one of two algae on the list of the world's 100
  worst invasive species [3].
- **California.** It appeared in two southern California lagoons in 2000,
  probably from a dumped aquarium [3]. Eradication was declared in 2006
  [3].
  - In 2001 the state banned *C. taxifolia* and eight other species [3].
  - After *C. prolifera* was found in 2021, a 2023 law banned the sale,
    possession, import and transport of every *Caulerpa* species from 1
    January 2024, except for bona fide research [3].
  - So sea grapes as food are illegal to possess in California (inference
    from the genus-wide wording; no food exemption was found).
- **Federal.** The aquarium strain of *C. taxifolia* has been a US federal
  noxious weed since 1999 [3].
- **Bans leak.** In 2006, 53% of surveyed aquarium stores still sold
  *Caulerpa* [3].
- **Why it matters.** A crop whose genus is banned in a major market;
  any game model of trade needs a biosecurity rule here.

**Finding 4 — it can't be refrigerated like a vegetable.**

- **Cold damage.** Sea grapes are sensitive to osmotic shock and low
  temperature, and are easily damaged in cold storage or tap water [4].
  For *C. racemosa*, spoilage is rapid below 15 °C and above 30 °C, with
  20 °C optimal [4].
- **Water.** Stored without seawater, sea grapes lost 20–40% of their
  water and 40–60% of their chlorophyll; stored in seawater they kept
  growing slightly [4].
- **Shelf life.**
  - Typical packaging keeps them less than a week [4].
  - In Japan, 100–200 g packs stay fresh about 7 days kept "chilled and
    moist" [1]. This conflicts with the cold-damage findings; probably
    "chilled" means cool rather than refrigerated (not confirmed).
  - Brine: sprinkling gives about 3 days at room temperature; 10% brine
    extended shelf life further [4].
  - An electric-field treatment extended shelf life to 12 days [4].
- **Why it matters.** A fresh product that must travel in seawater at
  room temperature, which limits trade distance.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial creeping stolons with upright
  branches; propagated vegetatively [1]. Wild stands are highly seasonal,
  peaking in sunny dry months [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Repeated partial harvests every ~45 days in ponds,
  keeping seed stock [1]; tank harvests every ~30–60 days [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 45–60 days in ponds [1]; 30
  (summer) to 60 (winter) days in Okinawan tanks [2].
- **Climate zones:** Tropical and subtropical Indian and Pacific Oceans
  [1][5]. Ideal temperature 22–28 °C [5]. Philippine trial ponds ran at
  28–31 °C [1].
- **Composition:**
  - Fresh sea grapes are about 95% water [6].
  - Dry basis (Thailand): 12.5% protein, 24.2% ash, 0.9% fat, 3.2% fibre
    [6]. Chinese and Vietnamese farms ranged from 8.7% to 18.6% protein
    [6].
  - Rich in iodine, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium and copper [6].
- **Edibility:** Eaten raw, mainly in salads [1].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sexual reproduction hasn't been
  observed in South-East Asia [1]. White empty "husks" may be plants that
  have spent themselves reproducing [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Short winter days slow tank growth; LED
  lighting improved length and thickness [2].
- **Yield:** 12–15 t fresh/ha a year in ponds [1]. Okinawa's total
  production is about 250 t a year [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine). Salinity: plants didn't survive
  5‰ or 55‰ [5].
- **Propagation:** Vegetative cuttings (stolons and fronds) [1].
- **Labor:** Pond farms are worked by hand (planting, weeding, washing in
  a dugout boat) [1]; hours not sourced.
- **Soil:** Pond bottoms, drained to 0.3 m for planting [1]. Lime as above
  [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** In seawater at about room temperature; days,
  not weeks (Finding 4) [4][1].
- **Establishment:** Replant bare patches a few days after planting;
  fertilise if fronds turn pale green to yellow [1].
- **Cultivars:** Okinawan farms use separate cool-season and hot-season
  seed stocks [2]. Vietnam farms both a native clone and an introduced
  one [6].
- **Weather response:** Heat and strong sun spoil branchlet form [2].
  Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine ponds, lagoons and tanks [1][2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Harvest when the pond bottom is uniformly
  covered [1]; product should be grass-green and succulent [1].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. The cyanobacterium *Lyngbya* is an
  epiphyte problem in culture [6]; competing seaweeds are weeded [1].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to the tropical Indo-Pacific [1]. No invasive record was found
    for *C. lentillifera* itself in this pass.
  - The genus includes major invaders (*C. taxifolia*, *C. prolifera*),
    and California bans all *Caulerpa* (Finding 3) [3].
- **Controlled environment:** Proven in land tanks in Okinawa (Finding 2)
  [2] and in recirculating systems in Vietnam [6].

## Hazards

- **Heavy metals.** Sea grapes absorb metals, so pond culture near
  polluted water is a concern; one Vietnamese study recommends controlled
  systems for that reason [6].
- **Natural toxins.** *Caulerpa* species contain cytotoxic compounds,
  chiefly caulerpenyne, that deter grazers [5]. Human poisonings from *C.
  lentillifera*: not found in this pass.
- **Iodine.** Rich in iodine [6]; no quantified figure found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (pond):** harvest by hand → wash thoroughly in seawater in a
  dugout boat → sell fresh [1].
- **Chain (Okinawa):** harvest from tanks → pack 100–200 g [1] → ship
  fresh.
- **Uses:** Fresh salad vegetable [1][2]; bioremediation of aquaculture
  wastewater [5]; antioxidant extracts (siphonaxanthin) in research [5].
- **Trade:** Exports of 827 t were recorded in 1982 [1]; no recent figure
  found.

## Open research questions

- **Production totals.** FAO records about 1,021 t fresh of *Caulerpa*
  aquaculture in 2020 [6], yet Mactan alone was put at 4,000 t a year [1]
  and Okinawa at about 250 t [2]. FAO data probably miss most of it.
- **Refrigeration conflict.** "Chilled" Japanese packs vs cold damage
  (Finding 4).
- **California rule.** Confirm whether the 2024 ban has any food or
  restaurant exemption.
- **Decision:** whether to keep `nama` as a proposed row.

## Estimates and derivations

**Mactan yield, about 10 t/ha a year.** Inputs: 4,000 t on about 400 ha
[1]. 4,000 / 400 = 10 t/ha, a little below the 12–15 t feasibility figure
[1], as expected for an average across farms.

**Okinawa unit value, about ¥3,000/kg.** Inputs: ¥700–800 million for 250
t [2]. 750,000,000 / 250,000 = ¥3,000/kg. That is higher than the ~¥2,000
quoted elsewhere [2]; the two figures come from different years and
probably different price points (farm gate vs total value).

**Weekly growth check.** Input: about 40% a day [2]. 1.4⁷ = 10.5, matching
the source's "tenfold in a week".
