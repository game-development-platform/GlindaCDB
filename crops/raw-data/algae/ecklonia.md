# Ecklonia kelps (*Ecklonia* spp.)

## Naming and scope

This covers the kelp genus *Ecklonia* (Laminariales, Lessoniaceae) as the
single cropID `ecklonia`. The genus has about seven species: four in the
Northern Hemisphere and three in the Southern [1].

| Species | Where | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| ***E. maxima*** (sea bamboo) | Cool west coast of South Africa (and Namibia) | Wild-cut abalone feed, Kelpak biostimulant, beach-cast alginate [2][3] |
| ***E. radiata*** (golden kelp) | Australia, New Zealand, South Africa | Dominant kelp of Australia's Great Southern Reef; an aquaculture target in Australia and New Zealand [1][5][6] |
| ***E. cava*** (gamtae) | Korea (esp. Jeju), central and southern Japan | Phlorotannin extracts and supplements, abalone feed [4] |
| ***E. stolonifera*** | Korea, Japan | Cultivated summer abalone feed [4] |
| ***E. kurome***, ***E. radicosa*** | Japan | Not researched here [1] |

These species differ in where they grow, what they're used for and how
they're farmed, more than most single rows in this project. A future pass
may want to split them (see Open research questions).

**Genus boundary:**

- A 2015 phylogeny confirmed *E. radiata* and *E. maxima* as distinct
  species. It found *E. radiata* to be a single species across South
  Africa, Australia and New Zealand, and the East Asian species a separate
  lineage [1].
- The same study suggested folding *Eisenia* and *Eckloniopsis* into
  *Ecklonia* [1].
- A 2020 multigene study then reinstated *Eisenia* as its own genus [1].
  So **arame (*Eisenia bicyclis*) stays its own row (`arame`)**, and
  *Eckloniopsis radicosa* is now treated as *Ecklonia radicosa* [1].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`arame`** (*Eisenia bicyclis*). Already listed. Its young sporophytes
  are virtually indistinguishable from *Ecklonia* [1]. Cross-linked.
- ***Laminaria pallida*.** Proposed as `laminaria_pallida`. It is South
  Africa's other commercial kelp, collected as beach-cast alongside
  *E. maxima* for alginate, and the bulk of kelp biomass on the northern
  West Coast [2].
- **`giant_kelp`.** In Tasmania, *E. radiata* has widely replaced lost
  giant kelp forests [6]. Cross-linked.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — *E. maxima* is a wild crop whose biggest customer is the
abalone farm.**

- **Abalone feed.** South African abalone farming grew as wild abalone was
  poached and overfished, and *E. maxima* became the main feed [2].
  Frond harvests reached 3,000–5,000 t fresh weight (FW) a year, then
  over 7,000 t FW [2]. A later source gives 5,542 t of fresh kelp used for
  abalone in 2010 [2].
- **Demand.** Abalone eat about 7% of their body mass a day, so a 100 t
  abalone farm needs about 5 t of fresh kelp fronds daily [2]. As early as
  2006 the resource was "approaching limits of sustainable harvesting" in
  some areas [2].
- **Beach-cast.** Cast *E. maxima* and *L. pallida* have been collected
  since at least 1953, sun-dried, milled and exported for alginate [2].
  Collection peaked at about 5,000 t dry weight (DW) in 1977 and has
  averaged 1,312 t DW a year since [2]. No commercial extraction happens
  in South Africa because of international (largely Chinese) competition,
  although lab alginate yields are 22–40% [2].
- **Management.** The coast is divided into 23 seaweed harvesting areas,
  and a 2015 policy noted 14 commercial right holders [2].

**Finding 2 — cutting only the fronds keeps the plant alive.** The usual
method had divers cut every *E. maxima* plant with a stipe longer than
about 50 cm [2]. Because abalone eat only the fronds, trials at Danger
Point tested frond-only harvest. It left the rest of the plant alive and
gave a higher yield, and is "likely to minimise damage" to the bed [2].

Even so, the Danger Point beds (about 22,973 t fresh mass over 336 ha)
were judged insufficient for projected abalone demand [2]. That works out
to about 68 t/ha standing stock (see Estimates). Bed biomass varies from 3
to 24 kg FW/m², with swings of up to 50% over time [2]. Destructive
harvest also hits three red algae that grow only on old *E. maxima* plants
[2].

**Finding 3 — Kelpak: a crop made from another crop's growth hormones.**
Fresh *E. maxima* has been harvested since 1979 to make Kelpak, a liquid
plant-growth stimulant [2][3]. Its "cell burst" process breaks the cells
without heat, freezing or harsh chemicals [3]. Measured contents [3]:

- Total auxins of 33.91 pmol/mL, chiefly indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and
  conjugates.
- Total cytokinins of 4.88 pmol/mL, isoprenoid and aromatic.
- Polyamines.

Because auxins dominate, the extract mainly promotes rooting [3]. The kelp's
own cytokinin profile shifts with the seasons, with free cytokinins in
summer and glucosides in winter [3].

The manufacturer claims over 1,200 field trials across 103 crops, use in
over 85 countries, and average yield increases of about 12% in field crops
[3]. These are manufacturer claims, and independent results are mixed:
one vegetable study found no yield gain over controls [3].

**Finding 4 — *E. radiata* has a heat threshold of about 23 °C, and
crossing it caused a forest loss that hasn't recovered.**

- **2011 heatwave.** A marine heatwave off Western Australia pushed
  temperatures more than 2 °C above normal, and up to 5 °C in places, for
  over 10 weeks along more than 2,000 km of coast [5]. This exceeded the
  roughly 23 °C net-growth threshold of *E. radiata* [5].
- **Losses.** By early 2013, 43% (963 km²) of west-coast kelp forest was
  gone. Losses across the Great Southern Reef reached about 2,300 km², with
  a 100 km range contraction at the warm edge [5]. Turf algae took over,
  and the kelp has not recovered 8 to 15 years later [5].
- **Genetics.** Low-diversity, low-latitude forests were devastated, while
  high-diversity high-latitude forests showed no canopy loss under similar
  anomalies [5].

**Finding 5 — *E. cava* is valued for its phenolics, not its bulk.**
*E. cava* is rich in phlorotannins (eckol, dieckol, phlorofucofuroeckol,
6,6′-bieckol), reported at up to about 18.3% of dry weight [4]. That figure
comes from a low-weight secondary source.

Commercial products:

- A Jeju company's ethanol extract (SeaPolynol / Seanol) is about 92%
  polyphenol, with dieckol the largest single component at 16.6% [4].
- Supplements from it have been sold in Japan and Korea since 2004 [4]. The
  US FDA acknowledged a dietary-supplement notification in 2008 [4]. An EU
  novel-food application was assessed by Ireland's food-safety authority in
  2016 [4].
- Dieckol stayed stable across processing temperatures [4].

Sources disagree on food use. One says *E. cava* is popular in Korea and
Japan as a food ingredient. Another says it has rarely been eaten and is
mainly abalone feed [4].

**Finding 6 — farmed *E. cava* yields poorly; its relative
*E. stolonifera* regrows and yields four times more.** In Korean rope
culture, *E. cava* gave about 2.6–3.6 kg wet weight per m of rope [4].
*E. stolonifera* gave about 12 kg/m [4]. The difference is that only
*E. stolonifera* regenerates blades from its holdfast, which makes it the
preferred biomass species [4]. Both are grown as summer abalone feed,
filling the August–November gap when *Undaria* and *Saccharina* aren't
available [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelps with a heteromorphic life cycle
  (macroscopic sporophyte, microscopic gametophytes) [4].
  - *E. cava*: matures mainly February–June (per a Korean source) [4].
  - *E. maxima*: a surface-canopy kelp with a long stipe [2].
  - *E. radiata*: a smaller understorey-to-canopy kelp [5][6].
- **Harvest pattern:**
  - *E. maxima*: wild frond cutting, either whole plants with stipes over
    50 cm or frond-only, plus beach-cast collection (Finding 1–2) [2].
  - *E. cava*: wild collection on Jeju [4], plus single-harvest rope
    culture.
  - *E. stolonifera*: regrowth from the holdfast allows repeat cropping
    [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Korean rope culture: 2 months indoor and
  2 months intermediate culture, then the main grow-out season (March to
  June of the following year was measured) [4]. Wild age to maturity was
  not sourced.
- **Climate zones:**
  - *E. maxima*: cool-temperate South African west coast (Cape Agulhas to
    at least Cape Columbine, and on to Namibia) [2].
  - *E. radiata*: temperate to subtropical Australia, New Zealand and South
    Africa [1][6].
  - *E. cava*: warm-temperate Korea and Japan [4].

  Thermal data:
  - *E. radiata* net-growth threshold about 23 °C (Finding 4) [5].
  - *E. cava* blade growth best at 15 °C in a temperature trial (5.8% fresh
    weight per day) [4]. Its gametophytes have a higher optimum (about
    25 °C) than arame's [4].

  Chill-hours/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
  Depth: in culture, *E. cava* nursery growth was best at 2 m and main
  culture at 1 m [4].
- **Composition:**
  - *E. cava*: phlorotannins up to about 18.3% DW [4] (secondary source).
    Also contains carotenoids, fucoidans and peptides [4].
  - South African kelps: alginate 22–40% DW [2].
  - *E. radiata*: biomass composition (alginate, glucose, phlorotannins)
    varies among New Zealand sites [6]. Its seasonal alginate pattern
    disagreed between two Australian states [6].

  No species-specific iodine value was found. No primary food-database
  entry was located.
- **Edibility:** *E. cava* is edible and used in supplements, with
  conflicting reports on how much it is eaten as food (Finding 5) [4].
  *E. radiata* is sold commercially as an edible seaweed in New Zealand
  [7]. *E. maxima* is described as edible by its biostimulant
  manufacturer [3], a weak source for food use.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Photoperiod was among the factors used to
  control regeneration and maturation of free-living *E. cava* gametophytes
  for seed production [4]. Specific values were not extracted.
- **Yield:**
  - Farmed *E. cava*: 2.6–3.6 kg wet weight per m of rope [4].
  - Farmed *E. stolonifera*: about 12 kg/m [4].
  - Wild *E. maxima*: 3–24 kg FW/m² standing biomass [2], about 68 t/ha at
    Danger Point (see Estimates).
  - South African frond harvest: 3,000–7,000+ t FW a year [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable.
- **Propagation:** Sexual. Zoospores are released from sori on mature
  thalli and collected onto seed string [4]. Free-living gametophyte
  cultures can be induced to mature for seed production [4]. Restoration
  and aquaculture work uses local wild stock: New Zealand *E. radiata*
  shows strong genetic structure, so farm genotypes could dilute wild
  diversity [6]. No clonal or viral degeneration risk was found.
- **Labor:** *E. maxima* is cut by divers or from boats [2][3]. No
  labour-hours figure was found.
- **Soil:** Not applicable (rocky reefs). Salinity: not sourced.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Beach-cast kelp is sun-dried and milled for
  export [2]. Fresh fronds go straight to abalone farms [2]. Kelpak is made
  from fresh kelp without a drying step [3]. Fresh *E. cava* for research is
  frozen at −20 °C [4].
- **Establishment:** Korean method: zoospores seeded on string, about 2
  months in indoor tanks, intermediate culture, then out to main culture
  ropes [4]. New Zealand trials compared vertical and horizontal lines and
  depths at a green-lipped mussel farm [6].
- **Cultivars:** None. *E. cava* grown in Jeju "magma seawater" tanks had
  higher polyphenol and dieckol content than wild plants [4], a
  culture-system effect rather than a cultivar. *E. cava* from a warmer bay
  in Japan grew shorter than plants from a cooler bay [4].
- **Weather response:** Marine heatwaves are the dominant risk for
  *E. radiata* (Finding 4) [5]. Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Fully marine, subtidal [2][4].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** *E. stolonifera* and *E. cava* fill the
  summer–autumn abalone-feed gap in Korea [4]. *E. maxima* frond-only
  harvest preserves the plant (Finding 2) [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Obligate red-algal epiphytes on old
  *E. maxima* plants are affected by harvest [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Indoor tank culture of *E. cava*, including
  "magma seawater" tanks on Jeju [4]. Indoor nitrogen-source trials aimed
  at mass production [4]. No closed-system data were found.

## Hazards

- **Arsenic (*E. radiata*).** Total arsenic is 40–153 mg/kg DW, almost
  entirely as arsenoribosides (organic arsenosugars) [7]. Growth stage did
  not affect concentration [7]. A New Zealand edible-seaweed survey
  including *E. radiata* found inorganic arsenic well under New Zealand's
  limit [7].
- **Iodine.** Not quantified for *Ecklonia* in this pass. **TO BE
  ADDRESSED** — the other kelps are all high, so this is a gap rather than
  evidence of safety.
- **Concentrated phlorotannin supplements.** Regulated as novel foods or
  dietary supplements (EU application, US notification) [4]. No safety
  findings were extracted.
- **Over-harvest.** South African frond harvest is near sustainable limits
  in some areas [2], and destructive harvest damages epiphyte habitat [2].
- **Climate.** *E. radiata* forests at the warm edge have collapsed and not
  recovered (Finding 4) [5].
- No fungal, ergot-type or disease hazard was found.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (abalone feed, South Africa):** *E. maxima* fronds cut by divers
  → delivered fresh to land-based abalone farms (about 5 t a day per
  100 t of abalone) [2].
- **Chain (biostimulant):** fresh *E. maxima* → cold "cell burst"
  extraction → liquid concentrate (Kelpak) → root dip, seed soak or foliar
  spray on crops [3].
- **Chain (alginate, export):** beach-cast *E. maxima* and *L. pallida* →
  sun-dried → milled → exported for alginate extraction abroad. Powdered
  kelp has also gone to Japan for fish feed [2].
- **Chain (supplements):** *E. cava* from Jeju → crushed → ethanol
  extraction → filtered and purified phlorotannin concentrate → capsules
  [4].
- **Chain (abalone feed, Korea):** rope-cultured *E. stolonifera* and
  *E. cava* → summer abalone feed [4].
- **Other:** health food, fertiliser, aromatherapy and cosmetics from South
  African kelp [2]; animal feed and fertiliser from *E. cava* [4]; cosmetic
  whitening extracts from *E. cava* [4].

## Open research questions

- **Split the row?** Whether `ecklonia` should become species rows
  (`ecklonia_maxima`, `ecklonia_radiata`, `ecklonia_cava`,
  `ecklonia_stolonifera`). Their uses, ranges and farming differ more than
  in most single rows.
- **Iodine.** No iodine values for any *Ecklonia* species.
- ***E. kurome*** (Japan): not researched.
- **Wild *E. cava* volume.** The Jeju collection volume wasn't sourced.
- **Current harvest data.** South African harvest statistics after 2010
  weren't found.
- **Phlorotannin figure.** The 18.3% DW value needs a primary source.
- **Biostimulant evidence.** Independent, non-manufacturer evidence on
  Kelpak yield effects is mixed and was only sampled.
- **Photoperiod.** Values for gametophyte maturation weren't extracted.
- **Proposed row.** Whether `laminaria_pallida` should become a full row.

## Estimates and derivations

**Danger Point *E. maxima* standing stock, about 68 t FW/ha.** Inputs:
22,973 t fresh mass in 336 ha of kelp beds [2]. Arithmetic:
22,973 / 336 = 68.4 t/ha = 6.84 kg/m². That sits within the 3–24 kg FW/m²
range reported coastwide [2]. Standing stock is not annual yield, and
regrowth rate after harvest wasn't sourced.

**Abalone feed demand cross-check.** Input: about 5 t of fresh kelp per
day for a 100 t abalone facility [2]. Arithmetic: 5 t × 365 = 1,825 t a
year per 100 t of abalone. At 7,000 t a year of harvest [2], that supports
about 7,000 / 1,825 × 100 ≈ 380 t of standing abalone at the stated
feeding rate. That is illustrative only: farms also use formulated feed
and *Ulva*.
