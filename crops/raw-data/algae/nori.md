# Nori (*Neopyropia yezoensis*, *Neoporphyra haitanensis* and relatives; formerly *Porphyra* / *Pyropia* spp.)

**Category:** Algae · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/algae/nori.md`
**Researched:** 2026-11-11

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This covers the farmed foliose red algae (order Bangiales, family
Bangiaceae) sold as nori (Japan), gim or kim (Korea) and zicai (China),
known in English as laver or purple laver [1][2]. It covers the single
cropID `nori`. The main farmed species are [1][2][3]:

- ***Neopyropia yezoensis*** (susabi-nori; formerly *Porphyra yezoensis*,
  then *Pyropia yezoensis*). The main species in Japan and Korea, and the
  northern Chinese crop.
- ***Neoporphyra haitanensis*** (tan zicai; formerly *Pyropia
  haitanensis*). The southern Chinese crop.
- ***Neopyropia tenera*** (asakusa-nori; formerly *Porphyra tenera*). The
  original Japanese nori, now largely replaced.
- Minor farmed species: *Pyropia seriata* and *P. dentata* (Korea) and
  *P. pseudolinearis* (Japan) [1].

The name has moved twice in fifteen years (Finding 2), so this doc uses the
2020 names for the two main species and gives the older ones alongside.
Most sources still say *Pyropia* or *Porphyra*.

Green laver (aonori) is a different product, made from green algae such as
*Ulva*, *Enteromorpha* and *Monostroma* [4]. It isn't covered here.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`tan_zicai`** (*Neoporphyra haitanensis*, proposed). It makes up about
  75% of Chinese output, and its climate differs sharply from the main
  species (Finding 6) [5]. It is in a different genus under the 2020 split
  [1], doesn't produce monospores [6], and is mostly eaten at home in
  China rather than exported [6]. This doc treats it as part of nori, but
  it may deserve its own row.
- **`laver`** (*Porphyra umbilicalis*, proposed). The North Atlantic laver
  of Welsh laverbread, a PDO product, gathered wild by hand [7]. It is the
  species in which the conchocelis phase was discovered [8]. It stays in
  *Porphyra* rather than *Pyropia* [1]. It is wild-harvested and is a
  different product from sheet nori.
- ***Neopyropia tenera* (asakusa-nori).** Not proposed. It was the
  traditional crop, but over 80% of its cultivation was replaced by
  *N. yezoensis*, mainly for disease resistance [2][4]. It is now
  nationally endangered in the wild in Japan (Finding 2) [2]. As a crop it
  differs mainly in disease tolerance, so it is covered here.
- ***Pyropia suborbiculata*.** Not proposed. An Asian species introduced to
  the Atlantic and authorised for aquaculture in the US [9]. It came up
  only as an invasion case.
- New Zealand's karengo (*Pyropia* spp.) is gathered wild [1]. It wasn't
  researched further.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — nobody could seed this crop until 1949, because half its
life cycle lives inside seashells.**

- **The discovery.** A pink filamentous alga that bores into shells had
  been described as a separate species, *Conchocelis rosea*. In 1949
  Kathleen Drew-Baker, working at Manchester on *Porphyra umbilicalis*,
  showed it is a phase of the laver life cycle [8]. Her short Nature paper
  appeared in October 1949 [8].
- **The life cycle.** The leafy blade is the haploid stage, and the
  shell-boring conchocelis is the diploid stage [8]:
  1. Fertilised blades release carpospores.
  2. Carpospores bore into shells and grow through summer as conchocelis.
  3. In autumn the conchocelis release conchospores.
  4. Conchospores settle and grow into winter blades [6].
- **Why the shells mattered.** She cracked it only after adding oyster
  shells to her tanks, following nine years of failed attempts [8].
- **The crisis it solved.** Japanese nori farming dates from the
  17th century [8][6], but it relied on wild spores. The crop was nicknamed
  "lucky" or "gambler's" grass [8]. In 1948, typhoons and coastal
  pollution brought about a collapse of production [8].
- **Japanese uptake.** Sokichi Segawa replicated her work, and Japanese
  researchers built artificial seeding of nets on it. That is the basis of
  the modern industry [8].
- **Recognition.** She never visited Japan. She is honoured there as
  "Mother of the Sea", with an annual Drew Festival on 14 April at Uto,
  Kumamoto, since 1953 [8].

**Finding 2 — the crop's scientific name is unstable, and the species
can't be told apart without DNA.**

- **2011 split.** A molecular revision kept *Porphyra* for the lineage of
  the North Atlantic *P. purpurea*. It moved the commercial species to
  *Pyropia* and created four new genera [1].
- **2020 split.** *Pyropia* was split again: *P. yezoensis* and *P. tenera*
  went to *Neopyropia*, and *P. haitanensis* to *Neoporphyra* [1]. A 2022
  critique argued that support for some of the new genera is weak, and
  that the line between "cold temperate" *Neopyropia* and "subpolar to cold
  temperate" *Pyropia* is hard to justify [1]. A 2024 paper describes the
  revision as still under discussion [9].
- **Cultivar-level names.** The farmed long-bladed forms (*yezoensis*
  f. *narawaensis* and *tenera* var. *tamatsuensis*) were left out of the
  2020 transfer. They got their *Neopyropia* combinations in a separate
  2020 note [1].
- **Why it matters here.** The species are close to impossible to identify
  in the field [1]. Several introductions went unnoticed until DNA
  barcoding found them (Finding 7) [9].
- **The original crop is now endangered.** *N. tenera* is nationally
  Critically Endangered + Endangered on Japan's Ministry of Environment red
  list. Several prefectures list it as locally extinct or critically
  endangered [2]. Japanese farmed *N. yezoensis* also has lower genetic
  diversity than wild Chinese populations [2].

**Finding 3 — the most valuable seaweed per tonne, with the same FAO
conversion problem as wakame, but pointing the other way.**

- **Scale.** FAO reported 2.9 Mt wet in 2019, 8.9% of world seaweed output
  [3]. South Korea, China and Japan produced 99.87% of the 2017 total [3].
- **Value per tonne.** At US$523 per wet tonne it has the highest value of
  any farmed seaweed. Kelp is US$141 and *Kappaphycus*/*Eucheuma* US$172
  [3]. An older FAO overview put nori at about US$16,000 per dry tonne,
  against US$2,800 for kombu [3].
- **The China conversion.**
  - China's yearbook gives 222,000 t dry for 2020 [3]. FAO converts this to
    about 2.2 Mt wet using a 10% dry-matter factor.
  - One industry source says the true wet-to-dry ratio for sheets is 5% [3].
    If so, FAO's wet figure is about half what it should be (see
    Estimates). For wakame the same 10% factor overstates output
    (`wakame`, Finding 1).
  - Back-calculating from Japanese and Korean sheet counts gives 6–10% dry
    recovery (see Estimates), so the 5% figure isn't confirmed.
- **Japan.**
  - Output peaked at 480,000 t wet in 1994 and has fallen to about
    250,000–300,000 t [3].
  - Sheet output was 10.68 billion in fiscal 2001 and 5.94 billion in
    fiscal 2024, about 56% of the 2001 level (see Estimates). Wholesale
    price hit a record ¥24.1 per sheet, more than double 2020 [10].
  - The 2022 harvest (4.8 billion sheets) was the lowest in 51 years,
    against domestic demand of about 7.5 billion sheets [10].
- **Korea.**
  - Korea now holds over 70% of the world laver market [11].
  - Gim exports were US$997 million in 2024 and a record US$1.13 billion in
    2025 [11]. They were US$222 million in 2015, so about 5 times higher
    (see Estimates) [11]. Gim has been Korea's top seafood export since 2019
    [11].
  - Korean nori is *N. yezoensis* for almost 70% of output [3], and makes up
    68% of the value of Korean seaweed production [3].
- **China's two crops.** *N. haitanensis* makes up about 70–80% of Chinese
  production [3][5]. About 90% of *N. yezoensis* products are exported,
  against about 10% of *N. haitanensis* [6].

**Finding 4 — a cut-and-come-again winter crop, seeded from a freezer bank
and kept clean by drying it out.**

- **Indoor phase.** Conchocelis is grown indoors on shells from about
  February or May to October [6]. Water temperature is raised to 27–28 °C
  in midsummer. Then temperature and day length are dropped to trigger
  conchosporangia [6].
- **Seeding.** Conchospores are seeded onto nets in September–October.
  Nets are wound through spore tanks on large wheels, or seeded outdoors
  [6]. Korea has over 160 companies making conchocelis-on-shell seed [6].
  About half of farms rear their own seed [6].
- **Harvest.**
  - First cut comes 40 days after outplanting for *N. haitanensis* and
    50 days for *N. yezoensis* [6].
  - After that, cuts come every 10–15 days, 10–12 times a season, over up
    to 5 months [6].
  - *N. yezoensis* regenerates through asexual monospores shed from blade
    margins [6].
- **Freezer bank.** Seeded nets with 1–3 cm thalli are air-dried until they
  lose 20–40% of their moisture (2–3 hours), then stored at −20 °C [6].
  Air-drying and freezing also kill the red-rot pathogen [12].
- **Desiccation as husbandry.** Nets are lifted out of the water for
  several hours a day, several times a week. This kills diatoms, fouling
  algae and invertebrate larvae, which can't survive drying as nori can
  [5]. Pole nets do this naturally at low tide [6].
  - *N. haitanensis* survives a week after losing 70% of its moisture [6].
  - *N. yezoensis* blades recover photosynthesis after 4 hours in air [5].
  - Floating nets, now dominant as farms move offshore, make drying harder
    [5].
- **Acid wash.** Nets are dipped in acid baths, since nori tolerates low pH
  better than its epiphytes. This is the standard disease treatment and
  accounts for about 30% of Korean farm costs [12]. It fails against
  *Olpidiopsis* and bacterial diseases [12].

**Finding 5 — clean water starves the crop: nori bleaches when nutrients
run out.**

- **What happens.** Discoloration ("bleaching", iro-ochi) occurs when
  dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus run low, and thalli lose colour and
  quality [13].
- **Thresholds.** FAO puts normal growth at 100–200 mg N/m³, inhibited
  below 50 mg N/m³ [6]. A Korean field study saw discoloration below 5 µM
  nitrogen (about 70 mg N/m³; see Estimates) and 0.4 µM phosphorus [13].
- **History.** Bleaching has been a problem in the Seto Inland Sea and the
  Ariake Sea since the 1990s [13]. Land-based nitrogen and phosphorus
  discharge has been regulated since 1979, and coastal dissolved nitrogen
  has declined [13]. In the early 2000s it cut some Japanese farms' output to about 40%
  of normal [13].
- **Diatom blooms.** Since 2008, winter blooms of the diatom *Asteroplanus
  karianus* in the Ariake Sea have stripped nutrients during the nori
  season [13].
- **Ariake 2022.** Red tides from low rainfall and warm water hit Saga, the
  top producer. Its output fell below 1 billion sheets for the first time in
  22 years, and its premium "Ariake Ichiban" brand wasn't certified that
  year [10].

**Finding 6 — two very different climates under one product name.**

- ***N. yezoensis* (cold).** Grows at temperatures below 18 °C [5]. Normal
  growth stops below 3 °C [6]. One Korean strain's photosynthesis collapsed
  to zero after 21 days at 20 °C [5].
- ***N. haitanensis* (warm).** Blades grow best at 15–26 °C [5], with a
  lower limit of 8 °C [6]. Conchospore release and seeding are best at
  25–27 °C [5].
- **Warming.**
  - Southern China's autumn "temperature rebound" now damages
    *N. haitanensis* farms. Heat-tolerant strains Z-26, Z-61 and ZS-1 have
    been bred and are widely grown [5].
  - Korea has bred gamma-ray mutants of *N. yezoensis* that grow above
    20 °C [5].
  - In Japan, warm autumns push seeding back about a month and shorten the
    season. Fish grazing on the nets has also increased [10].

**Finding 7 — introduced mostly by accident and without anyone noticing.
The one deliberate import was approved on a temperature-barrier argument.**

- **Deliberate import to Maine.**
  - In the 1990s, Coastal Plantations International imported Japanese
    *N. yezoensis* strain U-51 to Cobscook Bay, Maine. It took two years to
    get ICES permission [9].
  - The argument was that the conchocelis needs about four months at
    26.7 °C or more, then a sharp autumn drop, to release spores. Maine's
    bay could never provide that (temperatures converted from °F; see
    Estimates) [9].
  - Five universities monitored the site, with the farm to be shut if
    escapees were found [9].
  - The project didn't reach commercial production [9]. The company also
    ran into a 150-acre-per-company lease cap and court rulings restricting
    intertidal culture [9].
- **Now listed as non-native.** *N. yezoensis* (both f. *yezoensis* and
  f. *narawaensis*) and *Pyropia katadae* are on Massachusetts's list of
  non-native seaweeds [9]. A 2008 survey found several cryptic introduced
  Asian *Porphyra* species in the Northwest Atlantic [9].
- **North Sea.** A 2024 DNA-barcoding survey of Belgian and Dutch shores
  found *Py. yezoensis* plus three other Asian species. Three were first
  records for the Northeast Atlantic [9]. The likely vector is the oyster
  trade, and the site is a historic oyster-farming area [9].
- **Iberia.** *Py. suborbiculata* reached Spain and Portugal. Early
  Atlantic records were misidentified as *N. yezoensis* [9].
- **Pathogens.** The red-rot pathogen *Pythium porphyrae* has been found
  on native New Zealand and Chilean laver [12].
- **No international invasive listing found in this pass.**

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** A heteromorphic life cycle. Microscopic diploid
  conchocelis bores into shells and grows through summer. The macroscopic
  haploid blade is one cell thick in the sheet species and grows through
  winter [8][6][4][9]. The farmed blade season is roughly October to
  April–May [6].
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again. First cut at 40–50 days, then
  every 10–15 days, 10–12 cuts over up to 5 months [6]. Harvested by boats
  with cut-and-suction machines [4].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 40 days (*N. haitanensis*) or 50 days
  (*N. yezoensis*) after nets go out [6]. That follows about 5–8 months of
  indoor conchocelis culture [6].
- **Climate zones:**
  - Native to the temperate NW Pacific: Japan, Korea and China [2][6].
    *N. yezoensis* is native to northern China and *N. haitanensis* to
    southern China [6].
  - Temperature limits are in Finding 6 [6][5]. The Seaweed Insights
    overview gives 10–20 °C as suitable for the whole life cycle [3].
  - Chill/vernalization: no true chilling requirement. Spore release is
    triggered by an autumn temperature drop and shorter days [6][9].
  - Elevation: not applicable (intertidal and shallow sea).
- **Composition:**
  - USDA FoodData Central "Seaweed, laver, raw", as reproduced by
    aggregators [4]: 85% water, 35 kcal, 5.81 g protein and 5.11 g
    carbohydrate per 100 g, β-carotene 3,121 µg. That is about 39% protein
    on a dry basis (see Estimates).
  - FAO gives 25–35% protein for dried laver, against 8–15% for brown
    seaweeds [4]. Taurine is over 1,200 mg per 100 g dry [4].
  - Iodine is low for a seaweed (Hazards) [4].
  - Vitamin B12 was measured at 25–32 µg per 100 g dry, mostly
    non-coenzyme forms [4]. The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics
    says it isn't an adequate human B12 source [4].
- **Edibility:**
  - Eaten dried as sheets (hoshi-nori), toasted (yaki-nori, which turns
    the sheet green) or seasoned as a snack (gim snack, ajitsuke) [3][4].
  - Also eaten in soups (zicai tang) [3] and as fresh fronds in Korea [11].
  - Boiled to a pulp as laverbread [7].
  - No preparation is needed beyond drying. Raw consumption carries the
    hazards below.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Male spermatia fertilise carpogonia on
  the blade [6]. *N. yezoensis* also reproduces asexually by monospores [6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Conchosporangia formation is triggered by
  cutting day length to 8–10 hours in late August–September [6]. A Maine
  operation grew conchocelis under 16-hour light [9]. Blade growth uses
  high light, 5,000–8,000 lux [6]. No day-length effect on blade yield was
  found.
- **Yield:**
  - National totals are in Finding 3 [3][10][11].
  - Per-net and per-hectare yields: Not sourced. The Maine nets were
    1.5 × 18 m [9].
  - Korea expected 160 million 100-sheet bundles (16 billion sheets) in
    2025 [11].
  - One overview lists Korea's 2021 sheet output as "1.57 million sheets".
    That is probably a unit error [3].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:**
  - Sexual, through the carpospore–conchocelis–conchospore cycle. Seed is
    conchocelis on oyster or clam shells, or free-living conchocelis
    [8][6].
  - Korean and Chinese oyster shells take up conchocelis better than
    scallop or clam shells [6].
  - Clonal-type risk: farmed lines are narrow genetically. Japanese
    *N. yezoensis* is less diverse than wild Chinese stock [2].
  - Seed can be banked on frozen nets at −20 °C (Finding 4) [6].
- **Labor:** Labour-intensive [6]. Japan had about 45,000 nori farmers in
  1964 and about 20,000 at the time of that FAO paper (1980s) [4]. The ageing workforce and low automation
  are now Japan's main constraints [3]. Acid washing is about 30% of
  Korean farm costs [12]. No labour-hours figure was found.
- **Soil:** Not applicable (nets on poles or floats). Nutrients and
  salinity:
  - Nitrogen needs are in Finding 5 [6][13].
  - Salinity: best growth at 25 psu among 17–39 psu [5]. 5 psu cut growth
    57% over 28 days, while 55 psu had no significant effect [5].
  - Japan has bred a low-salinity-tolerant line of the related
    *N. tenuipedalis* for estuaries hit by heavy rain [5].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - Fresh thalli are processed straight into dried sheets [4].
  - Hoshi-nori keeps vitamins close to fresh levels [4].
  - Shelf life of dried sheets: Not sourced.
  - Seed nets are stored frozen [6].
- **Establishment:** Nets are seeded with conchospores, then hung in one of
  three systems: fixed poles (intertidal, exposed at ebb tide), semi-floating
  rafts, or floating rafts at depths of 10–20 m [3][6][5]. In Japan,
  cultivation requires a licence [4]. Korea had 2,150 seaweed aquaculture
  licences [11].
- **Cultivars:**
  - *N. yezoensis* f. *narawaensis* (Narawa-susabi-nori) and *N. tenera*
    var. *tamatsuensis*: vigorous, long-bladed farmed forms [1].
  - Korea had 13 registered *Pyropia* cultivars as of a 2020 review [3],
    plus gamma-ray heat-tolerant mutants of strain Sugwawon no. 104 [5].
  - China's heat-tolerant *N. haitanensis* strains Z-26, Z-61 and ZS-1 [5].
  - The Japanese strain U-51, used in Maine [9].
  - Crosses of *N. yezoensis* × *N. tenera* [2].
- **Weather response:**
  - Typhoons helped cause the 1948 collapse [8].
  - Warm autumns delay seeding and shorten the season [10].
  - Heat damages blades: *N. yezoensis* above about 18–20 °C,
    *N. haitanensis* during autumn temperature rebound [5].
  - Low rainfall has been linked to red tides and nutrient shortages [10].
  - Heavy rain drops salinity in estuaries [5].
  - Frost: there is no natural frost-quality effect, but freezing is used
    deliberately to store seed nets and kill pathogens [6][12].
- **Water regime:** Marine, intertidal to shallow subtidal. Periodic
  exposure to air is part of the husbandry (Finding 4) [6][5].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Repeated cuts within one winter season
  [6]. Saga's first auction of a season set record prices [10]. How grade
  changes from cut to cut: Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. The shell substrate is essential for
  the conchocelis phase [8].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to the NW Pacific (Japan, Korea and China) [2][6].
  - Introduced in the NW Atlantic: deliberately in Maine in the 1990s, and
    as cryptic introductions [9]. Also introduced in the southern North Sea
    as a non-indigenous species, probably via oysters [9].
  - Listed as non-native in Massachusetts [9]. No IUCN or national
    "worst invader" listing was found in this pass.
  - Movement rules: Maine requires a permit to import any live marine
    organism for introduction, and a hearing before first approving a new
    non-indigenous one [9]. The Maine import also needed ICES clearance
    [9].
  - Biosecurity also runs the other way: the red-rot pathogen now occurs
    outside Asia [12].
- **Controlled environment:**
  - The whole conchocelis phase is already done indoors in tanks [6].
  - Year-round land-based blade farming is under development in Korea:
    - The national fisheries ministry has put KRW 35 billion into a
      five-year R&D programme from 2025 [11].
    - Large food companies are running pilots [11].
    - A research institute has run a land-based system since
      November 2023 [11].
  - No commercial closed-system blade production was found.

## Hazards

- **Crustacean allergen.** Amphipods living on the culture nets end up in
  nori sheets. Their tropomyosin (37 kDa) cross-reacts with shrimp and
  lobster allergen in sera from crustacean-allergic patients [14]. This is
  a hazard of the product's bycatch rather than of the alga.
- **Cadmium.** EFSA found dried laver among the highest cadmium sources in
  European seaweed data, at about 1.68 mg/kg [14]. Korean market surveys
  found much less: 0.04–0.15 mg/kg in *N. tenera*, and 0.08–0.32 mg/kg in
  finished dried laver [14]. That is roughly an 18-fold gap between the
  European mean and the Korean mean (see Estimates).
- **Arsenic.** Lower than in brown seaweeds:
  - One Korean study found 15.6 mg/kg total arsenic in laver, against
    52.1 mg/kg in sea mustard (wakame) [14].
  - Another found 0.8–1.9 mg/kg in *N. tenera* [14].
  - A processing-plant survey reported finished products up to 39 mg/kg
    (snippet-depth only) [14].
  - Australia and New Zealand cap inorganic arsenic in seaweed at
    1 mg/kg [14].
  - Inorganic arsenic in nori specifically was not sourced.
- **Iodine.** Moderate. Measured values:
  - About 40–60 mg/kg dry [4].
  - About 23 mg/kg, from 232 µg per 10 g serving [4].
  - One low-weight supplement-industry page claims 2,400–3,100 mg/kg for
    *P. umbilicalis* [4]. That conflicts by 40–78 times (see Estimates).

  EFSA flagged some laver consumers in surveys at iodine intakes above
  20 µg/kg body weight per day [14].
- **Microbial.** Raw laver picks up bacteria from seawater. Processing
  needs control of total viable counts and coliforms [14].
- **Crop diseases** (losses quantified) [12]:
  - **Red rot** (*Pythium porphyrae*, *P. chondricola*). It costs Korea
    15–20% of output and 20–30% on price. Losses were 64% in Japan (1993)
    and 25–30% in China (2003). Yield fell 84% at Fuding (2008) and
    about 50% at Haizhou Bay (2018).
  - **Olpidiopsis disease** (an oomycete). Acid washing doesn't control it.
  - **Green-spot disease and diatom felt.**
- **Ecological.** Introduced, often cryptically, outside Asia (Finding 7)
  [9].
- No ergot-type contamination is applicable. Fungal-like oomycete disease
  is a crop loss, not a food toxin.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (sheet nori):** cut fronds → washed and scrubbed in seawater →
  minced → slurry poured onto frames or mats [8] (the paper-making method,
  used since about 1750) → hot-air dried at 40 ± 1 °C → hoshi-nori sheet,
  about 21 × 19 cm and 3 g [4][14][6].
  - Then toasted (yaki-nori) and/or seasoned (ajitsuke-nori, gim snack)
    [3][4].
  - Sold through cooperative auctions in Japan [3][10].
- **Chain (laverbread):** wild *P. umbilicalis* hand-gathered in Wales →
  boiled to a dark pulp → sometimes rolled in oatmeal and fried. Eaten
  with bacon and cockles [7].
- **Food products:** sushi wrappers, onigiri, seasoned snacks, side-dish
  nori, laver soup, and cut pieces in snack foods [3][10].
- **Low-grade nori:** discoloured nori has been studied as a source of
  porphyran and for fermented seaweed sauce [13].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** porphyran and other extract uses,
  instant soups, and nori jam or wine [4]. Not chased down this pass.

## Open research questions

- **Wet-to-dry conversion.** FAO's China figure uses a 10% factor. One
  industry claim says sheets are 5%, while Japanese and Korean sheet counts
  imply 6–10%. The answer swings China's wet tonnage by up to 2 times.
- **Per-net and per-hectare yield.** None found. This is the weakest
  agronomic gap for a crop model.
- **Iodine.** Values for dried laver span 23–60 mg/kg in mainstream
  sources, against one 2,400–3,100 mg/kg claim. Needs a primary source,
  for *P. umbilicalis* especially.
- **Cadmium.** About an 18-fold gap between EFSA's laver mean and Korean
  surveys: origin, species or method?
- **Inorganic arsenic** in nori specifically.
- **Taxonomy.** Whether to adopt *Neopyropia*/*Neoporphyra* names
  project-wide while the 2020 split is disputed.
- **Proposed rows.** `tan_zicai` (split out or keep under nori?) and
  `laver`.
- **Shelf life** of dried and toasted sheets, and the quality decline
  across successive cuts.
- **US commercial status.** One 2017 review says the US has no commercial
  *Pyropia*/*Porphyra* growers. A 2019 review says *P. umbilicalis*
  cultivation began in the Gulf of Maine after 2010 [9].

## Estimates and derivations

**China's wet tonnage, 2.2 Mt (FAO) or 4.4 Mt (at 5%).** Inputs: 222,000 t
dry (2020) [3]; FAO factor 10% [3]; claimed sheet ratio 5% [3].
Arithmetic: 222,000 / 0.10 = 2,220,000 t; 222,000 / 0.05 = 4,440,000 t.
Range 2.2–4.4 Mt depending on the factor. A Chinese survey-based wet
figure would replace it.

**Dry recovery implied by Japanese and Korean sheet counts, about 6–10%.**
Japan:

- Inputs: 5.94 billion sheets (FY2024) [10]; about 3 g per sheet [6][4];
  250,000–300,000 t wet [3].
- Arithmetic: 5.94 × 10⁹ × 3 g = 17,820 t dry. 17,820 / 300,000 = 5.9%,
  and 17,820 / 250,000 = 7.1%.
- At 275,000 t that is about 21.6 sheets per kg wet, or about 46 g of
  fresh nori per sheet.

Korea:

- Inputs: 16 billion sheets expected for 2025 [11]; 500,000–600,000 t wet
  [3].
- Arithmetic: 16 × 10⁹ × 3 g = 48,000 t dry. That is 8.0–9.6% of
  wet weight.
- This assumes Korean sheets also weigh 3 g. That assumption wasn't
  sourced, and Korean gim may be lighter.

The years and definitions differ (fiscal vs calendar), so treat these as
rough figures. They sit between FAO's 10% and the claimed 5%.

**Japanese sheet output vs 2001, about 56%.** Inputs: 5.94 vs 10.68 billion
sheets [10]. 5.94 / 10.68 = 0.556.

**Korean gim export growth, about 5.1 times (2015–2025).** Inputs:
US$222.25 million (2015) [11]; US$1.13 billion (2025) [11].
1.13 × 10⁹ / 222.25 × 10⁶ = 5.08.

**Discoloration threshold in mass units, about 70 mg N/m³.** Input: 5 µM
dissolved inorganic nitrogen [13]. Arithmetic: 5 µmol/L × 14.007 µg/µmol =
70 µg/L, which is 70 mg/m³. That falls between FAO's 50 mg/m³ inhibition
point and its 100–200 mg/m³ normal range [6]. Likewise, 0.4 µM phosphorus is
0.4 × 30.97 = 12.4 µg P/L.

**Dry-basis protein from USDA raw values, about 39%.** Inputs: 5.81 g
protein, 85 g water per 100 g [4]. 5.81 / 15 = 38.7%. That is slightly
above FAO's 25–35% range [4].

**Maine temperature barrier, in °C.** Source figures in °F [9]:

- Four months at 80 °F or more: (80 − 32) × 5/9 = 26.7 °C.
- A drop of 5–7 °F to the low 70s °F: 2.8–3.9 °C, down to about
  21–23 °C.
- Blades survive in water in the 50s °F, from about 10 °C.

These are consistent with FAO's 27–28 °C summer conchocelis regime [6].

**Iodine per sheet and per upper limit.** Inputs [4]:

- 23.2 mg/kg (232 µg / 10 g) and 40–60 mg/kg dry.
- US tolerable upper limit 1,100 µg/day.

Arithmetic: 1,100 / 23.2 = 47 g (about 16 sheets), and 1,100 / 60 = 18 g
(about 6 sheets).

Against the low-weight 2,400–3,100 mg/kg claim, the ratio is
2,400 / 60 = 40 to 3,100 / 40 = 78 times higher. A primary analytical
source for *P. umbilicalis* would resolve it.

**Cadmium gap, about 18 times.** Inputs: EFSA laver 1.675 mg/kg [14];
Korean *N. tenera* mean 0.090 mg/kg [14]. 1.675 / 0.090 = 18.6.

**Season length, about 190–200 days at sea.** Inputs: first cut at 40–50
days, then a season of up to 5 months (about 150 days) [6]. 40 + 150 = 190,
and 50 + 150 = 200. At 10–15-day intervals that allows 10–15 cuts. FAO
states 10–12 [6].
