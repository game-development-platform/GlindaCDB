# Gelidium (*Gelidium* spp.; "tengusa", "ocle")

## Naming and scope

This covers the agar-bearing red algae of the genus *Gelidium* (order
Gelidiales) [1]. It covers the single cropID `gelidium`, defined in the
status table as "*Gelidium* spp."

The main harvested species are [1]:

- ***Gelidium corneum***, also published as ***G. sesquipedale***: Spain,
  Portugal, Morocco, France and Algeria. Called "ocle" in Asturias [1].
- ***Gelidium amansii***, Japan's "tengusa": Japan, Taiwan and Korea.
  *G. elegans* is also harvested [1].
- ***G. robustum***: Mexico.
- ***G. lingulatum***, ***G. chilense*** and ***G. rex***: Chile.
- ***G. pristoides***: South Africa.

The close relative *Pterocladiella capillacea* is harvested in the Azores
and New Zealand [1]. India uses *Gelidiella acerosa*, a different genus
[1].

**This is a wild-harvest resource, not a farmed crop.** The game's crop
model may need a "managed wild bed" mode for it (Finding 1).

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`pterocladiella`** (*Pterocladiella capillacea*, proposed). It is the
  next most important source of bacteriological agar and agarose after
  *Gelidium* [5]. It is harvested in the Azores since the 1960s and in New
  Zealand [1]. Cosmopolitan, and it has been tested from fragments as
  farm seed stock [2].
- **`gelidiella`** (*Gelidiella acerosa*, proposed). India's source of
  bacteriological agar, because *Gelidium* is sparse there [2]. A tropical
  genus, and cultivation trials go back decades [2].
- **`gracilaria`**. The other agar crop, cross-linked. Gracilaria supplies
  about 80% of agar and *Gelidium* about 20% [2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — nobody has managed to farm it, so the world's microbiology
labs depend on wild seaweed beds.**

- **No farming yet.** No method for cultivating it has worked, despite
  repeated attempts [2]. The two approaches tried were fragments fixed to
  floating concrete cylinders and free-floating pond culture [2]. The main
  problem is low growth rate. Commercial cultivation would probably need
  genetic improvement first [2].
- **Abandoned attempts.** Taiwanese pond culture has stopped [1].
  Moroccan restocking failed because enforcement was weak [1]. In tests,
  *Gelidium rex* grew only 1.5–3.3% a day [2].
- **Japan's partial workaround.** Rocks are thrown into productive Izu
  beds and colonised at about 5 kg per m². Doubling a US$30,000-a-year
  output that way was costed at US$100,000 of rock (an old,
  undated estimate) [2].
- **Why it can't just be planted.** It grows on rocky seabeds in turbulent
  water, so it is harvested by divers or collected after storms [3].
- **Agar from it is irreplaceable.** Its naturally high gel strength and
  low gelling temperature are hard to match [1]. Bacteriological agar with
  a low gelling temperature comes only from *Gelidium* [5]. Carrageenan
  doesn't work as a substitute for culturing microbes [3].

**Finding 2 — the 2015 agar shortage: one country, one export quota, and
lab agar at triple the price.**

- **Supply concentration.** Global landings were almost 60,000 t a year
  from the 1960s to the 1980s. They fell to about 25,000 t a year in the
  2010s, below the 1950s level [1]. Morocco's share rose from 23% in the
  1960s to 82% [1].
- **The shortage.** Morocco restricted harvests and put quotas on foreign
  exports [3]. Wholesale agar hit about US$35–45/kg, nearly triple the
  previous price [3]. Thermo Fisher stopped selling some raw agar products
  [3].
- **Moroccan rules.** Up to 80% of Moroccan landings must be processed
  in-country [1]. A presentation by the review's authors lists a
  1 October–30 June closed season and landing limits of 5,440 t (2010) and
  6,040 t (2014). It also lists a 20% export share, with processing at
  Setexam in Kénitra [1]. This is low weight, from slides, and needs
  checking against Moroccan regulation.
- **The paradox.** Morocco's domestic market was flooded and its lone
  agar producer could buy at rock-bottom prices, while the rest of the
  world faced a shortage [3]. The European Commission objected under
  free-trade agreements, to little effect [3].
- **Demand.** Lab demand rose from 250 t bacteriological agar and 15 t
  agarose to about 700 t and 50 t [1]. Lab-supply companies take about
  900 t, dwarfed by the food sector's several thousand tonnes [3].

**Finding 3 — every major source has collapsed for a different reason.**

- **Japan.** Its harvest of up to about 15,000 t a year by the early 1940s
  made Japanese *Gelidium* the world's main agar source [1]. It has since
  fallen to 2,000–3,000 t, as cheaper imports, mainly from Morocco, killed
  harvesters' interest [1].
- **Portugal.** Several thousand tonnes a year in the 1970s are now barely
  200 t [6]. The causes were falling domestic demand and overharvesting in
  two of three areas [1].
- **Spain.** Landings were down to about 300 t in the 1990s and have not
  recovered [1]. The crash was not caused by overexploitation [1]. Harvest
  was closed after the 2002 *Prestige* oil spill [1].
- **The Basque coast.** Beds are in severe decline [7]:
  - Warming, more light, fewer nutrients and big storms are blamed [7].
    August water warmed from 22 °C to 23 °C over three decades, with a
    peak of 26.2 °C in 2003 [7].
  - Wave height exceedances rose 41% while biomass fell 45% [7]. The wave
    increase rips plants off during the growing season [7].
  - About 7,800 t of standing stock was lost along 30 km of coast [7].
  - The dominant *G. corneum* canopy of 1982 had practically gone by 2014
    [7].
- **Morocco.**
  - Landings peaked at 44,000 t in 2006 and have halved since [1].
  - Unlicensed divers increased tenfold, and harvest seasons weren't
    respected [1].
  - El Jadida biomass fell 33% between 1999 and 2003 [6].
  - Overharvested beds were taken over by another red alga, *Halopithys
    incurvus* [6].

**Finding 4 — harvest is by divers and by catching what storms rip loose.**

- **Divers.**
  - In Japan most of the harvest has come from women divers (ama) [4]. They
    traditionally reach about 10 m [4]. Men with diving gear work beyond
    about 10 fathoms (about 18 m) [4].
  - Portugal and Morocco use boat-based hookah divers, up to five per boat
    working 6–7 hours a day [6].
  - An experienced diver can collect 120–250 kg dry a day [4]. Japanese ama
    are said to take up to 300 kg [4].
- **Storm-cast.**
  - In Spain and France, detached floating thalli and beach-cast weed are
    gathered [6]. Boats net floating rafts, and tractors scoop up beach-cast
    weed [6].
  - Direct harvesting is not permitted in the French Basque Country [6].
  - In Morocco, 60–80% of the biomass is uprooted by winter storms but
    probably carried out to sea [6].
- **Seasons.** Portugal: July–December [6]. Japan (Izu): opens in early
  spring and is limited by sea conditions [4]. French Basque: storm-cast
  from the first autumn swells [6].
- **Quality.**
  - Deep-water plants are the best quality [4], and shallow-water weed is
    less desirable [4].
  - Storm-tossed Spanish material was of poor quality [1].
  - Izu tengusa that has been washed and sun-dried repeatedly to amber
    ("sarashi tengusa") sells at a premium [4].

**Finding 5 — natural kanten is frost-dried, and only in winter.**

- **Tokoroten** is a gel made from tengusa broth. It was a summer snack in
  Edo-period Tokyo [4].
- **Kanten** is freeze-dried tokoroten, a Japanese invention [4]. It
  could be made only in the colder months, December to February, and the
  name means "cold weather" [4]. In 1903 Japan had 500 kanten works [4].
- **Natural kanten today** survives in Nagano (stick kanten) and Gifu
  (string kanten) [4]. Each Yamaoka-cho maker blends three to five kinds of
  tengusa [4]. Climate change threatens these producers [4].
- **Industrial kanten** is made instead from alkali-treated *Gracilaria*
  [4].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:**
  - *G. corneum* is a perennial, clonal, rocky-subtidal species [6]. It
    forms stands at 3–12 m depth [7] and down to about 15 m [6], in
    wave-exposed sites [6].
  - It grows in calm summer and reaches peak size in early autumn [6].
  - Range: Atlantic from Britain to Mauritania, with the main
    concentrations in France, northern Spain, Portugal and Morocco [6].
- **Harvest pattern:** Wild harvest by plucking or cutting (divers), or
  storm-cast collection [4][6]. One season a year in most regions [4][6].
  Plucking stems from the base is damaging when uncontrolled [6].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable (wild perennial beds).
  Regrowth rate after harvest: Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:** Temperate to warm-temperate rocky coasts, with some
  tropical species [1][5].
  - *G. corneum* is declining at the warm end of its Basque range, where
    August means are 23 °C and the peak was 26.2 °C [7].
  - *G. capense* grew slowly at 10 °C [2].
  - Optimum growth temperature for commercial species: Not sourced.
  - Chill/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
- **Composition (dry basis):**
  - *G. corneum* / *G. sesquipedale*: 62.4% carbohydrate (52.9% agar,
    9.5% cellulose), 14.8% protein, 19.6% ash [5].
  - *G. pusillum*: 11.3% protein, 24.7% fibre, 21.2% ash, 2.2% lipid [5].
  - *G. amansii*: 75.2% carbohydrate, of which 58.6% is agar [5].
  - Fresh Azorean Gelidiales are 83–90% water, with 34–41% total dietary
    fibre dry [5].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as tokoroten and kanten, which are made by boiling
  and gelling [4]. Raw use: Not sourced.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Reproduction is mostly clonal [6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. High summer light stresses
  Basque *G. corneum*, causing yellowing and breakdown [7].
- **Yield:**
  - Standing crop: about 800 g/m² wet mean, up to 3,200 g/m², at El Jadida
    [6]. Venezuela: 0.5–1.4 kg dry/m² [5].
  - Landings: global about 25,000 t a year (2010s) [1]. Mexico about
    1,000 t; South Africa 500 t; New Zealand about 250 t; Chile about
    80–100 t [1].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:**
  - Natural beds regrow clonally from basal axes. Cropping that destroys
    the creeping axes slows regrowth [2].
  - Fragment and spore culture work in the lab but are not commercial [2].
  - Japan's thrown-rock "seeding" of beds [2].
- **Labor:** Diving is labour-intensive and costly, but gives excellent
  yields [4]. Harvested weed is sun-dried, about 10 days for tengusa [4].
  It is then sold through licensed concentrators in Portugal [6].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Needs rocky substrate and high water movement
  [3][6].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Sun-dried raw weed [4][6]. About 3–4 kg fresh
  makes 1 kg dry weed [4]. Sarashi tengusa is washed and dried repeatedly
  [4]. Shelf life: Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Not applicable (wild beds). Rocks can be added to
  extend beds (Japan) [2].
- **Cultivars:** None. Spanish *G. corneum* is said to give the
  highest-quality agarose [5].
- **Weather response:**
  - Storms both supply the storm-cast harvest and destroy beds by ripping
    plants off in the growing season [6][7].
  - Warming and high summer light stress beds [7].
  - Bad sea conditions cut diving days [4].
  - Frost: wild beds are not affected, but frost is an input to natural
    kanten (Finding 5) [4].
- **Water regime:** Marine, intertidal to subtidal, on wave-exposed rock
  [4][6].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Autumn storm-cast (Basque) [6]. Summer to
  autumn diving (Portugal) [6]. Spring opening (Izu) [4]. Gel strength of
  *P. capillacea* peaked in winter in the Canaries [5].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable.
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - No commercial *Gelidium* was found recorded as invasive.
  - *Gelidium vagum*, an Asian non-crop species, is non-native in the
    Netherlands (found 2011, established; hull-fouling and oyster
    vectors). It is rare in California (1994–95) [8].
  - Morocco's export quotas and licensing restrict movement of the raw
    material. The reasons given are overharvesting concerns and protecting
    the domestic agar industry, not biosecurity (Finding 2) [1][3].
  - No IUCN listing was found.
- **Controlled environment:** Pond and tank culture has been tried, and
  pond culture is the more controllable option [2]. None is commercial.

## Hazards

- **Food:** No specific toxin or poisoning record was found for *Gelidium*
  in this pass. That contrasts with *Gracilaria*'s ogonori poisoning
  (`gracilaria`, Finding 1). Heavy metals and iodine: Not sourced.
- **Agar additive safety (E 406):** Not sourced this pass.
- **Harvest hazard:** Commercial diving, with hookah diving from boats
  [6]. Accident data: Not sourced.
- **Resource collapse:** Overharvesting collapses the beds (Finding 3)
  [1][6].
- **Oil spills:** The *Prestige* spill closed the Spanish harvest [1].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (industrial agar):**
  1. Harvest and sun-drying [4][6].
  2. Sale through licensed concentrators (Portugal) [6].
  3. Extraction. Industrial agar yields are about 11–20% of dry weight:
     Japan 19%, Korea 11%, Spain 15–20%, Portugal and Morocco 17%,
     Mexico 10–16%. 17% is assumed as the global average [1].
  4. Graded into bacteriological, technical and food agar, and agarose
     [1][3].
  - Alkali pretreatment cuts sulfate, raises 3,6-anhydrogalactose and
    gel strength [5]. In Venezuelan *Gelidium* it raised gel strength
    about threefold [5].
- **Chain (tokoroten):** tengusa boiled → broth strained → cooled into a
  gel → cut into noodles [4].
- **Chain (natural kanten):** tokoroten frozen and thawed outdoors in
  winter, then dried [4].
- **Uses:** microbiology culture media (since the 1880s) [3]; molecular
  biology (agarose) [5]; food [4]; cosmetics and sunscreen ingredients
  [6]. Protein can be recovered from agar residues; in one study, 45.5% of
  the protein [5].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** paper-making interest [2].

## Open research questions

- **Split the genus row?** `pterocladiella` and `gelidiella` are proposed.
  Should *G. amansii* (Japan, Korea) and *G. corneum* (Atlantic) also be
  separate?
- **Game modelling:** this is a wild-harvest resource with quota,
  overharvest and storm-cast mechanics, not a farmed crop. Does the crop
  model need a wild-bed mode?
- **Morocco's current regulation** is only from slides; verify the
  primary text.
- **Regrowth rate after harvest** and growth optima for commercial
  species.
- **Food safety** (heavy metals, iodine, E 406): not sourced.
- **Dry-weight basis.** FAO gives 3–4 kg fresh per kg "dry weed" [4]
  (25–33% dry). Azorean fresh plants are 83–90% water [5], so 10–17%
  dry. Commercial "dry" weed probably retains moisture. Landings statistics
  also confuse wet and dry: FAO's Moroccan figures are exactly 3 times
  lower than national data [1].

## Estimates and derivations

**Global agar output from *Gelidium*, about 2,750–5,000 t.** Inputs: about
25,000 t a year of landings (2010s) [1]; industrial yields 11–20%, average
17% [1]. Assumption: landings are dry weight. Arithmetic:
25,000 × 0.17 = 4,250 t; range 25,000 × 0.11 = 2,750 to
25,000 × 0.20 = 5,000 t. If landings are wet, divide by about 3 [1].

**Global landings decline, about 58%.** Inputs: almost 60,000 → about
25,000 t a year [1]. (60,000 − 25,000) / 60,000 = 0.583.

**Lab demand growth, about 2.8 times.** Inputs: 250 + 15 = 265 t then;
700 + 50 = 750 t now [1]. 750 / 265 = 2.83.

**Fresh-weight equivalent of a diver's daily take.** Inputs: 120–250 kg dry
a day [4]; 3–4 kg fresh per kg dry [4]. 120 × 3 = 360 to 250 × 4 = 1,000 kg
fresh. For comparison, a Baja California diver team can take up to 1.5 t
fresh a day [4].

**El Jadida bed area implied, about 17–21 km².** Inputs: 13,700–16,800 t
wet biomass; 800 g/m² mean [6]. 13.7 × 10⁶ kg / 0.8 kg/m² = 17.1 km²;
16.8 × 10⁶ / 0.8 = 21.0 km². The source surveyed 20 km of coast, so this is
consistent.

**Moroccan landings now, about 22,000 t.** Input: half of the 2006 peak of
44,000 t [1]. 44,000 / 2 = 22,000 t. That is consistent with 82% of about
25,000 t = 20,500 t [1].
