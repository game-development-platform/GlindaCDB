## Naming and scope

This covers *Laminaria hyperborea* (Gunnerus) Foslie, the forest-forming
kelp of exposed north-east Atlantic coasts [1]. It covers the single cropID
`laminaria_hyperborea`, promoted from PROPOSED (flagged in
`laminaria-digitata.md`).

**Names.**

- **English.** Tangle, cuvie [1]. Also redware, cuvy, sea rod, mayweed [1].
- **Irish.** *Slat mara* ("sea rod", the stipe) and *feamainn Bhealtaine*
  ("May weed", the cast fronds) [1].
- **Norwegian.** *Stortare* [2].
- **Trade.** In Norway it is called "forest kelp" or "northern kelp" [2].
  The old alginate trade called it and *L. digitata* "stem" [3].

**Why it gets its own row (gate check, 2026-11-16).**

- **A distinct species,** told apart from `laminaria_digitata` by its rough,
  rigid stipe that snaps when bent [1]. It grows deeper, in more exposed
  water [1].
- **Europe's largest wild seaweed harvest,** by trawl in Norway (Finding 1)
  [2].
- **A different product.** The stipe gives one of the best gelling
  alginates, processed separately from the blade (Finding 3) [3].
- **Its own harvest system:** five-year rotating fields (Finding 1) [2].

**Relatives flagged:**

- `laminaria_digitata` (cross-linked).
- `laminaria_ochroleuca` (golden kelp), since researched in
  `laminaria-ochroleuca.md` (2026-11-16). It looks similar
  but is yellower with a smooth stipe [1]. Now cross-linked.
- No new rows proposed.

**Supply is wild-managed** (Finding 1). Saved to
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/laminaria-hyperborea.md` (issue #2).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — trawled in Norway on a five-year rotation.**

- **Volume.** About 150,000 t a year for 40 years, ranging roughly
  120,000–175,000 t [2]. Trawling has run since the 1960s [2]. Almost all
  Norwegian wild seaweed harvest is this species and knotted wrack [2].
- **Where.** From Rogaland to Nordland [2]. In 2017–18, the Trøndelag
  counties took 67,247 t of a 154,645 t national total [2].
- **Rotation.** The coast is divided into fields one nautical mile wide,
  each lettered A to E [2]. Each field is open once every five years,
  followed by four years of rest [2]. Southern Nordland uses five- or
  ten-year cycles [2].
- **No quotas.** Harvest is controlled by which fields are open, not by
  tonnage limits [2].
- **Rules.** Regional regulations are set by the Directorate of Fisheries
  (J-notices J-132-2023, J-167-2024 and J-131-2022) [2]. County working
  groups revise them every five years [2].
- **Season.** For example, 1 September 2025 – 31 August 2026 in
  Rogaland–Vestland [2].
- **Monitoring and closures.** Each spring the Institute of Marine Research
  films fields due to open, with control areas closed to trawling [2]. It
  can advise closing poor fields; four were held back for 2025–26 because
  canopy was under 0.8 m [2].
- **Who trawls.** Two companies, IFF (formerly DuPont Nutrition) and
  Nutrimar Seaweed AS [2].
- **Why it matters.** A model managed-harvest system: a rotation map, a
  scientific survey, an opening date and a closing rule.

**Finding 2 — what trawling does, and how the forest regrows.**

- **What's taken.** The trawl takes mainly the large plants [2]. Small
  plants survive and grow up [2]. Holdfasts stay on the sea bed [2].
- **Understorey bank.** A pool of small plants (under 25 cm) persists under
  the canopy for years [4]. These take over once the canopy is removed [4].
  This helps the species stay dominant [4].
- **Recovery.**
  - Cleared plots in early experiments were dominated by *L. hyperborea*
    again within two years [4].
  - Beds can reach pristine density and size in 2–6 years [4]. Full
    community recovery may take about 10 years [4].
- **2025 survey.**
  - Plants on harvested fields averaged 4.7 years old, against 6.2 years in
    control areas [2].
  - Biomass was 13 kg/m² on harvested fields, against 10 kg/m² on controls
    (after storm losses) [2]. Canopy height did not differ [2].
  - Epiphytes on the stipes do not fully re-establish within the five-year
    cycle [2].
- **Urchins.** Green sea urchins can graze kelp to barrens and stop
  regrowth [4]. In one 1990s trial, no regrowth was found 1.5 years after
  harvest where urchin densities were high [2].
- **Why it matters.** The forest regrows, but the community living on it
  lags behind.

**Finding 3 — the stipe is the prize: gelling alginate.**

- **Stipe vs blade.** The stipe has alginate with very long G-blocks, which
  makes it one of the best gelling alginates [3]. The industry processes
  stem and leaf in separate lines [3].
- **Historic Irish use.** Ireland dried and exported sea rods (stipes) from
  1948 to 1965 [3]. Before that, kelp was burned in shore kilns for ash and
  iodine [3]. No alginate was ever made in Ireland; material went to
  Scotland [3].
- **Beach-cast stipes.** Cast-up stipes were once collected in Ireland for
  Scottish alginate plants, but this seems to have stopped [3]. Supply was
  erratic because storms did not always bring stipes ashore [3].
- **Lipids.** Blades have more fatty acids than stipes, and a lower
  n-6/n-3 ratio (0.8:1 vs 3.5:1) [3].
- **Why it matters.** One plant, two products: the stipe is the high-value
  part.

**Finding 4 — Scotland said no to dredging.**

- **The proposal.** In 2018, Marine Biopolymers Ltd proposed Scotland's
  first industrial harvest [5]. It planned 125,700 t over seven years, with
  harvested patches rested for five years [5].
- **The ban.** In November 2018, MSPs amended the Crown Estate (Scotland)
  Bill to ban commercial removal of entire kelp plants from Crown Estate
  sea bed [5]. More than 10,000 people had petitioned against dredging [5].
- **The argument.**
  - Critics said the company ignored recovery times for other species in
    the kelp forest [5].
  - The company estimated Scotland's standing stock at about 20 million t
    and the industry at £300 million a year [5]. Treat this as low weight;
    it is a company claim.
- **Why it matters.** The same harvest is legal and managed in one country
  and banned in the next.

**Finding 5 — long-lived and cold-adapted.**

- **Lifespan.** Up to 20 years, longer in the north [1]. Plants in Finnmark
  reached 13–18 years, against 8–9 years at Vega [1].
- **Annual rings.** Age is read from rings in the stipe cortex [1]. A new
  blade grows below the old one from November [1].
- **Size.** To about 2–3.5 m, with blades up to 1.5 m [1].
- **Depth.** From extreme low water down to the light limit: typically 8 m
  in turbid coastal water, 30 m in clear water, and up to 47 m [1].
- **Range.** From the Murman coast of Russia and north Norway to central
  Portugal, absent from the Bay of Biscay [1].
- **Temperature.** Optimum growth about 10–15 °C, peaking near 15 °C [1].
  Survival limit about 21 °C [1]. Mortality rises above 20 °C [1].
- **Why it matters.** Slow-growing and old, so a harvest takes years of
  growth at once (inference).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial kelp, up to 20 years [1].
- **Harvest pattern:** Trawl harvest of each field once every five years
  [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Harvested plants average about 5 years
  old [2].
- **Climate zones:** Cold-temperate north-east Atlantic, Portugal to
  Russia [1].
- **Composition:** Gelling alginate in the stipe [3]. Fatty acids higher
  in blade [3].
- **Edibility:** Described as edible [3]; no food tradition found.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Spores from blade sori (Not sourced in
  detail).
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** New blade grows from November [1].
- **Yield:** 13 kg/m² standing biomass on harvested fields (range 3–22)
  [2]. Trawling removes 14–30 t an hour [2].
- **Water use:** Marine.
- **Propagation:** Natural recruitment from the understorey [4]. Not
  cultivated in Scotland; experiments only [5].
- **Labor:** Mechanised trawlers [2].
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Bedrock and stable boulders [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Not sourced for modern trawling. Historically,
  stipes were dried for export [3].
- **Establishment:** Not applicable (wild).
- **Cultivars:** None.
- **Weather response:** Storms remove biomass [2] and cast stipes ashore
  [3].
- **Water regime:** Marine, subtidal, wave-exposed [1].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Season set by regulation [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Hosts rich stipe epiphytes and fauna
  [4]; urchins graze it (Finding 2).
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** Native.
- **Controlled environment:** None.

## Hazards

- **Ecological.** Trawling reduces stipe epiphytes over the five-year cycle
  [2]. Effects on seabirds are poorly known [2].
- **Food:** Not sourced (not a food crop).

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** trawl harvest → separate stipe and blade → alginate extraction
  in separate lines [2][3].
- **Uses:** Gelling alginate [3]. Historically, ash, alkali and iodine from
  kelp kilns [3].

## Estimates and derivations

**Area trawled each year, at least about 1,150 ha.** Inputs: about 150,000
t a year [2]; about 13 kg/m² on harvested fields [2]. 150,000,000 kg ÷ 13
kg/m² ≈ 11.5 million m² ≈ 11.5 km² ≈ 1,150 ha. The trawl takes mainly large
plants, so the area worked is larger than this (inference).
