# Cottonii (*Kappaphycus alvarezii*; cropID `eucheuma`)

## Naming and scope

This covers *Kappaphycus alvarezii* (Doty) Doty ex P.C. Silva, a tropical
red alga (family Solieriaceae), traded as "cottonii" [1]. It is the main
source of kappa carrageenan [1]. It covers the single cropID `eucheuma`.

**Name conflict.** The cropID says `eucheuma` but the row's species is
*Kappaphycus*.

- The species was originally *Eucheuma cottonii*, then *Eucheuma alvarezii*
  (Doty 1985), and was moved to *Kappaphycus* in 1988 [1].
- The trade still says "cottonii", and Indonesian sources still write
  "*Eucheuma cottonii*" [1][2].
- The industry groups it with *Eucheuma denticulatum* ("spinosum", formerly
  *E. spinosum*) as "eucheumatoids". They are farmed the same way and often
  grown interchangeably [1][3].

So the cropID name fits the trade group, not the species. Renaming it, for
example to `cottonii`, is a call for you; this doc keeps the existing ID.

Taxonomy is confused by morphological plasticity, colour morphs, local
names and "names of convenience" [1]. The species can't be told apart
reliably without DNA [1].

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`spinosum`** (*Eucheuma denticulatum*, proposed). It yields iota
  rather than kappa carrageenan, with 30% or more sulfate against 28% or
  less for cottonii [1]. It needs strong currents [1], and may tolerate
  stress better [1]. It is now Zanzibar's main crop after cottonii
  collapsed there (Finding 4) [4]. Carrageenan type, sulfate, habitat and
  heat tolerance all differ, so it should be its own row.
- **`sacol`** (*Kappaphycus striatus*, proposed). Genetically distinct from
  *K. alvarezii* [1]. It is farmed alongside it in the Philippines and
  Malaysia, grows more slowly, and has poorer carrageenan [1][5]. One
  Indonesian source says it also yields iota carrageenan [2].
- ***Kappaphycus malesianus*** ("aring-aring"). Farmed in Malaysia [6].
  Not proposed; it came up only in a micropropagation study.
- ***Betaphycus gelatinum*** ("gelatinae", formerly *Eucheuma gelatinae*).
  A minor producer of beta, gamma and kappa carrageenan mixtures, about
  100 t a year in the 1980s [1]. Not proposed.
- **`irish_moss`** (*Chondrus crispus*). The original carrageenan source,
  which farmed cottonii displaced on labour cost [1][5]. Already a LISTED
  row; cross-linked.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a crop grown entirely from cuttings, and probably one clone
worldwide.**

- **One haplotype.** A 2006 genetic survey found that all *K. alvarezii*
  then cultivated worldwide share a similar mitochondrial haplotype [1].
  Samples from Hawaii and some from Africa are distinct [1]. *E.
  denticulatum* by contrast may have been "domesticated" several times [1].
- **Propagation.** Farms are restocked from vegetative cuttings of the
  last crop [6]. In Vietnam the plant doesn't reproduce sexually in local
  waters, so cuttings are the only route [6].
- **Strain fatigue.** Continuous cutting since commercial farming began
  around 1970 is blamed for "strain fatigue": loss of vigour, slower
  growth, and more ice-ice disease and epiphytes [6]. In Vietnam it has
  also lowered carrageenan quantity and quality [6].
- **Farmer selection.** Philippine farmers' own seed-stock selection was
  already blamed for declining yields in the late 1980s [6].
- **Fixes.** Tissue culture and spore-derived seedlings are being
  developed. A SEAFDEC protocol takes about 90 days in the lab [6].
  Vietnam cut seedling production from 240 to under 150 days and reports
  faster-growing, more disease-resistant lines [6]. Tissue-cultured
  plantlets in Myanmar grew 9.1–9.3% a day [7].

**Finding 2 — official production is probably several times too high.**

- **Global.** Carrageenophytes were 10.99 Mt wet in 2014, 77% of all
  farmed red seaweed [6]. Red seaweeds were 51.7% of world seaweed
  cultivation by tonnage in 2020, concentrated on
  *Kappaphycus*/*Eucheuma* and *Gracilaria* [3].
- **Indonesia** is the world's largest eucheumatoid producer [3].
  - Government statistics say production rose about tenfold from 2000 to
    2015, to 7 Mt wet in 2021 [3].
  - Industry estimates say 1.3–1.5 Mt [3], about one-sixth of the official
    figure [3].
- **Philippines.** Official output peaked at 1.8 Mt (2011), and was 1.3 Mt
  in 2021, against an industry estimate of 500,000 t [3].
- **Malaysia.** About 180,000 t official, against 30,000 t from industry
  [3].
- **Overall.** The official/industry ratios are about 5 times (Indonesia),
  2.6 times (Philippines) and 6 times (Malaysia) (see Estimates). That is
  the same kind of statistics gap as wakame and nori, but larger.
- **Value.** Indonesia's eucheumatoid earnings, from FAO trade data,
  doubled from US$122.3 million (2012) to US$258.7 million (2022) [3]. The FAO-cited trade in
  carrageenan seaweed was worth about US$1 billion in 2015 [4].

**Finding 3 — a 45-day cut-and-replant crop, with growth that varies
tenfold by site, season and strain.**

- **Method.** Cuttings of about 100 g are tied ("tie-tie") to lines at
  about 50 cm spacing [5]. The lines are fixed off-bottom between stakes,
  hung as floating long-lines or tied to rafts [1][5].
- **Cycle.** About 42–45 days, roughly six weeks, then harvest and
  replant [5]. Farming runs year-round in much of Indonesia, the southern
  Philippines and Malaysia [5].
- **Growth rates.**
  - 2.3–4.2% a day in the best months in the central Philippines
    (September–February), against the lowest in July–August [5].
  - 1.13–1.72% a day in an Indonesian seagrass trial [5].
  - About 0.7% a day for escaped wild plants in India [8].
  - Up to 9% a day for tissue-cultured plantlets [7].
- **Yield.** 3.6–15.8 kg fresh per metre of line in the best months [5].
  That is about 0.4–1.8 kg dry per metre (see Estimates).
  - A Kenyan trial got 0.75 kg dry per rope from off-bottom culture at
    0.5 m depth [5]. Only that method was profitable at US$0.50/kg dry [5].
  - Per-hectare yield: one industry source argues an average isn't
    meaningful, because line density varies so much [5].
- **Bigger cuttings.** A 2025 study argues farmers should look at
  absolute rather than relative growth. Smaller cuttings grow faster in
  percentage terms but add fewer grams a day [5].

**Finding 4 — a narrow 25–30 °C window, and warming has already
collapsed cottonii in Zanzibar.**

- **Optimum.** Best growth is at about 25–30 °C [4][7], with 28 °C the
  peak in one trial [7]. Growth slows above about 31 °C, and plants turn
  fragile above 33 °C [4][7]. 19 °C was below the recommended range in a
  Philippine farm [7].
- **Salinity.** Tolerance is 25–45 psu. Plants at 15 psu died within
  3 days [7].
- **Brazilian strain.** Drifting Brazilian specimens grew best at
  20–24 °C [7]. This conflicts with the tropical optimum, and matters for
  invasion risk (Finding 6).
- **Zanzibar.**
  - Farming began in 1989 with cottonii and spinosum imported from the
    Philippines [4]. Over 80% of the roughly 23,000 farmers are women [4].
  - Shallow-water temperatures rose from about 30 °C in the late 1990s to
    as high as about 38 °C in 2013 (converted from °F) [4].
  - Cottonii production fell 94% by 2015 [4]. Spinosum, which earns less
    but tolerates heat better, is now the main crop [4].
  - Deep-water tubular nets borrowed from Brazil keep some cottonii going
    [4]. That pushes farming beyond the shallows traditionally used by
    women, many of whom can't swim [4].
- **Elsewhere.** Commercial cultivation in Myanmar stopped in 2012 because
  of recurring ice-ice [7]. Farms on several islands in Semporna, Sabah,
  had stopped by 2018, by farmers' accounts [9].

**Finding 5 — ice-ice: a stress-triggered bacterial whitening that is the
crop's main loss.**

- **What it is.** Thalli whiten from the tips and eventually die [4]. It is linked to
  stress from rising temperature and falling salinity, plus opportunistic
  or pathogenic bacteria [9]. The Vibrio–Aeromonas and
  Cytophaga–Flavobacterium complexes are implicated, with low light also
  a trigger [9]. In Zanzibar, farm temperatures of 29.5–35.5 °C are a prime
  trigger [9].
- **Epiphytes.** *Neosiphonia* epiphytes (up to 80–85% of epiphyte cover
  is *N. savatieri*) break out in the dry-season transitions of
  March–June and September–November. They penetrate the host's cortex [9].
  Infection appears from about 21 days into a cycle [9].
- **Losses.**
  - Ice-ice and epiphytes cost the Philippines 15% of *Kappaphycus*
    production in 2011–2013 [9].
  - A low-weight web source claims outbreaks can destroy 30–80% of a
    season's crop [9].
  - Disease shrinks seed stock, cuts carrageenan quality and lowers
    prices [9].

**Finding 6 — farmed close to coral reefs, and has invaded them in Hawaii
and India.**

- **Hawaii.**
  - Introduced to Kāne'ohe Bay for cultivation in the 1970s, usually
    dated 1974 [8].
  - It has spread at about 260 m a year [8]. In some reefs it went from
    under 10% to over 50% cover in about two years, and it occupies up to
    80% of the substrate in places [8].
  - It overgrows and kills live coral [8], and is listed in Hawaii as a
    noxious aquatic weed [8].
  - Spread was long thought to be only vegetative, but reproductive plants
    have since been found there [8].
- **India.**
  - Introduced to the Gulf of Mannar Biosphere Reserve region for
    farming in the 1990s, with large-scale Palk Bay culture from 2001 [8].
  - By 2008 it had escaped and was smothering *Acropora* corals on
    Kurusadai Island [8]. Manual removal followed [8].
  - A 2010 survey put the invaded canopy at only 76.7 m², less than
    0.0035% of the reef area [8]. The severity is disputed.
- **Other invaded areas.** Wikipedia also lists Malaysia, the Sulu Sea and
  the southern Philippines [8]. These are within or near the native range,
  so treat that claim as low weight.
- **Brazil.** Brazil allows farming only in designated south-eastern and
  southern zones (Agronomic data, Invasiveness) [10].
- **A 2021 review** calls it a "destructive" non-native mostly cultured
  very close to coral [8].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial thallus, grown clonally. Farming runs
  year-round in Indonesia, the southern Philippines and Malaysia [5].
  Peaks are seasonal elsewhere: March–May (dry season) in parts of the
  Philippines, and September–February at an Antique site [5].
- **Harvest pattern:** Whole-crop harvest and replant every 42–45 days
  [5]. Some of each harvest is kept as next cycle's seed [6].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** About 45 days [5].
- **Climate zones:**
  - Native to the Indo-Pacific tropics, especially the Coral Triangle
    (Philippines, Indonesia and Malaysia), and to the Old World tropics
    westward to East Africa [1].
  - Grows in warm, clear, clean water [5]. Temperature and salinity limits
    are in Finding 4 [4][7].
  - Chill/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
- **Composition (dry basis):**
  - Indian seasonal survey: protein 12.7–23.6 g/100 g and fibre
    9.7–18.6 g/100 g [2].
  - A dried market sample from Tawi-Tawi had much lower protein,
    2.73 g/100 g, with 44.8 g carbohydrate and 17% moisture [2].
  - A commercial powder had 5.1% protein and 14.5% ash [2].
  - Sulfate content is 28% or less, typical of kappa [1].
  - One industry source says protein, fat and minerals make up 70–92% of
    dried biomass and are mostly discarded in processing [2]. That
    conflicts with 40–64% semi-refined carrageenan yields (see Open
    questions).
  - No primary food-database entry was found.
- **Edibility:** Eaten locally. It is sold fresh or dried in Tawi-Tawi
  markets in the Philippines [2], and Zanzibari farmers eat it straight
  from the water [4]. Mainly it is processed into carrageenan (Uses).
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sexual reproduction is rare in farmed
  populations. Hawaii was long vegetative-only, sexual reproduction was
  achieved in the lab [8], and none occurs in Vietnamese waters [6].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. Lab micropropagation used a
  13-hour light, 11-hour dark cycle [6].
- **Yield:** 3.6–15.8 kg fresh per metre of line (Philippines, best
  months) [5]. 0.75 kg dry per rope (Kenya) [5]. 421–3,310 kg dry per crop
  in a Philippine economic trial; the unit area wasn't stated in the
  abstract [5]. National figures and their conflicts are in Finding 2 [3].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:** Vegetative cuttings only, commercially [6]. Clonal
  degeneration risk is high (Finding 1). Clean or improved stock comes
  from tissue culture (micropropagation, callus regeneration) and from
  spore-derived seedlings, then sea-based nursery cages [6].
- **Labor:**
  - Tying cuttings to lines is labour-intensive and slow [5]. There is
    little mechanisation, and wet harvest is hand-carried, often by
    women [5].
  - Drying takes 5–7 days on the beach [2].
  - Zanzibar farming is over 80% women [4].
  - No hours-per-tonne figure was found.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Natural habitat is sandy-coral to rocky bottom
  just below low tide, in slow to moderate water flow [1]. Salinity
  tolerance is 25–45 psu, with growth and carrageenan yield both high at
  25 psu [7]. That is why abandoned shrimp ponds of 25 psu or more have
  been suggested as sites [7].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - Sun-dried, often re-dried and cleaned by traders, then compressed into
    50 kg bales for export [3][2].
  - About 35% moisture is the commercial standard for dried seaweed [2].
  - Shelf life: Not sourced.
- **Establishment:**
  - 100 g cuttings at 50 cm spacing [5].
  - Indian rafts were 3 × 3 m, stocked with 60 kg of seed each [5].
  - Off-bottom lines at about 0.5 m depth are exposed at low tide [5].
- **Cultivars:**
  - *K. alvarezii* var. *tambalang*, the dominant farmed form: faster
    growth and better carrageenan [5].
  - Var. *alvarezii* and var. *ajak-assi* [5].
  - Brown, green and red colour morphotypes [1][5].
  - Tissue-cultured "rejuvenated" lines (Vietnam; SEAFDEC) [6].
  - Strain selection started in the early 1970s [1].
- **Weather response:**
  - Heat above about 31 °C, and especially above 33 °C, damages it
    [4][7].
  - Rain-driven low salinity triggers ice-ice [9].
  - Hurricanes hit Caribbean farms; Hurricane Beryl, 2024, struck St
    Vincent [3].
  - Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine, shallow subtidal or low intertidal [1][5].
  Off-bottom farms are briefly exposed to air at low tide [5].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** A single stage per 45-day cycle. Growth
  rate falls with crop age and with larger starting cuttings [5].
  Carrageenan quality varies with season [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. The microbial community (holobiont) is
  implicated in ice-ice [9].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to the Indo-Pacific [1]. Introduced for farming to Hawaii,
    Micronesia, Kiribati, Tonga, the Society Islands, India, East Africa,
    Brazil, Venezuela and the Caribbean [1][3][4][8][10].
  - Invasive on reefs in Hawaii (listed as a noxious aquatic weed) and in
    India's Gulf of Mannar (Finding 6) [8].
  - **Brazil:**
    - A 2007 IBAMA instruction allowed cultivation only on the south-east
      and south coasts, and barred new or expanded farms pending new
      rules [10]. That instruction was revoked in 2008 [10].
    - Farming is now permitted only in designated areas of Rio de
      Janeiro, São Paulo and Santa Catarina [10]. Santa Catarina was
      authorised by IBAMA Instrução Normativa 01/2020 [10].
    - A risk model rates the north-eastern coral-reef coast high risk and
      advises against introduction there [10]. The south-east has been
      farmed since 2004 with no evidence of invasion [10].
  - **Venezuela** restricted farming in 2001, then permitted it again for
    three companies in 2020 [3].
  - No IUCN listing was found in this pass.
- **Controlled environment:** Lab tissue culture at 23–24 °C, followed by
  sea nurseries [6]. Tank and land-based shrimp-pond culture has been
  proposed [7]. No commercial closed-system grow-out was found.

## Hazards

- **Food additive safety (carrageenan).** EFSA's 2018 re-evaluation kept a
  group ADI of 75 mg/kg body weight per day for carrageenan (E 407) and
  processed *Eucheuma* seaweed (E 407a) [11]. It called the ADI temporary
  because of data uncertainties [11]. EFSA found no genotoxicity or
  carcinogenicity concern [11].
  - **Degraded carrageenan.** Poligeenan (10–20 kDa) is not authorised as a
    food additive. Debate over native carrageenan's safety centres on
    degraded fractions [11].
  - **Infant formula.** JECFA found up to 1,000 mg/L in infant formula not
    of concern [11]. EFSA is evaluating infant uses separately [11].
- **Processing hazard.** The alkali cook uses hot KOH at pH 12–14, which is
  corrosive [2].
- **Heavy metals.** Measured in edible market *K. alvarezii* in Tawi-Tawi
  [2], but values weren't extracted. Not sourced in usable form.
- **Crop disease.** Ice-ice and epiphytes (Finding 5) [9].
- **Ecological.** A documented reef invader (Finding 6) [8]. **TO BE
  ADDRESSED** — biosecurity regulation for cultivating outside the native
  range is now recorded under Agronomic data, but this project has no
  dedicated home yet for reef-impact assessments.
- No ergot-type contamination was found. The disease is bacterial and
  environmental, not a food toxin.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (dried raw seaweed):** harvest → sun-dried 5–7 days on nets or
  platforms [2] → re-dried, cleaned and baled (50 kg) by traders → export,
  mainly to China, or domestic processing [3].
  - About 11.6% of fresh weight is left as dry whole plant (see Estimates)
    [2].
- **Chain (semi-refined carrageenan, SRC / ATC / "processed Eucheuma
  seaweed", E 407a):** dried seaweed cooked in 6–12% KOH at 70–80 °C for
  2–3 hours [2] → washed to drop the pH → chopped, dried, ground [2].
  - Yield 39–64% of dry weight. Means: 53.9% (India), 39.0% (Indonesia)
    [2]. A lab maximum of 75.8% was reached at 25 ppt and 30 °C [7].
- **Chain (refined carrageenan, E 407):** alkali-treated seaweed is
  extracted and the carrageenan recovered by KCl gel precipitation [2].
  Detailed industrial yields: Not sourced.
- **Byproducts:** pressed sap used as liquid fertiliser. In an Indian
  process, 20 kg fresh gave 13.3 kg sap [2]. Extraction residues are used
  as a plant biostimulant [2].
- **End uses of carrageenan:**
  - Dairy desserts and flan, ice cream, meat and fish products, infant
    nutrition, confectionery and condiments [11].
  - Suspensions in drug and pet-food products [1].
  - Toothpaste and cosmetics [4].
- **Local value-added products:** Zanzibar soaps and powders. Raw dried
  seaweed sold at 300–500 TSh/kg, against 10,000 TSh/kg for processed
  powder [4].
- **Not otherwise detailed here:** bioplastic or film uses [2], and feed
  uses. Not chased down this pass.

## Open research questions

- **Rename the cropID?** `eucheuma` names the trade group and an old
  genus, not *Kappaphycus*. Also, should `spinosum` and `sacol` be split
  out as proposed?
- **True production.** Indonesia's 7 Mt official vs 1.3–1.5 Mt industry
  estimate. This is the biggest number uncertainty in the category so far.
- **Wet-to-dry ratio.** One Indian process gives 11.6% dry, and Indonesian
  processing data imply about 7.9% (12.7:1). The Indonesian figure's
  species basis is unclear.
- **Per-area yield.** No defensible per-hectare figure; per-metre only.
- **Composition conflict.** Protein 2.7% vs 12.7–23.6%, and the claim that
  70–92% of biomass is non-carrageenan vs 40–64% SRC yields. Semi-refined
  carrageenan includes cellulose, so these may not conflict, but the doc
  doesn't resolve it.
- **Optimum temperature.** 25–30 °C tropical vs 20–24 °C for Brazilian
  drifting specimens. Strain or acclimation?
- **Invasion severity in India.** Smothered corals and removal campaigns
  vs a 76.7 m² canopy estimate.
- **Heavy metals** in edible *K. alvarezii*. Values weren't extracted.
- **Hawaii introduction date**: 1970 vs 1974 in different sources [8].

## Estimates and derivations

**Official vs industry production.** Inputs [3]:

- Indonesia: 7 Mt official vs 1.3–1.5 Mt industry.
- Philippines: 1.3 Mt vs 0.5 Mt.
- Malaysia: 180,000 t vs 30,000 t.

Arithmetic: 7 / 1.5 = 4.7 and 7 / 1.3 = 5.4; 1.3 / 0.5 = 2.6;
180 / 30 = 6.0. Consistent with the "one-sixth" claim for Indonesia [3].

**Dry fraction of fresh seaweed, about 8–12%.** Inputs:

- Indian process: 20 kg fresh gave 2.31 kg dry whole plant [2].
  2.31 / 20 = 11.6%.
- Indonesian domestic processing: 47,200 t dry = 600,000 t wet [3].
  47,200 / 600,000 = 7.9%, a 12.7:1 ratio. The species basis on that
  page is not certain.

Range 8–12%. A farm-level drying study would replace it.

**Dry yield per metre of line, about 0.4–1.8 kg.** Inputs: 3.6–15.8 kg
fresh/m [5]; 11.55% dry fraction (above). 3.6 × 0.1155 = 0.42 and
15.8 × 0.1155 = 1.82 kg. That is rough, since moisture at "dry" sale is
about 35% [2], so saleable weight would be higher.

**Biomass multiplier per 45-day cycle.** Input: daily growth rates of
1.13–4.2% [5]. Assumption: the usual exponential definition of daily
growth rate. Arithmetic: e^(0.0113 × 45) = 1.66 and
e^(0.042 × 45) = 6.62. So a cutting ends at about 1.7–6.6 times its
starting weight.

**Zanzibar temperatures, converted from °F.** Inputs [4]: 86 °F in the
late 1990s, up to 100 °F in 2013, and above 88 °F described as
unfavourable. Arithmetic: (86 − 32) × 5/9 = 30.0 °C;
(100 − 32) × 5/9 = 37.8 °C; (88 − 32) × 5/9 = 31.1 °C.

**Zanzibar price falls.** Inputs [4]:

- Spinosum fell from 700 to 300 TSh/kg: 300 / 700 = 0.43, a 57% fall.
- Cottonii fell from 1,100 to 700: 700 / 1,100 = 0.64, a 36% fall.
- Value-added powder vs raw: 10,000 / 500 = 20 times, up to
  10,000 / 300 = 33 times.

**Cottonii share of 1980s eucheumatoid output, about 83%.** Inputs:
30,000 t cottonii, over 6,000 t spinosum, about 100 t gelatinae [1].
30,000 / 36,100 = 0.83.
