## Naming and scope

This covers *Eisenia bicyclis* (Kjellman) Setchell, a stipitate kelp
(order Laminariales) of Japan and Korea, sold dried and shredded as arame
[1]. English name: sea oak; Korean: daehwang [1]. It covers the single
cropID `arame`.

**Genus.** It was briefly placed in *Ecklonia* (as *Ecklonia bicyclis*);
a 2020 multigene study reinstated *Eisenia* [1]. See `ecklonia.md`, which
already keeps `arame` as its own row.

**Mostly a wild-managed resource.** Commercial arame is gathered from wild
beds by divers and from boats, in a set summer season (Finding 2) [2]. No
commercial farming was found; hatchery seedlings are used for forest
restoration, not crops (Finding 4) [3]. It is a candidate for the
wild-managed template (GitHub issue #2); notes are in
`crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/arame.md`.

**Name conflicts — which species is "Ise arame"?**

- **Arame and kajime are swapped regionally.** The Japanese names arame
  and kajime have been applied to *E. bicyclis* and *Ecklonia cava* in
  both directions, historically and today [1].
- **Mie sells sagarame as arame.** Mie Prefecture, the main source of
  dried arame, lists the harvested plant as "sagarame (commonly called
  arame)" [2]. Tokushima likewise calls its sagarame "arame", and treats
  sagarame (*Eisenia arborea* in Japanese usage) and *E. bicyclis* as
  separate species [1].
- **Sagarame is now a new species.** The 2020 study showed that Japanese
  "*E. arborea*" is distinct, and named it *Eisenia nipponica* [1]. Its
  type specimen was collected at Shima, Mie [1]. It grows on the Pacific
  coast of central Honshu, separately from *E. bicyclis* [1].
- **Consequence.** Packaged "Ise arame" is labelled *Eisenia bicyclis*
  [2], but on these sources much of it is probably *E. nipponica*. Not
  confirmed: no source tested the products. See Open questions.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`sagarame`** (*Eisenia nipponica*, formerly *E. arborea* sensu
  Arasaki; proposed). Possibly the main commercial "arame" (above). Mie
  data on its blade growth are included here where they bear on harvest
  [3], clearly marked.
- **`ecklonia`** (*Ecklonia* spp., incl. kajime and kurome). Cross-linked
  already. Ecklonia and Eisenia forests decline together (Finding 3) [3].
- **`hijiki`**. Arame looks like hijiki once dried, but is a kelp and
  milder [2]. Arame or kajime is sometimes added in hijiki processing to
  soften its bitterness and colour [1].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a perennial "underwater forest" kelp that branches with age.**

- **Form.** Grows 1–3 m tall on rocky reefs, attached by a root-like
  holdfast [1]. The single stipe forks after the second year, carrying
  many wrinkled blades [1]. Each year blades are shed and new ones grow
  [2].
- **Range.**
  - Japan: Pacific coast of eastern Honshu, Sea of Japan coast of
    north-western Honshu, and northern Kyushu [1]. Another source extends
    it to eastern Shikoku and the Seto Inland Sea [3]. It reaches farther
    north than *Ecklonia cava*, to Iwate Prefecture [1].
  - Korea: its only *Eisenia*; large stands at Ulleungdo and Dokdo [1].
    One source adds Jeju [1].
- **Life cycle.** A kelp: a large spore-producing plant alternates with
  microscopic male and female gametophytes [4]. Gametophytes grow best
  and mature at about 15–20 °C; at 25 °C they grow but don't mature [4].
- **Heat limit.** The upper growth limit is 29 °C [3].

**Finding 2 — gathered by ama divers in summer, sun-dried on the beach,
then steamed and shredded.**

- **Season and method (Mie).** July–September, by ama (women divers) and
  by fishers using box glasses (viewing boxes) [2]. Main areas: Ise, Toba,
  Shima and Minami-Ise [2].
- **Scale.** Most arame sold nationally is gathered around Toba and Shima
  in summer and processed in Mie, as leaf arame or shredded arame [2].
  Beach sun-drying in high season is a summer sight there [2].
- **Processing (commercial).**
  - Harvest → sun-dry → soak and wash → cut → steam → press → cut → dry
    [2].
  - One brand steams for five hours [2]. Another steams, then leaves it
    in the hot water for up to 12 hours [2].
  - Only the young, tender fronds are taken, according to sellers [2]
    (low weight: marketing).
- **Why it matters.** A clear harvest window, a gear and diver tradition,
  and a processing chain that turns a tough kelp into a soft food.

**Finding 3 — its forests are disappearing: warming, fish and urchins.**

- **National picture.** Japan's seaweed and seagrass beds shrank from
  about 200,000 to 125,000 ha between 1978 and 2007 [3]. The loss is
  called isoyake, "desertification of the sea" [3].
- **South and west: heat plus fish.**
  - The loss of *Eisenia bicyclis* and *Ecklonia* species in southern and
    western Japan is attributed to warming and more grazing fish [3].
  - In Nagasaki, water passed the 29 °C growth limit; beds were lost to
    heat in August 2016, and fish then grazed the survivors in autumn and
    early winter [3].
- **North-east: urchins.**
  - In Shizugawa Bay, the urchin *Mesocentrotus nudus* destroyed *E.
    bicyclis* forests in 2014–2015 [3].
  - The tipping point was about 15.5 urchins (712 g) per m² [3].
  - After 3.8 years of urchin removal, the forest spread offshore, but
    adult kelp recovered only on the nearshore half [3].
- **Mie.** Around Toshi Island, abalone landings fell sharply from about
  1993, and the arame and kajime forest on the Ise Bay side vanished [3].
  Suspected causes are fish grazing and turbid water [3].
- **Why it matters.** Arame beds also feed abalone and urchins, which are
  the inshore fishery [3]; so losing the kelp hits two harvests at once.

**Finding 4 — hatchery seedlings exist, but for restoring forests, not
farming.**

- **Seedlings.** The Toba City Fisheries Research Institute raises arame
  seedlings [3]. A Mie restoration manual collects parent plants in
  October and grows seed string in December–February [3].
- **Restoration at Toshi (from 2005).**
  - Seedlings on chains or stones failed: they toppled over or were eaten
    by fish [3].
  - Fishers learned scuba and covered seedlings with anti-grazing nets,
    which let them survive a year before transplanting [3].
  - The work won the Emperor's Cup in 2009, but forests haven't yet
    returned where they were lost [3].
- **Farming:** Not sourced. No commercial arame cultivation was found;
  one encyclopedia-style site says it is "widely cultivated" (low weight,
  unsupported) [5].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial; stipe forks after the second year [1].
  Blades are shed and replaced yearly [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** One summer harvest of fronds from wild beds,
  July–September [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not sourced for harvest size.
  Restoration seedlings are grown about a year before transplanting [3].
- **Climate zones:** Temperate Japan and Korea (Finding 1) [1].
  Gametophytes mature at 15–20 °C [4]; growth limit 29 °C [3].
- **Composition (dried):**
  - Calcium-rich: Mie says 12 times milk (low weight: promotional) [2].
  - Iodine high: about 70,000 µg/100 g dry, from a secondary source (low
    weight) [5].
  - Highest magnesium of six dried seaweeds in a 2025 study (reported
    second-hand) [5].
  - Phlorotannins (eckol, phloroglucinol) and fucoxanthin [5].
- **Edibility:** Eaten after steaming and drying, then soaking 10–15
  minutes; it doubles in volume [2].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Separate male and female gametophytes
  [4].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Gametophyte growth was best at 12–14 hour
  days in one study [4]. Effect on the adult plant: Not sourced.
- **Yield:** Not sourced. Mie gives no arame tonnage [2].
- **Water use:** Not applicable (marine).
- **Propagation:** Wild beds regrow from spores. Hatchery seed string from
  wild parent plants, for restoration [3].
- **Labor:** Diving and box-glass gathering [2]; figures not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Rocky reef; Mie's sagarame at 5–10 m [2].
  Earthquake uplift in 2011 shifted *E. bicyclis* to shallower depths at
  Shizugawa [3].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried; one specification gives 36 months, cool
  (10–25 °C) and dry [2].
- **Establishment:** Restoration seedlings need protection from grazing
  fish (nets) [3].
- **Cultivars:** None. (See `sagarame` for the species question.)
- **Weather response:** Heat waves kill beds (Nagasaki 2016) [3]. Frost:
  not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine, subtidal [1][2].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** July–September [2]. *Sagarame* data
  (Mie): blades are produced about six times faster in winter (0.49 a
  day) than in summer (0.08), and shed most just after maturity in winter
  [3]. So the summer harvest comes before peak shedding (inference).
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Grazers (urchins, fish) set bed
  survival (Finding 3) [3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native to Japan and Korea [1]. No invasive record was found in this
    pass.
  - **Harvest regulation:** A set season (July–September in Mie) [2];
    harvest by ama and box glass [2]. Who sets the season, and any
    quotas: Not sourced.
- **Controlled environment:** Seedling tanks only [3].

## Hazards

- **Iodine.** High (above) [5]. A German retailer warns of thyroid effects
  and suggests soaking an hour, then boiling 10 minutes in fresh water [5]
  (low weight).
- **Arsenic: low.** The UK survey found no inorganic arsenic in arame [5].
  A Spanish review put inorganic arsenic in arame, kombu, wakame and nori
  at 0.15–0.57 mg/kg dry, against 83–88 for hijiki [5].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (Mie):** dive or box-glass harvest → beach sun-dry → soak, wash,
  cut → steam (hours) → press, cut into strips → dry → leaf or shredded
  arame [2].
- **Uses:**
  - Arame-maki: rolled round saury or sardine fillets and simmered [2].
  - Vinegared salad (arame namasu), simmered dishes, miso soup [2].
  - The black flecks in konnyaku come from arame or hijiki [2].
  - Sautéed with vegetables and tofu; in salads and baking (Western) [2].
  - Not used for dashi stock, unlike kombu [2].
  - Abalone and urchin food in the wild; feeding on it boosts their
    growth [3].
- **Not detailed here:** health claims for phlorotannin extracts [5].

## Open research questions

- **Which species is sold?** Is Mie's commercial "arame" *E. nipponica*
  (sagarame), *E. bicyclis*, or a mix? A DNA test of products would
  settle it.
- **Yield and landings.** No tonnage found for Mie or Japan.
- **Harvest management.** Who sets the season, and are there size or
  quantity limits?
- **Iodine.** A primary measurement for arame wasn't found.
- **Decision:** whether to add `sagarame` as a proposed row.

## Estimates and derivations

**Sagarame blade production ratio, about 6 times.** Inputs: 0.49 and 0.08
blades a day [3]. 0.49 / 0.08 = 6.1, matching the source's "about six
times".
