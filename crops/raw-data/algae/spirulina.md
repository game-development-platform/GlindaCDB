# Spirulina (*Limnospira platensis*, formerly *Arthrospira platensis*)

## Naming and scope

This covers spirulina, the dried biomass of filamentous, spiral-coiled
cyanobacteria grown in alkaline water [1]. It covers the single cropID
`spirulina`.

**Not an alga, and not *Spirulina*.**

- **A bacterium.** Spirulina is a cyanobacterium (an oxygen-producing
  photosynthetic bacterium), kept in the Algae category by convention [1].
- **Three genus names.** The commercial organisms were long called
  *Spirulina*, then *Arthrospira* [1]. The true genus *Spirulina* is only
  distantly related, and no facility anywhere produces it [1].
- **Now *Limnospira*.** In 2019 the commercial species were moved to a new
  genus, *Limnospira*, because the type species of *Arthrospira* turned
  out to be distant from them [1]. A 2024 study cut the genus to two
  species, *L. platensis* and *L. maxima*, and found that neither can make
  microcystins [1].
- **Status-table name.** Updated to *Limnospira platensis* (formerly
  *Arthrospira platensis*), with *L. maxima* also grown (this pass).
- **Ranges.** *L. platensis* occurs in Africa, Asia and South America;
  *L. maxima* in Central America [1].

**Two supply routes.**

- **Farmed:** almost all commercial spirulina, grown in open raceway
  ponds (Finding 1) [2].
- **Wild-harvested:** dihé in Chad (Finding 3) [3]. That route is a
  wild-managed resource; its notes are in
  `crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/spirulina-dihe.md` (GitHub issue #2).

**Relatives flagged:** `chlorella` (LISTED) is the other farmed
microalga in this category, but a true green alga; high pH in spirulina
ponds keeps *Chlorella* out [2]. No new rows proposed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — grown in shallow, paddle-stirred alkaline ponds that weeds
can't survive.**

- **System.** Most spirulina is grown in open raceway ponds, stirred by
  paddle-wheels [2]. Culture pH is kept around 9–10, where bicarbonate
  dominates [2]. That high alkalinity keeps out protozoa and competing
  algae such as *Chlorella* [2].
- **Temperature.** Optimum growth is about 30–35 °C [2], and it tolerates
  culture temperatures up to 40 °C [2]. In southern Spain, outdoor culture
  worked above 15 °C, for about nine months a year [2].
- **Yield.**
  - Southern Spain: about 8.2 g dry per m² a day averaged over a year,
    about 30 t/ha [2].
  - Reported range: 10–13 t/ha a year in Inner Mongolia and China to over
    90 t/ha in Australia [2].
  - Portugal (industrial 1,000–4,000 m² ponds): 5.1–5.6 g/m² a day over
    the year, with winter output falling by more than 60% from the
    April–September peak of about 7.5 g [2].
  - Best case in 80 m² ponds: 30.2 g/m² a day at 0.10 m depth and 33%
    daily dilution [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop that needs a chemistry (high pH, bicarbonate)
  rather than a climate, and that self-weeds through that chemistry.

**Finding 2 — the Aztecs ate it, and the first factory grew it in their
lake.**

- **Tecuitlatl.** Spirulina was a food of the Aztecs and other
  Mesoamericans until the 16th century [4]. One of Cortés's soldiers
  described it being harvested from Lake Texcoco and sold as cakes [4].
  The Nahuatl name translates as "stone excrement" [4]. Use faded as the
  lakes were drained [4].
- **Sosa Texcoco.** In the 1960s scientists at Sosa Texcoco, a soda-ash
  company on the old lakebed, found spirulina growing in its evaporation
  basin [4]. In 1973 the world's first commercial spirulina plant opened
  there, in the spiral "El Caracol" basin [4]. It was the world's largest
  producer through the 1980s; the company went bankrupt in December 1986
  [4].
- **Why it matters.** A historic food route and a ready-made setting: an
  alkaline lake where the "weed" in a soda works turned out to be the
  crop.

**Finding 3 — Chad's dihé: a wild harvest run by women under customary
rules.**

- **Where.** Brackish, alkaline pools on the north-east shore of Lake
  Chad, which form at the end of the rainy season [3].
- **Who.** Only Kanembu women harvest dihé, passing the method from mother
  to daughter; men are banned from the water, in the belief that they
  would make it barren [3]. An elderly woman supervises the harvest, which
  starts early in the morning [3].
- **How.** Women scoop the floating algae, filter it through cloth or
  directly on the sand, sun-dry it into thin cakes, cut it into squares
  and finish drying on mats in the villages [3]. It is cooked into a
  bitter sauce [3].
- **Scale.** Each woman can gather about 4–8 kg a day [3]. One source puts
  Chad's output at over 250 t of dry dihé a year (low weight) [3].
- **Development.** An FAO and EU project from 2007 taught more hygienic
  harvesting, packaging and marketing; by 2010, 10 t of improved dihé had
  been sold, bringing 50 million CFA francs to 500 women [3]. The project
  coordinator estimated output could rise tenfold without harming the
  pools [3]. FAO said further testing and certification would be needed
  before export [3].
- **Threat.** Lake Chad has shrunk by about 90% since the 1960s [3].

**Finding 4 — the protein is real; the vitamin B12 mostly isn't.**

- **Protein.** About 60–65% of dry weight [2], or 50–70% across strains
  [2].
- **Phycocyanin.** A blue pigment-protein, up to 20% of dry weight [2];
  levels peak between midday and sunset, which sets the best daily
  harvest window [2].
- **B12.** Spirulina's B12 is mostly an inactive analogue
  (pseudovitamin B12) [5]. One analysis found 0.56 µg/g of true B12
  against 2.74 µg/g of the analogue [5]. France's food agency says it is
  not a reliable B12 source for vegans [5].
- **Minerals.** Iron 0.38–0.54 mg/g; calcium 1.48–1.8 mg/g; magnesium
  2.7–4.0 mg/g [5].
- **Why it matters.** A real protein crop, but a model shouldn't credit
  it with B12.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Continuous culture of a microscopic organism;
  harvested year-round where warm enough [2]. Outdoor production in
  temperate climates is seasonal (about nine months in southern Spain)
  [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous or semi-continuous, by filtering off
  part of the culture daily [2].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable; harvest begins within
  days of inoculating a pond (inference from daily dilution rates [2]).
- **Climate zones:** Tropical and subtropical alkaline lakes naturally
  [1]; farmed from Chad to Inner Mongolia (temperate continental, in
  plastic greenhouses) [2][3]. Needs culture above about 15 °C [2].
- **Composition:** See Finding 4 [2][5].
- **Edibility:** Eaten as dried powder, tablets or flakes [5]; in Chad as a
  sauce from dihé cakes [3].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable (bacterium; reproduces by
  fragmentation) [1].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Production follows sunlight; winter output
  drops by more than 60% in Portugal [2].
- **Yield:** 10 to over 90 t dry/ha a year; about 30 t is a realistic
  year-round figure for warm-temperate sites (Finding 1) [2].
- **Water use:** Freshwater is added daily to replace evaporation [2];
  culture medium can be reused [2]. Volumes: Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Inoculation from a starter culture [2].
- **Labor:** Wild harvest 4–8 kg a day per woman (Chad) [3]. Industrial
  labour: Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. Ordos farms use cheap semi-desert land beside
  alkaline lakes [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Dried to powder; shelf life Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Raceway depth about 0.1–0.3 m; paddle-wheel mixing
  [2]. Carbon is supplied as bicarbonate or CO₂, which can come from flue
  gas [2].
- **Cultivars:** Commercial strains are numbered (e.g. BEA 1257B, strains
  208 and 220) [2]. Native Ordos strains were found in 1995 [2].
- **Weather response:** Mid-summer productivity losses occur even in warm
  sites [2]; cold ends the season [2].
- **Water regime:** Alkaline, saline-to-brackish water (pH about 9–11)
  [2][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Midday to sunset for highest phycocyanin
  [2].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Contamination by other cyanobacteria
  is the main biological risk (Hazards) [5].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** No invasive record found in this pass.
  Its need for highly alkaline water likely limits escape (inference). The
  biosecurity concern runs the other way: toxic cyanobacteria getting into
  the culture [5].
- **Controlled environment:** Greenhouse raceways (Ordos) [2].

## Hazards

- **Microcystins from contaminants.** *Limnospira* can't make
  microcystins [1], but other cyanobacteria in the pond can [5]. The US
  Pharmacopeia rated spirulina Class A for safety in 2011, naming
  contamination with microcystins and heavy metals as the main concern
  [5]. Fifteen products averaged 0.15 µg/g of microcystins in 1998 and
  0.52 µg/g in 1999 [5] (via a secondary summary).
- **Heavy metals.** Spirulina accumulates metals from its water; lead,
  mercury and arsenic contamination was reported as widespread in
  supplements sold in China [5] (low weight: second-hand).
- **Phenylketonuria.** Contains phenylalanine; France's food agency advises
  people with phenylketonuria not to take it [5].
- **Beta-carotene.** 5 g a day gives 7–8.5 mg of beta-carotene, around the
  estimated maximum from supplements [5].
- **Wild harvest.** Wild spirulina carries more contamination risk, which
  is why FAO wanted dihé tested before export [3][5].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (industrial):** raceway culture → filter off part of the
  culture [2] → dry → powder or tablets [5]. Drying methods: Not sourced.
- **Chain (dihé):** scoop from pools → filter on sand or cloth → sun-dry
  cakes → cut and finish drying on mats [3].
- **Uses:**
  - Human food and supplements [1][5].
  - Animal and fish feed [2].
  - Phycocyanin extraction (a natural blue pigment) [2].
  - CO₂ capture from industrial flue gas (Chinese trials) [2].

## Open research questions

- **World production.** Over 10,000 t dry a year by one estimate [2]; FAO
  records only China, at 41,570 t in 2004 [2], which looks too high for dry
  weight (see Estimates).
- **Dihé rights.** How access to the pools is allocated among women and
  villages; notes for issue #2.
- **Storage life** of dried powder: Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**China's dry output around 2009, about 3,500 t.** Inputs: Ordos produced
over 700 t dry, about 20% of China's output [2]. 700 / 0.20 = 3,500 t dry.
FAO's 41,570 t for 2004 [2] is about 12 times higher, so it is probably
wet weight or a different basis.

**Spain yield check.** Input: 8.2 g dry/m² a day [2]. 8.2 × 365 × 10,000 /
1,000,000 = 29.9 t/ha, matching the source's 30 t.
