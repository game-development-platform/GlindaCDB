# Gracilaria (*Gracilaria*, *Gracilariopsis* and *Agarophyton* spp.)

## Naming and scope

This covers the farmed "gracilarioid" red algae (order Gracilariales), the
world's main agar source. Gracilaria is the raw material of the agar
industry worldwide [1]. It covers the single cropID `gracilaria`, which the
status table defines as "*Gracilaria* spp." There are about 284–300
species [1][2], and the line between species is not always clear [2].

The main farmed taxa are [1]:

- ***Gracilariopsis lemaneiformis*** (China). A different genus; the main
  agar species and abalone feed.
- ***Gracilaria chilensis*** (Chile; "pelillo"). Now also named
  *Agarophyton chilense* [1].
- ***Gracilaria gigas*** and ***Gracilariopsis longissima***.
- Pond-farmed tropical species in Indonesia, sold under names such as
  "*G. verrucosa*" [3].

Two more names matter as invaders rather than crops:

- ***Agarophyton vermiculophyllum*** (formerly *Gracilaria
  vermiculophylla*) [2].
- ***Gracilaria salicornia*** ("gorilla ogo") in Hawaii [2].

Old literature uses "*G. verrucosa*" and "*G. lichenoides*" loosely, and the
Guam toxic species was published under three different names [4][3].
Species-level claims in this doc are tied to the name each source used.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`gracilariopsis`** (*Gracilariopsis lemaneiformis*, proposed). It is
  China's second-largest farmed seaweed after kelp [5]. It is a
  cold-temperate species farmed on rafts with a north–south seasonal relay
  (Finding 3) [5]. About 70% goes fresh to abalone farms rather than to
  agar [6]. Different genus, climate, system and end use, so it may deserve
  its own row.
- **`pelillo`** (*Gracilaria chilensis* / *Agarophyton chilense*,
  proposed). Cold-water, bottom-planted in sediment and harvested by dredge
  (Finding 4) [3]. It makes up about 95% of Chile's farmed seaweed [7].
- ***Agarophyton vermiculophyllum*** and ***G. salicornia***. Not proposed
  as crops. Their invasion records are covered here (Finding 6).
- **`gelidium`** (already LISTED). The other agar genus; cross-linked.
  FAO reports that Chile, Spain and Japan produce 60% of agar [6].
- **`cottonii`**. The same Kāne'ohe Bay introductions (Finding 6) [2].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a raw seaweed you can't safely assume is edible: "ogonori"
poisoning.**

- **What happens.** Some *Gracilaria* eaten raw or lightly prepared has
  caused fatal poisonings. Prostaglandin E2 is the leading explanation [4].
- **Japan.** Fatal cases occurred in Sakata (1980), Tokyo (1982) [4] and
  Yokohama (1993; one death of two victims) [4].
- **Mechanism.** In the 1993 case, the alga produced more PGE2 when it was
  cut or soaked in fresh water. An enzyme, probably cyclooxygenase, acted
  on fatty acids in the meal and the victim's blood. That generated over
  30 mg of PGE2, and the victim died of hypotensive shock [4]. PGE2 appears
  to act more selectively on women [4].
- **Guam, 1991.** 13 people fell ill and 3 died after eating *G. tsudae*
  from the reef flats [4]. Cyanobacterial epiphytes are now considered the
  likely source of that toxin [4].
- **Hawaii, 1994.** *G. coronopifolia* served at a picnic sickened 7 of 13
  attendees within 15–90 minutes [4].
- **Why it matters.** The implicated seaweeds had not previously been known
  to turn toxic [4]. In Japan the risk is tied to preparation, especially
  freshwater soaking [4].

**Finding 2 — official production is inflated, and much of China's crop
never becomes agar.**

- **China.** 368,967 t dry in 2020, which FAO converts at 10% into 3.7 Mt
  wet [6]. About 70% of China's crop is sold fresh to abalone farms, and
  30% is dried for agar [6].
- **Indonesia.** Production began in the early 2000s, and the government
  reported 1.9 Mt wet in 2021 [6]. Industry estimates are 1–1.2 Mt [6].
  Almost all of it goes to agar [6].
  - Exports: 37,000 t dry (353,000 t wet) [6].
  - Domestic processing: 47,200 t dry (600,000 t wet) [6].
- **Chile.**
  - Farming began in 1982 because wild beds were overexploited. By 1991,
    84% of the roughly 60,000 t wet output was cultivated [3].
  - Farming peaked at up to 140,000 t [7]. Chile's farmed-seaweed output is
    now about 20,000–40,000 t a year, about 95% of it *G. chilensis*. The
    decline came from competition and lower productivity [7].
- **Agar.** About 55,000 t dry seaweed yields about 7,500 t agar worth
  US$132 million a year [6]. That is a 13.6% overall yield and about
  US$17,600 per tonne of agar (see Estimates).

**Finding 3 — China's crop is a cold-water seaweed farmed in the subtropics
by moving it north and south with the seasons.**

- **Temperature range.** Wild *Gp. lemaneiformis* grows best at about
  10–23 °C in northern China [5].
- **Heat-tolerant cultivars.** Cultivar 981 and its successors tolerate
  25–27 °C. They are widely cultivated on both southern and northern coasts
  [5].
- **Relay.** Northern stock is shipped south for winter, and southern stock
  north for summer [5].
- **Limits.** Even cultivar 981 can't survive summer in the South China Sea
  area. Above about 26 °C its metabolism fails and it decays [5]. Strain
  07-2, bred from 981, grows faster [5]. Mutants MT-17 and MT-18 are under
  development [5].
- **Scale.** 13,924 ha produced 610,824 t dry in 2022 [5]. That works out
  at about 44 t dry per hectare (see Estimates), three to four times
  Chile's literature figure. Not reconciled.

**Finding 4 — Chile's crop is "planted" into the seabed like a lawn, and
harvested with a dredge.**

- **Planting.** Bundles of fronds are pushed directly into intertidal
  sediment. In subtidal areas they are anchored with sand-filled
  polyethylene tubes [3]. The method selects for plants that regrow fast,
  tolerate sand abrasion and burial, and resist epiphytes [3].
- **Clonal and narrow.** About 90% is grown as sterile vegetative thalli
  [3]. The industry has had no genetic improvement programme, and has used
  the same material for years [7].
- **Seed volume.** Traditional planting needs about 10,000 kg of seaweed
  per hectare. Spore seeding needs only 30–40 kg of fertile (cystocarpic)
  plants [3].
- **Harvest.** Bottom farms are harvested with "arañas", towed dredges that
  take 2,000 kg in 30 minutes [3].
- **Yield.** 10–15 dry t/ha a year [3]. By 1987, 2,700 ha had been
  licensed [3]. A small 0.3 ha concession can yield up to 30,000 kg in a
  good season [3]. In rope culture, a 5 m line yields about 5 kg from
  1.8 kg seed [3].
- **Wild collapse.** The wild harvest fell from 110,000 to 36,500 wet t/yr
  between 1984 and 1988, a 67% drop (see Estimates) [3].

**Finding 5 — in Indonesia it is a pond crop grown alongside milkfish and
shrimp, on soil that needs liming.**

- **Ponds.** Grown in brackish-water ponds (tambak) [3]. Degraded
  acid-sulfate shrimp ponds were limed with 1–2 t dolomite per hectare and
  fertilised with 75–150 kg urea and 50–100 kg triple superphosphate per
  hectare [3].
- **Growth.** *G. verrucosa* stocked at 800 kg wet per hectare produced
  5 t dry per hectare over three months. Progressive harvests started at
  40 days [3].
- **Milkfish.** Milkfish at 2,000 per hectare improved quality by grazing
  epiphytes. But the fish tasted tainted unless harvested two weeks after
  the seaweed came out [3].
- **Polyculture yields.** One Central Java study recorded 8,885 kg/ha a
  cycle with milkfish and 7,203 kg/ha with shrimp [3].

**Finding 6 — two of the worst-documented seaweed invasions are
gracilarioids.**

- ***Agarophyton vermiculophyllum*.**
  - **Range.** Native to the NW Pacific. It has spread to virtually every
    high-salinity temperate estuary in Europe and North America [2]. It
    also reached Morocco (2002) [2].
  - **Records.** France 1996, Denmark and Sweden 2003, Germany 2005,
    Belgium 2011 [2].
  - **Vector.** Genetics trace most introduced populations to NE Japan, so
    the likely vector is Pacific oyster shipments [2]. Boats, fishing and
    aquaculture spread it further [2].
  - **Why it succeeds.** It tolerates 5–34 °C and very low salinities. On
    soft sediments it switched from sexual to asexual reproduction [2]. It
    produces herbivore-deterring metabolites [2].
  - **Damage.** In North Carolina it fouls nets and clogs cooling-water
    intakes [2]. In an Irish estuary it enlarged macroalgal blooms [2].
- ***Gracilaria salicornia* in Hawaii.**
  - **Introduction.** Brought to Waikīkī reefs in 1971, and Kāne'ohe Bay,
    for agar aquaculture trials [2]. It became the dominant benthic species
    in parts of Waikīkī [2].
  - **Cost of removal.** Manual removal takes about 6.54 person-hours per
    m² [2]. That is about 65,000 person-hours per hectare (see Estimates).
  - **Control.** A barge-mounted "Super Sucker" removes 270–450 kg an hour
    [2]. Native sea-urchin biocontrol followed, and by 2016 manual removal
    was no longer needed in Kāne'ohe [2].
  - **Eaten too.** It is now sold in Hawaiian markets and used in poke [2].
  - **Listing.** The IUCN Global Invasive Species Database has an account
    for it [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial thallus. A three-phase life cycle,
  with free-living haploid and diploid stages [2]. Farming is mostly
  clonal from fragments [3].
  - Season depends on system: year-round tropical ponds [3]; temperature-
    limited raft culture in China (Finding 3) [5]; Chile's season-
    dependent bottom beds [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Cut-and-come-again. Usually 40–60 days to harvest,
  with 60 days giving higher yield and gel strength [3]. Partial harvests
  leave about 10 cm to regrow [3]. Ponds are harvested progressively from
  40 days [3]. Complete removal is needed during *Rhizoclonium*
  infestations [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** 40–60 days [3].
- **Climate zones:** Tropical (Indonesia) to cold temperate (southern Chile,
  Atlantic Canada) [1].
  - *Gp. lemaneiformis*: about 10–23 °C wild; cultivars tolerate 25–27 °C
    [5].
  - A South African "*G. verrucosa*" grew best at 25 °C and died at 30 °C
    [8].
  - *A. vermiculophyllum* grows best at 11–30 °C and survives 2–35 °C [8].
  - Chill/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
- **Composition (dry basis):** Widely variable by species and site:
  - **Protein:** 10.9% (*G. gracilis*, Indonesia) [9]; 11.6% (*G.
    fisheri*) [9]; 14.3% (*G. edulis*) [9]; 21.6% (*G. tenuistipitata*)
    [9]; 22.8–25.3% (Indian *G. corticata* and *G. edulis*) [9].
  - **Lipid:** 0.2–7.1% [9]. Contains DHA [9].
  - **Ash:** 6.8–38% [9].
  - **Total dietary fibre:** 57–64% [9].
  - **Agar:** 16–24% with alkali pretreatment (Philippines) [10], and up to
    44% in Tanzanian *G. cornea* [10].
  - Fresh *G. edulis* is 87.1% water, so about 12.9% dry matter [9].
- **Edibility:** Eaten raw in salads and cooked across the Pacific and
  Asia. Hawaiian "ogo" is prepared with vinegar, soy and chili [4]. It is
  in poke [2], and sold in Japan as ogonori [4]. **See Hazards** before any
  raw use.
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Sexual reproduction is by non-motile
  male spermatia (general red-algal biology, not sourced this pass); most
  farming is clonal [3].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced for farming. *A.
  vermiculophyllum* grew best at 80–100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ [8].
- **Yield:**
  - Chile: 10–15 dry t/ha a year [3].
  - China: about 44 dry t/ha by back-calculation (Finding 3) [5].
  - Indonesian ponds: 5 t dry/ha over three months [3], or 3 t/ha a year
    with 25% of the pond planted, from an older FAO trial [3].
  - Land-based IMTA tanks (*A. vermiculophyllum*, Portugal): 0.7 kg dry per
    m² a month [11].
- **Water use:** Pond and tank systems use water exchange. Indonesian
  polyculture ponds changed 20% of their water every 2–3 days [3]. Open-sea
  farms: not applicable.
- **Propagation:**
  - Mostly vegetative fragments or bundles [3]. Clonal-degeneration risk is
    flagged in Chile, where poor genetic diversity and reused stock are
    said to hold production back [7].
  - Spore seeding of ropes is possible and far more seed-efficient
    (Finding 4) [3].
  - Sporelings can fuse into "coalescent" chimeric holdfasts [3].
- **Labor:**
  - Chile: planting is manual [3], and dredge harvesting is mechanised
    [3]. Chile's wild seaweed harvests in general have drawn unemployed
    miners, farm workers and others, which damaged beds [3].
  - China: machines strip seaweed from culture lines [6].
  - Indonesia: pond work is mostly manual (methods [3]).
  - No labour-hour figure was found for farming.
- **Soil:**
  - Pond culture is on mud or sandy-mud bottoms. Bottom planting works on
    sandy mud, and deep mud needs bamboo screens or nets [3].
  - Acid-sulfate pond soils need liming and N/P fertiliser (Finding 5) [3].
  - Chile's beds are on wave-sheltered soft bottoms, often brackish [3].
  - Salinity: a strongly euryhaline genus. *G. gracilis* grew best at
    5–10 psu in one trial, though it died at 0 psu [8]. *A.
    vermiculophyllum* tolerates 5–60 psu [8]. Several species grew best at
    20 psu [8].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - Sun-dried for agar [6][10]. Shade-dried material gave higher agar yield
    and gel quality than sun-dried [10].
  - China's abalone-feed share is shipped fresh, not dried [6].
  - Shelf life: Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Seed rates of about 10,000 kg/ha for Chilean bottom
  planting [3], 800 kg/ha wet in Indonesian ponds [3], and 1.8 kg per 5 m
  rope in Chile [3].
- **Cultivars:**
  - *Gp. lemaneiformis* 981, 07-2, Lulong No. 1, and the MT-17 and MT-18
    mutants [5].
  - Chile's *G. chilensis* populations differ in their farming traits [3].
  - No formal cultivar registry was found.
- **Weather response:**
  - Heat above about 26 °C kills Chinese crops [5].
  - Agar yield and composition vary between rainy and dry seasons
    [9][10].
  - Wave action damages intertidal Chilean beds [3].
  - Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine to brackish. Intertidal and subtidal soft
  bottoms, ponds, rafts and long-lines, and land-based tanks [1][5][3][11].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** 40–60 days per cut [3]. Agar yield and gel
  strength vary seasonally [10].
- **Soil biology:** Ponds rely on microphytobenthos (the "klekap" layer)
  that feeds milkfish [3]. Pond primary productivity correlates with
  *Gracilaria* yield [3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - *A. vermiculophyllum* and *G. salicornia* are documented invaders
    (Finding 6) [2]. *G. salicornia* has an IUCN GISD account [2].
  - Chile has transplanted *Gracilaria* north of its natural range [3].
  - Most farmed species are farmed within or near their native ranges [1].
  - No national rules on movement or cultivation specific to *Gracilaria*
    were found in this pass.
- **Controlled environment:** Proven as a land-based IMTA biofilter.
  - *A. vermiculophyllum* in 1,200 L tanks at a fish farm removed about
    40.5 g N per m² a month [11].
  - *G. bursa-pastoris* removed 56% of ammonium from fish effluent while
    growing 11.7% a day [11].
  - Commercial tank culture beyond pilot scale was not found.

## Hazards

- **Prostaglandin poisoning ("ogonori").** Deaths in Japan (1980, 1982,
  1993) and Guam (1991; 3 dead) [4]. There was a non-fatal outbreak in
  Hawaii (1994) [4].
  - The 1984 study isolated PGE2 from *G. verrucosa* soaked in fresh water
    [4].
  - The risk rises with cutting or freshwater soaking [4], and toxic
    cyanobacterial epiphytes may be a factor [4].
  

- **Agar.** No food-safety concern was found in this pass. A formal agar
  additive evaluation (E 406) was not sourced.
- **Crop pests.** Epiphytes threaten Chilean farms: 21 epiphyte species
  were recorded in 2017–2021 [7]. *Rhizoclonium* infestations force
  complete harvest [3].
- **Ecological.** Invasion (Finding 6) [2]. Also bloom formation in
  nutrient-rich estuaries [2].
- **Heavy metals:** Not sourced this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (agar):**
  1. Dried seaweed is washed.
  2. Alkali pretreatment, for example 5% NaOH at 90 °C for 3 hours [10].
  3. Hot-water extraction [10].
  4. How the gel is then recovered and dried was not sourced in detail.
  - Pretreatment converts sulfate and raises gel strength and melting
    point, but cuts yield by 25–56% [10].
  - Food-grade agar needs a gel strength above 350 g/cm², and commercial
    processing standards above 800 g/cm² [10]. Chilean agar reached about
    730 g/cm² at 23% yield [10].
  - Agarose, a purified fraction, is the highest-value product [10].
- **Chain (abalone feed):** fresh harvest → shipped undried to abalone
  farms (China, about 70% of output) [6].
- **Chain (food):** raw or blanched salads ("ogo", ogonori) [4][2].
  Caribbean "sea moss" drinks: not sourced.
- **Other:** biofilter biomass from IMTA [11]. Phycobiliprotein pigments
  extracted before agar (Chile), without loss of agar quality [10].
- **Agar end uses:** microbiological media, food gels, pharmaceuticals and
  genetic engineering [10].

## Open research questions

- **Split the genus row?** `gracilariopsis` and `pelillo` are proposed;
  should the genus row stay as an umbrella?
- **China's yield.** About 44 dry t/ha implied by national statistics vs
  10–15 for Chile. Is it a different area basis (raft surface only?),
  several crops a year, or statistics inflation?
- **Wet-to-dry ratio.** Indonesian trade data imply about 10.5% (export)
  and 7.9% (domestic). *G. edulis* is about 12.9% dry matter. FAO uses 10%.
- **Agar food-additive safety** (E 406) and heavy metals: not sourced.
- **Shelf life** of dried raw material.
- **Movement regulation** for gracilarioids, given the oyster-vector
  history.

## Estimates and derivations

**China, dry yield per hectare, about 44 t.** Inputs: 610,824 t dry from
13,924 ha in 2022 [5]. 610,824 / 13,924 = 43.9 t/ha. Compare Chile's
10–15 t/ha a year [3]. The area basis is uncertain.

**Agar yield and unit value.** Inputs: 7,500 t agar from 55,000 t dry
seaweed, worth US$132 million [6]. 7,500 / 55,000 = 13.6%;
132 × 10⁶ / 7,500 = US$17,600 per tonne. The FAO source is about
two decades old.

**Indonesian wet-to-dry ratios.** Inputs [6]: exports of 37,000 t dry =
353,000 t wet; domestic 47,200 t dry = 600,000 t wet.
37,000 / 353,000 = 10.5% dry; 47,200 / 600,000 = 7.9% dry. Inconsistent
within one source.

**Official vs industry, Indonesia, about 1.6–1.9 times.** Inputs: 1.9 Mt
official vs 1.0–1.2 Mt industry [6]. 1.9 / 1.2 = 1.58 and 1.9 / 1.0 = 1.9.
That is smaller than the eucheumatoid gap (`cottonii`, Finding 2).

**Pond growth multiplier.** Inputs: 0.8 t wet stocked → 5 t dry in three
months [3]. Assumption: 10–12.9% dry matter [9]. 5 t dry is about
39–50 t wet, so 49–62 times the stocked weight. That is with progressive
harvesting and regrowth.

**Chile wild-harvest collapse, about 67%.** Inputs: 110,000 → 36,500 wet
t/yr (1984–1988) [3]. (110,000 − 36,500) / 110,000 = 0.668.

**Seed saved by spore planting, 250–333 times.** Inputs: 10,000 kg/ha
vegetative vs 30–40 kg cystocarpic plants per hectare [3].
10,000 / 40 = 250; 10,000 / 30 = 333.

**Manual invasive removal per hectare, about 65,000 person-hours.** Input:
6.54 person-hours/m² [2]. 6.54 × 10,000 = 65,400.

**Chile small-concession yield, about 100 t/ha a season (wet basis
presumed).** Input: up to 30,000 kg from 0.3 ha [3]. 30,000 / 0.3 =
100,000 kg/ha. The basis isn't stated; if wet, at 10% dry that is
consistent with 10 dry t/ha.
