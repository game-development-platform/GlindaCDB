## Naming and scope

This covers the tiny marine microalgae sold and studied as
"Nannochloropsis", with the queued species *N. gaditana* as the anchor
[1]. It covers the single cropID `nannochloropsis`.

**Names.**

- **Not a green alga.** Nannochloropsis is a eustigmatophyte, in the
  same broad group as kelps and diatoms (Ochrophyta), not a green alga
  [1]. In Japanese aquaculture *N. oculata* has been called "marine
  chlorella" [2].
- **The queued species has moved genus.** In 2015 DNA analysis split the
  genus: *N. gaditana* and *N. salina* went to a new genus,
  *Microchloropsis* [1]. Most applied papers still say *Nannochloropsis
  gaditana* [3].
- **Species can't be told apart by eye.** No consistent visible features
  support the species split [1]. Strains with identical standard DNA
  barcodes can still differ a lot at genome level [1].
- **Scope choice.** Hatcheries sell "Nanno" without always naming the
  species [2]. This doc treats the row as the hatchery and biotech crop
  "Nannochloropsis" in the broad sense, with *M. gaditana* data marked.
  Status-table name updated to *Microchloropsis gaditana* (formerly
  *Nannochloropsis gaditana*).

**Relatives flagged:** `chlorella` (RESEARCHED; similar-looking but
unrelated); `haematococcus` and `dunaliella` (LISTED, other
microalgae). No new rows proposed.

**Supply is farmed** (Finding 2), so this is not a wild-managed resource.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the "bread and butter" of fish hatcheries: food for the
food of baby fish.**

- **Role.** In marine fish hatcheries, Nannochloropsis is the preferred
  microalga for feeding rotifers [2], tiny animals that are then fed to
  fish larvae. It is also added to larval tanks as "green water" [2].
- **Why.** It is rich in EPA (an omega-3 fatty acid), and rotifers fed on
  it significantly increased the survival of many cultured fish
  hatchlings [2].
- **No DHA.** It contains essentially no DHA, the other key omega-3, so
  hatcheries pair it with a DHA-rich alga such as *Isochrysis* [2].
- **Sold frozen.** Commercial pastes hold about 68 billion cells per ml
  (about 180 g dry per litre), keep two years frozen and about four months
  refrigerated [2].
- **Why it matters.** A crop whose customer is another crop: it sits at the
  base of the hatchery food chain.

**Finding 2 — grown in tubes, panels and raceways; Almería is the
research hub.**

- **Systems.** Grown in open raceways and in closed tubular and flat-panel
  photobioreactors; tubular reactors give better productivity and biomass
  quality [3].
- **Yields (outdoor, Almería unless noted).**
  - Raceways: about 22 g dry per m² of ground a day [3].
  - A raceway fed 20% wastewater centrate reached 32.4 g/m² a day, in a
    mixed culture led by *N. gaditana* [3].
  - Tubular: about 11–18 g/m² a day [3].
  - Flat panels: annual average 0.26 g per litre a day [3].
  - Seasonal swing: a two-year flue-gas raceway trial ranged from 0.025
    to 0.160 g/L a day as sunlight increased [3].
- **Cheap nutrients.** Wastewater centrate can replace fertiliser; one
  group estimates production cost below €0.5/kg that way [3] (a claim, not
  an audited cost).
- **CO₂.** Flue gas from a coal power plant has been used as the CO₂ source
  [3].
- **Why it matters.** A high-yield crop that fits industrial symbiosis:
  waste nutrients and waste CO₂ in, feed out.

**Finding 3 — an oil crop that genetic engineering doubled.**

- **Natural oil.** Fat content reaches about 30% of dry weight; EPA can be
  20–40% of the fat, depending on culture conditions [2].
- **Pure EPA.** Because it lacks DHA, its EPA can be purified to over 95%
  [2].
- **Engineering.** In 2017 ExxonMobil and Synthetic Genomics reported a
  modified *N. gaditana* strain whose oil rose from 20% to over 40%,
  without much loss of growth, by turning down a single gene regulator
  [4].
- **Why it matters.** A real-world case of a "tech-unlocked" strain; and a
  biofuel candidate, though no commercial fuel production was found.

**Finding 4 — not yet a legal food in the EU.**

- **Novel food.** In the EU, *Nannochloropsis* as a food ingredient needs
  authorisation under the Novel Food Regulation [5]. *N. gaditana*'s
  novel-food status hasn't been established [5].
- **Pending.** An application for *N. oculata* oil (about 25% EPA, as a
  supplement) was under EFSA evaluation as of the source [5].
- **Why it matters.** It is fish feed in law long before it is human food.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Single cells, 2–5 µm [1]. Year-round culture,
  with output tracking sunlight [3].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous, by daily dilution (e.g. 0.25–0.4 of the
  volume a day) [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable (continuous).
- **Climate zones:** Marine; *M. gaditana* strains come from temperate
  lagoons and bays (Cadiz, Morocco, Italy, Long Island) [1]. Culture-
  collection strains are kept at 14–20 °C, with known ranges such as
  11–16 °C and 13–22 °C for particular strains [1].
- **Composition:** Protein about 60% of dry weight; lipid about 15–30%;
  EPA 27% of fatty acids; ARA 3%; DHA not detected (commercial paste)
  [2].
- **Edibility:** Fed to rotifers and fish larvae [2]. Human food use not
  authorised in the EU (Finding 4) [5].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable.
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Light drives productivity; too much light
  causes photoinhibition in outdoor panels [3].
- **Yield:** About 11–32 g dry/m² a day outdoors (Finding 2) [3].
- **Water use:** Marine medium; recycled medium and artificial seawater
  used [3]. Volumes: Not sourced.
- **Propagation:** Inoculum grown indoors in bottles or bubble columns,
  then scaled up [3].
- **Labor:** Not sourced.
- **Soil:** Not applicable.
- **Storage (postharvest):** Frozen paste 2 years; refrigerated about 4
  months [2].
- **Establishment:** CO₂ supply about 1.9 ml per litre a minute was
  optimal in 3 m³ tubular reactors [3].
- **Cultivars:** Culture-collection strains (e.g. CCMP 527 used in
  Almería) [3]; one engineered high-oil strain [4].
- **Weather response:** Seasonal irradiance sets output [3].
- **Water regime:** Marine (about 30 g/L salt medium) [3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Not sourced.
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Contaminating microalgae (e.g. a
  cyanobacterium took over flat panels fed centrate) [3].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:** No invasive record found. An engineered
  strain raises containment questions (inference; not sourced).
- **Controlled environment:** Fully proven in closed photobioreactors [3].

## Hazards

- **Food status, not a toxin:** No toxicity reports found in this pass;
  the barrier to human use is regulatory (Finding 4) [5].
- **Ammonium stress.** Cultures fed centrate were stressed above 123 mg/L
  ammonium [3].
- **No other hazards found in this pass.**

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (hatchery):** grow in reactors → harvest and concentrate to paste
  → freeze → feed to rotifers or add to larval tanks [3][2].
- **Uses:**
  - Rotifer culture and enrichment; larval "green water" [2].
  - EPA oil (purifiable to over 95%) [2].
  - Biofuel research (engineered strains) [4].
  - Wastewater nutrient removal [3].

## Open research questions

- **Genus scope.** Keep `nannochloropsis` as a genus-level hatchery crop,
  or split *N. oculata* / *N. oceanica* (common in hatcheries) from
  *M. gaditana*?
- **Commercial output.** No production tonnage found.
- **Labor and water use:** Not sourced.

## Estimates and derivations

**Raceway yield per hectare, about 80 t dry a year.** Input: about 22 g
dry/m² a day (Almería raceway) [3]. 22 × 365 × 10,000 / 1,000,000 =
80 t/ha. This assumes the summer-weighted figure holds all year, which the
seasonal trial (Finding 2) shows it doesn't; a year-round figure would be
lower.
