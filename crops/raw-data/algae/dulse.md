## Naming and scope

This covers *Palmaria palmata* (Linnaeus) F.Weber & D.Mohr, a North
Atlantic red alga (order Palmariales). It was formerly called *Rhodymenia
palmata*, and its Irish names include dillisk, duileasc and creathnach [1].
It covers the single cropID `dulse`. It is distributed along North Atlantic
shores from about 40 to 80 °N [1].

One older range summary also lists Alaska–California, Japan and Korea [1].
Pacific dulse is a separate species (below) [2]. Whether every Pacific
record belongs to it was not checked.

**This is mostly a wild-managed resource.** It is hand-picked from the
foreshore in Western Europe, Canada (New Brunswick, Nova Scotia) and
Maine [1]. Cultivation has been demonstrated but is not yet a major supply
(Finding 2). It is a candidate for the wild-managed template (GitHub issue
#2); notes are in `crops/researchNotes/wild-managed/dulse.md`.

**Relatives flagged:**

- **`pacific_dulse`** (*Devaleraea mollis*, formerly *Palmaria mollis*;
  proposed). A Pacific species [2].
  - Oregon State University bred and patented a fast-growing,
    non-reproductive strain grown in tanks, the "bacon" dulse [2].
  - It is farmed commercially in land-based tanks in Oregon [2], and used
    as abalone feed in Hawaii [2].
  - It is tank-farmed rather than wild-harvested, a different species, and
    sexually sterile in its farmed strain, so it should be its own row.
- **`pepper_dulse`** (*Osmundea* spp., proposed). An unrelated red alga
  sharing the common name. It has its own harvest guidance in Wales [3].
  The shared name is a source of confusion; it may not be worth a row.
- Scottish "dulse" picking also includes *Dilsea* spp. [3], a further
  common-name overlap. Not proposed.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the females are microscopic and have never been found in the
wild.**

- **What you see on the shore.** The plants on the shore are either male
  gametophytes or tetrasporophytes [4]. Female gametophytes form microscopic
  crusts about 0.1 mm across, and have yet to be found in the wild [4].
  Males grow up to 50 cm, rarely 1 m [4].
- **How the cycle was proved.** It was completed in culture only after
  green mutant females were isolated and fertilisation observed in vitro
  [4].
- **No carposporophyte.** Unlike other red algae, the fertilised female
  doesn't produce a carposporophyte. A tetrasporophyte grows directly over
  the female instead [4].
- **Reproduction.** A 2025 study concluded the species reproduces mainly
  sexually [4].
- **Why it matters.** Hatchery seeding has to work from tetraspores, the bottleneck in cultivation (Finding 2) [5]. There is no known way to farm the
  females.

**Finding 2 — cultivation works in the lab and in tanks but has struggled
to pay at sea.**

- **Sea culture.**
  - Cultivation has succeeded on ropes in Ireland and in tanks in Germany
    [5].
  - But Werner & Dring (2011) concluded that cultivation wasn't feasible with
    then-current techniques [5].
  - Seeded-rope yields were only about 0.3–0.9 kg per metre per season [5].
  - Problems include variable seeding, spore loss and high spore mortality
    [5]. Tetraspores settle in 3 days and attach in 14 [5].
- **Land-based tanks.**
  - Scottish researchers reported biomass doubling in about a week in tank
    trials. They also reported lower iodine and metals than in wild plants,
    and year-round production [5].
  - A German team grew it vegetatively in tumble culture for 2 years [5].
- **Pacific dulse.** Oregon's Pacific dulse strain grows 9–14 kg a week in
  two tanks [2]. It went on to a 10-tank, then a 20-tank commercial farm
  [2].

**Finding 3 — Grand Manan: a century-old hand-picking and sun-drying
economy, now threatened by warming.**

- **History.** Dulse has been harvested in the Bay of Fundy, especially on
  Grand Manan Island, for more than a century [3].
- **Workforce.** About 100 people on Grand Manan made a living picking it
  in 1973 [3].
- **The harvest.** Pickers work at low tide, timed to tides of up to 5 m
  [3].
- **Drying.** The catch is spread in rows assigned to each harvester and
  dried in the sun and air for about 24 hours [3]. It needs about six hours
  of strong sun, which fog often prevents [3]. Wet weather in 1973 caused
  a shortage [3].
- **Quality.** Dark Harbour dulse, thick and richly flavoured, is
  considered the best [3].
- **Supply.** One wholesaler buys about 10 t a summer and can't meet
  demand [3].
- **Warming.** Bay of Fundy waters have warmed about 1 °C per decade over
  40 years [3]. Gulf of Maine autumn temperatures recently ran 2.3 °C above
  the long-term average [3].

**Finding 4 — nutrition and safety vary strongly by season.**

- **Protein.** Highest in winter–spring (21.9%) and lowest in
  summer–early autumn (11.9%) in one French study [6]. A Norwegian study
  found 14–30% [6].
- **Floridoside.** This sugar swings from under 5% of dry weight in
  January–March to over 20% in June–August [6].
- **Arctic Norway.** Iodine was high and varied seasonally. That limited
  recommended intake to 0.63–0.92 g dry a day for a 70 kg adult [6].
  Arsenic, cadmium and lead peaked in winter and spring [6].
- **Contrast.** A 2013 study of Danish, Icelandic and Maine samples found
  iodine, arsenic, mercury, cadmium and lead all below WHO limits [6].
  Iodine safety therefore depends on origin and season (see Open
  questions).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial, regrowing from the holdfast each
  year [1][4]. It grows and reproduces in winter and spring, with no or
  negative growth in summer and autumn [6]. Tetrasporangia occur all year,
  peaking in January–April in Irish waters [5].
- **Harvest pattern:** Hand-picking from the foreshore, late spring to
  about November [1][3]. Maine guidance:
  - Pick only attached plants, never drift or cast-up plants [3].
  - Pick selectively, leaving holdfasts, immature plants and older thick
    fertile plants [3].
  - Leave at least 25% of the biomass [3].
- **Days/years to first bearing:** Not applicable for wild beds. Tank
  culture doubles biomass in about a week [5]. Wild regrowth rate after
  picking: Not sourced.
- **Climate zones:**
  - Cold-temperate to Arctic North Atlantic [1].
  - Bay of Fundy water rarely exceeds 14–16 °C [3].
  - In cultivation, growth was highest at 12 °C among 12, 15 and 17 °C [5].
  - Upper survival temperature: Not sourced. Portuguese surveys list it
    among cold-water species at their southern edge [1].
  - Chill/vernalization: not applicable. Elevation: not applicable.
- **Composition (dry basis):**
  - Literature ranges: 73–89% moisture fresh; 12–37% ash; 8–35% protein;
    38–74% carbohydrate; 0.2–3.8% lipid [6].
  - Xylan is the main polysaccharide, 24–35% of dry weight [6].
  - It is a source of desmosterol [6].
  - Protein digestibility in vitro was about 30%, limited by the
    non-protein fraction [6].
  - Dry matter is 10–22% of wet weight [6].
- **Edibility:**
  - Eaten dried as a snack [3][6]. It is heated before eating
    on Grand Manan [3].
  - Fried as a bacon substitute, and added to bread, soups and fish [6].
  - Fresh plants contain appreciable vitamin C [6].
- **Pollination:** Not applicable. Male spermatia fertilise the
  microscopic female (Finding 1) [4].
- **Photoperiod sensitivity:** Not sourced. In tumble tanks, growth rose
  with irradiance with no chronic photoinhibition [5].
- **Yield:**
  - Wild: a Grand Manan wholesaler buys about 10 t a summer [3].
  - Sea rope culture: 0.3–0.9 kg per metre per season [5].
  - Tanks: doubling in about a week (Scotland) [5]; Oregon Pacific dulse at
    9–14 kg a week from two tanks [2].
  - Per-hectare wild yield: Not sourced.
- **Water use:** Land-based tanks need flowing or recirculated seawater
  [5][2]. Oregon's system recirculates and could run inland on saline
  wells [2].
- **Propagation:**
  - Wild: sexual, plus regrowth from perennial holdfasts [1][4].
  - Culture: tetraspore seeding of ropes in hatchery and nursery, about
    9 months [5]; or vegetative tumble culture [5].
  - The Oregon strain is non-reproductive and propagated vegetatively [2].
  - Plants removed with their substrate can't re-attach, and recruitment
    and recolonisation rates are poorly known [1].
- **Labor:** Hand-picking at low tide plus sun-drying [3]. About 100
  pickers on Grand Manan in 1973 [3]. No hours-per-tonne figure was found.
- **Soil:** Not applicable. It grows on rock, shells or other seaweeds
  (often epiphytic on kelps), from mid-tide to 3–6 m below low water
  [1][3]. It tolerates siltation [1].
- **Storage (postharvest):**
  - Sun-dried in about 24 hours, or brought indoors if not dry [3].
  - Freezing fresh plants at −20 °C lost over 40% of amino acids compared
    with holding them in 5 °C seawater [6].
  - Shelf life of dried dulse: Not sourced.
- **Establishment:** Not applicable for wild beds. Rope seeding:
  settlement in 3 days, attachment in 14, then about 9 months in the
  nursery [5].
- **Cultivars:** None for *P. palmata*. Brittany has two morphotypes [1].
  The OSU Pacific dulse strain is patented [2].
- **Weather response:**
  - Drying weather is critical: wet, foggy summers cut supply [3].
  - Warming waters threaten supply in the Bay of Fundy and Gulf of
    Maine [3].
  - Frost: not applicable.
- **Water regime:** Marine, intertidal to shallow subtidal [1][3].
- **Harvest stages / windows:** Late spring to November [1]. June harvests
  were the most nutrient-rich in an Atlantic Canada study [6].
- **Soil biology:** Not applicable. Epiphytes and endophytes infect tank
  cultures [5].
- **Invasiveness / biosecurity:**
  - Native North Atlantic [1]. No invasive record was found, and MarLIN
    lists it as not harmful [1].
  - **Harvest regulation** (these are rules on wild collection, not
    biosecurity):
    - **Ireland:** harvesting from state foreshore has required a licence
      since the Foreshore Act 1933 [3]. Since the 2021 Maritime Planning
      Act, every wild harvester must hold a Marine Usage Licence by July
      2028 [3]. New applications are paused until 2027 [3].
    - **Maine:** up to about 22.7 kg a day may be picked for personal use
      (converted from 50 lb). Commercial picking needs a Marine Harvesting
      Licence for Seaweed [3].
    - **Scotland:** hand-picking needs only the landowner's permission
      [3].
    - **Canada:** harvesters need a Fisheries and Oceans licence [3].
- **Controlled environment:** Land-based tank culture has been
  demonstrated (Scotland, Germany) and run commercially for Pacific dulse
  (Oregon) [5][2]. It allows year-round production and lower iodine and
  metal levels [5].

## Hazards

- **Kainic acid.** The Danish Veterinary and Food Administration warned
  that dulse may contain dangerous levels of this neurotoxin [6]. A 2013
  study found only very small amounts: a person would need about 150 kg of
  fresh dulse at once to reach effects seen in animals [6].
- **Iodine.** It varies by origin and season. Arctic Norwegian dulse limits
  safe intake to 0.63–0.92 g dry a day [6]. Danish, Icelandic and Maine
  samples were below WHO limits [6].
- **Heavy metals and arsenic.** Below WHO limits in 2013 samples [6]. In
  the Arctic, arsenic, cadmium and lead are higher in winter and spring
  [6].
- **Vitamin K.** Not alarmingly high, even for people on blood thinners if
  eaten in moderation [6].
- **Collection hygiene.** Avoid old beach-cast seaweed and polluted waters
  [6].

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (Grand Manan):** low-tide picking → spread on the ground in
  rows assigned to each harvester → about 24 hours of sun drying → sold to
  dealers and wholesalers [3].
- **Uses:**
  - Snack food; cooking ingredient for bread, soups and fish; bacon
    substitute [6].
  - Abalone feed, especially Pacific dulse [2].
  - Possible salmon feed supplement [5].
- **Value:** Danish retail prices for dried dulse were €3–6 per 25 g [5].
  Oregon dried snacks sell for about US$100 per pound (about US$220/kg)
  [2]. It is a premium seaweed, worth more per tonne than kelp [5].

## Open research questions

- **Iodine conflict:** "Very small" (2013, Danish, Icelandic and Maine
  samples) vs high enough to cap intake below 1 g dry a day (Arctic
  Norway). Origin, season or method?
- **Wild regrowth after picking** and sustainable harvest rates (Maine's
  25% rule is guidance, not data).
- **Upper thermal limit**, for warming projections in the Bay of Fundy.
- **Wild-managed template** (issue #2): where Grand Manan's assigned drying
  rows, tide windows and licensing go.
- **Pacific dulse naming**: *Palmaria mollis* vs *Devaleraea mollis*; the
  status of the OSU strain.
- **Shelf life** and per-area wild yield.

## Estimates and derivations

**Iodine content implied by the Arctic intake cap.** Inputs: maximum
0.63–0.92 g dry a day for a 70 kg adult [6]. Assumption: the cap is set by
a reference intake. The source's basis isn't stated in the abstract.

- At a 150 µg recommended intake: 150 / 0.63 = 238 and 150 / 0.92 =
  163 mg/kg dry.
- At a 1,100 µg upper limit: 1,100 / 0.63 = 1,746 and 1,100 / 0.92 =
  1,196 mg/kg.

Either way it is much higher than "very small" (2013).

**Kainic acid dose in dry weight.** Input: 150 kg fresh [6]; dry matter
10–22% [6]. 150 × 0.10 = 15 to 150 × 0.22 = 33 kg dry.

**Tank growth rate from a one-week doubling, about 9.9% a day.** Input:
doubling in about a week [5]. ln 2 / 7 = 0.099 per day.

**Maine personal limit, about 22.7 kg a day.** Input: 50 lb [3].
50 × 0.4536 = 22.7 kg.

**Oregon tank output.** Inputs: 20–30 lb a week, planned 100 lb [2].
20–30 × 0.4536 = 9.1–13.6 kg; 100 × 0.4536 = 45.4 kg a week.

**Seasonal protein swing, about 1.8 times.** Input: 21.9% vs 11.9% [6].
21.9 / 11.9 = 1.84.

**Oregon snack price per kg.** Input: about US$100 per pound [2].
100 / 0.4536 = US$220/kg.
