# Snake Melon / Armenian Cucumber (*Cucumis melo* Flexuosus group)

## Naming and identity

The non-sweet, long-fruited melons of the Flexuosus group (*C. melo* var.
*flexuosus*; Indian long melon is often written *C. melo* var.
*utilissimus*) [1][3][9]. Family Cucurbitaceae. Names:

- **English:** snake melon, Armenian cucumber, snake cucumber, yard-long
  cucumber, serpent melon, uri [6][9].
- **Levant:** faqqous (Arabic, from Hebrew *piqqus*); *qishut* in the
  Bible and Talmud [1].
- **India:** kakri, kakadee, tar (Punjabi) [3][4][9].
- **Others:** varunk (Armenian), chanbar (Persian), acur (Turkish),
  qiththa (Arabic, Iraq), tortarello or commarella (Italian), alficòs
  (Catalan) [9][11].

Not included:

- True cucumber (*C. sativus*), a different species that does not cross
  with it (by analogy with cantaloupe cross-pollination data, the closest
  relative with documented crossing behavior).
- Pickling melon (Conomon group) — a separate crop (see
  `crops/raw-data/vegetable/pickling-melon.md`).
- The Chate group (Egyptian 'Abdallawi' and *ajur*, Puglian *carosello*
  and *barattiere*), eaten the same way but a separate horticultural
  group; a candidate for its own entry only, not yet added [13].

Grounded against this project's pickling melon data (the other non-sweet
vegetable melon) and, by analogy, against cantaloupe — the same species,
useful for seed, water, and storage comparisons, though cantaloupe itself
isn't yet covered in this project.

## Distinctiveness compared to pickling melon and cucumber

Compared to pickling melon (the nearest crop in this project), snake melon
differs in several ways:

| | Snake melon | Pickling melon |
|---|---|---|
| Edible stage | young only, 6-10 days after flowering; mature fruit is inedible [1] | young (pickles) and mature (cooked) |
| Storage | days at ambient, up to 2 weeks at 10-13°C [7] | mature fruit hung for 6-10 months |
| Sex form | monoecious [1][3] | andromonoecious |
| Farming system | rain-fed Levant crop on stored winter moisture, with no irrigation after sowing [1]; irrigated spring crop in North India [3] | irrigated summer rice-fallow crop in Kerala |
| Products | raw salad vegetable and brine pickle [1][9] | narazuke (aged 1-3 years in sake lees); cooked mature fruit |
| Market name | Armenian cucumber, faqqous, kakri [1][3][9] | shirouri, narazuke, Mangaluru cucumber |

Compared to cucumber, which it resembles on the plate:

- **Different species:** it crosses with every other melon (cantaloupe,
  honeydew, pickling melon) but never with cucumber, so seed isolation
  rules run against other *C. melo* crops, not against cucumber.
- **Climate and water:** grown rain-fed in the Levant, with hand-watering
  only at sowing [1]. Sold in the US as a melon that keeps bearing in
  desert heat where cucumbers stop (secondary source) [14].
- **Market identity:** a widely known, distinct product with its own
  names across the Middle East, India, and the US [1][3][9].

On fresh use alone it is close to cucumber. The case for treating it as a
distinct crop rests on the dryland rain-fed farming system, the
melon-family seed and cross-pollination rules, and its own market
identity — taste and shape alone wouldn't justify it.

## Why Vegetable, not Fruit (both melons)

Both snake melon and pickling melon are classified here as vegetables,
not fruit, because crops in this project are classified by culinary use,
not botany — cucumber, eggplant, pepper, okra, chayote, and bitter melon
are all botanical fruits commonly treated as vegetables. Snake melon is
only eaten immature, raw or pickled like cucumber; ripe fruit is inedible
and has no dessert use [1]. Pickling melon is likewise never eaten as a
dessert fruit. The sweet dessert melons (cantaloupe, honeydew, oriental
melon) are a different case: eaten ripe, sweet, and stored/graded on
ripeness and sugar content. Neither vegetable melon shares that chain,
even though they share cross-pollination, seed-isolation, and water/crop-
coefficient behavior with the sweet melons as a useful analogy.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — a rain-fed dryland vegetable [1][2].**
- In Israel and the West Bank, snake melon is a traditional crop of Arab
  farmers in the Lower Galilee, Judea, Samaria, and the Jordan Valley,
  grown under rain-fed conditions [1]. It is "an important horticultural,
  mainly rain-fed, crop in Palestine" [2].
- Traditional practice, documented in a 2015 common-garden trial at
  Sandala (Jezreel Valley) [1]: field ploughed and fertilized beforehand;
  sown 23 March, 4-5 seeds per spot, rows 2 m apart and spots 1 m apart;
  each spot hand-watered with about 250 ml at sowing and again 5 days
  later, then no further irrigation; thinned to one plant per spot.
- Development was slow in a cold April. Harvest started about 55 days
  after sowing, every 3-4 days, and lasted until late June. The field
  then declined, "possibly due to water deficit and disease development"
  [1].
- Trial yield: 1.4 kg per plant over five sample harvests, about 2.8 kg
  per plant for the full season (range 0.8-2.3 kg in the samples). At
  5,000 plants/ha that is about 14 t/ha (derived; see Estimates below).
- Farmers keep their own seed. Landraces are adapted to rain-fed,
  low-input farming, and are being lost to replacement by improved
  varieties [2].

**Finding 2 — eaten young; mature fruit is not food [1].**
- Eaten "immature about one week post anthesis, and mature fruits become
  inedible." Fruit was tasted at 6-7 days (preferred) and 8-9 days after
  flowering (still acceptable).
- Hollow fruit (flesh separating from the seed cavity) rises from 9-17%
  at 6-7 days to 44-64% at 8-9 days — this is the quality clock.
- Marketable fruit is 10-30 cm long, picked every 3 days from day 55 for
  about 40 days. Unpicked fruit "would inhibit further fruit set."
- Ripe fruit turns yellow and smells strongly of muskmelon [6]; it is
  kept only for seed.

**Finding 3 — irrigated long melon (kakri) in North India [3][4][5].**
- Grown in summer on river beds and *diara* land (river islands and flood
  plains) in Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan. Likes
  25-30°C, 60-70% humidity, and deep, well-drained sandy or sandy-loam
  soil [4].
- Two crops a year in the North Indian plains: spring-summer and kharif
  [3].
- 'Pusa Utkarsh' (released 2025) [3]: sown mid-February to early March,
  2-3 seeds 2 cm deep in hills 60 cm apart on the slopes of channels 1.5 m
  apart (2.5 m when both sides are sown); seed rate 2.5-3.0 kg/ha;
  15-20 t/ha manure plus urea, superphosphate, and potash, with a urea
  top-dressing at the 10-12 leaf stage; first harvest 45-50 days after
  sowing; fruit 52 cm × 2.4 cm, 130-140 g; yield 29.1 t/ha (2022-2024
  mean), against 18.6 t/ha for the older national check 'Punjab
  Longmelon-1' (first harvest 55-60 days).
- Pests: red pumpkin beetle, fruit fly, aphids, whitefly; downy mildew in
  high summer rain and humidity; Fusarium wilt [3].
- Off-season crops (August to January) are not grown in the open field
  because of pests, birds, and wild animals. Under insect-proof net
  houses at 50 × 20 cm spacing with branch pruning, they gave about
  68 t/ha [4][5].

**Finding 4 — ancient and well-documented [1][9][12].**
- Faqqous is identified with the *qishut* of the Bible (eaten in Egypt)
  and the Talmud; the true cucumber reached the region much later [1].
- Roman writers Columella and Pliny described it; the emperor Tiberius
  reportedly had it year-round using mica-glazed frames [9].
- The Mishnah (Kilayim 1:2) rules that *kishut* and *melafefon* are not
  *kilayim* (forbidden mixed plantings) with each other, though one rabbi
  dissents (secondary source) [12]. See Religious/cultural notes, below.

**Finding 5 — US garden and market type [6][7].**
- In Florida it is grown "as a curiosity and for pickling": planted in
  late August in Gainesville, it gave usable fruit by late October [6].
  Cultural practice follows cucumber and cantaloupe. Powdery and downy
  mildew are the main diseases [6].
- Seed-trade 'Striped Armenian': 43 days from transplanting; harvest at
  8-18 inches; soil at least 21°C for sowing; pH 6-6.8; pick daily; cool
  in cold water; hold at 10-13°C and 90% relative humidity for up to 2
  weeks [7].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual trailing cucurbit vine, monoecious
  [1][6]. Warm season (Levant: sown late March, rain-fed; North India:
  mid-February to early March and again in kharif season; Florida: early
  spring or late August) [1][3][6].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous picking of young fruit every 2-4 days
  for about 40 days [1]. Two harvest stages: young (food) and mature
  (seed only).
- **Days to first harvest:** 45-60 days from sowing [1][3]; total season
  length about 100 days [1].
- **Climate zones:** Mediterranean, semiarid, arid (when irrigated),
  subtropical and tropical (North Indian plains), warm-temperate summer
  [1][3][6].
- **Composition:** Estimated at roughly 150 kcal/kg and 6.5 g protein/kg,
  by analogy with raw cucumber with peel (about 15 kcal and 0.65 g
  protein per 100 g) [10] — sugar hasn't built up in immature fruit [1],
  so ripe/sweet cantaloupe is the wrong stand-in. A direct composition
  analysis of immature flexuosus fruit would improve this.
- **Edibility:** Raw, unpeeled, when young [1][9]; mature fruit is
  inedible.
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (bees); monoecious — male
  flowers appear about 6 weeks after germination, female flowers about a
  week later, borne singly on side branches [1]; females solitary on
  short peduncles, males in clusters [3]. First female flower 54-61 days
  from sowing in the rain-fed system, with 2-16 female flowers per plant
  in an 11-day window [1]. Crosses with all *C. melo* types (cantaloupe,
  honeydew, pickling melon, oriental melon) but not with cucumber,
  watermelon, or squash (by analogy with cantaloupe cross-pollination
  data). Field populations show more heterozygosity than expected —
  "extensive cross pollination" and gene flow between fields [1]. Not
  parthenocarpic (no fruit set without pollination, though this specific
  point isn't directly sourced for snake melon).
- **Yield (fresh, kg/ha):** world-average estimated ~16,000 (between the
  rain-fed Levant landrace figure of ~14,000 and the older irrigated
  Indian check's 18,600) [1][3]; good ~29,100 ('Pusa Utkarsh' 3-year mean,
  irrigated) [3]; exceptional ~68,000 (net-house off-season crop, 50 × 20
  cm spacing, pruned) [4]; poor ~8,000 (derived from the lowest-yielding
  rain-fed landrace sample, 0.8 kg/plant) [1]. Figures are marketable
  young fruit, fresh weight, per planted hectare, from trial or
  research-station management; the rain-fed figure rests on the original
  study's own "double the five sample harvests" estimate [1].
- **Water use:** No published seasonal water-use figure. About 0.5 L per
  plant was applied at sowing (2 × 250 ml) in the Sandala rain-fed trial,
  with the rest of the crop relying on stored soil moisture [1]; the
  field declined in late June, "possibly due to water deficit" [1].
  Irrigated systems use furrow/channel irrigation (India) [3] or drip on
  plastic mulch (US seed trade) [7]. Crop-coefficient curve and rooting
  depth (about 0.9 m) are estimated by analogy with cantaloupe (same
  species and canopy), giving roughly 72 effective crop-days over an
  estimated 100-day season (20 days initial, 30 development, 40
  mid-season from flowering through continuous picking, 10 late decline).
  Drought tolerance: estimated high (the rain-fed crop finishes on
  minimal water and still bears, though irrigated Indian crops yield
  about twice as much, so stress does cost yield). Waterlogging
  tolerance: estimated low — downy mildew appears under high summer rain
  and humidity [3], and the Indian crop favors well-drained river-bed
  sand [4]. "Snake melons can finish a crop on the soil's stored winter
  moisture, but picking ends early when the ground dries out. Water at
  sowing; keep watering through the picking season if you have it"
  (paraphrased critical-water-stage note, based on [1]).
- **Soil:** Optimal pH ~6.4 (range 6.0-6.8) [7]. Grown on a wide range of
  soils: rain-fed sites on terra rossa, Mediterranean brown forest,
  rendzina, alluvial, and loessial soils in the Levant [1]; sandy or
  sandy-loam river-bed land in India [4]; well-drained loam and sandy
  loam rich in organic matter more generally [3]. Fertilizer practice in
  irrigated India: 15-20 t/ha manure plus roughly 69 kg N, 24 kg P2O5, and
  60 kg K2O per ha [3]; an Iraqi trial used 200 kg N/ha [11]. Nutrient
  uptake rates are estimated by analogy with cantaloupe.
- **Planting / spacing:** Direct-seeded in hills or spots — 2-3 seeds per
  hill in irrigated systems [3], 4-5 per spot in the rain-fed system [1];
  transplants are also used in the US seed trade [7]. Seed rate about
  2.75 kg/ha irrigated (range 2.5-3.0) [3], roughly 0.9 kg/ha estimated
  for the rain-fed spot system. Thinned to one plant per spot (rain-fed)
  or 1-2 plants per hill (irrigated) [1][3]. Spacing: rain-fed spots 2 ×
  1 m; irrigated channel hills 60 cm apart on 1.5 m channels [1][3]; US
  transplants at about 12 inches in rows 5-6 feet apart [7]. Seed mass
  about 36-38 mg [2][7]; estimated seed viability around 5 years in ideal
  storage, 2 years at ambient conditions, 5 years in an arid barn (by
  analogy with cantaloupe). Isolation distance from other *C. melo* crops
  estimated at 245-800 m (by analogy with cantaloupe). Mostly
  open-pollinated; Israeli commercial varieties and Tunisian F1 hybrid
  trials exist, but the hybrid production method itself wasn't researched
  for this crop [1][15].
- **Storage (postharvest):** 10-13°C and 90% relative humidity for up to
  about 2 weeks [7]; ambient (no cold chain) likely only 2-3 days
  (estimated). Cooling immediately after harvest via cold-water immersion
  is standard practice [7]. Fruit left on the vine goes hollow and soft
  by 8-9 days after flowering [1]. "Pick snake melons young, before the
  seeds harden, and dunk them in cold water. Kept cool (10-13°C) they
  last about two weeks; ripe yellow ones are only good for seed"
  (paraphrased storage note, based on [1][7]).
- **Flowering:** No vernalization or photoperiod requirement researched.
  No germination below 10°C; sow at soil temperatures of 21°C or above
  [7]; best at 25-30°C [4]. Yield differences between landraces correlate
  with April maximum temperature at their home sites, and a cold April
  slows early growth [1].
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hours data was found. Harvest dominates —
  about 15 picking passes over 40 days [1], handling small fruit (around
  130 g) [3]. Other labor estimated by analogy with cantaloupe, with less
  land preparation in the rain-fed spot system.
- **Field operations:** Hand-watering each sowing spot (about 250 ml,
  twice) [1]; thinning to one plant per spot [1]; picking by cutting the
  stem rather than pulling [7]; cold-water dunk to remove field heat [7];
  branch pruning in net-house production [4][5]. Two crops per year
  possible in the North Indian plains [3].
- **Cultivars:**
  - Baladi White (Baladi Abiadh) — Palestinian/Israeli landrace, the
    baseline for comparison: light green, long (length/width ratio about
    5.3), densely wrinkled; highest-yielding group studied; stays green
    longest [1][2].
  - Baladi Green (Baladi Akhder) — landrace: most vigorous, earliest and
    most female flowers, yet the lowest-yielding group [1].
  - Sahouri White (Sahouri Abiadh) — landrace, mainly Ramallah and
    Hebron: shorter (length/width about 4.1), straight parallel furrows,
    among the best-tasting; latest and fewest female flowers [1].
  - Sahouri Green (Sahouri Akhder) — landrace, Bethlehem: senesces
    earliest; most hollow fruit [1].
  - Pusa Utkarsh (open-pollinated, released 2025): light green, 52 cm ×
    2.4 cm, 130-140 g, crisp, not bitter; 29.1 t/ha; first harvest 45-50
    days [3].
  - Punjab Longmelon-1 (open-pollinated, older Indian national check):
    18.6 t/ha; 35.6 cm fruit; first harvest 55-60 days; described as a
    "shy bearer" [3].
  - Arka Sheetal (an Indian horticultural research institute release):
    immature fruit used for salad, picked 10 days after pollination [8].
  - 'FLI' (an Indian accession): very elongated (length/width about 15),
    snake-like; not grown in the Levant [1].
  - Striped Armenian (US heirloom, open-pollinated): fuzzy, ridged,
    alternating dark and light green stripes; harvest at 8-18 inches; 43
    days from transplanting [7].
- **Controlled environment:** Insect-proof net house and polyhouse
  off-season production in India, 50 × 20 cm spacing, single-stem or
  one-node pruning, about 68 t/ha [4][5]; greenhouse trials reported in
  Tunisia (secondary source) [15]; hydroponic viability not researched.

## Hazards

- Cucurbitacins (bitter compounds) occur in a minority of fruit, linked
  to landrace segregation and cross-pollination — bitter fruit appeared
  in 11 of 47 populations studied, about 25% of plants in 4 of them [1].
  Mitigation in practice is tasting and discarding bitter fruit, and
  roguing bitter-fruited plants from seed lines. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a
  fuller human-hazard write-up is deferred until this project has a place
  for this kind of health/safety data.
- No livestock-safety issue found in the sources reviewed.

## Religious and cultural notes

- In Jewish law, the Mishnah (Kilayim 1:2) rules that *kishut* and
  *melafefon* (melon) are not considered *kilayim* (forbidden mixed
  plantings) with one another and so may be sown together; one rabbi
  dissents (secondary source) [12]. Identifying *kishut* with snake melon
  follows [1]. Other Land-of-Israel produce laws (tithing, sabbatical
  year) apply to all crops generally and weren't researched specifically
  here.
- The biblical *qishu'im* (Numbers 11:5) is usually read as snake melon
  [1]; no ritual or festival use was found beyond this production-law
  context.

## Disorders

- Hollow fruit (flesh separating from the seed cavity): driven by fruit
  age past about 7 days post-flowering, worse in the Sahouri Green
  landrace; causes a quality penalty; prevented by picking every 2-3 days
  [1].
- Bitterness: see Hazards, above.
- Twisted and bent fruit: appears more in the Baladi landraces
  (genotype-driven); trellising is said to help, though this isn't
  directly sourced; affects grading only.

## Uses and processing

- **Fresh/pickle chain:** young fruit → cold-water dunk → graded (bitter,
  hollow, or soft fruit culled) → sold fresh or eaten raw [1][7]; or
  packed → salt brine, often with vinegar → pickle [9][11].
- **Seed chain:** ripe fruit → seeds scooped → washed and dried → seed
  [6] (by analogy with cantaloupe seed processing).
- **Not otherwise modeled:** the net-house off-season production system
  with branch pruning [4][5]; F1 seed production; cooked and stuffed
  dishes.

## Estimates and derivations

1. **Rain-fed yield, about 14 t/ha; poor tier 8 t/ha.** Inputs: 2.8
   kg/plant full-season estimate (1.4 kg measured across five sample
   harvests, doubled per the original study's own method); 0.8-2.3 kg
   range of sample yields; rows 2 m apart, plants 1 m apart, one plant
   per spot [1]. Plants per ha: 10,000 m² ÷ (2 m × 1 m) = 5,000. Average:
   2.8 × 5,000 = 14,000 kg/ha. Poor: 0.8 × 2 × 5,000 = 8,000 kg/ha. Range
   8-23 t/ha; the doubling factor is the weakest part of this estimate.
2. **Composition, 150 kcal/kg and 6.5 g protein/kg.** By analogy with raw
   cucumber with peel (15 kcal, 0.65 g protein, 95.2 g water per 100 g)
   [10], since young snake melon is eaten unpeeled and immature, before
   sugar builds up [1]. Range 120-200 kcal/kg; a direct proximate
   analysis of immature flexuosus fruit would replace this.
3. **World-average yield, 16 t/ha.** A round figure between the rain-fed
   landrace estimate (~14 t/ha) and the older irrigated Indian check
   (18.6 t/ha) [3], since much of the world crop is landrace or garden
   production with no formal statistics. Range 10-20 t/ha.
4. **Exceptional yield, 68 t/ha.** From a net-house trial reporting
   roughly 68 t/ha on a per-structure basis; the study counted only 700
   of the 1,000 m² structure as actually cropped, so per cropped area the
   figure would be closer to 97 t/ha [4]. The lower, per-structure figure
   is used here.
5. **Water-use analogy and ~72 effective crop-days.** Crop-coefficient
   curve (roughly 0.5 / 0.85 / 0.60 across stages) and rooting depth
   (0.9 m) are borrowed from cantaloupe — same species and canopy type.
   Stage lengths (20/30/40/10 days) follow the rain-fed timeline: sown 23
   March, first harvest about day 55, picking for about 40 days, decline
   in late June, for a total around 100 days. Effective crop-days ≈ 20 ×
   0.5 + 30 × 0.675 + 40 × 0.85 + 10 × 0.725 ≈ 71.5, rounded to 72.
6. **Drought/waterlogging tolerance and dry-day tolerance.** Estimated
   high drought tolerance because the traditional rain-fed crop gets
   about 0.5 L per plant total and still bears, though irrigated Indian
   crops yield roughly double, so water stress clearly does cost yield.
   Low waterlogging tolerance is inferred from the downy-mildew risk in
   wet summers and the river-bed-sand soil preference, not directly
   sourced. Estimated about 12 days before a dry-day yield penalty
   applies, derived from a rough soil-water-balance calculation.
7. **Growth curve.** Estimated for a roughly 100-day vine with a 40-day
   picking plateau that tails off as the rain-fed soil dries.
8. **Seed rate, multiplication, and seed-crop timing.** Seed mass about
   37.8 mg (453.6 g ÷ 12,000 seeds, from a US seed-trade figure) [7],
   close to the 36 mg mean reported for Palestinian landraces [2].
   Rain-fed seed rate: 4.5 seeds × 5,000 spots × 0.0378 g ≈ 0.9 kg/ha.
   Multiplication ratio (seed yield ÷ seed rate) estimated at about 90, by
   analogy with cantaloupe seed yield. Time from flowering to a mature
   seed fruit is not directly sourced for this crop; estimated at 110-130
   days from sowing by analogy with cantaloupe fruit maturation.
9. **Seed viability, isolation, seed yield, and processing — by analogy
   with cantaloupe** (same species, similar seed and fruit structure, and
   pollination behavior). Flexuosus fruit likely holds fewer seeds than a
   large cantaloupe, so seed yield per hectare may be lower than the
   200-300 kg/ha cantaloupe figure used as a starting estimate.
10. **Labor.** No sourced labor-hours data was found. Harvest is the
    dominant labor cost (about 15 passes in 40 days, handling small ~130
    g fruit); other labor tiers follow the general shape of cantaloupe
    labor requirements, adjusted down for less land preparation in the
    rain-fed spot system.
11. **Nutrient uptake and fertilizer practice.** Uptake rates estimated by
    analogy with cantaloupe. Fertilizer math: urea is 46% N, so
    (100 + 50) kg urea × 0.46 ≈ 69 kg N; single superphosphate is 16%
    P2O5, so 150 kg × 0.16 = 24 kg; muriate of potash is 60% K2O, so
    100 kg × 0.60 = 60 kg [3].
12. **Ambient shelf life and chilling threshold.** A 10-13°C holding band
    [7] implies chilling sensitivity below 10°C, similar to cucumber.
    Ambient shelf life of 2-3 days and a 24-48 hour pickling window are
    estimated, not directly sourced.
13. **Salinity tolerance.** Estimated by analogy with the FAO muskmelon
    salinity threshold (about 1.0 dS/m, moderately sensitive); snake melon
    may be more tolerant given its dryland cultivation history, but no
    direct source was found.
14. **Yield profile (peak, plateau, decline).** Average over the 40-day
    picking window: 16,000 ÷ 40 = 400 kg/ha/day. Middle harvests were
    highest, with yield falling from the third sample harvest onward [1],
    so peak is estimated at about 1.4× average, or 560 kg/ha/day. Plateau
    estimated at 14 days and decline at 19 days (7 + 14 + 19 = 40).
    Maximum standing days estimated at 2, since fruit goes hollow in
    44-64% of cases by 8-9 days after flowering [1], with an estimated
    0.25/day quality decay rate.

## Open research questions

- **Rain-fed dryland system.** No other vegetable crop researched so far
  for this project is grown on stored soil moisture with no irrigation
  after sowing — worth flagging as a distinct cultivation pattern, not
  just a variant of the usual irrigated-crop model.
- **Source conflicts, not resolved:** first harvest reported at 45-50
  days (irrigated, India) [3] vs. about 55 days (rain-fed) [1] vs. 63-65
  days (US seed trade/garden sources) [7][14]; fruit length at harvest
  reported as 10-30 cm marketable [1], best at 30-40 cm [9], or 8-18
  inches [7]; the net-house yield basis (1,000 m² of structure vs. 700 m²
  actually cropped) [4].
- **Weakest estimates:** world-average yield (no formal statistics
  exist); seed yield and multiplication ratio; labor figures; composition
  values; chilling threshold.
- **Hybrid-seed status:** mostly open-pollinated; Israeli commercial
  varieties and Tunisian F1 trials exist [1][15], but the crossing method
  is only estimated by analogy with cantaloupe. Seed yield, isolation
  distance, and seed-fruit timing are all analogy-based estimates, not
  directly sourced for this crop.
- **Chate group.** Egyptian 'Abdallawi' and *ajur*, and the Puglian
  *carosello* and *barattiere* landraces, are non-sweet melons eaten
  unripe like cucumber [13]. Many classifications treat them as a
  separate group from Flexuosus; they'd need their own distinctiveness
  check before being added as a separate crop, and might turn out to be
  better documented as a cultivar note here instead.
- **Indian naming.** Indian sources call kakri *C. melo* var.
  *utilissimus* [3][4], while at least one source lists 'Arka Sheetal' as
  *flexuosus* [8]. Both are treated here as the same long-melon type.
  Indian "kachri" (a small wild-type melon) is a different crop entirely
  [8].
- **Not researched:** Iraqi and Sudanese production in depth; Turkish
  *acur*; pickling ratios; photoperiod response; seed longevity specific
