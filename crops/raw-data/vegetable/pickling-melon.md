# Pickling Melon / Culinary Melon (*Cucumis melo* Conomon group)

## Naming and identity

The non-sweet melons of the Conomon group (*C. melo* var. *conomon*, *C.
melo* subsp. *agrestis* var. *conomon*) [1][2][3]. Known by many local
names:

- **Japan:** shirouri (白瓜), used for narazuke [1][3].
- **Korea:** wolgwa (월과) [1].
- **China:** yuegua (越瓜), regarded as the oldest domesticated melon form
  [2].
- **Southern India:** the culinary or vegetable cucumber — Mangaluru
  cucumber, golden melon, dosakai, Madras cucumber [2].

Not included:

- Snake melon / Armenian cucumber (Flexuosus group) — a separate crop
  (see `crops/raw-data/vegetable/snake-melon.md`).
- True cucumber (*C. sativus*).
- The sweet oriental melon, a separate crop in the dessert-melon group (not
  yet covered in this project).

It is filed as a **vegetable** because every use is culinary: pickled,
cooked, or eaten raw like cucumber, never as a dessert fruit [2].

## Distinguishing characteristics

It differs from the sweet dessert melons (cantaloupe, honeydew, oriental
melon) and from cucumber in several ways:

- **Products:** non-sweet, non-aromatic, and non-climacteric [2]; eaten
  young as a pickle (Japanese narazuke, aged 1-3 years in sake lees) [3];
  eaten mature as a cooked vegetable (sambar, dal, gravies — South India)
  [2].
- **Storage:** mature fruit keeps 6-10 months hung from the ceiling in
  coir rope [2] — unusually long among melons and cucumbers (an oriental
  melon keeps about 10 days by comparison).
- **Two harvest stages:** young, about 10 days after fruit set, for
  pickling and raw eating; mature, about 4 weeks after set, for cooking
  and storage [2].
- **Farming system:** in Kerala it is a summer rice-fallow crop,
  irrigated, at 25-30 t/ha [2]. Kerala Agricultural University has
  released named varieties [2].
- **Market names of its own:** shirouri and narazuke in Japan; Mangaluru
  sauthekai and dosakai in India [1][2][3].

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — one melon, two cuisines.**
- Japan: immature and mid-ripe fruit has traditionally been pickled [2].
  Narazuke uses salted melons packed in sake lees with salt, sugar, and
  mirin, aged one to three years. Young pickles are eaten locally in
  summer; amber ones are sold as narazuke [3]. The method dates to the
  Nara period (about 1,200 years ago) [3].
- Decline in Japan: demand for the pickles has fallen, and the Kyoto
  heirloom 'Katsura-uri' is at risk of extinction [2].
- South India: mature yellow or orange fruit is cooked; its crunchy flesh
  holds up in cooking [2]. Dishes include sambar (Kerala, Tamil Nadu),
  dosakayapappu with tuvar dal (Andhra Pradesh), koddilu and tangy gravies
  (coastal Karnataka), and spicy pickles [2].
- Seeds are in demand for beauty and nutrition products; seed juice is a
  folk remedy for dyspepsia [2].
- Fully ripe Japanese fruit is "too soft" for pickles and is rarely eaten
  [2].

**Finding 2 — long storage of mature fruit [2].**
- Fruits "can be stored up to 8-10 months without losing their
  freshness," bound in thin coconut-fibre rope and hung from the ceiling.
- A Udupi farmer stores them 6-7 months (April-October). Prices were Rs
  20/kg at harvest (April) and Rs 50-60/kg in other months (2017 figures).
- The 'Arunima' cultivar keeps up to 90 days in normal storage and is bred
  for off-season marketing.

**Finding 3 — cultivation practice (Kerala and Karnataka) [2].**
- Climate: warm weather and bright sun; a summer crop grown in rice
  fallows. Seasons are January-March and September-December; the rainy
  season is unsuitable. Best at 16-25°C (one source).
- Pollination: "highly cross pollinated," andromonoecious.
- Spacing: 2 × 1.5 m recommended. Farmers also use trenches with 1.2 m
  between rows and 60 cm (or 30 cm) between plants.
- Seed rate: 500-750 g/ha.
- Field work: seed soaked 12 hours before sowing; farmyard manure and
  burnt soil (sudumannu) added to the trench; earthing up and mulching
  with dry coconut fronds at 3 weeks; irrigation every 2 days.
- Flowering: male flowers from 3 weeks, hermaphrodite and female flowers
  from 4 weeks, full flowering by about 6 weeks.
- Harvest: first fruit about 8 weeks after sowing, picking for 2 or more
  weeks; one farm got about 250 fruits from a 3.5 m row, at 0.5-5 kg each.
- Yield: 25-30 t/ha in Kerala; 'Mudicode' 30.4 t/ha; 'Arunima' 27 t/ha
  (2.3 kg fruit, 60-day crop); 'Saubhagya' 65-70 days, suited to
  high-density planting.
- Pests: Epilachna beetle, cucurbit fruit fly, powdery and downy mildew,
  mosaic, and wild pigs, porcupines, and peafowl.
- Japan (Okinawa): local conomon types grew more vigorously and earlier
  than cucumber in the hot typhoon season [2].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual trailing cucurbit vine. Warm season
  (South India: January-March or September-December, avoiding the
  monsoon; Japan: summer) [2].
- **Harvest pattern:** Continuous picking over roughly 2-4 weeks [2].
  Two harvest stages, young and mature (see below).
- **Days to first harvest:** 55-90 days from sowing [2].
- **Climate zones:** Tropical (humid South India), subtropical, and
  temperate-summer (Japan, Korea) [1][2].
- **Composition:** Estimated at roughly 150 kcal/kg and 6 g protein/kg,
  by analogy with cucumber rather than a direct composition analysis (not
  sourced for this crop specifically).
- **Edibility:** Raw when young; cooked when mature [2].
- **Pollination:** Required; insect-pollinated (bees); "highly
  cross-pollinated," andromonoecious — male flowers from week 3,
  hermaphrodite and female flowers from week 4 [2].
- **Yield (fresh, kg/ha):** average ~27,500 (Kerala state average
  midpoint) [2]; good ~30,400 ('Mudicode') [2]; exceptional ~40,000
  (estimated, high-density drip-fertigated crops); poor ~12,000
  (estimated, rainfed or pest-affected crops). Also reported: roughly 250
  fruits from a 3.5 m row of 10-12 plants [2].
- **Water use:** No direct water-use study found for this crop; water
  needs and crop-coefficient curve estimated by analogy with cantaloupe
  (same species), roughly 120 effective crop-days. Irrigated every 2 days
  in farm practice, with drip fertigation trials at 50-100% of pan
  evaporation [2]. Drought tolerance: medium (Okinawa types tolerate heat
  and drought) [2]. Waterlogging tolerance: low — the rainy season is
  unsuitable [2]. "Pickling melons are a dry-season crop. Water every
  couple of days, but keep them out of the monsoon" (paraphrased
  critical-water-stage note).
- **Soil:** Optimal pH ~6.4 (range 6.0-6.8) [2]; well-drained,
  nutrient-rich soil with added organic matter; commonly grown on
  rice-fallow paddy soils [2].
- **Planting / spacing:** Direct-seeded, 3-4 seeds per pit thinned to 2
  [2]. Spacing 2 × 1.5 m (about 3,333 pits/ha) recommended; trench rows
  1.2 × 0.6 m; higher density used for the 'Saubhagya' cultivar [2].
- **Seed:** Farmers commonly save their own seed from mature fruit — one
  farmer kept his line for 60 years [2]. Seed rate 0.5-0.75 kg/ha [2];
  about 645 seeds per fruit reported for 'Arunima' [2]. Isolation
  distance not directly researched; being "highly cross pollinated,"
  isolation from other *C. melo* crops is needed (estimated by analogy
  with cantaloupe). F1 hybrids have been evaluated at a Bengaluru
  agricultural university, but the production method wasn't researched
  [2].
- **Storage (postharvest):** Mature fruit keeps roughly 240 days (about
  8 months) hung at ambient temperature in coir rope; 'Arunima' keeps
  about 90 days under normal storage; the crop is non-climacteric [2].
  Young fruit intended for pickling must be salted within a day or two
  [2][3].
- **Labor:** No sourced labor-hour data was found; rough estimates cover
  establishment (trenching, manure, sowing, thinning, earthing up,
  mulching), ongoing culture (irrigation, weeding, beetle picking),
  harvesting, and postharvest handling (rope-binding/hanging or salting).
- **Field operations:** Earthing up and mulching [2]; thinning to 2
  plants per pit [2]; binding fruit in coir rope and hanging for storage
  [2]; salting and lees-packing for narazuke [3].
- **Disorders:** Not researched beyond the pests and diseases listed
  above [2].
- **Cultivars:**
  - Kerala local / Mangaluru sauthekai (baseline): yellow or
    black-rinded, 0.5-5 kg, stores 6-10 months [2].
  - Mudicode (Kerala Agricultural University): 30.4 t/ha; oval fruit
    1.8-2.5 kg; first harvest 50-55 days, crop to 79-88 days [2].
  - Arunima (Kerala Agricultural University): 27 t/ha; cylindrical 2.3 kg
    fruit, 33 cm; 60-day crop; tolerant of downy mildew, susceptible to
    mosaic; 90-day storage [2].
  - Saubhagya (Kerala Agricultural University): short duration (65-70
    days), less vigorous, concentrated fruiting, small fruit;
    high-density planting [2].
  - RNSM-1 (Telangana sambar cucumber): striped, 250-500 g, sour-sweet
    [2].
  - Shirouri / Katsura-uri (Japan): picked young for narazuke; Katsura-uri
    is a Kyoto heirloom near extinction [1][2].
  - 'Ohama' (Okinawa local): best yield and quality of seven Okinawa
    types evaluated; earlier than cucumber [2].
- **Controlled environment:** Pot culture with support is possible [2];
  greenhouse production not researched.

## Hazards

- Immature fruit of some Indian types can be bitter [2]; bitterness may
  signal cucurbitacins. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller human-hazard

- No other hazards found in the sources reviewed. No livestock-safety
  notes found either.

## Uses and processing

- **Mature-fruit chain:** mature fruit → graded → hung in coir rope for
  months → cut and cooked; seeds used for seed or juice [2].
- **Pickling chain:** young fruit → halved, seeded, salted → packed in
  sake lees (with sugar, mirin) and aged 1-3 years → narazuke [3].
- **Not otherwise modeled:** sake lees as a brewing by-product input;
  Katsura-uri functional juice products [2].

## Estimates and derivations

1. **Composition and water analogies.** Energy estimated at about
   150 kcal/kg — a non-sweet, watery melon, closer to cucumber (about
   150 kcal/kg) than to sweet cantaloupe (about 340 kcal/kg). Water-use
   and crop-coefficient values are borrowed from cantaloupe (same
   species). A direct composition analysis would improve this.
2. **Yield tiers.** World-average and good tiers are sourced (Kerala
   25-30 t/ha; 'Mudicode' 30.4 t/ha) [2]. Exceptional (~40 t/ha) is
   estimated for high-density, drip-fertigated crops; poor (~12 t/ha) is
   estimated for rainfed or pest-affected crops.
3. **Growth curve and stress multipliers.** Estimated for a short,
   60-90-day annual vine; not directly sourced.
4. **Labor.** No sourced labor-hours data was found; figures are rough
   estimates based on the described hand-cultivation practices (trenches,
   frequent irrigation, beetle picking, binding and hanging fruit).
5. **Peak yield and standing quality.** Derived from 27,500 kg/ha spread
   over about 21 days of picking [2], giving roughly 1,300 kg/ha/day at
   peak. Mature fruit holds well in the field, so an estimated 14 days of
   standing quality with slow decay.

## Open research questions

- **Japanese practice not researched in depth.** Shirouri cultivation
  practice, yields, and narazuke ratios (salt, lees renewal) need a
  Japanese-language source; only an English Wikipedia summary of narazuke
  and the Indian agronomy literature were read for this pass.
- **Sake lees as an input.** Narazuke requires sake kasu, a brewery
  by-product that isn't modeled as a commodity anywhere in this project
  yet.
- **Seed and F1 production.** Seed yield and isolation distance weren't
  directly researched; F1 hybrids exist only in trial reports [2].
- **Bitterness mechanism.** Reported bitterness in some immature Indian
  fruit [2] is not confirmed to be cucurbitacin-driven.
