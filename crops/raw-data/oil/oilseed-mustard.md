# Oilseed Mustard / Rai, Raya (*Brassica juncea*, juncea group)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/oilseed-mustard.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Brassica juncea* (L.) Czern., the **juncea cultivar
group** (also called rai or raya) — the oilseed/condiment-seed type of
the species, distinguished from three other cultivar groups of the same
species that cover different organs/uses: leaf mustard (a vegetable
crop), root mustard (a root-tuber crop), and stem mustard (zha cai, not
yet researched for this project). This row covers the SEED crop grown
specifically for oil. The seed's two end uses — pressed oil (this row's
focus) and ground/prepared condiment seed (mustard paste, table
mustard) — share the same field crop and seed chemistry; condiment-seed
use is treated as a separate, not-yet-researched spice/herb crop. *B.
juncea* is a genuine allotetraploid species (AABB, 2n=36), one of the
three "Triangle of U" amphidiploids alongside *B. napus* (canola, AACC)
and *B. carinata* (Ethiopian mustard, BBCC) — the same two diploid
parents that combined to form it (one already farmed elsewhere in this
project as turnip/napa cabbage/bok choy's species) are relevant
comparison points. Canola is the closest sibling for grounding: both are
Brassica oilseeds with their own double-low vs. high-erucic cultivar-
group split and an Ogura-CMS hybrid system, and the same "one species,
two end uses" pattern. Ethiopian mustard and crambe are also useful
comparisons for the same meal-toxicity shape.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content and erucic acid both have wide, genuinely
unresolved cross-source ranges, with one high-oil pure line standing
out.** Oil content figures cluster between 28.6% and 45.7% across a
general review [1], while a Brazilian trial measured 37.7-44.3% [2] and a
dedicated QTL mapping study identified a pure breeding line reaching
about 49% oil, described by its own authors as the highest reported for
oilseed mustard at the time [3]. Erucic acid in traditional (non-canola-
quality) types commonly exceeds 50% of total seed oil fatty acids — one
source gives "very high, approximately 50%" and another states the
single dominant fatty acid contributes more than half the total fatty-
acid pool [1]. Modern low-erucic ("0-erucic," under 2%) breeding lines
exist via both conventional selection and gene-editing knockouts of the
fatty acid elongase gene homeoalleles, with one 2024 study reporting a
line with erucic acid reduced to undetectable levels and otherwise-
normal plant growth and oil content [4]. A genuine, sourced linkage-drag
trade-off is recorded in the breeding literature: the low-erucic donor
lines historically used and their derivatives are reported as late-
maturing, small-seeded, and lower in total seed oil than the high-erucic
parents they were crossed into [1] — a real agronomic cost tied to the
same trait improvement canola's own breeding program solved for its own
species.

**Finding 2 — yield figures span almost an order of magnitude depending
on region, trial conditions, and whether oil yield or seed yield is
reported.** India-specific figures gave 900-1,235 kg/ha [5]; a 2012
agronomic review instead gives a yield POTENTIAL of 1,500-3,000 kg/ha
under improved management with 38-42% oil [6]; India's own national
average productivity for 2024-25 is reported at 1,461 kg/ha [7]; and a
Brazilian tropical-conditions trial recorded one accession reaching
2,057 kg/ha seed yield with a derived oil yield of 910 kg/ha [2].
Sowing-date sensitivity is sharply quantified in at least one field
trial: sowing on 10 October gave the highest seed yield (1,449 kg/ha) in
that trial, while a late 10 November sowing gave only 643 kg/ha — a 56%
yield loss from one month's delay [8]. These figures are left as a
visible range rather than averaged, since they come from different
countries, decades, and management intensities; no single "world
average" figure was found from a primary source this pass.

**Finding 3 — days to maturity and seed rate both carry real source
disagreement, tied partly to winter vs. spring sowing and line vs.
broadcast seeding.** A 2012 review gives 105-160 days to maturity [6];
current variety notifications for India range from about 131-144 days
for standard-duration varieties down to 94-118 days for an early-
maturity group [9]; and a general agronomy guide gives a 110-140-day
sowing-to-harvest window [10]. Seed rate recommendations likewise
disagree: one guide gives 3.5-5 kg/ha [10], another 4-5 kg/ha for line
sowing vs. 5-6 kg/ha broadcast [9], and a third 4-5 kg/ha line vs. 6-7
kg/ha broadcast [11]. Row spacing is commonly cited as 45 cm x 15 cm for
line sowing, though a state-level table gives 30 x 10-15 cm for one
Indian state and 45 x 10 cm for another [11] — genuine regional
agronomic variation, not a single error.

**Finding 4 — the species is an allotetraploid (AABB, 2n=36).** *B.
juncea* originated as an interspecific hybrid between *B. rapa* (AA,
2n=20) and *B. nigra* (BB, 2n=16), followed by spontaneous chromosome
doubling [12]. Multiple independent sources — a genome survey paper, a
resynthesis study, a genome annotation database, and a karyotyping study
— consistently confirm 2n=36, n=18, AABB [12][13][14][15]. One table in a
chromosome-scale assembly paper lists "2n=18" in what appears to be a
notation slip against the same paper's own text and every other source,
which uniformly states n=18/2n=36 [13] — this inconsistency is recorded
rather than silently resolved. Published total genome-size estimates
vary by assembly and method: ~922 Mb by flow cytometry (2016) [16], ~1,068
Mb (book chapter) [17], ~1,056.53 Mb by k-mer analysis (newer assembly)
[17], and ~980 Mb from a gap-free telomere-to-telomere assembly [18] —
most recent sources converge on roughly 0.9-1.07 Gb. An older genome-
size-database entry (as cited secondhand by a Brassicaceae genome-size
review) instead gives a 1C value of 1.092 ± 0.001 pg with a 1x genome
size of 534 Mb [19] — this older figure and the newer sequencing-based
estimates are not reconciled here.

**Finding 5 — pollination biology is itself a genuine unresolved
conflict, but hybrid-seed sourcing adds a specific CMS system.** One
seed-company guide calls the species insect-pollinated and not self-
fertile (half-mile isolation), while a regulatory biology document
instead describes it as mainly self-pollinated with 20-30% outcrossing,
bees as the vector, and gene flow observed beyond 35 m [20]. Hybrid seed
development in Indian mustard relies predominantly on **Ogura CMS** (the
same cytoplasmic male sterility system, originally from a radish
protoplast fusion, already used for canola and documented elsewhere in
this project's research on radish and daikon), with newer generations of
restorer lines carrying progressively less extraneous radish DNA for
commercial use [21]. At least two independent field studies found that
the standard isolation distance in use (as little as 50 m, citing a
1988 source) was NOT sufficient to prevent outcrossing and recommended
the figure be reconsidered [21]; a separate certification-standard
source instead gives 200 m for foundation seed and 50 m for certified
seed [21]. A 2021-22 study tested non-woven pollination-control bags/
tents as a physical alternative to open-plot isolation, reporting 57%
more hybrid seed set on CMS lines than the comparison method [21]. A
hybrid row-design patent describes a 4-female-row-to-1-male-row ratio
with 6-8 inch spacing, and the same Indian mustard study found pollinator
abundance fell in rows positioned further from the male parent row [21].

**Finding 6 — drought stress is a major, sharply quantified constraint,
while salinity tolerance is genotype-dependent and includes a
specifically named tolerant cultivar.** Most *B. juncea* cultivars are
described as drought-susceptible, with yield losses reported up to 94%
under drought stress [22]; about 24% of India's *B. juncea* cultivation
is rainfed, contributing to generally low yields from moisture-deficit
stress in that portion of the crop [22]. Breeders have specifically
looked to *B. carinata* (Ethiopian mustard) as a drought-tolerance DONOR
species for introgression into *B. juncea*, since *B. carinata* is
described as the most drought-tolerant of the cultivated Brassica species
[23]. Salinity tolerance is more genotype-variable: a named cultivar is
reported as highly salinity-tolerant, able to withstand salinity up to 9
dS/m without significantly compromising seed yield, and has been
specifically recommended for cultivation on saline lands [24][25]. The
species as a whole is described as exhibiting high genetic variability
for salinity tolerance [24] — this is a trait that varies by cultivar
rather than a single fixed species-level rating.

**Finding 7 — flowering is the single stage where frost, the most
damaging fungal disease, and the most damaging insect pest all
converge.** A recent breeding review states that seedling and
reproductive stages are the most frost-vulnerable in this species [26],
naming frost alongside drought, heat, and salinity as the main abiotic
constraints on production. Separately, Sclerotinia stem rot is described
as the most serious fungal disease of Indian mustard, with its greatest
damage occurring specifically during flowering — symptoms begin as
water-soaked stem lesions that progress to cottony white fungal growth
[27][28]. The mustard aphid is independently described as one of the
most damaging Brassica pests, active at the vegetative and flowering
stages with a December-to-March risk window in the Indian cropping
calendar, causing yield losses above 50% in heavy infestations; one
extension source states that aphid resistance is NOT available in the
germplasm or released varieties it reviewed, so management relies on
insecticide sprays timed from flowering through early pod formation
[29]. White rust adds a third flowering-window disease, producing white
to cream pustules on leaves, stems AND inflorescences; the Indian gene
pool is described as highly susceptible overall, though a small number of
named resistant varieties exist, with resistance typically governed by
one to three major, race-specific genes [30]. No single source
quantifies the combined effect of frost, Sclerotinia, and aphid pressure
all converging on the flowering window at once.

**Finding 8 — soil pH tolerance is broad but directional, and seed
storage life estimates disagree by an order of magnitude depending on
the intended use.** An Australian extension guide for this crop in a
northwestern growing region states the species grows on most soils but
prefers pH (calcium chloride method) under 7.0 (heavy grey clay soils
specifically mentioned), and flags acid soils below pH 5.0 for an
aluminium-toxicity check with liming recommended if needed [31] — a
notably more alkaline-tolerant, acid-cautious profile than the related
leaf-mustard crop's own range sourced from leafy-vegetable-focused
sources. On seed storage: a genetic-resources conservation strategy
describes Brassica seeds generally as "orthodox" for long-term
conservation, viable for DECADES when dried to about 5% moisture [32]; a
regulatory biology report similarly states mature dry Brassica seed may
remain viable for years to decades under controlled conditions [32]; but
a grower-facing resource instead gives a much shorter practical figure of
up to 7 years in dry, dark, cool farm-level storage [32] — the gap
between "decades" (gene-bank conditions) and "up to 7 years" (ordinary
farm storage) is a real difference in storage regime, not a contradiction,
and is kept visible rather than collapsed to one number.

**Finding 9 — the mustard meal/cake livestock-feed toxicity picture is
governed mainly by rapeseed-meal research, since no *B. juncea*-specific
regulatory limit was found.** A European food-safety regulatory panel's
opinion on glucosinolates in animal feed (citing rapeseed meal and
press-cake feeding trials, the closest relative with regulatory data)
recommends limiting total glucosinolates to roughly 1-1.5 mmol/kg feed
for monogastric animals, with lower limits for young animals, and notes
that high concentrations can cause iodine deficiency [33]. A separate
2016 review of erucic acid in feed and food gives a pig no-observed-
adverse-effect level of 700 mg/kg body weight per day, and specifically
notes that adult pigs tolerate higher erucic acid levels than young
animals; the human provisional tolerable intake (7.5 mg/kg bw/day) is set
entirely separately from the animal-feed figures [34]. A regional
(Indian) dairy-industry source states that DETOXIFIED mustard cake can be
included up to about 10% of cattle rations when mixed with other
palatable feed, explicitly noting this is of uncertain regulatory
authority outside that context [35], and separately notes that ruminants
tolerate these compounds better than monogastrics, while palatability
drops with glucosinolate hydrolysis products and sinapine, reducing
voluntary intake [35]. This is the same three-tier shape (toxic/anti-
nutritive meal, ruminant-tolerant, monogastric-unsafe) already found for
crambe (which is FDA-capped at 5% of US cattle rations) and Ethiopian
mustard in this project's research so far.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual, direct-seeded, single-harvest crop.
  Grown as both a winter-sown and spring-sown crop across different
  regions/latitudes; India's winter-season crop is the dominant form in
  the sourced agronomy (Finding 3).
- **Climate:** Temperate, cool temperate, subtropical, semiarid — broadly
  consistent with this species' documented rainfed/drought-prone Indian
  cultivation footprint (Finding 6), though not independently sourced to
  a primary climate-zone table this pass.
- **Food status:** Seed is pressed for oil or ground for condiment use,
  not eaten raw as seed.
- **Pollination:** Unresolved source conflict (Finding 5): one source
  calls it insect-pollinated and not self-fertile; another describes it
  as predominantly self-pollinated with 20-30% insect-assisted
  (bee-vectored) outcrossing. Both figures are carried forward rather
  than resolved to one.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield range roughly 1,449-2,057 kg/ha
  (field-trial and accession figures); exceptional yield up to about
  3,000 kg/ha under improved management; poor yield as low as 643 kg/ha
  under late sowing (Finding 2). India's national average productivity
  (1,461 kg/ha) sits near the low end of the "good" range and is likely
  the most representative single figure for ordinary smallholder
  practice [7]. Oil content ranges 28.6-49% of seed (Finding 1); a ~49%
  pure breeding line is the documented ceiling, not a typical-farm
  figure.
- **Water use:** No crop-coefficient curve specific to this crop was
  found this pass. Drought tolerance is low: most cultivars are drought-
  susceptible, with yield losses up to 94% reported under drought stress;
  *B. carinata* is specifically sought as a drought-tolerance
  introgression donor, implying this species' own baseline tolerance is
  comparatively poor (Finding 6). Waterlogging tolerance was not sourced
  this pass. Salinity tolerance is moderate to high and cultivar-
  dependent, with one named cultivar tolerating up to 9 dS/m without
  significant yield loss (Finding 6).
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded, line-sown (45 cm x 15 cm common, with
  real regional variation) or broadcast at a higher seed rate (Finding
  3). Seed rate ranges 3.5-7 kg/ha depending on method and source. No
  transplanting path was sourced.
- **Soil:** Optimal pH around 6.5, tolerant range roughly 5.0-7.0, with an
  aluminium-toxicity check recommended on acid soils below pH 5.0
  (Finding 8) — notably more alkaline-tolerant and acid-cautious than the
  related leaf-mustard crop's own range, which came from leafy-
  vegetable-focused sources rather than oilseed agronomy.
- **Seed storage:** Genuinely conflicting figures by storage regime
  (Finding 8): "decades" under gene-bank conditions (dried to about 5%
  moisture, controlled) vs. up to 7 years under ordinary farm dry/dark/
  cool storage. Ordinary farm storage (7 years) is likely the more
  representative figure for practical purposes, but the gene-bank figure
  is recorded rather than discarded.
- **Genome:** Allotetraploid, 2n=36 (AABB), n=18 (Finding 4); total
  genome size approximately 0.9-1.07 Gb by modern sequencing-based
  estimates, with an older, unreconciled database-cited figure of 1C=
  1.092 pg / 1x=534 Mb also on record.
- **Cultivars:** **High-erucic traditional rai/raya types** — the
  historical and still-dominant form across South Asia; erucic acid
  commonly >50% of oil, used as a traditional cooking oil in-region but
  food-safety-restricted in jurisdictions like the US (Findings 1, 9).
  **"0-erucic" / canola-quality types** — low-erucic (<2%), low-
  glucosinolate breeding lines, first released as a canola-quality
  variety in 2003; food-safe everywhere as a cooking oil, the same
  double-low standard already established for canola (Finding 1). A
  **specifically named salinity-tolerant cultivar** tolerates up to 9
  dS/m without significant yield loss, recommended for saline-land
  cultivation in India (Finding 6). An **early-maturity group** (94-118
  days) vs. a **standard-duration group** (131-144 days) represents a
  real, currently-notified duration split in Indian variety
  recommendations (Finding 3). No eating-quality/flavor cultivar
  differences were sourced this pass — unlike the related leaf-mustard
  crop's own cultivar-level pungency and texture differences, this
  crop's cultivar axis is almost entirely about oil chemistry, duration,
  and stress tolerance, since the product is a pressed industrial/
  cooking oil rather than a fresh vegetable.
- **Propagation:** F1 hybrid seed exists and is in commercial
  development (first canola-quality hybrid variety-quality line released
  2003). Mechanism: Ogura CMS, restored with restorer lines of
  progressively reduced extraneous radish DNA (Finding 5) — the same CMS
  lineage already documented in this project's research for canola,
  radish, and daikon. Isolation for hybrid seed production is contested
  in the literature — standard distances (50-200 m depending on seed
  class) have been found insufficient to prevent outcrossing in at least
  two field studies, with physical pollination-control bags tested as an
  alternative.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Mustard aphid, Sclerotinia stem rot, and
  white rust, all concentrated at or around flowering, plus frost
  damage at seedling and reproductive stages (Finding 7).

## Hazards

- **Erucic acid and glucosinolates in press meal/cake**: a European
  food-safety regulatory recommendation limits total glucosinolates to
  roughly 1-1.5 mmol/kg feed for monogastric animals (lower for young
  animals), citing iodine-deficiency risk at high concentrations. A
  separate review gives a pig no-observed-adverse-effect level of 700
  mg/kg body weight/day for erucic acid, with adult pigs tolerating more
  than young animals; the human provisional tolerable intake (7.5 mg/kg
  bw/day) is set separately. Detoxified mustard cake can reportedly be
  included up to about 10% of cattle rations mixed with other palatable
  feed in at least one regional context, of uncertain regulatory
  authority elsewhere; ruminants tolerate these compounds better than
  monogastrics (Finding 9).
- **Jurisdiction-dependent human food-safety restriction on the crude oil
  itself** (not just the meal) for high-erucic traditional types: the US
  FDA bars mustard oil as a cooking oil, permitting only external/
  massage-oil use. This is a human food-safety issue on the oil itself,
  distinct from the meal/feed issue above.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — both the meal/feed toxicity profile and the
  jurisdiction-dependent crude-oil food-safety restriction are deferred
  for a fuller write-up until this project has a place for this kind of
  health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → pressed → crude oil (traditional high-erucic types:
  used regionally as a cooking oil, but food-safety-restricted in some
  jurisdictions; canola-quality low-erucic types: food-safe cooking oil
  everywhere) + meal/cake (toxic/anti-nutritive to monogastrics raw;
  partially detoxified cake usable at limited inclusion rates in
  ruminant rations).
- Ground/prepared condiment seed (mustard paste, table mustard) is a
  separate end use of the same field crop and seed chemistry, treated
  as a distinct (not yet researched) spice/herb crop rather than part of
  this oil chain.

## Open research questions

- Oil content, yield, days to maturity, and seed rate are all kept as
  visible ranges rather than single point figures (Findings 1-3); a
  dedicated regional agronomy source would narrow these for a specific
  growing region.
- The pollination-biology conflict (Finding 5) — "insect-pollinated, not
  self-fertile" vs. "predominantly self-pollinated, 20-30% outcrossing"
  — likely has one true resolution, since it affects this species as a
  whole regardless of which organ/cultivar-group is being grown; worth
  resolving with a dedicated pollination-biology source.
- The canola-quality (low-erucic) cultivar group is a real, distinct
  chemistry/food-safety profile from the traditional high-erucic type,
  similar in shape to canola's own double-low vs. high-erucic split.
- A real industrial/biofumigant food-flavoring product chain exists
  around mustard essential oil / allyl isothiocyanate distillation, not
  researched to any depth this pass.
- No vernalization, day-length, or flowering-to-harvest interval figure
  was sourced specifically for the oilseed type.
- No labor-hour figures were sourced this pass.
- A phytoremediation/heavy-metal-uptake trait (cadmium, lead, zinc,
  copper, nickel, arsenic) is documented for the related leaf-mustard
  crop of the same species; since this is plausibly a species-level
  trait, it may well apply here too, but was not independently confirmed
  for the oilseed cultivar group specifically.
- No direct primary-database genome-size reference was opened this pass;
  the older 1C=1.092 pg / 1x=534 Mb figure (cited secondhand) and the
  much newer sequencing-based 0.9-1.07 Gb estimates are left
  unreconciled.
- This is a long-domesticated crop species with no evidence found of
  significant wild/feral naturalization (one source notes it has not
  become an abundant weed in at least one region studied, under 0.1% of
  fields).

## Estimates and derivations

The Brazilian trial's 910 kg/ha oil-yield figure (Finding 2) is the
source's own paired result (seed yield × oil content for that specific
accession), not independently recomputed here; it falls within the
range implied by combining that trial's seed yield with the wider
28.6-49% oil-content range from other sources, offering rough cross-
validation without being an independent derivation. No other DERIVED
values are restated here as reusable formulas.
