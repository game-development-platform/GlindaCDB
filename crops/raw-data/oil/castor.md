# Castor (*Ricinus communis*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/castor.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-29

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Ricinus communis* L., family Euphorbiaceae, monoecious
diploid (2n=20). No name conflict and no lumped-species question — this
is the single cultivated castor species, grown worldwide from one
wild-type ancestor with enormous morphological diversity (stem colour,
seed size, raceme habit) but no named botanical varieties that rise to a
distinct crop-category status in the sources opened this pass.

This is a genuinely non-food oil crop: the seed is never eaten, and the
crop's single defining hazard — ricin — is far more severe than any other
hazard documented for this project's other oil crops so far.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content and seed yield both vary enormously across
genotype, environment, and even within one plant, and the two do not
track together.** A 28-genotype Mediterranean field trial found total
seed yield ranging from 1,747 to 2,985 kg/ha, with oil content averaging
42.7% on primary racemes and 46.1% on secondary racemes of the same plants
[1]. Other reviews give oil content as low as 35% or as high as 60%
depending on variety and environment [1]. A Sicilian irrigation-by-
nitrogen trial found its highest seed yield (4,154 kg/ha) under full
irrigation plus 120 kg N/ha [1]. Four Colombian varieties showed yield and
oil content moving in opposite directions: one yielded 3.5 t/ha, another
only 0.72 t/ha but with the best oil content (36.7%) of the four [1]. An
Indian nitrogen-rate study found 80 kg N/ha gave the highest seed yield
(1,009 kg/ha) while 60 kg N/ha gave the highest oil content (48.3%) on the
SAME genotype — yield and oil content responding to fertilizer in opposite
directions, not just across varieties [1]. This oil-content/yield
decoupling is a genuinely castor-specific pattern, more pronounced than in
the other oil crops researched for this project so far.

**Finding 2 — castor has a real, mature commercial F1 hybrid seed system,
built on a two-line pistillate × monoecious cross.** Indian hybrid castor
seed production uses a two-line system: a pistillate (all-female) line as
the seed parent and a monoecious line, or one carrying "interspersed
staminate flowers" (ISF), as the pollen parent [2]. The pistillate female
parent is naturally all-female (no male flowers at all under most
conditions), but a small fraction of pistillate plants develop
environmentally sensitive staminate flowers under certain temperature
regimes — these ISF-bearing plants are needed to multiply the pistillate
line itself (self-pollination), and roguing removes any plant that
reverts fully to monoecious during hybrid-seed crossing [2]. Expression of
ISF is governed by temperature-dependent genes; one Indian city's climate
was identified as ideal for hybrid-seed multiplication because it shows
little or no ISF expression, keeping the female line reliably all-female
during the cross [2]. India's first castor hybrid, GCH 3 (TSP 10R × JI
15), was released in Gujarat in 1968; the indigenous pistillate line VP 1
underlies later hybrids GAUCH 1, GCH 2 (1985), and GCH 4 (1986), which
remain in cultivation [2]. Hybrid seed production is a winter-season
activity, with isolation distances of 600 m for foundation seed and 300 m
for certified seed [2] — far larger isolation distances than some other
oilseed crops in this project require, consistent with castor's wind
pollination (Finding 6) needing a much bigger buffer than an insect- or
self-pollinated crop.

**Finding 3 — ricin is one of the most potent plant toxins known, confined
to the seed meal and completely absent from the pressed oil, which is why
castor oil is an ordinary commodity despite the plant's extreme seed
toxicity.** Ricin is a ribosome-inactivating protein; its B chain mediates
entry into the cytosol and its A chain then halts protein synthesis,
causing cell death [3]. Toxicity severity depends heavily on exposure
route, with inhalation and injection far more lethal than oral ingestion
of whole seeds [3]. Ricin is water-soluble, not lipid-soluble, so during
pressing it stays behind in the solid press-cake and does not partition
into the oil at all; any trace residue is further reduced by heat above
roughly 80°C during processing, and one poisonous-plants reference
confirms the oil itself "does not contain ricin, provided that no
cross-contamination occurred during its production" [3]. There is no
antidote for ricin poisoning and dialysis has no role in treatment [3].
The seed coat is hard and largely impermeable, so an intact, unchewed seed
can pass through a digestive tract (human or animal) without releasing
much ricin at all — the danger is specifically in a BROKEN or chewed
seed, or in inadequately detoxified meal [4].

**Finding 4 — castor meal/pomace retains the ricin hazard and is only
safely usable (as fertilizer, not feed) after thorough, hard-to-verify
detoxification, and documented livestock poisonings keep happening from
incomplete detox or feed-mix errors.** Castor meal's use as animal feed is
limited mainly by ricin plus the milder alkaloid ricinine; one review
calls for international regulation setting a ricin threshold because
detoxification "is not always performed thoroughly and controlled" in
practice [4]. Veterinary case reports describe sporadic animal
intoxications from "insufficiently detoxified fertilizer," and a Brazilian
horse outbreak traced to a feed-order mix-up in which castor cake (sold
commercially as fertilizer) was substituted for palm kernel cake [4].
Cattle poisoning is well documented and typically follows ingestion of
broken/chewed seed or large amounts of forage; signs include fever and
blood-stained diarrhea, and separate ricinine poisoning from leaves or
fruit pericarps has been linked to neurological effects [4]. Lethal-dose
figures differ sharply by species and source: one figure for horses puts
lethal whole-seed dose at 0.1 g/kg body weight, while a cattle figure
gives a lethal FRESH-LEAF dose of 20 g/kg body weight [4] — these two
numbers are not directly comparable (seed vs. leaf, different species) and
are kept separate rather than merged into one toxicity figure. A review of
upgrading castor meal for livestock use found high pH, moist heat, and
microbial treatments the most effective ricin-deactivation methods, but
notes the allergen component of the meal is harder to address and remains
an open, unresolved problem even after ricin itself is deactivated [4].

**Finding 5 — ricinoleic acid's hydroxyl group makes castor oil
chemically unique among seed oils, underpinning a large, genuinely
distinct industrial-chemistry use chain.** Castor oil's defining fatty
acid, ricinoleic acid (an 18-carbon monounsaturated fatty acid), carries a
hydroxyl group most seed-oil fatty acids lack, making it unusually polar
and opening chemical derivatization routes unavailable to ordinary
triglyceride oils [5]. Hydrogenation and saponification give
12-hydroxystearic acid, which reacts with lithium hydroxide or carbonate
to form high-performance lithium-soap greases; transesterification
followed by steam cracking gives undecylenic acid (a nylon-11 precursor)
and heptanal (a fragrance component); the oil is also used directly as a
biobased polyurethane polyol [5]. Global production runs 270,000-360,000
tonnes a year across coatings, varnish, lacquer, grease, lubricant,
plastics, leather treatment, surfactants, and ink [5]. None of these
derivative-synthesis steps (hydrogenation, saponification, steam
cracking) are farm-level activities, so this industrial-chemistry chain is
noted here descriptively rather than detailed as a production process.

**Finding 6 — castor is monoecious and wind-pollinated (both self- and
cross-pollinated depending on weather), not insect-pollinated, with female
flowers positioned above male flowers on the same raceme.** *R. communis*
carries separate male (staminate) and female (pistillate) flowers on the
same plant (monoecious), with female flowers positioned above male
flowers on the raceme; both flower types lack petals [6]. Pollination is
by wind, to a degree depending on weather conditions, and the species can
be both self- and cross-pollinated [6]. No sourced insect-pollination
component was found this pass. The species is found mainly as a natural
diploid (2n=20); autotetraploids have been produced artificially with
colchicine but are not described as a commercial form [6].

**Finding 7 — castor's growing season is long (140-190 days) and the
plant is genuinely perennial in warm climates, grown as a frost-killed
annual everywhere else.** One extension profile describes castor as
needing a 140-180 day growing season when grown as a temperate annual,
and notes the plant is a perennial in the tropics [7]. A Canadian
provincial agriculture ministry gives a wider 140-190 day range depending
on cultivar [7]. The plant is extremely frost-sensitive and needs
consistently high growing-season temperatures; one California extension
source specifies the soil must reach at least 58°F (14.4°C) at 8 inches
depth for three consecutive mornings before safe planting, and the
Wisconsin extension source advises harvest can begin 10-14 days after the
first killing frost [7]. One seed vendor lists a much shorter 100-120 day
figure for an ornamental mix, which likely understates true grain-type
field cultivars and is not relied on [7]. Seed suppliers describe the
plant as perennial in USDA zones 8-11, treated as an annual everywhere
colder [7].

**Finding 8 — capsule (raceme) maturity is strongly indeterminate in
traditional/open-pollinated types, forcing multiple hand-harvest passes,
while modern hybrids and mechanized production push toward more
synchronized, determinate maturity.** A US harvest-aid study notes that in
indeterminate castor lines, the oldest (lowest) racemes on a plant can
mature up to two months before the plant's later racemes are ready, and
early-maturing capsules often shatter and drop seed to the ground before
the whole plant is harvested [8]. Grower guidance for hand-harvest systems
recommends cutting mature racemes in 3-4 separate passes starting around
90-120 days after planting, sun-drying the cut capsules, then shelling or
beating them to free the seed; harvesting too early reduces seed weight,
oil content, and germination [8]. Mechanized systems instead rely on
harvest aids (defoliants, desiccants such as glyphosate or diquat) to dry
the whole plant down for a single combine pass, and purpose-built headers
(e.g. a Brazilian capsule-stripping header) strip capsules without cutting
the plant stem, feeding a combine for cleaning [8]. Sources disagree on
dehiscence itself: one source distinguishes dehiscent from non-dehiscent
capsule types within the crop, while a grower guide says shattering is
"not a problem" in some varieties — kept as a visible source conflict
rather than resolved [8]. This is a genuine dual-harvest-pattern crop:
hand-harvest, indeterminate-raceme systems amount to sequential picking
passes over an extended season, while determinate hybrid/mechanized
systems amount to a single harvest after desiccation.

**Finding 9 — castor is widely described as drought- and salt-tolerant
due to a deep, fast-growing root system, though sources vary in how
strongly they state this, and salinity thresholds have been directly
studied.** One source attributes castor's survival "in drought and saline
environments" to its "deep, extensive root system and fast growth
pattern" [9], while a Pakistani study takes a more cautious view of its
drought tolerance (full text not opened this pass, summary only) [9]. A
dedicated salinity-threshold study on castor bean emergence and stand
establishment was located but not opened in full this pass [9]; an
agricultural-research article specifically on castor's tolerance to soil
salinity was also located by title only [9]. No specific soil-pH optimum/
min/max figure was found in extension-style material this pass despite a
direct, repeated search — a real, acknowledged gap.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial shrub grown as a warm-season annual
  in temperate production (frost-killed); genuinely perennial in tropical
  climates (Finding 7). Growing season 140-190 days (140-180 days per one
  extension source, 140-190 depending on cultivar per another); a
  100-120-day figure exists for an ornamental seed mix and is not relied
  on for field-type cultivation.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, warm temperate; perennial in USDA
  zones 8-11, extremely frost-sensitive, excluding cooler zones entirely
  (Finding 7).
- **Food status:** Not a food crop — the seed is toxic raw and is never
  processed into a food product in any source opened this pass. Seed meal/
  pomace remains toxic (ricin) even after oil extraction unless
  deliberately and thoroughly detoxified (Findings 3-4); the oil itself
  contains no ricin and is used medicinally/industrially.
- **Pollination:** Monoecious, wind-pollinated, self- and
  cross-pollinated depending on weather conditions (Finding 6); no
  sourced insect-pollination component — the only wind-pollinated oil
  crop researched for this project so far, in contrast to crops with
  insect-only or bee-boosted self-compatible pollination systems.
- **Yield:** No single world-average figure. Trial ranges: 1,747-2,985
  kg/ha across a 28-genotype Mediterranean trial [1]; up to 4,154 kg/ha
  under full irrigation plus 120 kg N/ha in a Sicilian trial [1]; as low
  as 720 kg/ha in the lowest-yielding of four Colombian varieties, which
  also had the trial's best oil content (Finding 1) [1]. Oil content
  ranges 35-60% across sources depending on variety and environment,
  averaging in the low-to-mid-40s-to-high-40s% band across the trials
  opened this pass. Yield and oil content do NOT move together: the same
  genotype under different nitrogen rates showed its best yield and its
  best oil content at different nitrogen levels (Finding 1), and across
  varieties the highest-yielding one was not the highest-oil one either.
- **Water use:** No single authoritative crop-coefficient (Kc) table was
  located despite a direct search. Three independent regional studies give
  differing Kc curves and stage counts, none treated as the single
  authoritative figure: a Brazilian lysimeter study (early-maturing,
  low-height cultivar) gives mean Kc of 0.74/0.93/1.00/1.18/0.92 across
  five phenological phases [10]; an Indian drip-irrigated study gives Kc
  of 0.449/0.626/0.771/0.940/0.677/0.38 across six phases (germination-
  and-establishment, vegetative, flowering, capsule development, seed
  filling, maturity) [10]; a separate Brazilian high-density-planting
  lysimeter study measured crop evapotranspiration directly over 5-12 day
  intervals against FAO Penman-Monteith reference evapotranspiration
  without reducing it to one Kc-per-stage table in the text retrieved
  [10] — all three disagree on stage count and boundaries, kept visible
  as separate regional results rather than averaged. The Indian study's
  six-phase breakdown shows flowering and capsule development as having
  the highest Kc values (0.771 and 0.940 respectively), suggesting peak
  water demand there by the curve's shape, though this is an inference
  rather than a stated stress-sensitivity finding. No seasonal total
  (mm) water-use figure was found. Root system is qualitatively described
  as "deep, extensive" (Finding 9) but no specific depth figure was
  found.
- **Soil:** No soil pH optimum, minimum, or maximum figure was found
  despite a direct, repeated search — a real, acknowledged gap. Two
  dedicated salinity-tolerance sources (a stand-establishment threshold
  study and an article specifically on castor's soil-salinity tolerance)
  were located by title but not opened in full this pass.
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n=20 (10 haploid chromosomes) [6]. Genome size in
  megabases shows a spread across sources: an older nuclear-DNA-content
  survey gives roughly 323 Mb; a 2010 draft-genome paper reports
  approximately 350 Mb from a ~4.6x assembly (325 Mb using only scaffolds
  over 2 kb); a newer reference assembly comes to approximately 315.6 Mb
  [6]. No figure was independently converted to a 1C picogram value this
  pass.
- **Cultivars:** No sourced list of specific named open-pollinated or
  hybrid cultivars with comparative trait deltas (bloom timing, yield,
  seed size, storage) at a level of detail worth a full cultivar table was
  found this pass, beyond the specific hybrid lineage already given in
  Finding 2 (GCH 2, GCH 3, GCH 4, GAUCH 1, parent lines TSP 10R, JI 15,
  VP 1). This is a real gap, not a "no variation exists" finding — castor
  shows "enormous morphological diversity" in stem colour, seed size, and
  raceme habit per general sources, but no comparative trait table for
  named varieties was located and opened this pass.
- **Hybrid production:** India's two-line pistillate × monoecious/ISF
  hybrid system (Finding 2) is described as India's dominant commercial
  castor-growing system in the sources opened, though the hybrid share was
  not independently sourced to a specific national percentage. This is
  NOT a cytoplasmic-male-sterility (CMS) system, structurally distinct
  from CMS-based hybrid systems used in some other oilseed crops
  researched for this project. Technical level needed is medium: it
  requires a maintained pistillate line (itself requiring environmentally
  sensitive ISF expression for self-multiplication), roguing discipline,
  and large isolation distances (600 m foundation / 300 m certified
  seed), but no CMS-restorer genetics or doubled-haploid/marker
  technology. What happens if F1 hybrid castor seed is saved and
  replanted was not sourced this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window per planting (one growing
  season in temperate annual production; continuous in tropical perennial
  production, not independently sourced to a specific cycle count). The
  multiple raceme-maturity passes within one season (Finding 8) are a
  harvest-pattern distinction, not separate annual windows. Hand-harvest:
  3-4 passes starting 90-120 days after planting, sun-dried, then shelled
  or beaten to free seed. Mechanized: whole-plant chemical desiccation
  (glyphosate, diquat, or similar) followed by a single combine pass,
  using purpose-built capsule-stripping headers.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed only; no transplant or nursery path
  sourced.
- **Stand life:** Perennial grown as an annual in temperate zones
  (frost-killed each year); genuinely perennial shrub in tropical
  climates (Finding 7). One year to first harvest in temperate annual
  production; productive life years for the tropical perennial path were
  not sourced this pass.

## Hazards

- **Ricin** (plant toxin, ribosome-inactivating protein): confined to
  seed meal/press-cake after oil extraction; whole intact seeds are much
  less hazardous because the hard seed coat limits release unless the
  seed is broken or chewed. The pressed oil itself is safe — ricin is
  water-soluble and does not partition into the oil during pressing
  (Finding 3). Trigger: broken, chewed, or ground seed; inadequately
  detoxified press-cake/meal fed to livestock or used as fertilizer
  without sufficient heat/pH/microbial treatment (Findings 3-4).
  Mitigation: oil pressing itself (ricin stays in the cake, not the oil);
  for the cake, heat above roughly 80°C, high pH, or microbial
  detoxification treatments before any feed use — though the allergen
  component of the meal remains hard to address even after ricin is
  deactivated (Finding 4). Residual risk is real and documented —
  veterinary case reports describe livestock poisonings from
  "insufficiently detoxified" commercial castor-cake fertilizer and from
  feed-mix substitution errors (Finding 4); this is not a risk that can be
  assumed eliminated by default, since detoxification quality varies in
  practice. No specific regulatory ricin threshold was found sourced this
  pass; one review explicitly calls for international regulation setting
  such a threshold, implying none currently exists in a form the source
  could cite. There is no antidote, and dialysis has no role in treatment
  (Finding 3). **TO BE ADDRESSED** — this is the most severe livestock/
  human-safety hazard documented across this project's oil-crop research
  so far, and a fuller write-up is deferred until this project has a place
  for this kind of health/safety data.
- **Ricinine** (plant alkaloid, milder than ricin): found in leaves and
  fruit pericarps. Trigger: ingestion of leaves or fruit pericarps,
  documented in cattle. Linked to neurological effects in the cattle cases
  reported (Finding 4). No mitigation or regulatory information was
  sourced this pass.
- Castor is not a food crop, and the oil itself — the only part of the
  plant intended for human consumption/use — is ricin-free by mechanism
  (Finding 3), so no separate human food-safety hazard applies.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → harvested (hand-passes or mechanized desiccation/
  combine) → pressed for crude oil (medicinal/industrial use; ricin-free)
  and press-cake (usable as fertilizer, or as livestock feed only after
  thorough detoxification, given the hazards above).
- The oil's unique ricinoleic-acid chemistry supports a large downstream
  industrial-chemistry chain (lithium-soap greases, nylon-11 precursor,
  polyurethane polyol, fragrance components), none of which are
  farm-level processes and so are not detailed further here (Finding 5).

## Open research questions

- No kcal/protein figure exists for castor, since it is not a food crop;
  noted here as a flag in case this project ever needs a concrete
  non-food-crop data example.
- No authoritative crop-coefficient (Kc) table was located; three
  independent regional studies give three different stage-count and
  magnitude patterns, none reconciled, and no seasonal water-use total
  was derived from them.
- No soil pH tolerance figure was found at all, a notably deeper gap
  than for some of this project's other oil crops.
- Two dedicated castor salinity-tolerance sources were found by title (a
  stand-establishment threshold study and an article specifically on
  soil-salinity tolerance) but neither was opened in full this pass — a
  gap of time/effort rather than source availability.
- No seed-viability-years or germination-rate figures were found at all.
- No F2 behaviour for saved F1 hybrid castor seed was sourced, despite
  how well-documented the rest of the hybrid-breeding system turned out
  to be.
- No seed storage-day or ambient-shelf-life figure was found for castor
  seed.
- No named open-pollinated or hybrid cultivar comparison table was found
  at a level of detail worth a full cultivar entry, despite castor being
  described in general sources as showing "enormous morphological
  diversity."
- Castor's severe ricin hazard raises a genuinely open question about
  whether an enclosed or off-world growing environment should even
  attempt this crop, and if so, under what containment discipline for
  seed handling and press-cake disposal — flagged as a design question as
  much as a research one.
- No dehulling, shelling-loss, or cake-to-oil extraction-ratio figure was
  found distinct from the oil-content percentages already given in
  Finding 1.
- No soil chemistry (boron/chloride class, micronutrient demand,
  heavy-metal accumulation), soil biology, light (DLI/light-saturation),
  or atmosphere (CO2-response) data were found.
