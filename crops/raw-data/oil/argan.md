# Argan (*Argania spinosa*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/argan.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Argania spinosa* (L.) Skeels, the sole species of its
genus, family Sapotaceae. No name conflict — "argan" always refers to this
species, endemic to a restricted region of southwestern Morocco (and a
small outlying population in Algeria). The closest other oil crop
researched for this project is oil palm: both are long-lived, slow-maturing
perennial trees whose fruit yields oil through a multi-step mechanical
chain (depulp, crack, press), both have a fruit organ whose pulp and
kernel/nut are processed separately, and both sit in semi-wild-to-managed
agroforestry systems rather than intensive monoculture. Argan differs
sharply in scale of mechanization: where oil palm's chain is industrial
(mills, hydraulic presses), argan's chain is still substantially
hand-labour, cooperative-based, and only partially mechanized even today.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — kernel oil content is highly tree-dependent, and the
measurement method matters more than any single "typical" figure
suggests.** A Moroccan study of 18 trees found average kernel oil content
of 51.9% (range 47.6-55.16%) by chemical extraction; a larger 150-tree,
5-provenance survey found a much wider spread of 38.45-62.54% [1].
Mechanical pressing of 18 genotypes gave only 37.2-43.8% extracted oil, a
lower figure reflecting extraction-method losses (pressing never recovers
100% of the chemically-extractable oil) rather than a genuinely different
tree population [1].

**Finding 2 — per-tree yield is low, and per-hectare yield is essentially
unsourced, which is unusual for a crop this commercially significant.** A
Moroccan field study found average annual production per tree of 8.62 kg
fresh fruit, 0.253 kg almonds (kernels), and 0.133 L oil; an Israeli trial
of high-yielding selections estimated 0.9 kg oil per tree for the best
performers [1]. No reliable per-hectare oil yield figure was found this
pass — one per-hectare fruit figure located (1.5-22.5 kg/ha) is almost
certainly a unit error (too low by roughly three orders of magnitude for a
tree crop) and was not relied upon. Tree density in traditional,
semi-wild argan forest stands is itself not well characterised in the
sources opened.

**Finding 3 — the traditional processing chain historically ran straight
through a goat's digestive tract, and that practice is now actively
discouraged.** In parts of Morocco, goats were allowed to climb and browse
argan trees directly, and kernels were later recovered from their
droppings for pressing [2]. This practice has sharply declined: modern
cooperatives, which now produce much of the commercial oil, avoid it for
hygiene, traceability, and (per one source) odor reasons — "oil made with
goats smells like goats" [2]. Separately, overbrowsing by goats, sheep,
and camels is a major driver of the argan forest's own decline (Finding
12), so the same animal plays both a historical processing role and a
present-day conservation threat, for different reasons.

**Finding 4 — depulping is a real, distinct step before cracking, not a
detail folded into harvest.** Once fruit is dried, the fibrous pulp is
removed by hand to expose the hard nut, which is then cracked separately
to free the kernel(s) inside [2][5].

**Finding 5 — nut-cracking, not pressing, is the labour bottleneck of the
entire chain, and it has resisted mechanization.** Shell-cracking is done
by hand with round stones, and is repeatedly described in sourced accounts
as the single most laborious step in argan oil production; attempts to
mechanize it are reported to have failed [5]. Several labour estimates for
the full traditional chain cluster in the 15-24 hour range per litre of
finished oil, though the sources (largely blogs, a student feasibility
thesis, and marketing pages, not peer-reviewed) disagree on exact figures
and are lower-confidence [5]. One account gives roughly 100 kg of dried
fruit and 15 hours of hand labour yielding about 60 kg of nuts, converting
to about 6.5 kg of kernels [5] — consistent with a low overall
fruit-to-kernel mass fraction. By contrast, semi-mechanical pressing
(after cracking) is reported to cut the pressing step itself from about 20
hours to about 2 hours per litre, though traditional hand-pressing is
credited with a higher-tocopherol finished oil [5].

**Finding 6 — roasting is the single step that separates culinary from
cosmetic-grade oil, and it is a real compositional, not just flavour,
distinction.** Cosmetic-grade oil is cold-pressed from unroasted kernel,
typically deodorised for a near-scentless product; culinary-grade oil is
pressed from kernel roasted over fire, giving the traditional Moroccan
table oil used in amlou (a dip of roasted almonds, argan oil, and honey,
ground with stones) [3][5].

**Finding 7 — composition is fairly consistently sourced, but the
health-benefit evidence is thinner than popular claims suggest.**
Peer-reviewed analysis gives oleic acid (~43%) and linoleic acid (~36%) as
the dominant fatty acids, with γ-tocopherol averaging 483 mg/kg as the
principal tocopherol, alongside polyphenols, sterols, squalene, and
triterpene alcohols [4]. The strongest human evidence found is a Moroccan
study of regular virgin-oil consumers showing lower plasma lipoperoxides
and higher alpha-tocopherol levels than non-consumers, with the authors
suggesting the oil's minor components (not just its fatty-acid profile)
may be responsible [4]. Broader claims about cardiovascular or diabetes
benefit rest on small human or animal studies and are explicitly flagged
by at least one secondary source as needing larger trials.

**Finding 8 — the tree is exceptionally long-lived and slow to reach
useful production, with wide-ranging lifespan figures that are not
resolved to one number.** An FAO monograph gives a productive lifespan of
125-150 years, while a separate preprint source gives up to 150-200 years
[6]. Trees begin fruiting around 5-6 years from seed, but do not reach
maximum production until roughly age 60 [6] — one of the longest
juvenile-to-peak-production spans in this project's crop research so far.
Trees can also skip a fruiting year entirely under drought stress [6], an
alternate-bearing-like behaviour driven by water deficit rather than by a
fixed biennial cycle.

**Finding 9 — the tree carries a documented, if mechanistically
unidentified, human skin-contact hazard.** Multiple published case reports
describe allergic contact dermatitis from topical argan oil use (scalp
psoriasis treatment, decorative face makeup removal), and at least one
anaphylaxis case has been reported from argan oil exposure [7]. The
specific allergen responsible has not been identified in any of the
sourced case reports. This is a different clinical mechanism (allergic
contact dermatitis, generally Type IV) than a food-ingestion allergy (Type
I), even though both are immune-mediated.

**Finding 10 — argan oil's high price makes it one of the world's most
adulterated edible/cosmetic oils, and the adulteration problem is itself
well-studied.** One survey of 28 products labelled "100% argan oil" found
four failed authenticity testing, most likely blended with cheaper
vegetable oil; sunflower oil is identified as the most common adulterant
given its low cost and availability, with soy and hazelnut oil also
reported [8]. A 2003 Moroccan quality standard exists specifically to
protect producers and consumers against counterfeit product, and a
substantial analytical-chemistry literature (NMR, HPLC, infrared, and
fluorescence spectroscopy) has been developed purely to detect
adulteration, including one 2023 method sensitive to adulterant levels as
low as 0.35% [8].

**Finding 11 — the press-cake byproduct carries a real but non-acute
antinutritional factor (saponins).** Argan press cake (33% DM protein from
mechanical extraction, higher from solvent extraction, with 7-25% DM
residual oil) contains roughly 4% saponins, which make it bitter and
astringent, reducing feed intake and impairing protein and mineral/vitamin
uptake [9]. A specialist feed database holds that these saponins do not
harm ruminants directly and pass out in the urine, and notes that early
reports linking saponins in milk to child diarrhoea have not been
confirmed by later work [9]. Detoxification (soaking/boiling, or treatment
with sodium bicarbonate or citric acid) is reported to substantially cut
saponin content and to raise milk yield in a goat feeding trial by about
24% versus untreated cake [9].

**Finding 12 — the species' own conservation status is itself
agronomically relevant, since the same forest area is both the crop's
production base and a UNESCO biosphere reserve under active decline
pressure.** The Moroccan argan forest has been part of UNESCO's biosphere
reserve network since 1998, but press and academic coverage describe it as
having shrunk by roughly 40% from a historical extent of over 14,000 km²,
driven by overgrazing (goats, sheep, camels), firewood/charcoal
extraction, clearing for commercial agriculture (including competing olive
cultivation), and prolonged regional drought [10]. A 2021 remote-sensing
study found mean tree density per hectare actually rose between 1967 and
2019, but its authors argue this masks real degradation — more, smaller,
weaker trees rather than genuine recovery [10]. The traditional Agdal
rotational-grazing system is cited as a better-balanced management
approach than unrestricted browsing [10]. This is an environmental/
land-management reality rather than a plant-toxin or food-safety hazard.

**Finding 13 — the genome-size literature is a single, very recent
measurement with no second study to cross-check it against.** A 2023
Botany Letters study reports 2C = 1.48 pg (1C ≈ 723.72 Mbp) across both
accessions studied — described by its authors as the first nuclear DNA
content measurement for the species, notably small for the genus's
chromosome count (2n = 2x = 20), which the authors suggest may reflect a
dysploidy process within Sapotaceae [11]. This is the opposite situation to
some other crops' own genome-size conflicts (two disagreeing figures): a
single, recent, methodologically clean figure with no competing
measurement to check it against.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen, thorny, long-lived perennial tree;
  perennial bearing pattern.
- **Time to first fruiting:** About 5-6 years from seed [6].
- **Climate:** Restricted to a narrow transitional arid/semi-arid zone of
  southwestern Morocco (plus a small Algerian outlier population) —
  unusually narrow among oil crops researched for this project [10].
- **Composition:** Crude oil is essentially pure fat; oleic acid (~43%)
  and linoleic acid (~36%) dominate the fatty-acid profile, with
  γ-tocopherol averaging 483 mg/kg [4]. Culinary-grade oil is eaten as
  pressed (amlou, table oil); cosmetic-grade oil is not intended for
  consumption.
- **Pollination:** Insect-pollinated (entomophilic), allogamous, and
  mostly self-incompatible — hand- and free self-pollination trials both
  gave only about 0.2% fruit set, versus up to 5.3% from cross-pollination
  in the same trial [1]. Orchard-design guidance from the same source
  recommends alternating pollinizer rows. Whether the floral system is
  better described as monoecious or hermaphrodite with strong
  self-incompatibility was not resolved in the sources opened.
- **Yield:** No reliable per-hectare figure (Finding 2). Per-tree figures
  only: 8.62 kg fresh fruit, 0.253 kg kernel, 0.133 L oil per tree per
  year in one Moroccan study; 0.9 kg oil/tree for high-yielding
  selections in an Israeli trial [1]. Kernel oil content ranges 37-62%
  depending on tree, provenance, and extraction method (Finding 1) — wide
  tree-to-tree and provenance variability is itself the headline finding,
  not noise to average away.
- **Water use / drought response:** A dimorphic root system — a deep
  taproot reaching about 4 m in the core of the species' main range in one
  study, against an older cross-species tree-rooting-depth maximum
  estimate of about 7 m — plus shallow lateral roots for near-surface
  nutrient uptake [13]. Severe drought triggers leaf shedding as a
  transpiration-reducing response, and root hydraulic conductivity
  measurably decreases as drought intensifies [13]. No FAO-56-style crop
  coefficient (Kc) table, stage-length figures, or measured seasonal
  water-use figure were found sourced for the species. One domesticated
  orchard trial (Laâyoune, Morocco) irrigated with saline water reports
  soil pH 7.95 and irrigation-water electrical conductivity 16.45 dS/m
  (soil classed moderately saline, irrigation water classed highly
  saline) [14], with earlier work (cited within that source, not
  independently verified) suggesting the species can tolerate water
  salinity at germination and early growth stages. Drought tolerance is
  judged High and waterlogging tolerance Low based on the converging
  taproot/climate/leaf-shedding evidence, though this is a qualitative
  judgement call rather than a measured index.
- **Propagation:** Direct seed (low germination, about 25% in one
  Moroccan trial, attributed mainly to hard-seed-coat dormancy), stem
  cuttings, or grafting; apical split grafting in autumn is reported as
  the most successful and most widely used commercial method [6]. No
  dedicated seed-orchard or seed-yield-per-hectare figure was found; seed,
  cuttings, and graft material appear to be taken from ordinary
  production or wild-stand trees. No F1 hybrid seed system exists for this
  species.
- **Stand life:** Productive lifespan given as 125-150 years by one FAO
  monograph and up to 150-200 years by a separate preprint source, not
  resolved to one figure (Finding 8) [6]. Maximum production is not
  reached until about age 60 [6]. No named end-of-life agronomic decline
  cause or pruning regime was found, unlike some other tree crops
  researched for this project.
- **Soil / site:** No sourced pH tolerance band, nutrient uptake figures,
  or nitrogen-fixation data (not a nitrogen-fixing species). The single
  Laâyoune trial's pH and salinity figures are site-specific observations,
  not a formal tolerance class.
- **Postharvest storage:** Fruit is sun-dried promptly after gathering,
  then depulped and cracked; no cold-chain regime was sourced. Cracked,
  dried kernels are reported (not independently confirmed) to store and
  ship reasonably, similar to other tree-nut oilseeds, though no specific
  shelf-life figure was found.
- **Labor:** No per-hectare labor figures were found; sourced figures are
  per-litre-of-oil and themselves lower-confidence (blog/thesis-level, not
  peer-reviewed). Cracking is repeatedly singled out as the dominant
  labor share of the traditional chain. Much of today's commercial
  processing is organized through women's cooperatives, a well-documented
  socioeconomic feature of the crop.
- **Harvest pattern:** Single annual summer fruiting season; fruit is
  hand-picked or gathered once fallen. No sourced evidence of multiple
  separate fruiting flushes per year.
- **Genome:** 2C = 1.48 pg (1C ≈ 723.72 Mbp), karyotype 2n = 2x = 20, per
  the single 2023 flow-cytometry study available (Finding 13) [11].
- **Controlled-environment viability:** No sourced precedent — a
  slow-growing, multi-decade, deep-taprooted dryland tree with a juvenile
  period of 5-6 years and peak production not reached until about age 60
  is a poor fit for hydroponic, enclosed, or off-world growing systems; no
  spaceflight or controlled-environment trial was found or expected.

## Hazards

- Documented human skin-contact allergy (allergic contact dermatitis, and
  at least one reported anaphylaxis case) from topical use of argan oil;
  the specific allergen has not been identified in the sourced case
  reports (Finding 9). **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this
  contact-hazard class is deferred until this project has a place for
  this kind of health/safety data.
- Press-cake saponins (~4% of press cake), reducing feed palatability/
  digestibility but not reported as acutely toxic to ruminants;
  detoxification (soaking/boiling, sodium bicarbonate, or citric acid
  treatment) improves palatability and milk yield in feeding trials
  (Finding 11). **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of livestock-feed
  antinutritional factors is deferred for the same reason.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** whole fruit → sun-dried → depulped (pulp discarded, nut
  kept) → shell cracked (kernel freed) → kernel pressed either raw
  (cosmetic-grade oil) or after roasting (culinary-grade oil); press cake
  is a byproduct usable as livestock feed after saponin detoxification.
- Argan's two-grade-from-one-input pattern (same organ, different
  preparation producing two distinct commodities) is a notable structural
  feature; by comparison, other crops in this project with a seed-vs-leaf
  or similar split instead separate by organ rather than by preparation.

## Open research questions

- Per-hectare yield figures are essentially unsourced (Finding 2): only
  per-tree figures were found, and no reliable tree-density-in-
  traditional-stands figure exists to convert them to a per-hectare
  basis.
- No quantified yield benefit for grafted versus seedling trees was
  found, despite grafting being described as the dominant commercial
  propagation method.
- The species' floral sexual system is not resolved — sources variously
  describe it as "allogamous monoecious" and, separately, in terms
  implying self-incompatible hermaphrodite flowers.
- No water-use figure of any kind (not even a rough one) was found for
  the species, unlike some other tree crops researched for this project
  where at least a rough per-plant daily water-use figure existed.
- The documented salinity tolerance (Laâyoune trial) is a genuinely
  unusual degree of sourced salt tolerance for a tree crop, but has not
  been reduced to a formal salinity-tolerance class.
- The goat-browsed/goat-dropping kernel-recovery pathway (Finding 3) is a
  real, historically documented but declining traditional practice,
  worth a closer look if this project ever wants to model unusual
  animal-mediated processing steps.
- Argan forest decline and UNESCO biosphere-reserve status (Finding 12)
  is a genuinely significant land-management and conservation topic
  tightly bound up with the crop's own production base; a strong
  candidate for this project's wild/dual-mode flora research, given that
  much of current production still comes from semi-wild, protected
  traditional forest stands rather than planted monoculture orchards.
- No sourced list of named commercial cultivars or breeding lines was
  found — argan is still substantially a semi-wild/traditional-forest-
  stand crop rather than a cultivar-bred one, with domestication/
  selection trials (e.g. the Israeli high-yield-selection trial) still an
  active research area.
- No sourced soil chemistry (boron/chloride tolerance class, micronutrient
  demand, heavy-metal accumulation), soil biology, light/shade-tolerance,
  or atmosphere/CO2-response data were found.

## Estimates and derivations

No DERIVED values were formally computed in this synthesis; the source
material's own estimate/analogy framework (converting a 5-6 year
juvenile period to a day count, carrying over a generic vegetable-oil
calorie figure, and judgement-call drought/waterlogging tolerance ratings)
is reflected above in the Agronomic data and Open research questions
sections rather than restated as a separate derivation list, since none of
it rests on a reusable formula — each is either a direct unit conversion
or a qualitative judgement call clearly flagged as such in Research
findings and Agronomic data.
