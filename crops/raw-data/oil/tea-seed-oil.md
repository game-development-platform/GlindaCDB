# Tea-Seed Oil (*Camellia oleifera*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-30

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Camellia oleifera* Abel. (oil-tea camellia; also
"tea oil camellia," "oiltea-camellia"), a distinct species from the
tea-leaf plant *Camellia sinensis* but the same genus. Despite the name
overlap, this crop has nothing to do with tea leaves or caffeine; it is
grown purely for the oil pressed from its seed. Argan and shea butter are
useful comparison crops: all three are slow-maturing, long-lived
perennial trees whose seed/kernel is cracked/hulled and pressed for a
high-value oil, with a genuine byproduct-recovery story in the press-
cake. Tea-seed oil's own distinguishing story is its exceptionally high
oleic-acid content (rivaling or exceeding olive oil) and a hexaploid
genome — the only polyploid tree oilseed this project has researched
(camelina was the only other polyploid oil crop this project has
researched, an annual allohexaploid).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — propagation and time-to-bearing figures are genuinely
disputed and use mismatched units, a familiar pattern from argan and shea
but with a wider raw-figure spread.** Seed-grown trees are reported to
begin bearing fruit about 8 years from planting and remain "highly
productive" for up to 80 years [1]. Seed propagation requires cold
storage (0-2°C for 15-30 days) before sowing; elite-selection stem
cuttings have been tested and rooted under mist, but are not described as
the dominant commercial route [1]. A distinct, separate propagation story
is high-crown grafting used as a REHABILITATION technique on already-
standing, low-yielding wild/naturalized stands (not a planting-stage
choice): one study found grafted scions flowered and set fruit by the
3rd year after high-grafting, with higher oil yields by the 4th year
[1]. Historical per-area yield in China was very low for a long time
(37.5-86.85 kg oil/ha), with newer high-yield cultivar selections
reaching 525-1125 kg oil/ha [1] — a roughly 10-15x yield-improvement
range from selection breeding alone, a larger sourced improvement factor
than this project has found for any other oil crop. No reliable per-tree
kg figure was found, the same gap this project has now found repeatedly
for argan and shea.

**Finding 2 — the genome is hexaploid, the only polyploid tree oilseed
this project has researched.** Cultivated oil-tea camellia is 2n = 6x =
90 chromosomes [7]. A diploid wild relative is considered the ancestor
of the hexaploid cultivated form, with a genome of about 2.95 Gb, ~76%
repetitive sequence, and high heterozygosity [7]. The hexaploid genome
itself "resisted full decoding" for a long time because of its high
ploidy, repetitiveness, and large size, only being chromosome-scale-
assembled as recently as 2025 (via long-read sequencing plus chromosome-
conformation data) [7]. Genome size tends to increase across the wider
genus, plausibly from frequent polyploidization events — this species
sits at the high end of that trend [7].

**Finding 3 — seed oil content is sourced at two different,
unreconciled figures depending on measurement basis.** Kernel
composition is reported as roughly 25-35% oil, 10-16% tea saponin, and
15-19% crude protein (patent background literature) [2]; a separate
cultivar study found WHOLE DRIED SEED oil content averaging 47.83%
across ten new cultivars, versus 47.06% for wild seed [1] — both figures
are kept rather than reconciled, likely reflecting kernel-only vs.
whole-seed measurement bases.

**Finding 4 — pollination biology is unusually well-characterized
mechanistically, more so than most oil crops this project has
researched.** *C. oleifera* is obligately cross-pollinated and self-
incompatible, with the self-sterility specifically attributed to a
PREZYGOTIC LATE-ACTING mechanism — lower ovule penetration and double-
fertilization rates in selfed ovules than in cross-pollinated ones,
rather than a simpler pre-fertilization barrier [6]. A separate review
reports self-pollination fruit set of only 3.26% under natural
conditions, versus presumably much higher cross-pollination fruit set
(the exact cross figure was not given in the source opened) [6].
Flowering is autumn-winter, insect-pollinated [6]. Flower and fruit DROP
(not just low fruit set) is described as a key bottleneck limiting yield
in recent cultivation [5] — a related but distinct yield constraint from
the self-incompatibility finding.

**Finding 5 — the oil is exceptionally high-oleic, among the highest of
any oil this project has researched, with a genuine culinary identity as
"eastern olive oil."** The most reliable sourced figures put oleic acid
at 82-84% of the fatty-acid profile [4], with a wider but less
rigorously sourced 75-85% range reported elsewhere [4] — either figure
exceeds typical olive oil's oleic range and is markedly higher than shea
butter's own already-high but more variable oleic content (37-55% for
West African shea). This gives the oil greater heat stability than most
conventional cooking oils, and it is widely used for cooking in China,
especially Hunan Province, where it is sometimes called "eastern olive
oil" [4]. It also has well-documented cosmetic/dermopharmaceutical use
(proven effective in emulsions) and industrial uses (soap, margarine,
hair oil, lubricants, paint) [4] — a three-way food/cosmetic/industrial
use spread comparable in breadth to argan's own cosmetic/culinary split,
but without argan's same-kernel-two-grades structure (tea-seed oil is
one oil serving all three markets, not two preparation-dependent
grades).

**Finding 6 — the press-cake's tea saponin is a genuine, currently
under-exploited co-product.** Traditional (and even much modern)
processing simply discards the saponin-bearing cake as manure or burns it
as fuel [2]. China produces more than 690,000 tonnes of this residue
every year, with most of it still wasted this way rather than processed
for its saponin [2]. Several competing extraction technologies exist
with very different reported yields — ethanol extraction of cake gave
about 14.2% saponin recovery under optimized conditions; a water-based
precipitation method for SHELLS (not cake) reported up to 94.66%
saponin extraction; subcritical water extraction recovered both oil
(94.07%) and saponin (74.21%) simultaneously; a microwave-puffing-plus-
enzymatic pretreatment raised OIL extraction yield from 53% to 95% [2].

**Finding 7 — anthracnose is a severe, named disease threat, more
precisely characterized than most pest/disease findings this project has
sourced for oil crops.** A 2023 review calls anthracnose the primary
disease of *C. oleifera*, capable of causing severe fruit drop, bud
loss, branch-tip dieback, and even whole-plant decline, with flower-and-
fruit drop rates reported to rise to 70-80% in disease cases (a separate
paper puts yield loss at 50%+) [3]. The causal pathogen's identity is
itself disputed between sources: a recent review names one fungal
species as the predominant pathogen across Chinese growing regions,
while older work attributes the disease mainly to a different, related
fungal species [3] — kept as an open disagreement rather than resolved.
Resistance varies meaningfully by cultivar/variety, with resistant
selections showing stronger defensive-enzyme activity after inoculation
[3].

**Finding 8 — climate suitability is narrower and more precisely mapped
to named Chinese provinces than for most other oil crops this project
has researched.** A national climate-suitability study found the most
suitable area (~98×10⁴ km²) concentrated in four named provinces, with
one holding the largest suitable area and a second the next largest [8];
a separate 2020 climate-modeling study under future climate-change
scenarios found three provinces (including those two) most suitable
[8]. A regional soil trial found acidic purple soil most favorable for
growth, ranking acidic purple soil > red soil > alkaline purple soil
[8] — the only sourced soil-type comparison found, though no numeric
soil pH optimum could be confirmed from a reliable source (a
commercially-oriented blog's pH 5.0-6.5 figure was found but explicitly
NOT relied upon, since it could not be cross-checked against a primary
agronomic source).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Long-lived evergreen perennial tree, grown in
  orchards, naturalized stands, and wild/semi-wild populations [1].
- **Climate:** Temperate, subtropical. Concentrated in a cluster of
  named Chinese provinces — humid subtropical climate [8].
- **Food status:** Widely used as a cooking oil in China, especially
  Hunan Province (Finding 5). Essentially pure fat; no sourced calorie
  figure specific to this oil, though pressed tree-seed oils generally
  cluster around 8700-9000 kcal/kg.
- **Pollination:** Obligately cross-pollinated, self-incompatible — a
  prezygotic late-acting mechanism (lower ovule penetration and double-
  fertilization in selfed vs. crossed ovules), one of the more
  mechanistically well-characterized self-incompatibility stories among
  oil crops this project has researched (Finding 4). Natural (self)
  fruit set is only 3.26%. Pollinators are insects, unspecified species;
  autumn-winter flowering is itself unusual timing for insect
  pollination. No managed-hive system was described in the sources
  opened.
- **Yield / composition:** Good yield 525-750 kg oil/ha (newer high-
  yield selections); exceptional yield up to 1125 kg oil/ha (top
  reported figure); poor yield 37.5-86.85 kg oil/ha (historical Chinese
  average "for a long time") [1]. No reliable per-tree kg figure was
  found, the same gap this project found for argan and shea. Seed oil
  content is 25-35% of kernel per patent literature, or ~47-48% of whole
  dried seed per one cultivar study — both figures kept, unreconciled
  (Finding 3). The roughly 10-15x yield-improvement factor from cultivar
  selection breeding alone is itself a headline finding, a larger
  sourced improvement ratio than found for any other oil crop this
  project has researched.
- **Water use:** No crop-coefficient figure was found for this crop in
  the sources opened this pass. Flowering and fruit-set (autumn-winter)
  are likely the most yield-sensitive period, given the crop's already-
  low natural fruit-set rate and a separately documented flower/fruit-
  drop bottleneck in recent cultivation; no sourced drought-specific
  yield-loss figure exists for this stage. Drought tolerance is likely
  medium, inferred from its humid-subtropical native range, which does
  not suggest strong drought adaptation the way argan's or shea's dry-
  savanna/semi-arid ranges do. Waterlogging tolerance is likely low,
  inferred from its documented preference for well-drained acidic soils.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed (cold-stored 0-2°C for 15-30 days
  before sowing) or grafting (either as ordinary planting-stage
  propagation, or as a rehabilitation technique applied to existing low-
  yield wild/naturalized stands via high-crown grafting) [1].
- **Soil:** No reliably sourced pH optimum was found (a commercial blog's
  pH 5.0-6.5 figure was found but explicitly not relied upon, lacking a
  primary-source cross-check). A regional field trial found acidic
  purple soil most favorable for growth, ranking acidic purple soil >
  red soil > alkaline purple soil — the clearest sourced soil-preference
  signal, even without a numeric pH optimum [8].
- **Stand life:** Years to first harvest: about 8 (seed-grown); 3-4
  years post-graft for rehabilitative high-crown grafting on existing
  stands (not a planting-stage figure) [1]. Productive lifespan: up to
  80 years [1].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Anthracnose (Finding 7) is the primary
  documented disease threat, with disputed pathogen identity between
  sources.
- **Genome:** Hexaploid, 2n=90 (Finding 2). A diploid wild relative's
  genome size (about 2.95 Gb) scaled by ploidy (6x vs. 2x = 3x) gives an
  estimated hexaploid genome of roughly 8.85 Gb, though this assumes
  simple ploidy-proportional scaling and has not been confirmed against
  a direct hexaploid genome-size measurement — the actual 2025
  chromosome-scale assembly's total size was not given in the sources
  opened this pass, only its sequencing method.
- **Cultivars:** A cultivar-selection breeding program has clearly been
  active (the ~10-15x per-hectare yield-improvement figure is explicitly
  attributed to newer high-yield selections), but no named, individually-
  described commercial cultivar list was found sourced this pass beyond
  a reference to "ten new cultivars" in one oil-content study and one
  named cultivar used for the reference genome assembly. **Wild/
  unselected seed stock**: historical baseline, still widespread, common
  especially in naturalized/semi-wild stands; historically very low oil
  yield per hectare (37.5-86.85 kg oil/ha); wild seed oil content
  (47.06% in one study) close to but slightly below newer cultivar seed
  (47.83%) [1]. **Newer high-yield cultivar selections**: modern
  breeding selections, increasingly planted, driving the sourced yield
  improvement; per-hectare oil yield 525-1125 kg/ha, a roughly 10-15x
  improvement over historical wild-stand yields; slightly higher average
  whole-dried-seed oil content [1].
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one annual autumn fruiting
  season per the sources opened this pass; no sourced evidence of
  multiple separate fruiting flushes per year.

## Hazards

No food-safety hazard was found sourced this pass for the oil itself;
the one genuine hazard concern (tea saponin) affects the PRESS-CAKE
byproduct (feed/environmental disposal), not the finished oil, which is a
long-established, widely-eaten cooking oil in China with no sourced
toxicity concern.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fresh capsule → hulled/dehusked → dried → pressed
  (traditional hot-press or modern cold-press) → crude oil (cooking oil
  in China, especially Hunan Province; cosmetic/dermopharmaceutical use;
  industrial uses including soap, margarine, hair oil, lubricants, paint)
  + press cake (saponin-bearing; traditionally discarded as manure or
  burned as fuel, though several extraction technologies exist to
  recover tea saponin, currently practiced only on a small minority of
  China's total cake residue, Finding 6).
- Traditional processing (screening, drying, shelling, crushing, steaming
  the powder, packing into cakes, pressing) is described as the
  conventional prior-art sequence in patent literature, with cold
  pressing as a quality-preserving modern alternative.

## Open research questions

- Oil-content figures (kernel 25-35% vs. whole dried seed 47-48%) likely
  reflect different measurement bases rather than genuine disagreement,
  but this was not confirmed from a source directly comparing the two
  bases.
- Self-incompatibility vs. flower/fruit-drop are related but distinct
  yield constraints (Finding 4); whether the two share a common cause or
  are independent constraints was not addressed in the sources opened.
- No reliable soil pH optimum was found, despite how well-studied this
  crop otherwise is.
- Cross-compatibility with diploid wild relatives or other *Camellia*
  species was not addressed in the sources opened — relevant to any
  future genetic-diversity question.
- The hexaploid genome size is estimated by ploidy-scaling from a
  diploid relative, not directly sourced; the actual 2025 chromosome-
  scale assembly's total size (which would supersede this estimate
  entirely) was not given in the sources opened this pass.
- The press-cake saponin-extraction technologies (Finding 6) vary widely
  in reported yield across different extraction methods and are not
  reconciled to one preferred method.
- No named commercial cultivars with per-cultivar trait deltas were
  found, despite clear evidence of active breeding/selection.
- No per-tree yield figure of any kind was found; only per-hectare
  figures were sourced (both historical and newer-cultivar), the same
  gap this project has now found for argan, shea, and tea-seed oil — a
  recurring pattern across tree-oilseed crops this project has
  researched.

## Estimates and derivations

The hexaploid genome-size estimate (~8.85 Gb) is derived from the
diploid relative's genome size scaled by ploidy (3x), not an independent
measurement, and is flagged as such above. The drought/waterlogging-
tolerance ratings and generic C3 photosynthesis placeholder are
judgement calls flagged as such in Research findings and Agronomic data
above, rather than formula-based derivations. No other DERIVED values
are restated here as reusable formulas.
