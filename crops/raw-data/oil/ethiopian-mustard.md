# Ethiopian Mustard (*Brassica carinata*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/ethiopian-mustard.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Brassica carinata*, also called Abyssinian mustard,
Abyssinian cabbage, Texsel greens, or by its Ethiopian names gomenzer/
habesha gomen (Amharic) and raafuu (Oromo). It is its own allotetraploid
species (genome BcBcCcCc), distinct from other mustard-family oilseed
crops this project may research (which are different species and
genomes).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content and seed yield both vary considerably by
genotype, altitude, nitrogen rate, and climate, and the crop is reported
to carry LESS oil than its oilseed-Brassica relatives, not more.** An
Ethiopian pot study of 11 genotypes at three altitudes found the highest
oil percentage, 41.79%, in genotype "yellow dodolla," and concluded
high-to-mid altitudes maximize both seed and oil yield [1]. A 2025
germplasm evaluation of 49 accessions at two central-Ethiopian sites
found oil content was negatively associated with both protein and
glucosinolate content, and identified four accessions (17545, 21373,
24203, 24495) performing consistently well across multiple traits at both
sites [2]. Older Indian agronomic work found seed yield was highest at 30
cm × 10 cm spacing (222,222 plants/ha), and that oil content fell
significantly with each successive increase in nitrogen rate up to 90
kg/ha [3]. A Saskatchewan variety trial reported *B. carinata* seed has
HIGHER protein and LOWER oil and crude fibre content than *B. napus*
(canola) or *B. rapa* seed [4]; an Indian review places Ethiopian
mustard's oil content 3-6 percentage points below Indian mustard's (*B.
juncea*) [5]. Across these sources oil content ranges roughly 32-42%,
distinctly lower than some other oilseed crops' ranges this project has
researched. No single world-average yield figure was located this pass;
the Saskatchewan trial's own conclusion — that none of its 11 *B.
carinata* lines was adapted for immediate production on the Canadian
prairies — is itself a useful data point about the crop's climate fit, not
a universal failure.

**Finding 2 — the crop's erucic acid and glucosinolate content are
explicitly why it has historically lost out to rapeseed/canola as a food
oil in its own homeland, even though newer breeding has reduced both
traits.** In Ethiopia, Ethiopian mustard is grown as both an oilseed and
leaf-vegetable crop, but "its oil has drawbacks: it has high levels of
undesirable glucosinolates and erucic acid, which is why rapeseed is
often preferred for oil" [6]. A plant-information sheet states older and
wild types carry high erucic acid and glucosinolate levels, with newer
breeding having reduced them [7]. A 2008 Czech review names high erucic
acid and high meal glucosinolate content as the crop's main drawbacks,
set against real agronomic strengths [8]. A 2021 sulfur-fertility study
found glucosinolates protect the plant from pests and disease, but reduce
palatability and feed value when the meal is used as a protein source,
and that limiting sulfur fertility lowers glucosinolate content but can
also cut seed yield — a genuine three-way tradeoff between plant defence,
feed safety, and yield [9]. A feed-industry article reports the meal has
roughly 40-42% crude protein, and that its glucosinolate toxicity in
animals works through thiocyanate interfering with thyroid function, with
symptoms similar to those documented for other Brassica oilseed meals
[10].

**Finding 3 — Ethiopian mustard is a genuine dual-use crop in its
homeland: oilseed AND leaf vegetable from the same plant.** "At its
earlier stages of development the leaf is used as vegetable either by
thinning or topping, and seed can also be used" [11]. The plant is grown
as a leaf vegetable with a mild flavor, known as habesha gomen (Amharic)
or raafuu (Oromo); commercial leaf production remains small-scale but is
"slowly gaining popularity in rural as well as urban areas" [11]. No
sourced data on whether leaf thinning measurably reduces the subsequent
seed yield, or how commercial Ethiopian growers actually schedule the two
harvests against each other, was found this pass.

**Finding 4 — no commercial hybrid seed system was confirmed for
Ethiopian mustard, despite genuine hybrid-breeding research activity at
one major seed company.** A US patent document describes *B. carinata*
as self-compatible, "with no genetic or physiological barriers to
selfing" [12] — patent language, not a peer-reviewed measurement, so kept
labelled as such. One seed company (which acquired carinata germplasm,
patents, and regulatory data from an earlier developer) has described
carinata hybrid-breeding research at its Saskatchewan facility, and
offered a hybrid product in Europe with a "pilot launch and a full
rollout in autumn 2024" — but the same reporting notes there were, at the
time, no commercial carinata acres in Canada at all [13]. Named
commercial varieties with US patents (AGR044-M01, DH-129.B036,
AGR159-4A1A, DH-20.141, AGR044-312E) are all described as open-pollinated
lines, not hybrids [13][14]. Whether the European hybrid rollout proceeded
as planned, and what breeding system (CMS, chemical hybridization, or
otherwise) it uses, were not confirmed this pass — a genuinely open
question rather than a confirmed absence.

**Finding 5 — the crop is an allotetraploid completing the classic
"Triangle of U" set of Brassica genomes, and is reported more resistant
to pod shattering than other oilseed Brassicas, though this varies by
accession.** *B. carinata* is BcBcCcCc, 2n = 34, formed by hybridization
of the B-genome (*B. nigra*, BB, 2n = 16) and C-genome (*B. oleracea*,
CC, 2n = 18) diploid species; a 2022 chromosome-scale assembly reports a
1.31 Gbp reference genome, completing the three classic allotetraploids
of the Triangle of U alongside canola (*B. napus*, AACC) and *B. juncea*
(AABB), both separately significant oilseed crops in their own right [15].
A 2017 study screened 83 *B. carinata* accessions by pod rupture energy
(a direct measure of pod strength) and reported quantitative trait loci
for shatter resistance [16]; a 2024 companion study identified QTL and
candidate genes for the same trait, linking it to pod length, and states
researchers generally report *B. carinata* as MORE shatter-resistant than
other oilseed Brassicas overall, though individual accessions vary widely
[17]. This is a genuinely different genome architecture from some other
Brassica oilseed crops' own allotetraploid or hexaploid origins — all sit
at different corners of the same historical hybridization triangle, or
outside it entirely.

**Finding 6 — Ethiopian mustard has a real, certified role as a
sustainable-aviation-fuel feedstock with actual passenger-flight
history.** Qantas flew a Los Angeles-Melbourne flight in 2018 on biofuel
made from *B. carinata* seed, developed by a biosciences company, tested
against ASTM D7566 and D1655 specifications with a maximum 50% biofuel
blend permitted [18]. United Airlines and partners flew a transatlantic
Boeing 787 flight using a 30% carinata-seed-oil biojet blend [18].
Carinata is described as "the first oilseed to be certified sustainable
by the Roundtable on Sustainable Biomaterials (RSB)" [18]. One seed
company's stated production target is Argentina, where an earlier
developer ran an 18-month pilot project; carinata there is drilled on
marginal land taken out of food-crop production, or grown as a cover crop
between main cash crops [19]. One (undated, so treated cautiously) news
source gives area figures of roughly 50,000 ha in Brazil, 30,000 ha in
Argentina, and 6,600 ha in Uruguay [19]. The seed company itself markets
carinata as having 42-45% "inedible oil" in winter environments as a
second-generation biofuel feedstock, with no stated intention of building
its own crushing plant [20]. No 2025-2026 confirmation of current
purchasing or supply volumes was found; the 42-45% marketing figure is
kept visible as a separate, higher claim rather than folded into Finding
1's sourced 32-42% range, since it comes from a company selling its own
bred lines rather than an independent trial.

**Finding 7 — the crop shows a genuinely useful disease/pest-resistance
and drought/heat-tolerance profile for an oilseed Brassica, with blackleg
resistance specifically strong enough to be used as a breeding donor for
canola.** Carinata-derived blackleg resistance is described as "superior
to other sources of resistance available in B. napus," and has been moved
into canola via interspecific crosses; a university research project
begun in 2007 studied carinata-derived doubled-haploid lines against
different blackleg pathotypes [21]. A government source states research
found carinata "more resistant to some pests, such as blackleg and
aphids, than other oilseed crops" [22] — a general claim without
supporting quantitative data, kept labelled as such. A seed-company page
lists Alternaria, Sclerotinia, and Phoma as the crop's main diseases and
flea beetle and pollen beetle as its main pests, without giving
resistance figures [22]; carinata is also listed as a host for white
mould (Sclerotinia) in at least one breeding study, which used carinata
as a donor of B-genome stem-rot resistance into *B. napus* without
reporting carinata's own resistance level [22]. A Pakistani study lists
heat and drought tolerance, pest and disease resistance, and
yellow-seeded germplasm together as traits "rare among Brassica oil
crops" [23]; a 2008 Czech review similarly notes better tolerance to
semiarid conditions and to disease/insects than comparable oilseeds [8];
a plant-information sheet adds that the crop has an extensive taproot,
needs only moderate rainfall, and flowers earlier when grown in dry
regions [7]. No quantitative drought trial or peer-reviewed feed-toxicity
study was found this pass beyond what is already cited in Finding 2.

**Finding 8 — Ethiopian mustard has a long domestication history in the
Ethiopian highlands, with the exact dating resting on sources of uneven
rigor.** The crop is thought to have originated in the Ethiopian
highlands, with adjoining parts of East Africa and the Mediterranean
coast also mentioned in some sources [24]; it is among the oldest crops
cultivated in Ethiopia, locally known as "Gomenzer" [24]. A doctoral
thesis places its cultivation in the 4th-5th millennium (the retrieved
text was cut off before giving a firmer date) [24]; a separate
plant-database page states cultivation back to the 4th millennium BC, but
that site is a general plant encyclopedia rather than a peer-reviewed
historical source, so this date is marked unverified rather than adopted
outright [25]. The first flight of an aircraft powered entirely by
carinata-derived biofuel took place on 29 October 2012 [26] — six years
before the Qantas passenger flight in Finding 6, marking a real gap
between early proof-of-concept and later commercial/certified use.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual broadacre oilseed, also grown as a leaf
  vegetable; an annual cycle is assumed by analogy to other oilseed
  Brassica crops, since no specific days-to-maturity figure was
  retrieved this pass.
- **Climate:** Grown across Ethiopian highlands at altitude, Argentina/
  Brazil/Uruguay marginal and cover-crop land, and tested (unsuccessfully
  for immediate adoption) on the Canadian prairies (Finding 1); no single
  authoritative climate-zone table was found, so this is inferred from
  sourced growing regions.
- **Food status:** Leaf is edible at early growth stages; seed is not a
  direct food (Finding 3) — it is pressed for oil or used as biofuel
  feedstock.
- **Pollination:** Self-compatible, reportedly with no genetic or
  physiological barrier to selfing (patent-document claim, Finding 4)
  [12]. No sourced outcrossing rate, insect-pollinator visitation data, or
  gene-flow study was found this pass — a genuine gap, not a confirmed
  absence, since the search specifically targeting this question
  returned nothing on the topic at all.
- **Yield / composition:** Oil content commonly 32-42% of seed weight
  across Ethiopian genotype/altitude trials and Saskatchewan agronomic
  trials (Finding 1); one seed company's own marketing figure for its bred
  winter-environment lines claims 42-45%, kept separate as a
  company-sourced upper bound rather than folded into the independently
  trialled range (Finding 6). Seed protein is HIGHER and oil and crude
  fibre LOWER than canola/*B. rapa* in the Saskatchewan trial (Finding 1)
  [4]. No single world-average seed-yield-per-hectare figure was located
  this pass; one Ethiopian thesis's reported figure of 194,556 kg/ha for
  one genotype is explicitly flagged by the source review itself as an
  implausible units/transcription error and is NOT carried into this file
  as a usable value [1].
- **Water use:** A plant-information source describes the crop as
  needing only moderate rainfall and having an extensive taproot [7]; a
  Pakistani source groups heat and drought tolerance among the crop's
  standout traits for an oilseed Brassica [23]; a 2008 Czech review
  reports better semiarid tolerance than comparable oilseeds [8]. The
  crop flowers earlier when grown in dry regions, per the same
  plant-information source [7] — a drought-escape strategy (shortening
  the cycle to flower before water runs out) rather than a
  drought-tolerance strategy in the stress-physiology sense. No dedicated
  FAO-56-compatible crop coefficient (Kc) curve or numeric
  crop-water-requirement figure was located for this crop this pass; it
  appears to have even less published irrigation-management literature
  than some other oilseed crops this project has researched.
- **Establishment:** No sourced seeding-rate, planting-depth, or
  establishment-practice data specific to Ethiopian mustard was located
  this pass, unlike some other oilseed crops' detailed extension-sourced
  establishment data. Given the genuine differences already found between
  this crop and its Brassica relatives on other traits (oil content,
  shatter resistance), it would not be safe to assume another Brassica
  oilseed crop's establishment figures apply by uncredited analogy.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Blackleg: carinata-derived resistance
  described as superior to resistance sources available in canola, used
  as a breeding donor into *B. napus* (Finding 7) [21]. Aphids and "some
  pests": a general, unquantified claim of better-than-average resistance
  versus other oilseed crops [22]. Alternaria, Sclerotinia (white mould),
  and Phoma: named as the crop's main diseases by a seed-company source,
  without carinata-specific resistance figures; one breeding study used
  carinata as a B-genome stem-rot-resistance donor without reporting
  carinata's own disease level [22]. Flea beetle and pollen beetle: named
  as the main insect pests, with insecticides as the usual control method
  [22]. No quantitative carinata-specific Sclerotinia or Alternaria
  resistance study was located this pass, distinct from the
  clearly-sourced blackleg finding.
- **Genome:** Allotetraploid, 2n = 34, genome BcBcCcCc (B-genome from
  *B. nigra* + C-genome from *B. oleracea*), 1.31 Gbp chromosome-scale
  reference assembly reported in a 2022 study, completing the three
  classic allotetraploids of the Triangle of U alongside canola (AACC)
  and *B. juncea* (AABB) (Finding 5) [15]. No picogram-level genome-size
  figure (the Kew-C-value format) was located, only the base-pair
  assembly size and chromosome count.
- **Cultivars:** Most comparative cultivar-level data located this pass
  comes from Ethiopian germplasm accession numbers (research-collection
  identifiers, not named commercial varieties) and from seed-company US
  patent documents (named but comparatively thin on sourced trait deltas
  beyond "open-pollinated"). **Yellow dodolla** is an Ethiopian landrace/
  genotype, open-pollinated, used for both oil and leaf vegetable, with
  the highest oil percentage (41.79%) among 11 genotypes tested across
  three altitudes [1]; high-to-mid altitude was identified as best for
  maximizing both seed and oil yield in this trial [1]. **Accessions
  17545, 21373, 24203, and 24495** are germplasm-bank accessions
  evaluated at two central-Ethiopian sites, consistently high-performing
  across multiple traits among 49 accessions tested [2]; oil content was
  negatively associated with protein and glucosinolate content in this
  germplasm set [2]. **AGR044-M01, DH-129.B036, AGR159-4A1A, DH-20.141,
  AGR044-312E** are commercial lines with US patents, open-pollinated (not
  hybrid, per the patent text), used for oil (biofuel/HEFA feedstock) and
  meal, with no comparative trait-delta data located at a level of detail
  worth repeating here beyond their patented, open-pollinated status
  [13][14].
- **Propagation:** True seed only; no vegetative propagation path
  sourced or expected for this species. No confirmed commercial hybrid
  seed system — every named commercial variety located is described as
  open-pollinated, though one seed company has described active
  hybrid-breeding research and a planned European hybrid rollout with no
  confirmed commercial hybrid product, breeding system, or hybrid seed
  yield/isolation data located this pass (Finding 4).
- **Seed crop:** No seed-crop-specific or general cycle-length figure was
  sourced this pass. No sourced isolation distance for carinata-to-
  carinata seed-production blocks, or any data on cross-pollination risk
  with other Brassica oilseed crops that share the same Triangle-of-U
  hybridization history — a genuinely relevant question given that
  carinata's own genome is literally built from two of the three diploid
  species that also contribute to those other allotetraploids, but not
  directly addressed in any source opened this pass. No seed-crop-
  specific yield figure distinct from the general seed-yield figures in
  Finding 1 was found this pass.
- **Harvest:** Single stage for the seed/oil chain (direct harvest at
  maturity); single window for each of the two harvests (leaf
  thinning/topping and seed harvest), with no sourced multi-flush or
  multi-cohort pattern within either product chain. The leaf-vegetable
  harvest (thinning or topping at early growth stages, Finding 3) is a
  genuinely different organ taken at a genuinely different point in the
  plant's life cycle from the seed harvest, rather than a maturity-stage
  choice on the same organ. Whether and how commercial Ethiopian growers
  actually combine both harvests from a single planting, versus growing
  leaf-vegetable and oilseed crops separately, was not resolved this
  pass. No sourced combine-settings, harvest-timing, or labor-operation
  detail specific to Ethiopian mustard's seed harvest was located this
  pass — the shatter-resistance literature found here (Finding 5)
  addresses breeding/genetics, not field harvest practice.
- **Postharvest storage:** No dedicated Ethiopian-mustard seed-storage-
  life or moisture-management study was located this pass, a gap shared
  with some other oilseed crops this project has researched.

## Hazards

- Meal glucosinolate content limits livestock feed inclusion and acts
  through the same thiocyanate/thyroid mechanism documented for other
  Brassica oilseed meals; the oil's own erucic acid content is why it has
  historically been passed over for rapeseed/canola as an edible oil in
  Ethiopia itself (Finding 2). **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of
  this Brassicaceae-family glucosinolate livestock hazard is deferred
  until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
- No human food-safety issue was identified for the leaf vegetable or the
  (non-food) seed oil in the sources opened this pass.
- No sourced phytotoxicity, allelopathy, or other field hazard beyond the
  glucosinolate/erucic-acid feed issue already covered above.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (oilseed path):** seed → harvested at maturity → pressed for
  crude oil (biofuel/HEFA feedstock, non-food due to erucic acid and
  glucosinolate content) and meal (ruminant feed, subject to the
  glucosinolate limitation above).
- **Chain (leaf path):** young plant → thinned or topped → eaten as a
  mild leaf vegetable, a genuinely separate product from the oilseed
  chain, taken at an earlier growth stage.
- HEFA hydroprocessing of the oil into ASTM D7566-certified sustainable
  aviation fuel, and biodiesel transesterification, are industrial
  refinery-level chemistry, not farm-level processes, and are not detailed
  further here (Finding 6).

## Open research questions

- No single world-average seed-yield figure was located this pass; one
  reported figure of 194,556 kg/ha for a single genotype is flagged by
  the source review itself as an implausible units/transcription error
  and was not carried into this file.
- The Saskatchewan trial's conclusion that none of its 11 tested lines
  was adapted for immediate Canadian-prairie production is itself a
  useful climate-boundary data point, not fully reconciled against the
  crop's described heat/drought tolerance elsewhere.
- Whether and how commercial Ethiopian growers combine the leaf-thinning
  harvest and the seed/oil harvest from a single planting (sequential,
  same plant, different organs) was not resolved.
- Whether a planned European hybrid carinata product has actually
  launched, and what breeding system it uses, was not confirmed — a
  genuinely open research question, not a settled absence.
- No 2025-2026 confirmation of current sustainable-aviation-fuel
  purchasing volumes, or of the current state of Argentina's carinata-to-
  SAF supply chain, was found; the cited acreage figures for Brazil/
  Argentina/Uruguay come from an undated news source and are kept
  labelled as such.
- No quantitative carinata-specific Sclerotinia or Alternaria resistance
  study was located, distinct from the clearly-sourced blackleg-
  resistance finding; no quantitative drought trial or peer-reviewed
  feed-toxicity study beyond what Finding 2 already cites.
- A broad establishment/agronomy gap: no sourced cycle length, seeding
  rate, planting depth, soil pH, salinity or waterlogging tolerance, crop
  coefficient curve, growing-degree-day accumulator, flowering-biology
  detail, or combine-settings/harvest-timing figure was located for this
  crop this pass — Ethiopian mustard has markedly thinner published
  production-agronomy literature than some other oilseed crops this
  project has researched, concentrated instead in Ethiopian breeding/
  germplasm theses and company patent documents.
- No sourced outcrossing rate, insect-pollinator visitation data, or
  gene-flow study for *B. carinata* specifically — a genuine search gap,
  not a confirmed "no cross-pollination observed" result of the kind
  found for some other crops versus other *Brassica* species.
- No picogram-level genome-size figure (only the base-pair assembly size
  and chromosome count) was located.
- No sourced isolation distance for carinata-to-carinata seed-production
  blocks, and no data on cross-pollination risk with other Triangle-of-U
  species, despite carinata's genome sharing a diploid progenitor species
  with each of them.
- No seed-crop-specific seed yield figure distinct from the general
  seed-yield range in Finding 1.
