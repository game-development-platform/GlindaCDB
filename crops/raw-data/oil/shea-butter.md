# Shea Butter (*Vitellaria paradoxa*)

**Researched:** 2026-10-30

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Vitellaria paradoxa* C.F. Gaertn. (shea tree), the
sole species of its genus, family Sapotaceae — the same family as
argan, making this the second Sapotaceae oilseed tree this project has
researched. "Shea butter" names both the tree/crop and its finished
product in common usage. Two subspecies exist with non-overlapping
geographic ranges: subsp. *paradoxa* across West Africa (Senegal to the
Central African Republic) and subsp. *nilotica* in East Africa (Uganda,
South Sudan); they are not modeled as separate entries this pass (no
sourced evidence of a materially different trait beyond butter oleic-
acid variability, Finding 4), but are flagged as a cultivar-note-level
distinction. Argan is the closest comparison crop: both are slow-
maturing Sapotaceae trees whose fruit is processed through a depulp →
crack → press chain into a kernel fat, and both sit substantially in
semi-wild/traditional agroforestry systems rather than intensive
monoculture. Shea differs sharply from argan in one respect worth
flagging up front: its fruit pulp is a genuine, widely eaten human food
(sweet, fresh), not an inedible byproduct — the opposite of argan's
fibrous, non-food pulp.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the fruit's pulp is a real human food, not a processing
byproduct, a genuine contrast with argan's own fruit.** Shea fruit pulp
is sweet and vitamin-rich, eaten fresh as a snack across the tree's
entire range [1]. This is a structurally different relationship between
a fruit's flesh and its oil-bearing nut than argan's own research found
(argan pulp is fibrous, non-food, historically goat-browsed or
composted).

**Finding 2 — per-tree fruit yield is reasonably well sourced but shows
wide site-to-site variation, and the fruit-to-seed conversion ratio is
one of the more precisely sourced figures this project has found.**
Average per-tree yield is commonly cited at 15-20 kg fresh fruit/year,
with optimum trees reaching about 45 kg [1]; a Mali field study found
considerably lower site means (about 6-11 kg/tree), reflecting
agroclimatic zone and land-use differences [1]. One sourced conversion
gives roughly 400 g of dry seed per kilogram of fresh fruit [1] — a
cleaner, more directly stated figure than the equivalent conversion this
project found for argan, where no comparable ratio was located.

**Finding 3 — nut-cracking follows a preservation step (parboiling) not
present in argan's chain.** After depulping, nuts are traditionally
parboiled shortly afterward specifically to halt germination and
preserve the kernel before sun-drying and shell-cracking [2][3]. Storage
research on dried nuts found that hermetic-bag storage holds insect
damage below 30%, versus about 80% in jute or woven-polypropylene sacks,
at a starting moisture content of about 7.3% [6]; fungal spoilage (named
*Aspergillus* species) is a separately documented risk, causing viability
loss, taint, discoloration, and rot [6].

**Finding 4 — kernel oil content is, like argan's, highly variable by
tree and study, but the sourced range skews notably higher at its upper
end.** A Ugandan district-level study of subsp. *nilotica* found kernel
oil content of 50.5-54.4% [1]; individual ethnovarieties ranged far
wider, from about 38% up to about 70% in some soft-pulped types [1].
Butter composition is dominated by stearic and oleic acid together
(roughly 85-90% of the fatty-acid profile), and the ratio between the two
determines whether the finished butter is firm or soft [1]. West African
(subsp. *paradoxa*) butter is markedly more variable in oleic content
(37-55%) than the consistently high-oleic Ugandan (subsp. *nilotica*)
butter [1] — a genuine, sourced subspecies-level compositional
difference, though not modeled as a separate entry this pass.

**Finding 5 — shea butter is solid at room temperature, unlike most
other pressed oils this project has researched.** Unlike most other
pressed oils (liquid at room temperature), shea butter is solid, a
direct consequence of its high stearic/oleic saturated-fat content
(Finding 4) [1]. It has three distinct destinations: the dominant global
cosmetic/skincare use; a traditional West African cooking fat with
centuries of culinary use; and a narrower, FDA-GRAS-affirmed US food use
specifically for confections, frostings, and sweet sauces (refined,
food-grade "sheanut oil" only, not a blanket food approval) [4]. Shea nuts
are classified as tree nuts, and some allergists advise tree-nut-allergic
patients to avoid shea-derived products as a precaution, though formal
documented allergic-reaction case reports were not found this pass, only
an advisory stance and one 2003 study finding shea nut proteins "compare
favorably" with other allergenic nuts in a laboratory assay [4].

**Finding 6 — shea butter's dominant real-world identity is as a major
rural-women's income source, more so than any other oil crop this
project has researched.** An estimated three million African women work
directly or indirectly with shea butter production across its range [5].
Fair-trade certification (e.g. a UNDP-backed Ghanaian cooperative, a
Burkina Faso union exporting to France since 2001 and Canada since 2004)
is reported to raise prices toward Fairtrade minimums, though one
academic analysis concludes the net benefit cannot be cleanly determined
given the complexity of the factors involved, and fair-trade volumes
remain a small share of total exports in at least one documented case
(11.6% in 2006) [5]. This commerce/labour-economics dimension is this
crop's single most distinctive real-world fact, comparable in weight to
jatropha's biodiesel-boom-failure history or safflower's dual-product
economics.

**Finding 7 — the press-cake byproduct carries three documented
antinutritional factors, not one, a broader antinutrient profile than
any other oil crop's press-cake this project has researched.** Shea nut
cake (the residue after fat extraction) contains saponins, tannins, AND
theobromine, which together limit its feed value [7]. Ruminants tolerate
it better than monogastrics; above about 30% of the ration it affects
digestibility of the rest of the feed, while for poultry it is described
as having low nutritive value and genuine toxicity concern without
further treatment [7]. Boiling, fermentation, or NaOH treatment have been
proposed as detoxification methods, the same general method family used
for argan's own saponin-bearing cake, but no dedicated shea-specific
saponin-dose or detoxification-efficacy study was found this pass [7].

**Finding 8 — pollination research suggests the tree's low fruit set is
driven by pollinator scarcity, not floral self-incompatibility, though
self-incompatibility itself was not directly tested in any source
found.** A 2018 Burkina Faso/northern Ghana study of subsp. *paradoxa*
found 88% of flower visitors were bees, most frequently small stingless
bees [8]. A separate university thesis found open-pollinated flowers set
significantly less fruit than hand-pollinated flowers, with no
difference in fruit set from pollen sourced 50 m, 500 m, or 1000 m away,
or between flowers of different style lengths — the authors attribute the
low natural fruit-set rate at least partly to low pollinator-vector
activity [8]. A separate peer-reviewed paper describes the species as
having hermaphroditic, self-pollination-capable flowers alongside a
predominantly cross-pollinated mating system [8] — genuinely different
from argan's own clearly self-incompatible pollination story, though the
degree of self-compatibility here was not quantified by any fruit-set
percentage in the sources opened, in contrast with argan's precisely
sourced 0.2% self vs. 5.3% cross figures.

**Finding 9 — the tree's own long-term landscape role (parkland
agroforestry) is agronomically entangled with its yield, in a way
distinct from argan's separate forest-decline story.** Shea trees are
typically retained (not planted from scratch) within farmed parkland
systems across the Sudanian savanna belt, and a Ghana intercropping study
found yam co-cultivation beneath shea canopy yielded less (11.5 t/ha)
than open-field yam (20.8 t/ha), a real agroforestry tradeoff [9].
Separately, mistletoe (Loranthaceae) parasitism is documented to affect
fruit yield, with its impact varying across habitat types in a Benin
study [9]; tree density itself is reported lower in both cultivated and
fallowed land than in undisturbed bushland in northern Ghana, a
regeneration concern distinct from argan's own overgrazing-driven
decline [9]. Drought stress is reported to cause up to 40% seedling
mortality in some regions, though a Burkina Faso study of mature parkland
trees found they actively down-regulate canopy transpiration under
stress, suggesting some water-use self-regulation once established [9].

**Finding 10 — propagation timelines are dramatically disputed between
traditional seed-grown trees and a recent, still-experimental grafting
technique.** Traditional seed-grown trees are reported to take anywhere
from 8 to 25 years to first fruiting, with environmental stress (bush
fires, drought) lengthening this toward the upper end by stunting growth
[2]. A Ghanaian grafting program reports cutting this to 3-6 years, with
the first successful shea grafting reported in 2008; Ugandan grafting
research is described as still preliminary, with further experiments
needed before firm method recommendations can be made [2]. Full
production is not reached until roughly age 45 in one source, and the
tree is reported productive for up to 200-300 years in parkland systems
when properly maintained [2][9] — among the longest juvenile-to-peak and
total-lifespan figures this project has sourced, in the same range as
argan's own exceptionally long-lived profile.

**Finding 11 — the genome is small, diploid, and backed by a complete
chromosome-scale reference assembly, unlike argan's single-study genome
figure.** *V. paradoxa* is diploid (2n=24) with a genome assembled at
658.7 Mbp from 162.6 Gb of long-read sequencing data, annotated with
38,505 coding genes [10]. This is a materially more robust genomic
resource than anything sourced for argan (a single flow-cytometry figure
with no second study to check it against), reflecting shea's status as
an active crop-improvement research target.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Long-lived deciduous perennial tree, savanna-
  parkland habit. Typically retained (not planted from scratch) within
  farmed parkland rather than planted in pure stands (Finding 9).
- **Climate:** Semi-arid, tropical. Restricted to the Sudanian savanna
  belt, Senegal to Uganda/South Sudan, split into two non-overlapping
  subspecies ranges — genuinely one of the more geographically specific
  oil-crop ranges this project has sourced, after argan's even narrower
  single-country range.
- **Food status:** The traditional West African cooking-fat use and the
  narrower FDA-GRAS-affirmed US confectionery use both involve eating the
  refined/food-grade butter as pressed; cosmetic-grade product is not
  intended for consumption (Finding 5).
- **Pollination:** Predominantly cross-pollinated (insect, mainly small
  stingless bees) with hermaphroditic, self-pollination-capable flowers
  also present (Finding 8). Self-incompatibility was not established
  either way this pass, unlike argan's precisely sourced figures. No
  isolation distance was found; one hand-pollination study found no
  fruit-set difference between pollen sourced 50 m, 500 m, or 1000 m
  away, suggesting isolation distance may not be agronomically
  meaningful at these scales. The two subspecies' ranges do not overlap
  in the wild (separated by a watershed divide or, per one study, a
  mountain range), so natural subspecies crossing is not a practical
  concern.
- **Yield / composition:** No per-hectare figure was found this pass, the
  same gap this project found for argan; per-tree figures only: 15-20 kg
  fresh fruit/tree/year average, up to 45 kg for optimum trees; a Mali
  field study found site means of about 6-11 kg/tree (Finding 2). Kernel
  oil content is 38-70% of kernel weight depending on tree/ethnovariety/
  study (Finding 4); roughly 400 g dry seed (nut) per kg fresh fruit is
  one sourced conversion.
- **Water use:** No crop-coefficient figure was found for shea in the
  sources opened this pass. Young seedlings are drought-vulnerable (up to
  40% mortality reported in some regions under drought stress), but
  established trees appear considerably hardier, actively down-
  regulating canopy transpiration under water stress rather than simply
  failing (Finding 9). Waterlogging tolerance is likely poor by general
  dryland-savanna-tree inference, not independently sourced this pass.
  Much of current production is wild/semi-wild parkland trees receiving
  no irrigation.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed (traditional, wide stress-dependent
  juvenile period) or grafting (recent, still-experimental; wildlings
  reported more economical as rootstock than nursery seedlings) [2]. One
  Ugandan nursery source gives about one year in nursery before
  transplanting or direct field-planting [2].
- **Soil:** No pH figure or tolerance band was found this pass, a search
  gap acknowledged directly by the sources reviewed, despite shea being
  an economically significant and heavily studied crop. No salinity
  tolerance or intolerance statement was found either.
- **Stand life:** Years to first harvest: 8-25 (seed-grown, stress-
  dependent) or 3-6 (grafted, experimental) [2][10]. Full production is
  not reached until roughly age 45 in one source [2]. Productive
  lifespan: up to 200-300 years in well-maintained parkland systems per
  one source [9]; a separate source gives up to 250 years, both kept as a
  documented range rather than resolved to one figure, the same
  treatment this project gave argan's own disputed lifespan figures.
  Wildlings are reported more economical as grafting rootstock than
  nursery seedlings, though no yield/timing comparison figure was found
  [2].
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n=24; a chromosome-scale reference assembly of
  658.7 Mbp, annotated with 38,505 coding genes (Finding 11) [10].
- **Disease/pest profile:** No named non-pest physiological disorder was
  sourced this pass, distinct from the fungal storage pathogens (Finding
  3) and the mistletoe parasitism (Finding 9), which are post-harvest and
  biotic-stress issues respectively, not physiological disorders of the
  standing tree.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one annual fruiting season
  (roughly April-August) per the sources opened this pass; no sourced
  evidence of multiple separate fruiting flushes per year. Hand-gathered,
  mostly fallen fruit. Post-harvest chain: depulp (pulp eaten, Finding 1)
  → parboil and sun-dry the nut (Finding 3) → crack shell. The
  traditional full extraction chain (crushing, hand-kneading with water
  for up to 2 hours at one cooperative, boiling to separate the floating
  oil, cooling to solidify) is described as extremely arduous and time-
  consuming, using large quantities of water and firewood — one UK-funded
  study found a mechanical press cut processing time for 25 kg of
  kernels from 9.5 to 3.25 hours and water use from 90 to 1.7 litres [7].
- **Cultivars:** No sourced list of named commercial cultivars or
  breeding lines was found this pass — like argan, shea is still
  substantially a semi-wild/traditional parkland crop. The most
  significant sourced sub-crop distinction is the two subspecies.
  **Subsp. *paradoxa* (West African)**: predominantly seed-propagated,
  with grafting trials reported from Ghana; the traditional basis of
  West African shea production for centuries; the majority of global
  shea production; more variable oleic-acid content in the finished
  butter (37-55%) than subsp. *nilotica* [1]; larger production volume
  and the bulk of the sourced fair-trade/cooperative economic literature
  (Finding 6). **Subsp. *nilotica* (East African)**: predominantly seed-
  propagated, with grafting trials reported from Uganda, described as
  still preliminary; the traditional basis of East African shea
  production; a smaller production base but the subject of more recent
  genomic-resource research (Finding 11); consistently high-oleic butter
  composition (less variable than subsp. *paradoxa*'s) [1]; larger
  leaves and flower parts (sepals, filaments, styles) than subsp.
  *paradoxa* [8].

## Hazards

- **Shea nut allergen caution**: shea nuts are classed as tree nuts; some
  allergists advise tree-nut-allergic patients to avoid shea-derived
  products as a precaution, though no formal documented allergic-
  reaction case report was found this pass, only an advisory stance and
  one 2003 study finding shea nut proteins "compare favorably" with
  other allergenic nuts in a laboratory assay (Finding 5).
- **Press-cake antinutritional factors**: shea nut cake carries three
  documented antinutritional factors (saponins, tannins, theobromine),
  limiting feed value especially for poultry/monogastrics without
  further treatment (Finding 7).
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — both the shea nut allergen caution and the
  press-cake antinutrient profile are deferred for a fuller write-up
  until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fruit → hand-gathered (fallen or ripe) → depulped (pulp
  eaten fresh, Finding 1) → nut parboiled and sun-dried (halts
  germination, Finding 3) → shell cracked → kernel pressed (traditional
  crush/knead-with-water/boil-and-skim, or modern mechanical pressing) →
  crude shea butter (cosmetic/skincare use; traditional West African
  cooking fat; narrower FDA-GRAS-affirmed US confectionery use) + press
  cake (limited livestock feed use given its antinutrient profile,
  Finding 7).
- Shea butter production is predominantly organized through women's
  cooperatives across the tree's range, the dominant real-world income
  source this crop provides (Finding 6) — a commerce/labour-economics
  dimension, not a cultivation detail, but this crop's single most
  distinctive real-world fact.

## Open research questions

- The two subspecies have non-overlapping wild ranges and a genuinely
  sourced compositional difference (oleic-acid variability in the
  finished butter, Finding 4); they are the natural split point if a
  future pass wants geography-specific trait deltas, more clearly
  differentiated than most cultivar-note-only splits this project has
  made for other crops.
- Self-incompatibility is not established either way; unlike argan's
  precisely sourced self-vs-cross fruit-set percentages, no source found
  this pass directly tested or quantified shea's self-compatibility — a
  dedicated hand-pollination self-vs-cross trial would resolve this.
- No soil pH figure of any kind was found, a search gap acknowledged
  directly by multiple sources reviewed, despite shea being an
  economically significant and heavily studied crop.
- The fair-trade/cooperative economics story (Finding 6) is the most
  distinctive real-world fact about this crop (three million women,
  cooperative production, contested fair-trade benefit) but is a
  commerce/labour-economics topic without an obvious place in a simple
  crop-data framework, similar to oil palm's deforestation controversy
  or argan's adulteration problem.
- Parkland agroforestry tradeoffs and mistletoe parasitism (Finding 9)
  are both genuinely yield-relevant real-world factors (the yam-
  intercropping tradeoff, mistletoe parasitism's documented fruit-yield
  impact) without a clean place in a simple crop-data framework.
- No seed-crop production-scale figures (isolation distance, minimum
  population, seed yield per hectare) were found, the same gap this
  project found for argan and oil palm.
- No named commercial cultivars were found; the cultivar notes above
  substitute the two subspecies plus propagation-method categories
  instead, the same thin-cultivar pattern already seen in argan and oil
  palm.
- Much shea production still comes from wild/semi-wild parkland trees,
  not planted orchards — at least as strong a candidate for a "wild and
  dual-use" flora classification as argan, if this project develops one.
