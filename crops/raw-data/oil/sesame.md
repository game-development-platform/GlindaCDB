# Sesame (*Sesamum indicum*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/sesame.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-29

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Sesamum indicum* L., family Pedaliaceae, self-
pollinated diploid (2n=26) — the single cultivated sesame species, with
no name conflict. The one real internal split is black vs. white (tan)
seed-coat colour, a modest cultivar-level distinction (Finding 9) rather
than grounds for separate treatment, the same "shared agronomy, different
cosmetic/chemistry delta" pattern already seen in sunflower's
confectionery/oilseed split and canola's double-low/HEAR split. Flax is a
useful comparison crop: both are small, self-pollinating oilseeds with no
F1 hybrid system, though sesame's defining agronomic problem — capsule
shattering — has no real analogue among other oil crops this project has
researched.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content is high and widely variable across germplasm,
and this is a genuine achene/capsule seed crop, not a fruit-oil crop.** A
103-landrace Turkish germplasm study found oil content ranging from
41.3% to 62.7%, averaging 53.3% [1]; a separate multi-country comparison
found seed oil content from 49.22% (India) to 56.16% (Nigeria) [1]. Oleic
and linoleic acid together make up more than 80% of the oil's fatty
acids, with the Turkish landraces averaging 37.2% oleic and 45.7%
linoleic, though a commonly cited reference range (33-44% oleic, 40-50%
linoleic) and the Turkish figures do not fully agree, left visible as
ordinary cross-study variation rather than a sharp conflict [1]. This is
a higher and more variable oil-content range than most other oil crops
this project has researched (sunflower 46-51%, canola ~41-45%, flax
35-45%).

**Finding 2 — no commercial F1 hybrid seed system was found; sesame is
predominantly self-pollinating, and the species' central breeding problem
is capsule shattering, not hybrid vigor.** Unlike sunflower (CMS + bee-
mediated cross) and canola (Ogura CMS + restorer), no source found this
pass described a commercial F1 hybrid sesame seed-production system,
cytoplasmic male sterility line, or male-sterility-based cross [2]. The
dominant breeding effort instead targets indehiscence (non-shattering
capsules): wild-type sesame capsules shatter readily at maturity,
scattering seed before or during harvest; an indehiscent mutant was
discovered in 1943, and modern breeding (e.g. commercial "IND" varieties)
still works from that mutation, selecting open-pollinated lines that
retain measurably more seed in the capsule 4-9 weeks past the ideal
harvest date than earlier non-dehiscent types [2]. One patent describes
crossing shatter-resistant donor lines with standard sesame and finding
that F1 plants show NO shatter resistance at all — the trait only
segregates out in the F2 generation, where individual plants are then
screened and selected toward homozygous inbred lines, with roughly
100-250 crosses made per breeding cycle to find the right gene
combination [2]. This makes sesame's breeding story structurally
different from most other oil crops this project has researched: no
hybrid-seed economics drive the commercial seed trade here at all, and
the trait everyone is chasing is a harvest-loss trait, not a yield or
oil-content trait per se.

**Finding 3 — self-pollination is the rule, but insect-mediated
outcrossing is real and sets seed-certification isolation distances.**
Sesame's own flowers are predominantly self-pollinating, but cross-
pollination by insects (sesame has no wind pollination component at all)
averages around 5% and can range from 0% to 50% depending on pollinator
activity and weather [3]. Indian seed-certification standards set
isolation distances of 100 m for foundation seed and 50 m for certified
seed, plus five border rows of the same variety around the seed-
production plot as a buffer; time isolation (staggering sowing dates so
flowering periods don't overlap) is explicitly discouraged in the same
source [3]. This 50-100 m range sits between flax's very short 1-10 m
isolation (truly minimal outcrossing) and canola's 400 m-½ mile isolation
(fully insect-dependent-yield, self-compatible but far more outcrossing)
— a genuinely intermediate position among oil crops this project has
researched. Rouging (removing off-type plants) is recommended at
vegetative, flowering, and maturity stages, and sources flag sesame's
small, easily shattered seed as itself a mechanical-mixing risk in seed
production, separate from the pollination-outcrossing risk [3].

**Finding 4 — harvest timing is a direct trade-off between shatter loss
and seed maturity, and the main documented defense is better harvest
technique, not just better genetics.** Two Brazilian cultivar trials
found the lowest shattering losses and best yields at specific harvest
windows — 116-132 days after emergence for one cultivar, 90-105 days
(varying by crop year) for another — with seed at harvest typically still
too wet for direct storage and requiring field or mechanical drying first
[4]. A post-harvest loss study identified field drying, pre-harvest
shattering, and threshing as the crop's three main loss points, citing
both over-drying and late harvest as contributing causes [4]. A Bulgarian
mechanization study found that feeding cut stems laterally into a combine
header (rather than the conventional reel-feed orientation) cut seed-
shattering losses by up to a factor of four [4] — a genuinely crop-
specific harvest-engineering fact not seen for other oil crops this
project has researched.

**Finding 5 — roasting the seed changes the pressed oil's own antioxidant
chemistry, converting one lignan into a more powerful antioxidant and
setting a temperature ceiling.** Raw sesame seed and its unroasted oil
contain the lignans sesamin and sesamolin plus gamma-tocopherol, all
contributing to sesame oil's unusually high natural oxidative stability
even before any roasting [5]. A 2000 Korean study found that roasting the
seed before pressing INCREASES oil induction time (oxidative stability)
with roasting temperature up to a point, and that this gain correlates
strongly (r=0.934 above 170°C) with rising sesamol content — a smaller
phenolic compound that is NOT present in the raw seed but is generated
from sesamolin by heat during roasting — while sesamin and sesamolin
themselves both fall as roasting gets hotter [5]. A separate 2010 study
found the highest oxidative stability at 247°C for 28 minutes, again
attributing it to sesamol generated from sesamolin [5]. A 2022 study
found that primary and secondary lipid oxidation set in above 250°C,
meaning the antioxidant benefit of roasting has an upper temperature
limit past which roasting itself starts degrading the oil [5]. Gamma-
tocopherol, by contrast, is simply destroyed by heat (one study recorded
a fall from 70.59 to 26.73 mg% at 230°C) [5]. This roasting-dependent
chemistry is the direct mechanistic reason "toasted sesame oil" and
"raw/cold-pressed sesame oil" are different trade products with
different flavour and stability profiles, not just a marketing
distinction.

**Finding 6 — charcoal rot and Fusarium wilt are the two major named
soil-borne diseases, and no genotype found this pass was immune to
either.** Charcoal rot is described by Egyptian researchers as the most
destructive sesame disease; the fungus survives between crops as
microsclerotia in soil and plant debris, and an Iranian field study links
its growing severity to climate warming, finding that none of the tested
genotypes were immune [6]. Fusarium wilt is the other confirmed causal
pathogen alongside charcoal rot in pathogenicity studies [6]. A 2022
Egyptian resistance screen found three named cultivars to be the most
resistant among those tested, and an earlier 2008 study found that
branch number and seed colour correlated with infection levels,
suggesting these as possible screening traits, though the correlation's
practical strength was not detailed in the source found [6]. No
phyllody, soil-pH tolerance, drought-tolerance, or waterlogging-tolerance
source was found this pass despite a direct search.

**Finding 7 — safe long-term storage requires unusually dry seed, and
the moisture/longevity relationship is itself a measured, nonlinear
curve.** A 2024 review identifies seed moisture as the single most
important factor in sesame seed longevity and recommends storage at 6%
moisture or lower, with viability holding up to about 12 months under
good conditions [7]. A separate 1986 study found that seed longevity
falls LOGARITHMICALLY as moisture rises from 2% to 15% — meaning the
viability cost of letting stored seed get even slightly wetter
accelerates, not just adds up linearly [7]. Other extension-level
sources give looser practical targets (6-8% at harvest, below 60%
relative humidity, up to 5 years under some conditions), and these
looser figures are kept visible alongside the more precise 6%-moisture/
12-month figure rather than silently preferring one over the other [7].
6% moisture is markedly drier than canola's 8% long-term storage target —
a real, sourced difference in storage-moisture sensitivity between the
two seed crops, not an estimate.

**Finding 8 — sesame is one of the newest entries on the US major-food-
allergen list, and under-labeling was a documented, measured public-
health problem before that change.** A 2019 survey of nearly 52,000 US
households found sesame allergy affects roughly 0.2% of the population,
making it the ninth most common food allergy in the country, comparable
in prevalence to soy and pistachio allergy [8]. Reactions range from
hives to anaphylaxis [8]. A 2021 study examining self-reported allergic
reactions found that more than 56% of products implicated in those
reactions did NOT declare sesame on their ingredient label, and
separately noted that many consumers do not recognize terms like
"tahini" as meaning sesame [8]. A 2023 US regulatory change added sesame
as the ninth major food allergen requiring explicit labeling, putting it
on the same regulatory footing as peanuts, tree nuts, and shellfish [8].
This is a genuinely different kind of hazard from other oil crops this
project has researched — not a toxin needing processing to remove, but
an allergen needing disclosure.

**Finding 9 — black and white (tan) seed-coat colour is a real, sourced
but modest cultivar-level difference, not grounds for a separate crop
entry.** The only direct two-cultivar comparison found this pass measured
black-seeded sesame at 49.07% oil and white-seeded sesame at 47.02%,
with oleic acid at 47.03% (white) vs. 45.46% (black) and linoleic at
35.01% (white) vs. 33.79% (black) [9]. These differences are a few
percentage points each, on the same growing method, harvest process, and
processing chain.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Warm-season annual. Cycle length 90-132 days:
  one extension guide gives 90-110 days to physiological maturity for
  commercial varieties plus 20-40 days of field drying; Brazilian trials
  found optimum harvest windows of 90-105 and 116-132 days after
  emergence depending on cultivar and crop year; an Indian sowing-date
  trial reported 120.16 days for a specific sowing date [1][4].
- **Climate:** Warm temperate, subtropical, tropical — a general warm-
  season-annual range inferred from trial-site locations cited across
  sources (Texas, India, Sudan, Turkey, Egypt, Iran, Brazil), not
  independently sourced to a dedicated climate-zone list this pass.
- **Food status:** Seed is widely eaten raw, toasted, or hulled directly,
  and ground into tahini/halva without oil extraction.
- **Pollination:** Predominantly self-pollinating (not required); modest
  insect-mediated cross-pollination occurs on top of the self-pollination
  baseline (Finding 3: average ~5%, range 0-50%; no wind-pollination
  component at all). Self-pollination alone is sufficient for the crop's
  normal seed set; insect visitation adds only the modest outcrossing
  above, not an obligate yield requirement. No quantified bee-visitation
  yield-boost study was found for sesame, unlike for canola.
- **Yield / composition:** No world-average figure was independently
  sourced this pass. A single-source best-case figure of 1,234 kg/ha was
  cited in a seed-production source (not independently cross-checked).
  Two top-performing cultivars in a Turkish second-crop-system trial
  reached 2,184 and 2,149 kg/ha. Seed oil content is 41.3-62.7%,
  averaging 53.3% across a 103-landrace Turkish study; 49.22-56.16%
  across a separate multi-country comparison (Finding 1).
- **Water use:** Three regional (Indian) lysimeter/field studies give
  differing crop-coefficient curves, none treated as a single
  authoritative curve: one Maharashtra lysimeter study gives locally
  derived coefficients of 0.53/0.82/0.98/0.39 (initial/development/mid/
  end) against stated reference values of 0.35/0.74/1.10/0.25 for the
  same four stages over a roughly 95-day season [10]; a South Gujarat
  summer-sesame study gives reference coefficients of 0.69/0.90/1.04/0.65
  (drip) vs. 0.72/0.98/1.05/0.74 (border) for initial/mid/development/
  late stages, recommending a dual-crop-coefficient modeling approach
  (18.0% water savings under drip vs. border) [10]; a Rajasthan
  gravimetric-lysimeter study gives a much wider and differently staged
  range (0.3-0.7 early, 0.8-1.5 vegetative, 1.6-1.9 flowering, 0.3-1.2
  seed-set, averaging 0.9 for the season) [10] — all three studies
  disagree on both stage boundaries and magnitude, kept visible as three
  separate regional results rather than averaged. The Rajasthan study
  reports seasonal evapotranspiration totals of 495 mm (irrigated) and
  362 mm (rainfed) [10]. Flowering shows the highest crop-water-use
  coefficients in the Rajasthan study (1.6-1.9), suggesting it as the
  crop's peak water-demand stage, though this is inferred from the
  curve's shape rather than a stated stress-sensitivity finding.
  Sesame is widely described in general agronomic literature as
  relatively drought-tolerant for an oilseed, consistent with the lower
  362 mm rainfed figure, but not independently quantified against a
  dedicated drought-tolerance study this pass. No waterlogging-tolerance
  source was found.
- **Establishment:** Direct-seeded. No seed rate in kg/ha or row spacing
  was found this pass.
- **Soil:** No soil pH tolerance (optimal/min/max) figure was found at
  all despite a direct search — a deeper gap than for some other oil
  crops this project has researched. No salinity table or general
  comparative salinity statement was found either.
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n=26. A flow-cytometry study of 75 accessions
  from one country plus one additional accession measured a 2C DNA value
  of 0.77-1.0 pg (753-978 Mbp), averaging 0.85 ± 0.037 pg (831 Mbp),
  giving a derived 1C value of roughly 0.42 pg (approximately 416 Mbp)
  [11]. This flow-cytometry-derived figure sits noticeably ABOVE the
  species' published reference-genome assembly lengths — a genome
  database entry gives an estimated genome size of 337-357 Mbp, a 2014
  assembly came to approximately 293.7-357 Mb depending on the specific
  figure cited within that paper, and a 2022 improved assembly reached
  309.35 Mb with 97.54% chromosome-anchoring and a 13.48 Mb contig N50
  [11] — the assembled lengths (roughly 294-309 Mb) sitting below the
  flow-cytometry estimates (roughly 416-453 Mb at the high end),
  consistent with unassembled repetitive sequence and gaps, a pattern
  this project has also seen for oil palm's genome-size figures. Kept as
  a genuine, sourced range rather than resolved to one number.
- **Cultivars:** The one real, well-documented cultivar-level distinction
  is black vs. white (tan) seed-coat colour (Finding 9), a modest oil-
  content/fatty-acid-profile delta on identical agronomy. **Black-seeded
  type**: 49.07% oil content; oleic 45.46%, linoleic 33.79% [9]; widely
  grown, especially favoured in East Asian markets per general trade
  knowledge (not independently sourced). **White (tan)-seeded type**:
  47.02% oil content; oleic 47.03%, linoleic 35.01% [9]; widely grown,
  the dominant type in many Western and Middle Eastern tahini/halva
  markets per general trade knowledge. **Indehiscent (shatter-resistant)
  commercial lines**: bred from a 1943 mutant discovery, holding
  measurably more seed in the capsule 4-9 weeks past ideal harvest timing
  than earlier non-dehiscent types [2]; this trait exists purely to
  reduce harvest shatter loss, not a product-chemistry distinction; F1
  crosses between indehiscent and shattering lines show NO shatter
  resistance, the trait only segregates out in F2 (Finding 2). No
  sourced list of specific named commercial breeding lines beyond a few
  commercial indehiscent-series names and a short resistant-cultivar
  list was found at a level of detail worth a full cultivar table.
- **Propagation:** No commercial F1 hybrid sesame seed-production system
  was found in the sources opened this pass (Finding 2). All located
  breeding-program descriptions work from open-pollinated inbred-line
  development (crossing, selfing to homozygosity, selection), not a
  male-sterility-based hybrid cross. Isolation distances of 100 m
  (foundation seed) / 50 m (certified seed) are used in Indian seed-
  certification standards, plus five border rows of the same variety,
  with rouging recommended at vegetative, flowering, and maturity stages
  (Finding 3).
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one annual cycle per
  planting, harvested once. Sickle-cutting or mechanical harvest with
  lateral stem-feeding to reduce shatter loss (Finding 4); timing is a
  direct trade-off between seed maturity and shatter loss, with no
  single universally cited optimal day-count. Field or mechanical drying
  to 6% moisture is the key post-harvest step (Finding 7).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Charcoal rot and Fusarium wilt are the two
  confirmed major soil-borne diseases, with no genotype found immune to
  either in the sources opened this pass; branch number and seed colour
  were found to correlate with Fusarium-wilt infection level in one 2008
  study, suggesting possible screening traits (Finding 6). Phyllody was
  named as a search target but returned with no usable detail this
  pass — a real, acknowledged gap.

## Hazards

- **Sesame allergy**: sesame is one of the US's major food allergens as
  of a 2023 regulatory change, affecting roughly 0.2% of the US
  population (comparable in prevalence to soy and pistachio allergy),
  with reactions ranging from hives to anaphylaxis (Finding 8). Under-
  labeling was a measured, documented problem before the regulatory
  change: a 2021 study found over 56% of products implicated in self-
  reported sesame-allergy reactions did not declare sesame on the
  ingredient label, and many consumers did not recognize terms like
  "tahini" as meaning sesame. This is a disclosure/allergen hazard,
  mechanistically unrelated to the cyanogenic-glycoside hazards this
  project has documented for cassava and flax.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of the sesame allergen hazard
  is deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → sickle-cut or mechanically harvested (at or near
  physiological maturity, 90-132 days from sowing) → field- or
  mechanically dried to a 6% moisture target → pressed → oil (raw/cold-
  pressed, or roasted/toasted — a distinct trade product with different
  flavour and oxidative-stability chemistry, Finding 5). Seed is also
  eaten whole, raw or toasted, or dehulled, and used directly (without
  oil extraction) in tahini, halva, garnishes, and baked goods. No
  sourced dehulling-yield ratio was found for the hulled/dehulled trade
  form.

## Open research questions

- No confirmed kcal/100g or protein figure was located for either the
  raw seed or the pressed oil, a deeper gap than for sunflower or canola.
- Crop-coefficient values diverge across three regional Indian studies
  with no single authoritative curve identified.
- No sourced F1 hybrid seed system was found, in contrast with several
  other oil crops this project has researched — confirmed as a genuine
  crop-biology difference (Finding 2), not an incomplete search, though a
  niche commercial hybrid system could still exist without having
  surfaced in general web search.
- No quantified bee-visitation yield-boost study was found for sesame,
  unlike for canola; the pollinator relationship is mapped as
  outcrossing-level only, not yield-boosting, purely because no boosting
  study was found — this may understate a real effect.
- No soil pH tolerance figure was found at all, a deeper gap than for
  canola or even flax.
- No salinity table or general comparative salinity statement was found
  either, a deeper gap than for canola.
- Phyllody (a phytoplasma disease) was named as a search target but
  returned with no usable detail.
- No seed-crop production-scale figures (minimum population, dedicated
  seed-production-field yield, seeding rate, or row spacing) were found.
- No hulled/dehulled seed process ratio was found — a real, distinct
  trade form (dehulling removes the hull, used for tahini and lighter-
  coloured garnish seed) with no sourced yield figure this pass.
- No sourced sesame-specific hydroponic/microgreen viability check was
  found, left entirely unresearched rather than estimated by analogy.
