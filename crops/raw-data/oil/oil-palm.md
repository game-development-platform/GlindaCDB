# Oil Palm (*Elaeis guineensis*)

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Elaeis guineensis* Jacq. (African oil palm), family
Arecaceae (palms). "Oil palm" unqualified always means this species in
agricultural usage, as distinct from the South/Central American *Elaeis
oleifera* (American oil palm, a different species used mainly as breeding
material via the OxG interspecific hybrid, Finding 12) and from other
named "palm oil" sources that are not treated as a separate crop here.
Olive is a useful comparison crop for the fruit-oil extraction pattern,
though oil palm is distinctive among the oil crops this project has
researched for yielding two chemically distinct commercial oils from one
fruit.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil comes from two separate organs of the same fruit, with
different oils.** The fibrous mesocarp (flesh) presses into crude palm
oil (CPO), 17-22% of fresh fruit bunch (FFB) weight at the mill floor,
rising as the palm matures [1]; the kernel inside the nut presses
separately into palm kernel oil (PKO), a chemically different, mostly-
lauric-acid oil much closer to coconut oil than to CPO [7][8]. One fruit
yields two commercially distinct oils with different fatty-acid profiles
and different food and industrial uses — a genuinely two-oil processing
chain, distinct from every other single-oil tree crop this project has
researched so far.

**Finding 2 — the planting material is a dura × pisifera (tenera) hybrid,
and the cross is controlled by a single gene for shell thickness.** The
shell-thickness locus is co-dominant and monogenic. Dura palms are
homozygous wild-type, have a thick shell (2-8 mm) and lower oil yield
(about 5.3 t oil/ha/yr); pisifera palms are homozygous mutant, have no
shell at all but are usually female-sterile; tenera palms are the
heterozygous F1, with a thin shell, a fibre ring around the nut, and the
highest commercial oil yield (about 7.4 t/ha/yr) [9]. Because pisifera is
female-sterile, tenera seed cannot be grown by simply letting tenera
palms self-pollinate (that gives a 1:2:1 dura:tenera:pisifera
segregation, not a useful commercial seedlot) — it has to be made fresh
every generation by hand-crossing a dura mother with pisifera pollen.

**Finding 3 — the fruit is extremely perishable from the moment it is
cut.** Free fatty acids (FFA) in the mesocarp rise rapidly once the bunch
is harvested, driven by lipase activity and bruising; one study found FFA
highest within 1-2 days of harvest, and mills aim to sterilize (which
halts the enzyme) within about 24 hours of cutting [12][13]. FFA level is
the primary price grade for crude palm oil, so the window between field
and mill floor is the single most labour-critical link in the whole
chain.

**Finding 4 — threshing (bunch-stripping) produces a real, if minor,
byproduct stream: empty fruit bunches (EFB).** EFB is roughly a fifth of
bunch weight [15][16]. It is not useful as mill fuel (described as "the
most problematic waste product" for combustion) but is a real-world
field mulch and nutrient-return practice, now standard at most mills; it
also has secondary minor uses as a lignocellulosic fibre source [15][16].

**Finding 5 — the mesocarp-pressing byproduct (the nut) is not
discarded: it continues down a second real chain into a second oil.**
After CPO is pressed out, the nut (shell + kernel) is cracked; the kernel
presses into palm kernel oil, and the press-cake left over is palm
kernel cake, a genuine livestock feed ingredient (cattle, dairy) [15].
The shell itself is burned for mill steam/power, or made into charcoal
and activated carbon [15][16].

**Finding 6 — composition differs sharply between the two oils, and that
difference is the whole reason both are kept as separate commercial
products.** CPO is roughly half saturated fat (about 49%), mostly
palmitic acid, and is naturally deep orange-red from a high beta-carotene
content (it is described as the largest natural dietary source of
tocotrienols, though a reliable per-100g figure was not found) [10][11].
PKO is far more saturated (about 80-82%), mostly lauric acid, and —
unlike CPO — has essentially no carotenoids, so it is pale, not red
[10][11]. Both oils are energy-dense, about 860-885 kcal per 100g [10].

**Finding 7 — the hybrid-seed chain is a genuinely reproducible low/
medium-tech process, built on hand pollination.** Commercial dura ×
pisifera seed production crosses a dura seed-parent pool with a pisifera
pollen-parent pool [9]. Dura female inflorescences are bagged before the
flowers open (to exclude stray pollen) and then hand-pollinated with
pisifera pollen collected from the pollen-parent block; the resulting
bunch is harvested and processed as seed, not sent to the mill [9]. This
is the same general hand-pollination technique this project has already
documented for other palm and fruit crops with controlled seed
production.

**Finding 8 — the seed itself is strongly dormant and needs a long,
specific heat treatment before it will germinate at a useful rate.**
Without treatment, germination can fall below 25% after 8 months of
storage [8]. The commercial protocol heats seed at about 40°C for roughly
60-80 days; one dura × pisifera trial found 90-95% germination after
50-70 days of 39°C heat treatment, improving with duration up to a point,
and reported clear progeny-by-duration interaction (genotype-specific
optimal duration) [8]. After germination the seedling spends 10-12 months
in a (often two-stage) nursery before field planting [5][6]. This long,
high-overhead propagation pipeline — hand-cross, harvest, heat-treat,
nursery — is one of the most elaborate seed-to-field chains this project
has researched so far, closer in spirit to grafted fruit trees than to
any annual crop.

**Finding 9 — standard field density is a fixed equilateral-triangle
grid, not a simple row spacing.** About 143 palms/ha is the commonly
cited optimum, from palms set roughly 9 m apart in a triangular pattern
(rows about 7.8 m apart) [5][6]. A Nigerian 16-year trial corroborates a
143-160 palms/ha optimum in the same triangular layout [6]. This
triangular (rather than rectangular) planting geometry is a pattern this
project has not documented for any other crop so far.

**Finding 10 — the main disease has no cure, and the main insect pest is
mechanistically linked to it.** Basal stem rot, caused by *Ganoderma
boninense*, has no effective control method; current research leans on
biological control (mainly *Trichoderma* spp.) and sanitation (removing/
burning infected debris, since basidiospores spread the disease), with no
fully resistant variety yet available [13]. The rhinoceros beetle
(*Oryctes rhinoceros*) breeds in the same rotting oil-palm biomass that
also harbours *Ganoderma* inoculum, tying the two problems together
agronomically even though they are a fungus and an insect [13]. Bagworm
is managed with *Bt* spraying, beneficial-plant intercropping, and
pheromone traps [13].

**Finding 11 — pollination now depends on an introduced weevil, and that
weevil's own population is itself a live agronomic concern.**
*Elaeidobius kamerunicus* was deliberately introduced to Malaysia in the
late 1980s specifically to raise fruit-set and bunch yield (oil palm's
native pollinators are West/Central African and were absent from the
crop's new range); a 2026 review flags declining weevil populations as a
current concern for fruit set in parts of the industry [2]. This makes
oil palm's pollination story unusually concrete for a wind/insect-
pollinated tree crop, but it also means the wild-pollination baseline
(without the weevil) is poorly characterised in the sources found.

**Finding 12 — genome-size sources genuinely disagree, by more than a
factor of one, and the disagreement is itself documented and explained in
the primary literature.** A 1997 flow-cytometry study gave 2C = 3.76 ±
0.09 pg [14]; two 2014 re-measurements, using a different internal
standard, gave 2C = 4.32 ± 0.173 pg and 2C = 4.35 ± 0.03 pg respectively,
and the authors explicitly note that earlier genus-level estimates
conflict with each other, which is why they re-measured it [14]. This is
kept as a genuine, documented disagreement rather than averaged away.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen tree, single-trunked and unbranched,
  growing taller every year; continuous year-round bearing in equatorial
  climates once mature, not a seasonal or annual crop.
- **Climate:** Tropical. Best yields within about 7° of the equator, in
  areas with high, well-distributed rainfall and no marked dry season;
  West Africa's own native climate is not the best for the crop by this
  standard, which is part of why the industry's centre of production
  shifted to Malaysia and Indonesia [18].
- **Food status:** Crude palm oil is edible as pressed, though refined
  before most retail/food-industry use. Not toxic.
- **Pollination:** Monoecious (separate male and female inflorescences on
  the same palm, alternating in phase). Pollination today depends
  commercially on an introduced weevil (*Elaeidobius kamerunicus*,
  Finding 11); wind pollination also occurs but is secondary outside the
  species' native range. Self-fertile in principle; segregation
  (Finding 2) rather than infertility is the real constraint on
  farm-saved seed.
- **Yield / composition:** World average roughly 20,000-24,000 kg FFB/ha,
  good 26,000-37,000 kg FFB/ha, exceptional 30,000+ kg FFB/ha on coastal
  soils, poor around 15,000 kg FFB/ha [3][4]. CPO extraction rate is
  17-22% of FFB weight [1]; combining the FFB yield range with the
  extraction rate gives roughly 4-6 t CPO/ha/yr in good conditions,
  reaching 7-9 t/ha/yr in favourable conditions per one independently
  sourced review — the two figures cross-check well against each other.
- **Water use:** Literature range for mature, well-watered palms gives a
  crop coefficient of roughly 0.8-1.0 (some sources cite 0.75-1.2) [17].
  Young palms (18-33 months) measured lower in one Brazilian study (Kc
  0.71) [17]. No FAO-56-style seasonal stage table exists for this crop,
  since it bears continuously rather than on an annual cycle. A Colombian
  irrigation report gives adult-palm water requirement as roughly
  350-500 L/palm/day, with a 3-day sprinkler interval suggested where
  irrigation is limited [17]. Water deficit reduces bunch number and
  bunch size, though this was not quantified in the sources opened this
  pass. Waterlogging tolerance is poor by general palm-agronomy
  consensus, not independently sourced to a specific threshold this
  pass.
- **Establishment:** Seed is sown in a nursery (not direct-field-sown;
  seed dormancy and the long juvenile period make nursery-raising
  essential), then transplanted as a seedling after 10-12 months, at a
  sourced height criterion of 118-179 cm [5][6]. Bearing starts about
  18-24 months after field planting, i.e. roughly 30-36 months from
  germination once the nursery period is added [3][4]. A two-stage
  nursery (pre-nursery then main nursery) is typical for large-scale
  planting (>500 ha) [5].
- **Stand life:** Replanting age is about 25 years, though palms can
  physically live to about 100 years; growers replant well before
  biological decline because a very tall palm (15-20+ m) becomes
  impractical to harvest by hand sickle [4]. Peak yield is generally
  placed around year 8 [3].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Basal stem rot (*Ganoderma boninense*, no
  cure) and rhinoceros beetle (breeding in the same decaying biomass that
  harbours *Ganoderma*) are the major linked threats (Finding 10).
  Bagworm is a secondary pest managed with *Bt* spraying and other IPM
  measures.
- **Genome:** 2C = 4.32-4.35 pg in more recent (2014) re-measurements; an
  older (1997) study gives a materially different 2C = 3.76 ± 0.09 pg —
  both figures and the documented disagreement between them are kept
  rather than silently averaged (Finding 12).
- **Cultivars:** The three fruit-form classes are not cultivars in the
  usual sense — they are three genotypes at one shell-thickness locus
  underlying essentially every commercial variety. **Dura** (wild-type
  shell genotype): lower oil yield (about 5.3 t oil/ha/yr), thick shell
  (2-8 mm); maintained as the seed-parent pool. **Pisifera** (shell-less
  genotype): no shell at all, usually female-sterile; maintained only as
  a breeding-garden pollen-parent pool, never planted commercially for
  oil. **Tenera** (the dura × pisifera F1 hybrid, the modern commercial
  standard): higher oil yield (about 7.4 t oil/ha/yr), thin shell with a
  fibre ring around the nut; the overwhelming majority of commercial
  plantings worldwide. A fourth, distinct genetic group — an interspecific
  hybrid between *E. guineensis* and the related American oil palm
  species (one named Costa Rican breeding line with 53-56% American-
  oil-palm genetic background) — was developed as a disease-tolerant
  alternative where basal stem rot is severe; it shows slower height
  growth (easier/longer-lived harvesting) and tolerance to bud rot and
  reportedly to *Ganoderma* in some lines, with reported high yield
  potential (>300 kg bunches/palm/year) in one trial, though yield
  effects across this hybrid type generally are described as mixed in
  the wider literature, not uniformly positive [19]. No sourced list of
  named commercial dura/tenera/pisifera breeding lines (the kind of
  named-variety list this project typically finds for other crops) was
  found this pass.
- **Propagation:** Essentially all commercial planting material is the
  dura × pisifera hybrid described in Finding 2; open-pollinated dura
  seed exists and germinates normally but is commercially irrelevant
  given its much lower oil yield. Farm-saved seed from a tenera palm's
  own fruit gives a 1:2:1 segregating progeny — only 1/4 come out tenera,
  so farm-saved tenera seed is a real yield trap, not a viable seed-
  saving strategy.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, continuous/rolling window — not a seasonal-
  flush crop. Harvested year-round, roughly every 10-14 days per palm,
  using a chisel for young, short palms and a pole-mounted sickle for
  mature, tall palms [6]. The 1-day FFA-driven transport deadline
  (Finding 3) is the dominant real constraint on the harvest-to-mill
  chain, more so than raw labor hours.
- **Byproducts:** Empty fruit bunches (roughly a fifth of bunch weight,
  used as field mulch, Finding 4); palm kernel cake (a genuine livestock
  feed ingredient); palm kernel shell (burned for mill fuel, or made into
  charcoal/activated carbon). Mesocarp fibre and palm oil mill effluent
  are additional real mill byproducts not covered in further detail here.

## Hazards

No plant-toxin or food-safety hazard was found for crude palm oil or
palm kernel oil specifically in the sources checked this pass. The
crop's real controversies — tropical deforestation, peatland drainage,
biodiversity loss, and the ongoing dietary-saturated-fat debate — are
environmental/public-health/policy topics, not a plant-toxin or food-
safety hazard in the usual sense, so no `TO BE ADDRESSED` hazard entry is
recorded here; a land-use/environmental note would be the more
appropriate place for the deforestation/peatland topic if this project
takes that up later.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (mesocarp):** fruit bunch → harvested → threshed/stripped
  (within the 1-day FFA window) → mesocarp pressed → crude palm oil
  (food oil, refined for most retail/industrial use) + empty fruit
  bunches (field mulch byproduct).
- **Chain (kernel):** pressed mesocarp residue (the nut) → cracked →
  kernel pressed → palm kernel oil (food/industrial oil, closer in
  composition to coconut oil) + palm kernel cake (livestock feed) + shell
  (mill fuel, charcoal, activated carbon).
- **Hybrid seed production:** dura female inflorescences bagged before
  anthesis → hand-pollinated with pisifera pollen → bunch harvested and
  processed as seed (not sent to the mill) → seed heat-treated (roughly
  40°C for 60-80 days) to break dormancy → germinated → 10-12 month
  nursery → field transplant.

## Open research questions

- Triangular planting-grid geometry (143 palms/ha on an equilateral-
  triangle grid, Finding 9) is a real, sourced fact distinct from the
  simple row-and-plant spacing this project has used for every other
  tree/perennial crop researched so far.
- No sourced seed-garden seed yield, seed-crop spacing, isolation
  distance, or minimum-plant-population figure was found for the dura
  seed-garden/hand-pollination operation, despite the hand-pollination
  technique itself being well-sourced.
- Two source conflicts are kept visible rather than resolved: genome size
  (1997 vs. 2014 measurements, Finding 12), and the exact FFA-rise timing
  window ("within 24 hours" vs. one study's "highest 2 days after
  harvest" — plausibly reconciled as fruit-position-within-bunch
  variation, but not independently confirmed).
- No sourced soil pH range, texture tolerance, or nutrient-uptake-per-
  tonne figure was found this pass, despite oil palm being one of the
  most agronomically studied tropical tree crops in the world.
- No sourced list of named commercial breeding lines/cultivars was found
  this pass; the genotype-class structure above substitutes for a
  conventional cultivar list.
- The interspecific hybrid with the American oil palm species (Finding
  12 cultivar note) is a real, increasingly significant disease-tolerant
  alternative with its own genetics, growth habit, and mixed yield
  picture — a candidate for separate treatment as a distinct crop/cross
  rather than a cultivar note, if this project revisits it.
- Mesocarp fibre and palm oil mill effluent are both real mill
  byproducts not covered here in process detail; the effluent's biogas-
  capture angle in particular is an industrial wastewater-treatment topic
  without a clean analogue among this project's other oil-crop writeups.
