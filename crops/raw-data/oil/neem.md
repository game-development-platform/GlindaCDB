# Neem (*Azadirachta indica*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/neem.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Azadirachta indica* A.Juss., family Meliaceae, a
fast-growing evergreen (or briefly leaf-shedding in extreme drought) tree
native to the Indian subcontinent and now very widely planted throughout
the tropics and subtropics. This is the single species behind "neem" in
agronomic and pharmacological literature. It is categorized here as an
oil crop rather than a medicinal crop, since seed oil is neem's single
most economically dominant real product even though the tree is at least
as famous for biopesticide and traditional-medicine use. Jatropha is a
useful comparison crop: both are toxic-seeded, multi-use, non-food
perennial trees whose oil's real economic case is industrial/biopesticide
rather than culinary, both have a documented press-cake detoxification
literature with feeding-trial results, and both combine a dominant
commercial use with a secondary traditional-medicine use. Neem diverges
from jatropha in three important ways: neem's toxin (azadirachtin and
related limonoids) is deliberately exploited as the VALUABLE product (the
seed oil IS the biopesticide, unlike jatropha's curcin/phorbol esters,
which are purely a hazard to manage), neem has a far deeper and
longer-standing traditional-medicine literature ("the village pharmacy"),
and neem is reported as drought-tolerant in a way that is not tied to a
documented large-scale plantation-failure history, unlike jatropha's own
disputed boom-era drought claims.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the seed's oil extraction and depulping chain is
reasonably well described, but the seed-to-oil yield conversion rests on
only loosely related per-tree figures, not a dedicated field-trial
per-hectare study.** To obtain neem oil, fruit is collected (a mature
tree yields roughly 35-50 kg fruit/year in one source), the pulp is
removed from the seed, and the kernels are pressed in industrial
expellers or traditional hand/bullock-operated wooden presses (ghanis); a
warm-press variant uses 40-50°C [1]. A large Indian industry additionally
solvent-extracts the oil remaining in the press-cake using hexane [1].
One source gives a rough fruit-to-seed conversion (about 350 kg fruit
yields about 30 kg seed, ~8.6%) [1], used here as the best-available
yield-chain basis for lack of a better-sourced figure — a notably thin
basis for a species this economically important.

**Finding 2 — reported oil content varies widely by provenance and
rainfall, a pattern this project has now seen repeatedly across tree
oilseeds, but with an unusually wide top-to-bottom spread for neem
specifically.** One source puts oil yield at 40-43% of seed dry weight,
citing a Rajasthan provenance trial with a high of 43.2% (Banswara
region) and a low of 32.4% (Jaisalmer region) [2]. Other sources report
roughly 45% [2], up to 50% [2], or — in a wider 2015 literature review —
a spread from 25% to 45% across kernels generally [2]. Oil content is
reported to rise with rainfall: among international provenances tested, a
Bangladesh provenance gave the highest tested oil content (48.6%) [2].
This file does not pick one figure; the full 25-50% range is carried,
with the 40-43% figure flagged as the most commonly cited central
estimate.

**Finding 3 — azadirachtin, the compound responsible for neem's own
toxicity, is simultaneously its single most economically important
product.** Azadirachtin is a tetranortriterpenoid limonoid, and the seed
kernel is its richest source in the plant, making the seed (not the leaf
or bark) the most effective organ for biopesticide production [3].
Mechanistically, a 2024 cotton-bollworm study found azadirachtin-A acts
on several protein targets at once — a multi-target mode of action the
study's authors suggest may make resistance development harder for
pests, compared with a single-target insecticide [3]. In *Spodoptera
litura* larvae, azadirachtin damages the midgut through apoptosis, and a
separate study reports it also disrupts insect endocrine/neuroendocrine
function [3]. Dose-response is target- and formulation-dependent: in one
*S. litura* study, antifeedant effects appeared around 10-100 ppm and
growth inhibition around 1-10 ppm; a tea-pest field trial found about
50,000 ppm (a bulk field concentration, not comparable to the lab doses)
gave 65% control, versus under 30% at 300-1,500 ppm [3]. Azadirachtin is
a registered biopesticide in more than 40 countries; commercial products
vary widely in concentration [3]. This two-faced relationship — the same
compound is both the reason the seed and leaf are hazardous to eat/ingest
and the entire basis of the crop's industrial value — has no precedent
among the other toxic-seeded oil crops this project has researched, where
the toxin (ricin, curcin, phorbol esters, glucosinolates) is purely
something to manage or remove.

**Finding 4 — propagation is dual-path (seed or vegetative) like several
other oil trees this project has researched, but neem's sourced
years-to-bearing figures show an unusually wide, only partly reconciled
spread, with grafting shortening a reportedly very slow seed-grown
timeline.** Neem is commonly grown from seed, with fresh-seed germination
around 90%, and can also be propagated by root cuttings [4]. A
grafting-method comparison found cleft grafting most successful among
cleft, side, and budding grafts, giving better and earlier flowering than
other methods within three years of grafting [4]. Years-to-bearing
sources conflict sharply: one forestry-journal study describes
seed-grown neem as slow-growing, taking MORE THAN 15 YEARS to reach
flowering and seed production [4]; a cultivation guide instead says
fruiting starts at 4-5 years with full bearing at 10-11 years [4]; another
cultivation guide says yield stabilizes from the ninth year [4]; a
separate general source gives fruiting starting around 3-5 years with
full production at 10-12 years [4]. This file keeps all these figures
visible as a genuine, unresolved source conflict rather than picking one.

**Finding 5 — pollination is a documented weak point, with a 2025 study
specifically attributing neem's poor natural fruit set to pollination
failure rather than resource limitation, and a notably low
self-incompatible fruit-set figure.** A 2025 study found neem is
ambophilous (both wind- and insect-pollinated), with protandrous flowers
(pollen released before the stigma becomes receptive) [5]. The same study
concludes neem REQUIRES cross-pollination because it is self-
incompatible, and reports natural fruit set at only approximately 5% —
attributed to poor pollen landing, a low pollen-to-ovule ratio, pollen
wastage, limited tree-population distribution, and too few pollinators
[5]. The study went on to test supplemental pollination by pollen
suspension as a remedy, implying this low fruit-set figure is seen as a
real agronomic problem worth engineering around, not just an academic
curiosity [5]. A caveat worth flagging: a 2024 study of *Melia azedarach*
(Maha-neem, a close relative in the same family) found no substantial
fruit-set difference between natural pollination and autogamy
(self-pollination), which the study's own authors read as evidence
favoring self-compatibility in THAT species [5] — directly conflicting
with neem's own self-incompatibility finding, though for a different
species, so it does not resolve or undermine the neem-specific result. No
dedicated controlled self-vs-cross pollination trial specifically for
*A. indica* (beyond the 2025 study) was found this pass.

**Finding 6 — the seed cake is nutrient-rich and toxic in a similar way
to other toxic-seeded oil crops' press-cakes, but neem's detoxification
literature is unusually mature, with quantified removal percentages and
real feeding-trial substitution limits.** Neem cake's bitterness and
toxicity come from limonoids — named compounds include azadirachtin,
nimbin, and salannin [6]. In one Indian study, water washing alone
removed 82.53% of azadirachtin, 53.13% of nimbin, and 35.89% of salannin;
water washing FOLLOWED BY urea treatment improved this to 83.27%
azadirachtin removal and 100% removal of both nimbin and salannin [6].
Water-washed kernel cake was reported palatable to cattle despite its
bitter smell [6]. Longer-term feeding trials found detoxified neem cake
safe as a soybean-meal-nitrogen replacement up to 50% in male lambs, and
up to 75% of soybean-meal protein in a goat study without affecting
carcass characteristics or organ histopathology [6]. A review nonetheless
cautions that many technical, economic, and food-safety issues remain
before these detoxified products could be broadly recommended for
livestock, and commercial "detoxified" product analyses still show
residual toxin (one listed 0.032% azadirachtin, 492 TIU/g
trypsin-inhibitor activity, 0.025% salannin) [6] — so detoxification is
real and quantified, but incomplete.

**Finding 7 — neem's traditional-medicine use is unusually deep and
broad, earning it the epithet "the village pharmacy" in India, though
clinical efficacy evidence is explicitly described as thin.** Bark, wood,
sap, leaves, flowers, fruit, seeds, and oil are ALL used medicinally in
traditional Indian practice [7]. Leaf and bark preparations have
historically been used against intestinal worms, constipation, leprosy,
and skin conditions such as eczema and scabies [7]. More than 140
compounds have been isolated from different parts of the tree, with
nimbolide cited as one example of a compound with laboratory-reported
bioactivity; leaf studies report a broad spread of laboratory/animal-
level pharmacological properties (immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory,
antihyperglycaemic, antiulcer, antimalarial, antifungal, antibacterial,
antiviral, antioxidant, antimutagenic, anticarcinogenic) [7]. A
dermatology review is explicit that, despite this extensive traditional
use in India, there is no evidence of clinical efficacy for most of these
specific claims [7] — one of the clearest, most direct honesty-labeled
caveats this project has found for any crop's medicinal-use claims. The
leaf is worth treating as its own harvested product, distinct from the
fruit/seed oil chain, since it is gathered on a different schedule for a
different (non-oil) use.

**Finding 8 — neem's cultivation envelope is genuinely well-sourced as
drought-hardy and thermally tolerant, but the sources directly disagree
on salinity tolerance, an unusually visible three-way conflict for a
soil-tolerance trait.** Neem is described as highly drought-tolerant,
able to survive 7-8-month dry seasons once established, with a normal
temperature range of roughly 9.5-37°C (though it tolerates more extreme
heat) [8]. Suitable rainfall is given as roughly 450-1,200 mm/year by one
agronomy portal, while another source reports successful introductions
at only 200-250 mm/year [8]. Soil pH preference clusters near neutral to
slightly alkaline: one source gives 6.2-7.0 as preferred with tolerance
from 5.0-8.0; another reports good performance from pH 5.0-8.5, with best
growth on deep, well-drained soils at pH 6.0-6.5; the tree does poorly on
high-clay soils or soils above about pH 8 [8]. Waterlogging is the
clearest limit: neem should not be planted where soils become waterlogged
or seasonally flooded [8]. Salinity tolerance is a genuine three-way
source conflict: one landscape-plant profile rates it POOR, a general/
commercial source describes it as salt-TOLERANT, and an agronomy
extension source takes a middle position (does not grow well on sodic
soils; saline sites need supplemental watering during summer/drought)
[8] — kept visible rather than resolved. The tree's extensive root
system is credited for its drought-hardiness, and it can grow from sea
level to about 1,500 m elevation [8].

**Finding 9 — neem is a documented, named environmental weed/invasive
species in parts of Australia and sub-Saharan Africa, a real land-
management fact.** Neem is regarded as an environmental weed in
Australia's Northern Territory, northern Queensland, and northern Western
Australia, with its largest Australian infestations along the Gilbert
River (Queensland), the Victoria River Downs area (Northern Territory),
and the Ord River (Western Australia); seedlings have also been found
germinating in dense shade at riparian/revegetation sites near Townsville
[9]. Concern extends beyond simple spread: one source raises concern over
neem's potential impact on native insect populations, and cites evidence
that neem extracts can affect aquatic wildlife such as fish and tadpoles
[9] — a real ecological-toxicity concern mechanistically plausible given
the azadirachtin biopesticide activity that makes the seed oil
commercially valuable in the first place (Finding 3). Neem is also
reported as a weed across much of sub-Saharan Africa and in the Middle
East, with large infestations in Ghana's Accra plains and elsewhere in
West Africa, though Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda do not list it as a
noxious weed [9]. Seeds spread down watercourses and via fruit-eating
birds [9].

**Finding 10 — the published chromosome-level genome assembly finds no
evidence of recent whole-genome duplication, making neem a diploid genome
in contrast to some other oil trees this project has researched that are
polyploid.** A 2022 chromosome-level assembly anchors the genome at 281
Mb across 14 chromosomes (implying a haploid number of n=14; the diploid
2n count was not explicitly stated in the sources opened, though the
chromosome-level assembly structure strongly implies it), with 115 Mb of
repetitive elements and 25,767 protein-coding genes [10]. The study
explicitly concludes *A. indica* did not experience a whole-genome-
duplication event after the ancestral core-eudicot γ event, though some
genes and genome segments show evidence of recent small-scale
duplication [10] — notably, the authors suggest these duplication events
may be responsible for the EXPANSION of terpene biosynthesis genes that
underlies azadirachtin and the wider limonoid chemistry central to
Finding 3, with chromosome 13 specifically carrying most of the
species-specific terpene genes [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Evergreen (or briefly leaf-shedding only under
  extreme drought) perennial tree — the only oil tree this project has
  researched so far described as genuinely evergreen across most of its
  range rather than drought-deciduous (jatropha, argan) or seasonally
  deciduous (shea). Bears repeated annual fruit crops once established.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, semi-arid. Native to the Indian
  subcontinent; very widely introduced across the tropics/subtropics;
  drought-tolerant to 7-8-month dry seasons once established (Finding
  8), so includes semi-arid zones unlike most other oil trees this
  project has researched. Grows from sea level to about 1,500 m
  elevation; tolerates "extremely high temperatures" beyond its normal
  9.5-37°C range, though no specific upper kill threshold was sourced.
  No frost-kill threshold was sourced, though its tropical/subtropical/
  semi-arid range suggests limited cold tolerance.
- **Food status:** Not a food crop; oil, seed, and cake are all toxic
  raw/unprocessed to some degree (azadirachtin, nimbin, salannin), and
  ingestion of the oil specifically is a documented human poisoning
  hazard, especially in infants. Detoxified cake is a partial exception
  for livestock feed only, not human food (Finding 6).
- **Pollination:** Ambophilous (wind and insect), protandrous, self-
  incompatible, requiring cross-pollination; natural fruit set only
  approximately 5% (Finding 5) [5] — the lowest sourced natural fruit-set
  figure for any oil crop this project has researched so far. The low
  figure is attributed to poor pollen landing, a low pollen-to-ovule
  ratio, pollen wastage, limited tree-population distribution, and too
  few pollinators. A 2025 study tested supplemental hand-pollination
  (pollen suspension) specifically because natural pollinator service was
  judged insufficient, implying real-world pollinator scarcity is a
  genuine production constraint. Cross-compatibility with the related
  *Melia azedarach* was not addressed.
- **Yield / composition:** No per-hectare field-trial figure was found
  this pass for any tier; only per-tree fruit-yield figures (35-50 kg
  fruit/tree/year) and one fruit-to-seed conversion ratio (~8.6%) exist
  (Finding 1) — a genuinely thinner per-hectare yield basis than most
  other oil trees this project has researched. Oil content is 25-50% of
  seed/kernel depending on provenance and rainfall, with 40-43% most
  commonly cited as a central estimate (Finding 2) — one of the widest
  sourced oil-content ranges this project has found for any oil crop.
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-compatible crop coefficient (Kc) table was
  found. Drought-hardiness is explicitly credited to an "extensive root
  system," a stronger qualitative anchor than for some other oil trees
  this project has researched where no rooting-depth claim was found at
  all. Waterlogging tolerance is explicitly sourced as poor: the tree
  "should not be planted where soils become waterlogged or seasonally
  flooded" (Finding 8) — a more directly sourced statement than for most
  other oil trees this project has researched. Rainfall tolerance ranges
  widely: 450-1,200 mm/year per one source, with successful introductions
  reported at only 200-250 mm/year per another (Finding 8). Saline-site
  growers are advised to provide supplemental watering during summer/
  drought periods, per one agronomy extension source.
- **Soil:** Best growth at pH 6.0-6.5; tolerance range 5.0-8.5 across
  sources; poor growth above pH ~8 and on high-clay soils (Finding 8).
  Well-drained loamy, sandy, or clay-loam soils are preferred. Salinity
  tolerance is a genuine three-way source conflict, kept visible rather
  than resolved (Finding 8).
- **Establishment:** Triple propagation path — seed, root cuttings, AND
  grafting (cleft grafting found most successful among cleft/side/budding
  methods) — a wider sourced propagation-method spread than for some
  other oil trees this project has researched. Fresh seed germinates at
  about 90%; seed viability is described only qualitatively as short
  ("viable mainly when fresh"), with no specific days/months-to-decline
  figure found. Grafted plants flower earlier (within three years of
  grafting) than seed-grown plants.
- **Stand life:** Widely conflicting years-to-bearing figures (Finding
  4): more than 15 years to flowering (one study) vs. 3-5 years to first
  fruiting with full production at 10-12 years (other sources) vs. yield
  stabilizing from the ninth year (another source) — the widest sourced
  years-to-bearing spread this project has found for any oil tree so
  far. Productive life: one cultivation guide estimates economic life at
  150-200 years; several lower-quality sources claim over 200 years under
  ideal, unmanaged conditions, but these were flagged in the sources
  opened as likely low-quality content and are weighted lightly here — if
  the 150-200-year estimate holds, this would be among the longest-lived
  oil crops this project has researched.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No specific pest or disease entry was sourced
  for neem itself this pass, despite the tree's own biopesticidal
  chemistry — a genuine open question for whether neem shows any
  self-protective effect, in contrast with jatropha's sourced "not
  resistant to pests and diseases despite toxic seed" finding for a
  different, non-self-protective toxin.
- **Genome:** Diploid implied (haploid n=14, 2n count not explicitly
  stated), 281 Mb across 14 chromosomes, 115 Mb repetitive elements,
  25,767 protein-coding genes; no evidence of post-γ whole-genome
  duplication, though recent small-scale gene/segment duplications are
  implicated in the species' own terpene-biosynthesis-gene expansion
  (Finding 10) [10].
- **Cultivars:** No sourced list of named commercial cultivars with
  comparative trait deltas (yield, oil content, growth habit, pest/
  disease resistance) was found this pass — notably thinner than even
  castor's or jatropha's own already-thin cultivar information, somewhat
  surprising for a species this widely and long cultivated. The only
  genuinely distinguished genetic groups sourced are PROVENANCES
  (geographic seed sources with different oil-content figures), not
  named cultivars in the usual sense. **Unselected/wild-provenance seed
  stock** (e.g. Rajasthan's Jaisalmer region) has lower sourced oil
  content (32.4%) [2]. **Higher-oil-content provenances** (e.g.
  Rajasthan's Banswara region, Bangladesh) have higher sourced oil
  content (43.2% Banswara; 48.6% Bangladesh, the highest tested) [2].
- **Propagation:** No commercial F1 hybrid seed system was sourced this
  pass; propagation in the sources opened here is by seed, root
  cuttings, or grafting (cleft grafting preferred), not a maintained
  hybrid cross, the same no-hybrid-system pattern seen in jatropha.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one annual fruit crop per
  year from an established perennial stand, once production stabilizes.
  Fruit ripens over several weeks and is collected as it falls naturally
  or by beating branches, then depulped to free the seed, then dried
  before pressing. A separate leaf harvest (for traditional-medicine use)
  happens year-round as needed, on an unrelated schedule.

## Hazards

- **Azadirachtin (and related limonoids: nimbin, salannin)** — a plant
  toxin that is UNIQUELY, in this project's research so far, also the
  deliberately exploited biopesticide active ingredient (Finding 3).
  Found in seed, oil, and press cake; detoxified cake is a partial
  exception for livestock feed only, not human food (Finding 6). Trigger:
  ingestion of crude oil, raw seed, or undertreated cake; documented human
  poisoning risk, with infants and young children specifically named as
  most vulnerable. Mitigation: water-washing (removes 82.53% azadirachtin,
  53.13% nimbin, 35.89% salannin from cake) with optional urea treatment
  (improves removal to 83.27%/100%/100% respectively). Residual risk is
  real even after detoxification — commercial "detoxified" products still
  show residual toxin; a review explicitly cautions many technical/
  economic/food-safety issues remain before broad livestock
  recommendation.
- **Acute human poisoning from neem oil ingestion**: a severe, specifically
  named syndrome distinct from the general limonoid-toxicity entry above
  — reported in infants and young children, including metabolic acidosis,
  seizures, kidney failure, encephalopathy, and severe brain ischemia.
  This appears to be among the most severe acute human-ingestion hazard
  profiles this project has sourced for any oil crop's finished product
  (as opposed to raw seed/cake).
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of both the limonoid press-cake
  hazard and the acute neem-oil poisoning syndrome is deferred until this
  project has a place for this kind of health/safety data. Crude neem
  oil is explicitly described as not a cooking oil, and the sources
  opened did not describe any sanctioned human-food use of it.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fruit → collected (fallen or beaten from branches) →
  depulped (seed freed) → dried → pressed (expeller/ghani, or hexane
  solvent extraction of residual press-cake oil) → crude oil (dominant
  use: biopesticide; secondary uses: traditional medicine, cosmetics) and
  press cake (most commonly used directly, untreated, as a biopesticidal
  soil amendment; a minority is water-washed and optionally urea-treated
  for partial detoxification before limited livestock feed use).
- **Leaf** is harvested separately, year-round, for traditional-medicine
  use, directly usable without further processing.

## Open research questions

- Per-hectare yield figures are essentially unsourced: only per-tree
  fruit-yield figures and one fruit-to-seed conversion ratio were found,
  with no sourced plantation tree-density figure to convert them — more
  surprising than for some other tree oilseeds given how widely and
  commercially neem is cultivated worldwide.
- Cross-compatibility with the related *Melia azedarach* (Maha-neem) was
  not addressed; that species' own pollination study found a conflicting
  result (implying self-compatibility), raising an interesting but
  unresolved question of how closely related the two species' breeding
  systems actually are.
- No named commercial cultivars were found at all, only regional
  provenances with different oil-content figures — somewhat surprising
  for a species this widely and long cultivated.
- No pest or disease entry was sourced for neem itself, despite the
  tree's own biopesticidal chemistry — an interesting open question for
  whether neem shows any self-protective effect against its own common
  pests/diseases.
- The genome-to-chemistry link (gene-family duplication driving
  terpene-biosynthesis expansion, Finding 10) is a genuinely interesting
  finding without an obvious place in a simple ploidy/genome-size
  framework.
- No sect-specific religious/ritual-use entry was found despite neem's
  clear centrality to South Asian traditional culture — flagged as a
  possible under-search rather than a confident absence, given how
  thoroughly documented neem's general cultural and medicinal
  significance is.
- No seed-viability-duration, germination-rate-beyond-year-1, soil-
  nutrient-uptake, or storage-days figure was found at all for neem —
  each a real gap for a species this economically important and widely
  studied.
- The salinity-tolerance three-way source conflict (Finding 8) is kept
  visible rather than resolved — a genuinely unusual degree of direct
  disagreement for a soil-tolerance trait.
- Neem's weed/invasive-species status in Australia and parts of Africa
  (Finding 9) is a real land-management fact without an obvious place in
  a simple crop-data framework, similar to land-use controversies
  documented for some other oil crops this project has researched.
