# Jatropha (*Jatropha curcas*)

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Jatropha curcas* L., family Euphorbiaceae, diploid
(2n=22), a drought-deciduous perennial shrub or small tree native to
Mexico and Central America and widely introduced elsewhere in the
tropics/subtropics as a biodiesel crop. This is the single cultivated
species behind the name "jatropha" in the biodiesel literature (other
*Jatropha* species exist but are not the subject of commercial oil
cultivation sourced here). Castor is a useful comparison crop for
grounding: both are toxic-seeded, non-food Euphorbiaceae oilseeds whose
toxin stays mostly in the press-cake, both are grown partly for
industrial/biodiesel use, and both show an oil-content/yield decoupling
pattern across trials. Jatropha diverges sharply from castor on growth
habit (perennial woody shrub vs. castor's warm-season annual/short-lived
perennial), on toxin identity (curcin/phorbol esters vs. ricin, with a
materially different detoxification difficulty), and most importantly on
its real-world history: jatropha's promised agronomic case (drought
tolerance, marginal-land yield) was widely oversold during a global
2000s-2010s biodiesel investment boom and then did not hold up under
field conditions, leading to extensive plantation abandonment.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — jatropha seed is acutely toxic to humans and animals, with
curcin and phorbol esters as the two main implicated toxins, and a
documented but still partly contested mechanism.** The common toxic
genotype's seeds contain curcin, a ribosome-inactivating toxalbumin that
halts protein synthesis (the same general mechanism category as castor's
ricin, though a distinct molecule), and phorbol esters, diterpene
compounds with a strong purgative effect; other compounds including
additional diterpenes and lectins also contribute [1]. Human poisoning is
mostly accidental seed ingestion, often in children; a hospital
toxicology reference reports as few as 3 seeds can be toxic, with a
lethal dose of roughly 7-8 seeds, though most patients recover with
supportive care (fluid/electrolyte correction, activated charcoal) [1].
Plant-cell genotoxicity studies found the oil acts as both an aneugen
(causing lagging/sticky chromosomes) and a clastogen (chromosome breakage)
[1]. A 2020 study specifically challenges the long-standing
phorbol-ester-as-primary-toxin consensus, arguing instead that
hydroxy-octadecenoic acids are the main toxin behind acute lethality,
pulmonary hemorrhage, and diarrhea in animal kernel-cake studies; phorbol
esters remain the more widely cited explanation, so this is kept as a
visible, unresolved source conflict rather than picked one way [1]. A
risk-assessment review derived an acute reference dose of 139.64 µg/kg
body weight (as TPA equivalents) for phorbol esters and a sub-chronic
health-based guidance value of 0.0105 mg/kg/day, and set a 99.5%
detoxification target for any seed oil intended for food use [1] — a
notably well-quantified toxicology figure for a seed toxin, more
precisely characterized in the regulatory literature than castor's ricin.

**Finding 2 — jatropha is propagated by seed OR by stem cuttings, with a
real, sourced tradeoff between the two methods, and no sourced commercial
F1 hybrid seed system.** Seed propagation produces large genetic
variability in growth, biomass, seed yield, and oil content, since
open-pollinated seed segregates [2]. Cuttings root readily — one study
found 2000 ppm IBA gave 96.5% rooting in spring cuttings, and a 40 cm
stem cutting 2.5-3.0 cm thick was described as suitable for quick
regeneration even without rooting hormone [2]. A direct seed-vs-cuttings
comparison found cutting-propagated plants flowered about 4 days earlier
than seed-propagated plants, but seed-propagated plants had better
biometric and seed-quality traits; cuttings tend to produce more branches
but fewer seeds [2]. No commercial F1 hybrid seed system was sourced this
pass — unlike castor's well-documented two-line hybrid system, jatropha
breeding and multiplication in the sources opened here relies on
open-pollinated seed or vegetative cuttings, not a maintained hybrid
cross.

**Finding 3 — jatropha is monoecious with a strongly male-skewed flower
ratio, and can be both protandrous and protogynous, supporting both
self- and cross-pollination, with cross-pollination giving materially
higher fruit set.** Flowers are unisexual and monoecious, with most
plants flowering after the rainy season; one study found an average
male-to-female flower ratio of 20:1, rising sharply as temperatures fell
[3]. The species can be both protandrous (male parts mature first) and
protogynous (female parts mature first) depending on the individual
flower, and is capable of producing seed through both self- and
cross-pollination [3]. One study found only 32.9% fruit set under
controlled self-pollination versus 89.7% under natural (open, presumably
insect-assisted) pollination, and cross-pollinated flowers showed
significantly higher fruit set than self-pollinated flowers [3] — a
materially larger self-vs-cross fruit-set gap than this project has
sourced for any other self-compatible oil crop so far.

**Finding 4 — oil content and seed yield both vary widely by genotype,
age, fertilization, and region, continuing a recurring oil-content/yield
decoupling pattern seen in other toxic-seeded oil crops this project has
researched.** A Colombian field trial found seed yields of 2,430-4,746
kg/ha with seed oil content of 38.7-40.1% (w/w); nitrogen-plus-potassium
fertilization roughly doubled total oil output, from 947 to 1,900 kg
oil/ha, without the oil-content percentage itself changing nearly as much
as the seed yield did [4]. A Chinese seed-source selection study found
its best-performing sources reached a peak of 783 kg oil/ha at age three —
described by the study's own authors as 2.7 times the sourced
national-average best yield [4]. Indonesian accession studies report seed
dry weight around 5,500-6,000 kg/ha at five years of age, though the
source text was ambiguous about whether this was a seed or a fruit
(pod-inclusive) figure [4]. Industry/extension sources (lower
reliability, not relied on as primary) claim up to 7 t seed/ha and
2.2-2.7 t oil/ha under good conditions; a separate literature review
instead reports much lower realized field yields (roughly 0.35-1.4
t/ha/yr, a figure the search process itself flagged as possibly a unit
typo), explicitly noting "a large gap between projected and actual seed
production" [4]. Rigorously measured field trials in the sources opened
this pass cluster around 2-5 t seed/ha and 0.8-2 t oil/ha, with oil
content typically 33-40% of seed weight.

**Finding 5 — the seed cake is nutrient-rich and usable as fertilizer
without detoxification, but is NOT safe livestock feed without thorough
detoxification that review literature describes as achievable only at
laboratory scale.** The cake's toxicity comes from phorbol esters, curcin,
and trypsin inhibitors acting together, which makes full detoxification
difficult; a 2018 review in a peer-reviewed food-chemistry journal covers
chemical, physical, biological, and combined detoxification processes,
concluding detoxification has succeeded at laboratory scale but the
process is too complex for small-scale or local/on-farm use [5]. A
university thesis tested lactic-acid-bacteria silage fermentation
combined with ethanol or sodium-hydroxide solvent extraction, finding
ethanol extraction more effective at removing phorbol esters [5]. After
fermentation, most nutrients remain in the effluent, which can still be
recycled as fertilizer even without full toxin removal [5]. Separately,
phorbol esters themselves show documented molluscicidal, insecticidal,
and fungicidal activity in laboratory and field trials — a real but
not-yet-commercialized biopesticide angle [5].

**Finding 6 — the oil (not just the seed) has real non-food industrial,
cosmetic, and traditional-medicine uses beyond biodiesel, concentrated in
the plant's native Mesoamerican range.** A 2026 review describes the
genus as having originated in the Americas, with its highest genetic
diversity in Mexico and Central America, where it is often grown as a
hedge/live fence and used in traditional medicine for its insecticidal,
wound-healing, and anti-inflammatory properties, applying the whitish
latex directly [6]. A separate ethnobotany paper on the Olmec region of
Veracruz, Mexico confirms *J. curcas* is still used there to treat skin
diseases, though that paper's informants were working with the common
toxic genotype rather than the rare non-toxic one (Finding 7), so the
medicinal-use finding does not by itself establish that the oil/latex
used is detoxified or food-safe [6]. A biodiesel-industry patent
background note states the oil is also used in soap and cosmetics
manufacture in various tropical countries [6].

**Finding 7 — a rare non-toxic genotype of *J. curcas* exists, but has
been registered only in Mexico, and is a materially different food-safety
case from the common toxic genotype grown for biodiesel worldwide.** A
2018 genetic study reports non-toxic genotypes registered only in Mexico,
with relatively high SNP diversity in the non-toxic type concentrated in
northern Veracruz state, identified as the probable origin of this
germplasm [7]. The common cultivated genotype grown for biodiesel
elsewhere in the tropics/subtropics (the type behind essentially all of
Findings 1, 4, 5, 8) is the toxic type; the non-toxic genotype is not
described in the sources opened this pass as being in wide commercial
cultivation for food use.

**Finding 8 — jatropha's reputation as a drought-tolerant, marginal-land
biodiesel crop drove a major 2000s-2010s global planting boom whose
promised yields and land-use assumptions did not hold up under field
conditions, and the resulting wave of plantation failures is itself a
well-documented, citable fact about this crop.** Millions of hectares
were planted across India, Asia, and Africa in the 2000s, often with
financial incentives including free seedling supply, on the strength of
claims that jatropha could grow on degraded/marginal land and tolerate
drought [8]. Field results disappointed: a Burkina Faso Sahelian-zone
drought study found all seedlings died within 2 years of establishment,
dying specifically during peak dry season, leading the study's authors to
conclude *J. curcas* may not be as drought-resistant as earlier
literature suggested [8] — directly conflicting with a separate review's
claim that more recent studies confirm the species can thrive in arid
conditions via a "drought-avoidance" strategy (leaf drop, Finding 9) [8].
Researchers studying the "marginal land" framing found the definition
itself was often applied loosely: a Ghana-focused study found land
assumed to be unused and degraded was often not actually idle, and a
Dominican Republic project report is quoted as observing that "marginal
land often bears marginal results" [8]. Reported side effects of the
failed boom included accusations of land grabs, conversion of food-crop
land to jatropha, and clearing of natural vegetation [8].

**Finding 9 — jatropha's actual drought response is a leaf-drop
(deciduous) drought-avoidance strategy rather than a true
drought-tolerance mechanism, and this distinction likely explains much of
the boom-era overselling in Finding 8.** The plant is drought-deciduous,
dropping its leaves in dry conditions as a water-conservation strategy,
which some reviews describe as a "drought-avoidance" mechanism letting it
survive dry periods rather than actively grow through them [9] (the
specific physiological leaf-drop-as-avoidance mechanism is stated
qualitatively rather than quantified with a specific threshold or
dose-response curve this pass). This matters directly for yield
expectations: a plant that survives drought by shedding leaves and
effectively pausing growth is not the same thing as a plant that
maintains yield under water stress, which is consistent with the gap
between jatropha's drought-survival reputation and its disappointing
realized seed yields under the marginal, low-input conditions the
boom-era plantations actually used (Finding 8).

**Finding 10 — jatropha is not pest- or disease-resistant despite its
toxic seed, and mosaic virus is a specifically severe, yield-destroying
threat.** A Senegal River valley survey explicitly notes that despite the
toxicity of its seed oil, jatropha is "not resistant to pests and
diseases" [10]. Jatropha mosaic disease, caused by begomoviruses spread
by whitefly vectors, is described as a major obstacle to large-scale
cultivation; vectors that acquire the virus from an infected plant can
transmit it to an uninfected plant within 48 hours [10]. One Indian study
found mosaic virus infection cut oil content by 41.78% compared to
healthy plants — a severe, specifically quantified yield/quality impact —
and found that monocrotophos and confidor insecticide treatments reduced
both whitefly vector populations and disease incidence [10]. Other
reported pests and diseases include cutworms (which cut the stem near
the soil surface, potentially killing the plant), tip borer caterpillars
(attacking during blooming), and in the Philippines, fungal leaf spot and
marginal necrosis [10].

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Drought-deciduous perennial shrub/small tree;
  not an annual crop at any stage of its sourced cultivation. Bears
  repeated annual seed crops once established, with no sourced
  single-harvest-then-terminate path.
- **Climate:** Tropical, subtropical, warm temperate. Native to Mexico/
  Central America; extremely widely introduced across the tropics/
  subtropics for biodiesel cultivation during the 2000s-2010s boom
  (Finding 8). Drought-deciduous rather than frost-hardy, so likely
  excludes cool temperate and colder zones, though no specific frost-kill
  threshold was sourced this pass.
- **Food status:** Not a food crop in its common cultivated (toxic)
  genotype; the seed is acutely toxic raw (Finding 1). Seed and seed cake
  remain toxic (curcin, phorbol esters) even after oil extraction;
  detoxification to food-safety standards is achieved only at laboratory
  scale per review literature (Finding 5). The rare non-toxic Mexican
  genotype (Finding 7) is a distinct, unresolved case.
- **Pollination:** Monoecious, both protandrous and protogynous,
  strongly male-flower-skewed (20:1 male:female ratio in one study,
  rising as temperatures fell), self- and cross-compatible, but
  cross-pollination gives materially higher fruit set than selfing
  (89.7% natural vs. 32.9% selfed in one study) (Finding 3). The "natural
  pollination" figure implies insect involvement (most likely bees/flies
  typical of Euphorbiaceae, though no specific pollinator taxon was named
  in the sources opened this pass) [3]. This self-vs-cross fruit-set gap
  (57 percentage points) is the largest this project has sourced for any
  oil crop researched so far.
- **Yield / composition:** No single world-average figure. A Colombian
  field trial found seed yields of 2,430-4,746 kg/ha with 38.7-40.1% oil
  content (Finding 4) [4]. The oil-content figures across sourced field
  trials cluster at 33-40% of seed weight, narrower than castor's wider
  35-60% range. Nitrogen-plus-potassium fertilization roughly doubled
  total oil output without the oil-content percentage itself moving
  nearly as much — a milder version of the yield/oil-content decoupling
  pattern seen in castor. A literature review explicitly found "a large
  gap between projected and actual seed production," consistent with the
  boom-and-bust history in Finding 8.
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-compatible crop coefficient (Kc) table was
  found despite a direct search — a deeper water-physiology gap than
  castor's own partially-sourced, conflicting regional Kc studies. No
  rooting-depth claim was found for jatropha (unlike castor's sourced
  "deep, extensive root system" claim) — jatropha's drought mechanism is
  instead framed around leaf-drop (Finding 9), a different and
  less-sourced gap than castor's. No deficit-response curve, seasonal
  water-use total, salinity-threshold study, or soil pH figure was found
  this pass.
- **Establishment:** Direct seed OR stem cuttings (Finding 2); a real,
  sourced flowering-timing and yield-structure tradeoff exists between
  the two methods. No seeding rate, spacing, or nursery-days figure was
  found this pass.
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n=22; one karyotype study reports a chromosomal
  formula of 12 metacentric + 10 submetacentric chromosomes, consistently
  diploid across the populations examined [11]. Genome size shows a
  spread across sources: flow cytometry gives an average 2C value of 0.85
  pg, described by its own authors as "relatively small," in the same
  size range as rice; a separate review cites approximately 370 Mb; one
  genome assembly comes to 320.5 Mb, and a newer chromosome-scale assembly
  of a named variety comes to approximately 339 Mbp [11].
- **Cultivars:** No sourced list of named commercial cultivars with
  comparative trait deltas (yield, oil content, growth habit) at a level
  of detail worth a full cultivar table was found this pass. The one
  genuinely distinct genetic group sourced is the rare non-toxic Mexican
  genotype (Finding 7), though no comparative agronomic trait data
  distinguishing it from the common toxic genotype was found — only its
  existence, registration location (Mexico only), and genetic-diversity
  concentration (northern Veracruz) were sourced.
- **Propagation:** No commercial F1 hybrid seed system was sourced this
  pass (Finding 2); propagation in the sources opened here is
  open-pollinated seed or vegetative cuttings only, unlike castor's
  well-documented two-line hybrid system.
- **Stand life:** Perennial shrub/small tree, drought-deciduous
  (leaf-drop in dry conditions, Finding 9) — a true multi-year standing
  perennial rather than an annual, unlike most of this project's other
  oil crops. Seed production begins before full stabilization; sources
  disagree on the exact stabilization year — "4-5 years depending on soil
  fertility" in one source, "4 years" (maximum yield, two accessions) in
  another, "2-3 years" (best yields) in a third — kept as a visible
  source conflict rather than resolved to one number. Productive life
  estimates range from about 30 to 40 years in grower-oriented sources; a
  seed-vendor marketing claim of 60 years of lifespan (50 years of seed
  production) is noted but not relied on given its promotional origin.
  One source notes a sharp decline in seed yield after a light frost,
  implying frost (not age alone) as one documented decline trigger,
  though this is a single-event effect rather than an aging-curve figure.
  Periodic coppicing (cutting back to about 1 m height) is reported
  roughly once per decade in one source as a rejuvenation practice, with
  no sourced yield-recovery curve.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — one annual seed crop per
  year from an established perennial stand, once production stabilizes.
  Dried pods/capsules are collected manually or mechanically at or near
  dry maturity, then seed is separated/dehulled; no sourced ratio or
  time-limit figure for the separation step, and no hours-per-hectare
  figure for either harvest or separation.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Jatropha mosaic disease (begomovirus,
  whitefly-vectored) is a major obstacle to large-scale cultivation,
  cutting oil content by as much as 41.78% in one study (Finding 10).
  Other pests/diseases include cutworms, tip borer caterpillars, and
  (in the Philippines) fungal leaf spot and marginal necrosis. Despite its
  toxic seed, the plant is explicitly not resistant to pests and diseases.
  No general plant-virus/whitefly-vector mitigation mechanic exists in
  this project yet to attach this hazard to in a structured way.

## Hazards

- **Curcin** (ribosome-inactivating toxalbumin): found in seed and, by
  extension, seed cake after pressing. Trigger: ingestion of seed (as
  few as 3 seeds reported toxic in humans, lethal dose roughly 7-8 seeds)
  or inadequately detoxified seed cake (Findings 1, 5). Mitigation:
  lab-scale-only detoxification (chemical, physical, biological, or
  combined treatments; ethanol solvent extraction found more effective
  than NaOH in one study) — explicitly described as too complex for
  small-scale/local/on-farm use. Residual risk is real; most human
  poisonings are accidental and most patients recover with supportive
  care, but fatalities have been reported. A risk-assessment review sets
  quantified reference doses (acute reference dose 139.64 µg/kg bw,
  sub-chronic health-based guidance value 0.0105 mg/kg/day) and a 99.5%
  detoxification target for food use.
- **Phorbol esters** (diterpene toxin, purgative; also a documented
  molluscicide/insecticide/fungicide): found in seed and seed cake.
  Trigger and mitigation as for curcin above. A competing 2020 study
  argues a different compound class (hydroxy-octadecenoic acids) is
  actually the primary acute toxin in kernel cake — an unresolved
  scientific disagreement kept visible rather than picked (Finding 1).
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this seed/seed-cake toxicity
  hazard, including the livestock/feed-specific profile, is deferred
  until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
  Jatropha is not a food crop in its common cultivated genotype, and the
  sources opened did not describe any sanctioned human-food use of the
  toxic-genotype oil or cake; the rare non-toxic Mexican genotype
  (Finding 7) could eventually warrant separate food-safety research if
  it is ever studied as a food crop in its own right.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → harvested (dried pods, manual or mechanical) →
  separated/dehulled → pressed for crude oil (dominant use: biodiesel;
  secondary uses: soap/cosmetics manufacture, topical traditional
  medicine, Finding 6) and seed cake (usable as fertilizer without
  detoxification; NOT safe as livestock feed without thorough,
  effectively laboratory-scale-only detoxification).
- The standing plant is also used traditionally as a hedge/live fence in
  its native range, and its phorbol esters show documented but
  not-yet-commercialized biopesticide (molluscicidal/insecticidal/
  fungicidal) activity — both noted here descriptively rather than
  modeled as production processes (Findings 5, 6).

## Open research questions

- No kcal/protein figure exists, since jatropha is not a food crop in its
  common cultivated genotype; noted here as a flag alongside castor for
  a future non-food-crop data example.
- The rare non-toxic Mexican genotype (Finding 7) is a real, named,
  sourced genetic group with no comparative agronomic trait data (yield,
  oil content, growth habit, actual food-safety certification)
  distinguishing it from the common toxic genotype — worth a dedicated
  follow-up before deciding whether it deserves separate treatment as a
  food crop.
- The phorbol-ester-vs-hydroxy-octadecenoic-acid primary-toxin
  disagreement (Finding 1) is a live, unresolved scientific question, not
  just a sourcing gap.
- No seed storage days, seed-viability-years, germination-rate, soil pH,
  salinity-threshold, or crop-coefficient figure was found at all for
  jatropha, each a deeper gap than castor's own equivalent.
- No kcal/protein figure exists for the non-toxic genotype either, since
  no source this pass established it is in wide food use despite being
  non-toxic.
- The live-fence/hedge planting use (Finding 6) is a real, named
  traditional use of the standing plant not easily captured by a
  commodity/process framework.
- The seed-cake biopesticide use (Finding 5) is real and lab/field-
  trial-documented but not commercialized.
- Mosaic-virus disease (Finding 10) is among the most severe, specifically
  quantified pest/disease threats this project has sourced for any oil
  crop so far (41.78% oil-content reduction in one Indian study), yet
  this project has no general plant-virus/whitefly-vector mechanic to
  attach it to.
- No seed/pod harvest-labor-hours or dehulling-ratio figure was found,
  despite the dual manual/mechanical harvest and separation methods
  being clearly sourced.
- The boom-and-bust history (Finding 8) and the leaf-drop
  drought-avoidance mechanism (Finding 9) are kept as two distinct
  findings rather than merged, since one is a socioeconomic/land-use
  history and the other is a physiological mechanism — worth keeping
  that distinction in any future narrative treatment of this crop.
