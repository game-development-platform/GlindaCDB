# Flax / Flaxseed / Linseed (*Linum usitatissimum*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/flax.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-29

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Linum usitatissimum* L., the single cultivated flax
species. "Flax," "flaxseed," and "linseed" are not different crops:
"linseed" is the British/Commonwealth name for the SEED and its oil,
"flaxseed" the North American name for the same thing, and "flax" the
plant/fibre name — all one species, one file. The one real internal
question is whether fibre-type and oilseed-type flax should be treated as
two separate crops. This file answers no, after checking (Finding 2): a
Latvian comparison found oil content and fatty-acid composition differ
little between fibre-flax and oilseed-flax varieties, and seed yield
within fibre types depends more on the specific variety than on the type
category itself [2] — the same "shared agronomy, cultivar-group-not-
species-split" pattern this project has used for other oilseed crops
with a dual commercial type.

This file researches and models the oilseed (linseed) path: seed, oil,
and meal. Flax fibre processing (retting, scutching, hackling) is
deliberately left out of scope here, since this project has not yet
begun researching fibre crops as their own category; flax is flagged as a
strong future candidate for that eventual research effort, given how
closely the fibre supply chain is tied to this same species.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content and omega-3 fraction are both unusually
well-sourced and unusually HIGH for a seed oil crop.** Flaxseed runs
35-45% oil, with 45-65% of that oil's fatty acids being alpha-linolenic
acid (ALA, an omega-3) [1]. One Belarusian fibre-flax variety-screening
trial found ALA as high as 61.8-65.1% in one specific variety — though
that same high-ALA variety yielded only about 21 centners/ha (≈2,100
kg/ha), well below the trial's other entries, an explicit yield/
composition trade-off within a single trial [1]. This ALA fraction is
dramatically higher than most other oilseed crops this project has
researched so far.

**Finding 2 — fibre-type and oilseed-type flax overlap closely in seed
chemistry, but diverge sharply in plant architecture and the seed/fibre
yield trade-off.** A Northern Ireland field study found fibre-type and
linseed-type plants had similar total biomass at the START of flowering,
but then diverged — flax (fibre type) kept a faster STRAW growth rate
after flowering, while linseed (oilseed type) grew SEED faster, leaving
fibre yields about 1,300 kg/ha higher in the fibre type and seed yields
about 800 kg/ha higher in the oilseed type [3]. A South African source
describes the morphological split plainly: fibre types are tall and
slender with few branches and small seed capsules; oil types are bushier
with more branching and heavier seed capsule production [3]. An Alberta
trial independently confirmed seed yield is negatively correlated with
fibre content across the type spectrum [1] — the two traits trade off
directly against each other on the same plant architecture, not via a
separate chemistry difference (Finding 1's composition data shows the
chemistry itself barely differs by type).

**Finding 3 — isolation requirements for flax seed are dramatically
shorter than for some other oilseed crops, a direct, sourced signal of
how little outcrossing this species actually does.** Canadian pedigreed-
seed standards require only 1 m (3 ft) separation from the same variety
and 3 m (10 ft) from a different variety or non-pedigreed flax for the
Foundation/Registered classes, rising to 3 m/10 m (same variety) and 10
m/33 ft (other varieties) for the stricter Select class [4]. This
contrasts sharply with other oilseed crops this project has researched
requiring isolation distances on the order of hundreds of metres for the
same general seed-purity purpose — a roughly 40-130x difference,
reflecting that flax is predominantly self-pollinating while those other
crops, though sometimes also self-compatible, outcross enough via
insects to need a much wider buffer. (US state seed-certification
standards for ornamental "blue flax" give much larger isolation figures —
1,320 ft Foundation, 660 ft Registered, 330 ft Certified — but this is a
different *Linum* species/use case from the oilseed crop modeled here,
not directly comparable without confirming it is the same species [4].)
Canada's confined-field-trial rules for GM/novel-trait flax separately
require only a 10 m buffer from other *Linum* species (50 m if large
harvest equipment was shared), which is a biosafety/regulatory-trial
standard, not a seed-purity standard [4].

**Finding 4 — the same oilseed harvest that gives the food/feed products
ALSO supplies North America's main source of flax fibre, as straw, not
from a dedicated fibre crop.** "In North America, the main source of
flax fibre is straw left over from the linseed industry" [3] — meaning
the fibre supply chain in that region is a downstream byproduct of the
oilseed industry's harvest residue, not grown-for-purpose fibre flax the
way European linen production typically is.

**Finding 5 — flaxseed's cyanogenic glycosides share a compound with
cassava (linamarin), but the practical hazard profile and intended
audience differ substantially.** Flax seed contains linustatin,
neolinustatin, and linamarin [5]; cassava's well-documented cyanogenic-
glycoside hazard is specifically linamarin and lotaustralin. Because
linamarin is the literal same molecule in both crops, this is worth
flagging as a shared-mechanism hazard class rather than two unrelated
issues. But the PRACTICAL profile differs sharply from cassava's: flax's
total cyanogenic glycoside content in a 21-variety screening ran only
0.74-1.60 g/kg (as CN⁻), with linamarin itself a MINOR component (only
2-14% of the total — the majority is linustatin/neolinustatin, not
linamarin) [5], and the INTACT seed poses essentially no risk since
stomach acid denatures the releasing enzyme before any meaningful
hydrolysis occurs [5] — a much milder, more easily managed hazard than
cassava's bitter-cultivar levels. Flax's practical risk case is narrower
and different: it concerns GROUND/crushed seed and MEAL left
insufficiently heat-treated, not whole intact seed eaten as food.

**Finding 6 — flax's two major diseases (pasmo and Fusarium wilt) have
opposite primary control strategies, and one of them has gotten WORSE
over time rather than being a stable, managed risk.** Fusarium wilt's
main control is resistant/moderately-resistant varieties, with crop
rotation as a secondary lever — a Manitoba source suggests at least 3
years between flax crops, while a Polish study found a full 6-year
rotation was needed to actually eliminate the pathogen from infested soil
[6]. Pasmo, by contrast, is described by one Canadian source as now the
MOST PREVALENT disease of flax on the Canadian Prairies, capable of
cutting yield by 50% in severe infestations, and explicitly called out as
a disease that "has gotten worse" historically (widespread epidemics
alongside powdery mildew) even as pasmo-resistance breeding continues
[6]. Pasmo overwinters on flax stubble, making stubble management
directly relevant to the SAME rotation decision that also controls
Fusarium wilt, even though the two diseases are unrelated organisms with
different primary control levers.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Spring-sown cool-season annual; an autumn/
  early-spring-sown option exists per one source but was not
  independently detailed.
- **Cycle length:** 90-120 days, with a real spread across sources (one
  heirloom-seed retailer gives 100-110 days; a gardening-guide source
  gives a wider 90-120 day range; a plant database gives "3-4 months...
  though autumn or early spring sown crops can take 6" with the
  explanatory clause cut off in the source as retrieved) — the 90-120
  range used here spans the first two sources and excludes the
  unconfirmed 6-month outlier [7].
- **Climate:** Temperate / cool temperate; sowing guidance specifically
  for the US Upper Midwest (Minnesota/North Dakota: sow April-early May,
  15-20 days before the last average killing frost) [7] was the only
  region-specific sowing-timing source found.
- **Composition:** No raw-seed composition figure (kcal/protein) was
  independently sourced this pass, though oil content and ALA fraction
  ARE sourced (Finding 1). Whole or lightly ground seed is edible as a
  nutritional supplement, subject to the heat/acid caveat in Finding 5;
  it is NOT raw-edible in bulk ground/crushed form without some heat or
  acid exposure.
- **Pollination:** Predominantly self-pollinating; not required.
  Occasional insect visitation was not independently quantified this
  pass. Finding 3's very short isolation distances are themselves the
  strongest available evidence for how little outcrossing actually
  matters to this crop, more informative than a direct pollination-
  mechanism source found this pass. No pollinator-dependence or
  yield-boost study was found, unlike some other oilseed crops this
  project has researched with well-sourced bee-visitation-yield-gain
  findings — a genuine difference in what this pass found, not
  necessarily in what is true of the crop.
- **Yield:** No single world-average figure. An Idaho/North Dakota-region
  extension source gives 700-1,600 lb/acre (≈785-1,793 kg/ha) for
  EXPERIMENTAL oilseed flax yields, explicitly noting dryland yields
  would be expected lower than the irrigated figures this range likely
  reflects [1]. One study found INTENSIVE cultivation (vs. an
  "economical" approach) raised seed yield of two linseed cultivars by
  80-102% on average [1] — a relative, not absolute, exceptional-tier
  figure. One Turkish study found delaying sowing exposed the seed-
  filling period to high temperatures and reduced yield, with the
  first/earliest sowing date giving the best results [1].
- **Water use:** No FAO-56-compatible crop coefficient (Kc) curve, no
  secondary citation of one, and no seasonal water-use figure of any kind
  was found despite a direct search — a more complete absence than for
  some other oilseed crops this project has researched, where at least
  conflicting Kc curves were found. Seed-fill is inferred as the
  water/temperature-sensitive stage from the sowing-date/temperature-
  exposure study [1]: high temperature during seed-fill specifically
  reduces yield, with delayed sowing being the mechanism that exposes the
  crop to it.
- **Soil:** pH guidance conflicts across three sources — one gives pH
  6.0-7.5 as the crop's preferred range, a second gives pH 5-7, and a
  third describes the soil only qualitatively as "mildly acidic" [7];
  kept visible as a spread rather than resolved to one number. Soil
  texture is described as "sandy to loamy, well-drained" per one
  gardening-level source [7], not independently verified against an
  agronomic source. No salinity-tolerance figure or comparative
  statement was found for flax at all, unlike some other oilseed crops
  this project has researched.
- **Storage:** No safe-storage-moisture target or day-count figure was
  found at all despite a direct search — a notably deeper gap than for
  some other oilseed crops this project has researched, where a precise
  moisture figure (with a temperature and oil-content interaction) was
  found.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — combine harvest at
  physiological maturity. No flax-specific swathing/straight-cut/
  desiccant-timing harvest-method source was found despite a direct
  search, unlike some other oilseed crops this project has researched
  with thoroughly-sourced harvest-method guidance.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Pasmo (most prevalent flax disease on the
  Canadian Prairies, up to 50% yield loss in severe cases, overwinters on
  stubble, resistance breeding ongoing) and Fusarium wilt (controlled
  mainly via resistant varieties, with crop rotation of 3-6 years as a
  secondary lever) are the two major named diseases (Finding 6) [6].
- **Genome:** No genome-size figure (not even a caveated assembly-length
  figure) was found despite a direct search — a deeper gap than for some
  other oilseed crops this project has researched.
- **Cultivars:** The two real, well-documented cultivar groups are
  fibre-type and oilseed-type (Finding 2), differing in plant
  architecture and the seed/fibre yield trade-off rather than in seed
  chemistry. **Oilseed (linseed) type** has a bushier architecture, more
  branching, and larger/more seed capsules; open-pollinated, self-
  pollinating, farm-saveable; the dominant commercial type for food/feed/
  industrial oil, widely grown especially in North America (Canada a
  major world producer); used for cold-pressed food oil, industrial
  drying-oil chemistry (paints/varnishes/linoleum), and meal (livestock
  feed, with antinutritional caveats); seed yield roughly 800 kg/ha
  higher than fibre type in the one direct comparison sourced [3].
  **Fibre (flax/linen) type** is tall, slender, with few branches and
  small seed capsules; same self-pollinating/open-pollinated system; the
  traditional European linen-production type, still grown where linen
  textile production is the primary goal (in North America, by contrast,
  fibre supply instead comes from oilseed-industry straw, Finding 4, so
  this type specifically is less common there); fibre yield roughly
  1,300 kg/ha higher than oilseed type in the one direct comparison
  sourced [3]. Named variety-level comparisons were found at a
  Polish-trial level of detail ('Artemida' vs. 'Hermes') but were not
  independently re-verified or expanded on for this file's cultivar
  entries.
- **Propagation:** True seed only. No hybrid seed system was found —
  flax is predominantly self-pollinating with no sourced CMS/hybrid-seed
  system, unlike several other oilseed crops this project has
  researched that do have one. Farm-saved seed from this
  self-pollinating, open-pollinated crop is a normal practice in
  principle, though this was not independently confirmed against a
  dedicated flax seed-saving source this pass.

## Hazards

- Cyanogenic glycosides (linustatin, neolinustatin, and a minor fraction
  of linamarin) are present in the seed (Finding 5). Intact seed poses
  essentially no risk since stomach acid denatures the releasing enzyme
  before meaningful hydrolysis occurs. The practical risk case concerns
  ground/crushed seed and meal left insufficiently heat-treated, not
  whole intact seed eaten as food. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — a fuller
  write-up of this cyanogenic-glycoside hazard class, including its
  shared mechanism with cassava's better-known hazard, is deferred until
  this project has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
- Flaxseed meal carries a separate livestock-feed-specific
  antinutritional profile: a laxative effect at high inclusion, pyridoxine
  (vitamin B6) antagonism specifically in nonruminant animals, and
  residual linamarin and linase persisting in meal from low-temperature
  oil extraction (as opposed to high-temperature/solvent extraction,
  which denatures the enzyme) [5]. Documented broiler/poult growth and
  feed-efficiency reductions occur at high dietary flax-flour inclusion
  [5]. **TO BE ADDRESSED** for the same reason as above.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → combine-harvested at physiological maturity →
  pressed for crude oil (food-grade cold-pressed oil; industrial
  drying-oil chemistry for paints, varnishes, and linoleum) and meal
  (livestock feed, subject to the antinutritional caveats above). Straw
  byproduct from the oilseed harvest is North America's main fibre
  source (Finding 4), though the fibre-processing chain itself (retting,
  scutching, hackling) is out of scope for this file.

## Open research questions

- No raw-seed composition (kcal/protein) figure was found, as opposed to
  the oil-content and ALA-fraction figures, which ARE sourced.
- No flax-specific isolation/outcrossing-rate study was found beyond the
  pedigreed-seed distance standard — the short distances are strong
  indirect evidence of low outcrossing, but not a direct
  pollination-biology study.
- No safe-storage-moisture target was found at all, a notably deeper gap
  than for some other oilseed crops this project has researched.
- No flax-specific swathing/straight-cut/desiccant-timing harvest-method
  source was found, despite a direct search.
- No salinity-tolerance table or even a general comparative statement was
  found for flax, unlike some other oilseed crops this project has
  researched.
- No genome-size figure, not even a caveated assembly-length figure, was
  found.
- Growing-cycle days, soil pH range, and seed-oil extraction efficiency
  all rest on ranges from conflicting sources, kept visible rather than
  resolved — a notably higher conflict-density than some other oilseed
  crops this project has researched, reflecting that flax turned out to
  have a wider spread of informal/horticultural-level sources competing
  with more rigorous agronomic ones for several of its basic facts.
- No named commercial variety performance list beyond two Polish
  fibre-type names mentioned only secondhand this pass — not
  independently re-verified or expanded into a full cultivar entry.
- No crop coefficient curve or seasonal water-use figure of any kind was
  found — the complete absence, not merely a conflict between sources, is
  itself notable compared to some other oilseed crops this project has
  researched.
- No soil chemistry (nutrient uptake), soil biology, light, atmosphere
  (CO2-response), or enclosed-space/hydroponic-viability data were
  found.
