# Tung Oil Tree (*Vernicia fordii*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/tung-oil-tree.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Vernicia fordii*, also called the tung tree or
tungoil tree; formerly classified as *Aleurites fordii*, a name still
used in some older extension and nursery sources. Jatropha is a useful
comparison crop for grounding: both are toxic-seeded, non-food
Euphorbiaceae oil trees with a well-documented real-world boom-and-
decline commercialization history, though tung diverges sharply on toxin
identity (disputed, possibly saponin-based, rather than jatropha's
curcin/phorbol-ester profile) and on the specific industrial destination
of the oil (a drying oil for finishes/coatings, not a biodiesel
feedstock).

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content is consistently high across sources, and the
oil's industrially valuable drying property is attributed to a single
dominant fatty acid.** A Chinese germplasm study reports seed oil content
ranging 52-64% by weight; other sources give a somewhat lower range (up
to 58% in a plant database, roughly 50% in a general encyclopedia entry,
likely a rounded figure) [1]. The oil's drying ability comes mainly from
alpha-eleostearic acid, an unusual conjugated trienoic fatty acid making
up roughly 80% (w/w) of total fatty acids in tung oil [1] — this single-
compound dominance is a cleaner, more concentrated chemistry story than
most other oil crops this project has researched, whose drying/non-
drying character typically depends on a broader fatty-acid profile
(e.g. linoleic/oleic ratios).

**Finding 2 — per-tree and per-hectare yield figures vary by source and
measurement basis, with a Chinese breeding program reporting a specific
elite-genotype record.** A plant encyclopedia source gives roughly 30-50
kg of seed per mature tree per year; a plant database instead reports a
field-level figure of 4.5-5 tonnes of fruit per hectare [2]. Chinese
breeding work names an elite genotype reaching 2,152.31 g of oil per
tree — the single most individually sourced elite-genotype figure found
this pass, though not placed in context against a named baseline the way
jatropha's own Chinese seed-source study explicitly compared its result
against a national average [2]. A late-1990s global estimate gives
annual world production of roughly 500,000 t of *Vernicia* fruit from
about 170,000 ha, yielding about 90,000 t of oil [2] — implying a rough
average oil yield near 530 kg oil/ha at that production scale, a figure
derived here, not stated directly by the source.

**Finding 3 — a closely related species, *Vernicia montana*, produces a
similar oil traded together with *V. fordii*'s own oil under the same
commercial "tung oil" name.** *Vernicia montana* seeds yield a quick-
drying oil called "abrasin oil" or "Chinese wood oil"; because the two
species' oils are similar, *V. montana* and *V. fordii* oils are traded
together as tung oil [1]. No source opened this pass gave a species-
level market-share split, or described any compositional difference
between the two species' oils beyond "similar" and "quick-drying." This
file models only *V. fordii*, recording *V. montana* as a related-species
open item rather than a second entry.

**Finding 4 — toxicity is consistently and strongly attested across
sources, but the specific toxin class is a genuine, unresolved cross-
source disagreement.** Every source consulted agrees the plant is highly
toxic in all parts, including fruit and seed, with as few as one seed
potentially fatal to a child per one university extension source [3].
Reported symptoms include severe stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea,
weakness, slowed breathing, and poor reflexes, and may be fatal [3]. But
the sources disagree sharply on the toxin itself: one names "glycoside
and phytotoxin" without further specificity, another names "alkaloids,"
and a third states the seed contains saponins, described as poisonous
but poorly absorbed by the body [3]. No source opened this pass named
phorbol esters or any ribosome-inactivating protein analogous to
jatropha's curcin or castor's ricin, despite this being the natural
comparison point given the shared Euphorbiaceae family with both those
crops — a targeted toxicology-literature search did not surface results
this pass. Separately, contact with the leaves is reported to cause an
allergic skin rash in many people, compared explicitly to poison ivy by
one invasive-species database [3] — a foliage-contact hazard distinct
from, and in addition to, the seed-ingestion toxicity.

**Finding 5 — pollination is insect-mediated and the species is self-
fertile, with a late-blooming cultivar bred specifically to reduce frost
damage to flowers.** The species is monoecious with entomogamous
(insect) pollination; bees are named as pollinators, and the species is
described as self-fertile [4]. One North Carolina-origin cultivar is
described as late-blooming specifically because later flowering reduces
the flowers' susceptibility to frost damage [4] — a cultivar-level trait
directly engineered against the frost-sensitivity problem named in
Finding 6, a cleaner cause-and-effect breeding story than most cultivar
notes this project has sourced for other oil crops.

**Finding 6 — the tree is frost- and drought-tender, with sourced site-
selection guidance for escaping frost damage.** Extension sources
describe the tree as not very cold-tolerant, requiring a minimum
temperature above about 5°C, and as drought- and frost-tender overall
[5]. One source specifically recommends contour-planting on high rolling
land to escape frost damage — an orchard-siting practice exploiting
cold-air drainage [5]. This sourced climate fragility is the direct
agronomic mechanism behind the late-winter freeze damage named as a
major cause of the US industry's decline in Finding 8 — unlike
jatropha's own boom-and-bust story, where the gap was between PROMISED
drought tolerance and disappointing REALIZED yields, here the climate
vulnerability itself was known and partially bred against (Finding 5's
late-blooming cultivar) but ultimately not sufficient to prevent
industry collapse.

**Finding 7 — soil pH guidance is a genuine, visible source conflict,
kept unresolved rather than averaged.** One plant database gives a
tolerated range of pH 5.3-7.3 and calls for "rich, well-drained soil"; a
general plant encyclopedia gives a narrower pH range of 6.0-7.5; an
older horticultural reference instead states pH 6.0-6.5 is best and
separately claims the tree requires "lime-free soil" [5] — a direct
tension with the other two sources' more neutral-to-slightly-alkaline-
tolerant ranges, since lime raises soil pH. No single source reconciles
these, so all three are recorded rather than picked.

**Finding 8 — the US Gulf Coast commercial tung-oil industry has a
well-documented, specifically dated rise-and-fall history, driven by a
combination of climate risk, storm damage, and economic substitution.**
The USDA began evaluating tung trees for US introduction starting in
1905, and by 1912 research had identified the Gulf Coast as meeting the
tree's specific growing-condition requirements [6]. By the 1940s, the US
tung-oil industry had grown to nearly 10 million trees in production,
with mills across the Gulf Coast [6]. Decline is attributed to several
compounding causes across sources: late-winter freezes (directly tied to
Finding 6's sourced frost-tenderness), hurricane damage, synthetic
chemical substitutes for tung oil, competing plant-derived drying oils,
and imported tung oil competing on price [6]. One 1969 hurricane is
named by one source as "the final straw" for an already-declining local
industry [6]. A later, separately dated blow came decades afterward: a
2005 hurricane destroyed the largest remaining US orchard, in
Mississippi, which at the time produced about 20% of the domestic
tung-oil supply [6]. Some sources note renewed interest in restarting
Gulf Coast tung-oil production for biofuel and industrial uses,
connected to breeding work on a sterile ornamental cultivar (Finding 9)
[6]. This two-hit history (a 1969 industry-ending decline, then a 2005
destruction of the last major remnant production) is a more specifically
dated and causally layered decline story than jatropha's single global
2000s-2010s boom-and-bust narrative.

**Finding 9 — a sterile, late-flowering ornamental cultivar exists
specifically to allow landscape use of this tree without its invasive
seed-spread risk.** A USDA-developed cultivar is a late-flowering,
sterile ornamental selection that produces virtually no nuts [6][7].
Named yield-oriented cultivars bred for higher yield and smaller tree
size include several historical commercial selections [7]. Propagation
is either by true seed (generative, producing larger but less uniform
trees) or by grafting high-producing selections onto seedling rootstock
(vegetative; budded trees yield more and more uniformly, with more
consistent oil content and fruit-maturity timing) [7] — this seed-vs-
graft tradeoff is structurally similar to jatropha's own seed-vs-
cuttings tradeoff, though the specific traits favored by each propagation
method differ.

**Finding 10 — the tree is a documented invasive species in parts of the
southeastern US, spreading by both seed and root suckers.** Tung oil
tree is listed as an invasive species in Florida and Alabama, and
flagged as a weed concern in Florida, Georgia, and Alabama more broadly
[5][8]. One invasive-species database describes the suckering habit
specifically as having allowed the species to flourish and spread in the
southeastern US outside cultivation [8].

**Finding 11 — the genome has been fully sequenced and chromosome-scale
assembled, with a documented ancient whole-genome triplication shared
across core eudicots but no further lineage-specific duplication.** A
2019-2020 chromosome-scale genome assembly anchored 1.06 Gb (95.15%) of
sequence onto 11 pseudochromosomes, with an estimated total genome size
of 1.31 Gb and a low heterozygosity rate of 0.0976%; the assembled genome
carries 28,422 predicted genes and over 73% repeat sequences [9]. The
genome shows an ancient triplication event shared by core eudicots
generally, but no further whole-genome duplication across the roughly
34.55 million years of the tung-tree lineage's own subsequent
evolutionary history [9]. The chloroplast genome is separately sequenced
at 161,528 bp [9]. No 2n chromosome count or 2C picogram-level genome-
size figure was located this pass, despite the unusually detailed
Mb-scale assembly data — the 11-pseudochromosome figure is the only
chromosome-number-adjacent datum found.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Perennial tree. Not applicable in the usual
  annual-crop sense; see Stand life below for years-to-bearing and
  productive-life figures.
- **Climate:** Temperate, subtropical — sourced from the US Gulf Coast's
  own historical commercial range and the tree's sourced frost/drought
  tenderness (Finding 6), which explicitly excludes it from colder
  temperate zones or genuinely tropical ones without more specific
  threshold data.
- **Food status:** Not a food crop; toxic in all parts, including fruit
  and seed; as few as one seed potentially fatal to a child (Finding 4);
  no human food use found for any plant part; leaf contact also causes
  allergic skin rash in many people (Finding 4).
- **Pollination:** Monoecious, insect-pollinated (entomogamous); bees
  named as pollinators; self-fertile (Finding 5). No quantified self-vs-
  cross fruit-set comparison (of the kind sourced for jatropha) was found
  for tung this pass.
- **Yield / composition:** Seed oil content commonly 52-64% by weight in
  a Chinese germplasm study, with lower figures (up to 58%, or a rounded
  ~50%) in other, less rigorous sources (Finding 1). Per-tree yield 30-50
  kg seed/year (one source) or 4.5-5 t fruit/ha (a field-level figure
  from a different source, not reconciled against the per-tree figure)
  (Finding 2). A late-1990s global-scale estimate (500,000 t fruit from
  170,000 ha, yielding 90,000 t oil) implies a rough average oil yield
  near 530 kg oil/ha at that historical production scale — derived, not
  stated directly. No single reconciling modern world-level yield figure
  was located this pass.
- **Water use:** Described as drought-tender by extension sources,
  alongside its sourced frost-tenderness (Finding 6) — a genuinely
  different water-stress profile from jatropha's own leaf-drop drought-
  avoidance mechanism; no equivalent avoidance mechanism was sourced for
  tung this pass. No stage-level water-deficit response or dose-response
  curve was located. No dedicated crop-water-requirement figure was
  located this pass, the same recurring gap already flagged for several
  other oil crops this project has researched (camelina, Ethiopian
  mustard, crambe).
- **Establishment:** Propagated by true seed (generative) or grafting of
  high-yielding selections onto seedling rootstock (vegetative);
  seedling trees tend to grow larger but less uniformly, while grafted/
  budded trees yield more and more uniformly, with more consistent oil
  content and fruit-maturity timing (Finding 9). Seedlings are typically
  grown in nursery beds for 1-2 years before field transplanting [5].
  Seed germination is reported slow; a seed-supplier source recommends a
  hot-water soak and 24-hour standing-water scarification treatment [5].
  No sourced seeding rate, planting depth, or spacing figure was located
  this pass.
- **Soil:** Genuine three-way source conflict (Finding 7): 5.3-7.3 (one
  plant database) vs. 6.0-7.5 (a general encyclopedia) vs. 6.0-6.5 with
  an explicit "lime-free soil" requirement (an older horticultural
  reference) — kept visible rather than resolved. "Rich, well-drained
  soil" is named as a cultivation requirement by one source, implying
  poor waterlogging tolerance by inference rather than by a direct
  sourced statement. No salinity tolerance or root-depth figure was
  found this pass.
- **Stand life:** Begins bearing fruit in the third year after planting;
  commercially productive by the fourth or fifth year; reaches maximum
  production at 10-12 years (Finding 9) — closely paralleling but
  distinct in exact figures from jatropha's own conflicting 2-5-year
  stabilization range. Average US tree lifespan is reported at about 30
  years across the sources opened this pass [7] — shorter than
  jatropha's own sourced 30-40-year (or promotionally-claimed 60-year)
  productive-life range. No sourced decline-cause figure distinct from
  the general frost/hurricane risks was located this pass.
- **Disease/pest profile:** No specifically named fungal or bacterial
  disease, or quantified pest-resistance/susceptibility figure, was
  located for this crop in the sources opened this pass — a notable gap
  relative to jatropha's own specifically quantified mosaic-virus-
  disease finding.
- **Genome:** 1.31 Gb estimated total genome size (1.06 Gb, 95.15%,
  anchored onto 11 pseudochromosomes in a 2019-2020 chromosome-scale
  assembly), 28,422 predicted genes, over 73% repeat sequences, low
  heterozygosity (0.0976%); an ancient whole-genome triplication shared
  with core eudicots generally, but no further lineage-specific
  duplication across ~34.55 million years of the tung lineage's own
  evolutionary history (Finding 11). No 2n chromosome count or 2C
  picogram-level genome-size figure was located this pass, despite the
  otherwise detailed Mb-scale assembly data.
- **Cultivars:** A genuinely well-sourced, functionally differentiated
  cultivar set for this crop — more cultivar-level nuance than most oil
  crops this project has researched, spanning both yield-oriented
  commercial selections and a deliberately sterile ornamental breeding
  line. One **late-blooming selection** (grafted/vegetative propagation,
  historical North Carolina research origin): later flowering
  specifically reduces flower susceptibility to frost damage, a direct
  breeding response to Finding 6's sourced frost-tenderness; no
  quantified frost-survival-rate figure located this pass. Several
  **yield-oriented commercial selections** (grafted/vegetative
  propagation, historical US Gulf Coast breeding era): bred for higher
  yield and smaller tree size [7]; no individual comparative trait-delta
  figures located at a level of detail worth repeating here. One
  **sterile ornamental selection** (vegetative propagation, USDA-
  developed, ornamental nursery trade): late-flowering, sterile, produces
  virtually no nuts — bred specifically to allow non-invasive landscape
  use of the species (Finding 9); its entire purpose is to NOT produce
  the harvestable organ, so it serves a fundamentally different purpose
  than the other cultivars.
- **Propagation:** True seed (slow germination, scarification-assisted)
  or grafted/budded vegetative material onto seedling rootstock (Finding
  9) — a genuine dual-propagation-path tree crop, structurally similar
  to jatropha's own seed-vs-cuttings choice but using grafting rather
  than simple stem cuttings as the vegetative route. No F1 hybrid seed
  system was found or implied by any source opened this pass;
  propagation is described throughout as either true seed (open-
  pollinated, self-fertile) or grafting of selected genotypes onto
  seedling rootstock — a clonal-selection breeding model, not a hybrid-
  cross one.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — fruit collected at or near
  natural drop/maturity, typically autumn; one annual fruit crop per
  established tree. No sourced maturity-stage choice affecting product,
  method, or destination was found for this crop. No sourced hours-per-
  hectare figure for fruit collection or dehulling (seed separated from
  the fruit before pressing) was found this pass.

## Hazards

- **Disputed toxin class (all plant parts, including fruit and seed)**:
  as few as one seed potentially fatal to a child; symptoms include
  severe stomach pain, vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, slowed breathing,
  and poor reflexes, and may be fatal (Finding 4). The specific toxin
  class is a genuine, unresolved cross-source disagreement (glycoside/
  phytotoxin vs. alkaloids vs. saponins), with no source naming phorbol
  esters or a ribosome-inactivating protein analogous to jatropha's
  curcin or castor's ricin despite the shared Euphorbiaceae family.
- **Leaf-contact allergic skin rash**: compared to poison ivy by one
  source, a foliage-contact hazard distinct from, and in addition to,
  the seed-ingestion toxicity (Finding 4).
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — given the genuine uncertainty about the toxin
  class itself (as opposed to jatropha's and castor's own well-
  identified toxins), a fuller write-up of this hazard is deferred
  pending a more conclusive toxicology source, rather than written on an
  unresolved chemical-identity basis.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** fruit → collected (fallen or shaken down at maturity,
  typically autumn) → dehulled (seed separated from fruit) → pressed →
  oil (industrial drying oil for finishes/coatings; historical lamp-oil
  use in China, now superseded). Seed cake byproduct processing was not
  sourced in detail this pass.
- The US Gulf Coast tung-oil industry's rise-and-fall history (Finding
  8) is a genuine agricultural-economics and natural-disaster case
  study, not a cultivation detail, but this crop's single most
  distinctive real-world fact.
- Invasive/weed status in Florida, Alabama, and Georgia (Finding 10) is
  an ecological/land-management topic, similar in kind to land-use
  topics this project has documented for argan, jatropha, and neem.

## Estimates and derivations

The ~530 kg oil/ha average-yield figure is computed from the late-1990s
global estimate of about 500,000 t fruit from about 170,000 ha, yielding
about 90,000 t oil (Finding 2): 90,000 t oil ÷ 170,000 ha ≈ 0.529 t/ha ≈
529 kg oil/ha. This is a historical-average figure across a broad,
likely mixed-quality global production base (including both *V. fordii*
and *V. montana* orchards, per Finding 3's traded-together pattern), not
a sourced per-hectare figure for well-managed *V. fordii* specifically;
the elite-genotype per-tree figure in Finding 2 cannot be directly
converted to a per-hectare figure without a sourced planting density, so
the two figures are not reconciled. No other DERIVED values are restated
here as reusable formulas.
