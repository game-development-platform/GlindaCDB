# Camelina (*Camelina sativa*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/camelina.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Camelina sativa*, also called false flax or
gold-of-pleasure. No name conflict in the sources opened.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content, seed yield, and the crop's defining trait
(exceptionally high omega-3 content) all vary considerably by genotype,
sowing date, and climate, but the omega-3 story is consistent across
sources.** A 2026 Turkish genotype/sowing-date study found the best
genotype reached 40.2% oil, with early sowing linked to higher oil content
and high flowering/seed-fill temperatures linked to lower oil content [1];
a companion Turkish study found oil content ranging 36.5-41.4% across
genotypes [2]. A multi-location European/Canadian trial found seed yield
ranging 1.1-2.7 Mg/ha for the top genotype, with cycle length 80-110 days
and accumulated growing degree days near 1200 across sites [3]. A
five-year Chinese study reported a mean yield of 1,946 kg/ha (range
1,274-2,650 kg/ha) [4]. Even in the harshest conditions tested (southern
Greece), yields around 1 Mg/ha were still reached [5]. Across all these
sources, the main fatty acid is alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, omega-3) at
roughly 35-45% of the oil — an unusually high omega-3 fraction for a
farmed oilseed, and the primary reason camelina is being actively
re-developed as a modern crop [6]. No single world-average yield figure
was located this pass.

**Finding 2 — camelina is predominantly self-pollinating, with no sourced
commercial hybrid seed system.** A patent document on camelina variety
development describes pedigree selection as a method used in plants that
are largely self-pollinated in nature, naming camelina alongside soybean,
wheat, rice, and safflower [7]. A commercial "SO" variety series (SO-40
through SO-110) were developed as true-bred, open-pollinated populations
selected from crosses between accessions from different countries, with
natural crossing (by wind or bees) or hand-crossing both used during
development, but no male-sterility or other hybrid-seed-production system
was found [8]. Genetic diversity in modern commercial cultivars is
reported as limited, which is described as complicating further breeding
[9].

**Finding 3 — a documented crossing boundary and a documented crossing
risk sit side by side, and the two are easy to conflate.** One regulatory
document states that no cross-pollination, natural or forced, has been
observed between *C. sativa* and members of the *Brassica* genus
(canola/rapeseed, mustards, etc.) [10] — a reassuring boundary given how
many other oil crops are *Brassica* species. However, a separate study
reports that viable hybrids WERE produced between *C. sativa* and its own
wild relatives within the genus *Camelina* (not *Brassica*), with the
hybrid seed in that study showing even greater viability than some
parental seed [10] — kept visible here as two distinct, non-contradictory
findings about two different taxonomic boundaries, not reconciled into
one claim.

**Finding 4 — camelina is an allohexaploid formed from three distinct
wild-species subgenomes, a genuinely unusual genetic architecture.**
*C. sativa* is 2n = 6x = 40, formed roughly 65,000 years ago from a
hybridization event [11]. The paternal genome donor is the diploid
*C. hispida* (2n = 2x = 14) [11]; the maternal donor is a tetraploid
*C. neglecta*-like lineage (2n = 4x = 26), recently assembled and named
*C. intermedia* by Mandáková & Lysák (2022), whose two maternally-
inherited subgenomes show high collinearity with two of camelina's three
subgenomes [11]. A related wild species, *C. microcarpa*, has two
distinct cytotypes (2n = 38 and 2n = 40); genome-wide data suggest the
2n = 40 cytotype shares all three of camelina's subgenomes, while the
2n = 38 cytotype is more distantly related [12]. The exact identity of
the maternal tetraploid progenitor and its relationship to *C. microcarpa*
remain actively studied, per these same sources [11][12].

**Finding 5 — camelina has a genuinely low shattering risk at maturity,
but is unusually sensitive to post-ripening handling losses because of
its tiny, light seed.** A grower guide states camelina has a low risk of
shattering at maturity, but is particularly sensitive to losses during
cutting, threshing, and field handling once ripe, recommending a slow
combine forward speed (3-4 km/h), harvest within 7-10 days of maturity,
and very low fan airflow because the seed is so light [13]. A controlled
study found direct combining at 75% pod ripeness balanced seed-yield loss
against seed protein/oil content, with early harvest giving 9.5-23.6%
higher yield than mid/late harvest across trials, and total direct-combine
losses around 11.7% (about 7.25% from mechanical pod disturbance, about
4.45% from combine settings like fan speed and sieve size) [14]. A 2024
study comparing swathing-then-combining against direct combining found
swathing did not improve seed loss or yield and had lower performance and
higher cost than direct combining, though a separate cited study found
swathing losses as high as 11% [15]; swathing is noted as mainly useful
when camelina is grown as a summer catch crop [13].

**Finding 6 — the seed meal's glucosinolate load is well above the
recommended feed-safety ceiling for monogastric animals.** Glucosinolates
are called the major limiting factor for camelina seed meal in animal
feed, adding bitterness and becoming toxic at high levels [16]. An Alberta
broiler study of screw-pressed camelina meal found significantly more
tibial dyschondoplasia (a bone-growth disorder) at 16% and 24% dietary
inclusion than at 0% or 8%, though no pathological cardiac or thyroid
changes were reported up to 24% [17]. A pig study found camelina meal
improved feed efficiency but significantly increased liver weight, and
reported the meal's glucosinolate load at roughly 24 micromol/g — well
above the roughly 2 micromol/g ceiling generally recommended for
monogastric animals [18]. A specialist feed database classifies camelina
feed products as of "moderate toxicity" for glucosinolate content, and
specifically notes camelina seed contains no progoitrin, the particular
glucosinolate that forms the thyroid-toxic compound goitrin found in some
other Brassicaceae [19]. Active breeding programs are working to lower
camelina's seed glucosinolate content specifically to address this
feed-safety limit [16].

**Finding 7 — camelina has a real, ASTM-certified role as a sustainable
aviation fuel (SAF) and biodiesel feedstock, the source of its own modern
research-interest revival.** ASTM International approved an annex to its
D7566 jet-fuel specification in 2011 covering HEFA (hydroprocessed esters
and fatty acids) fuel, explicitly naming camelina as a permitted feedstock
alongside jatropha and algae, with blending capped at 50% with
conventional jet fuel [20]. The finished blend is certified against the
long-standing Jet A specification, ASTM D1655 (or the UK's equivalent
Defence Standard 91-091) [20]. The US Air Force flew an F-22 on a 50/50
camelina-blend test in March 2011, and the US Navy's Blue Angels flew on a
camelina blend in September 2011 [21]. A lab-scale study found neat
camelina jet fuel missed the freezing-point specification by 2°C without
additives, but reported a net energy ratio of 1.36 and a 57%
greenhouse-gas emission reduction versus conventional jet fuel [21]. A
2024 industry report notes HEFA fuel today is made mainly from used
cooking oil and animal fat (together capable of meeting at best about 2%
of aviation's fuel demand), with camelina promoted specifically as a way
to grow dedicated feedstock on marginal, degraded land rather than
compete with food-oil supply [22] — connecting directly to Finding 8's
marginal-land tolerance.

**Finding 8 — camelina is genuinely drought- and cold-tolerant and shows
real, if stage-dependent and under-characterized, salinity tolerance.** A
2025 study describes camelina as one of the most drought-tolerant Brassica
oilseed species, identifying the flowering-to-seed-filling window as the
most drought-sensitive stage [23]. Seasonal crop evapotranspiration
correlates strongly with seed yield (r² = 0.82) in one study [24]. Line
responses to drought vary, with at least one line yielding significantly
MORE under drought than under well-watered conditions (0.44 g/plant vs.
0.26 g/plant) [23] — an inverted drought response worth flagging as a
single-example trait. Seedlings tolerate frost well: no seedling damage
reported at 12°F (-11°C) in Montana trials [25], and no stand losses
observed at 20°F (-7°C) in a Pennsylvania extension source [26]; frost
tolerance after germination (versus at germination) is noted as still
needing field confirmation in one USDA study [27]. Winter-annual-type
overwintering survival is cultivar-dependent and markedly less reliable
than seedling frost tolerance: in a Minnesota trial the best cultivar
(Joelle) averaged only 64% winter survival across three growing seasons,
and freeze hardiness is explicitly named as a trait still needing
improvement for winter camelina to see wider adoption [28]. Salinity
tolerance is reported as stage-dependent: a 2025 study found germination
largely unaffected by salinity up to about 8 dS/m, with only a 25% decline
beyond about 35 dS/m — described as greater tolerance than several other
minor oilseeds — but the same study and a companion dissertation both
state that full-crop-cycle salinity tolerance (versus germination-stage
tolerance only) remains largely unstudied, and that alkaline salinity
specifically remains a major constraint in Mediterranean field trials
[29][30]. European marginal-land trials report an average yield of 0.94
Mg DM/ha, with one Greek trial run on low-fertility soil (pH <5.5), and
autumn sowing identified as the most conservative strategy for southern
European marginal land [31].

**Finding 9 — camelina is reported strongly resistant to two of the most
damaging Brassicaceae field diseases (blackleg and Alternaria black
spot), while remaining susceptible to several others.** A review states
*C. sativa* is highly resistant to Alternaria black spot and to blackleg
of crucifers [32]; a Montana study found no symptoms on camelina varieties
inoculated with two blackleg races known to be virulent on canola, though
this covered only a few varieties and two races [32]. Sclerotinia
resistance varies by genotype: a 30-genotype study found significant
differences in disease index, with genotype CS370 showing high
resistance, and the resistance mechanism is reported as a complex
inheritance pattern tied to cell-wall monolignol production [32].
Resistant genotypes for sclerotinia stem rot, brown girdling root rot, and
downy mildew can all be found among *C. sativa* accessions [32]; however,
the crop remains susceptible to clubroot, white rust, and aster yellows
disease [32], and camelina seed can transmit Turnip Yellow Mosaic virus
[33]. Flea beetles are identified as the most significant insect pest
where studied [33], though a dedicated resistance study found measurable
natural variation in flea-beetle feeding damage across accessions (not
independently confirmed beyond the search-snippet level this pass).

**Finding 10 — camelina has a very long and genuinely interesting history
as a domesticate, with its origin point recently revised.** Archaeological
finds suggest cultivation in Europe for at least 3,000 years [34], but a
2022 study placed the crop's actual domestication in the Caucasus region,
6,000-8,000 years ago — a notably older and geographically different
origin than the "grown alongside flax in Europe" folk history might
suggest [35]. Camelina reached North America most likely as a weed
contaminant in flaxseed shipments, not as a deliberate introduction [36].
Its oil fueled lamps before petroleum-era lighting (natural gas, propane,
electricity) displaced it, and it was also eaten directly in some
traditional contexts [37]. Cultivation declined sharply in Western Europe
through the 1930s-40s and was largely abandoned after 1950 [38][39],
before its 21st-century revival as a drought-tolerant, marginal-land,
high-omega-3, and aviation-biofuel crop (Findings 7, 8).

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual broadacre oilseed; spring types (80-120
  days, midpoint around 100 days) and winter-annual types (180-210 days)
  [40].
- **Climate:** Grown across Northern Great Plains US/Canada, Northern
  Europe, Mediterranean marginal land, and China; no single authoritative
  climate-zone table was found, so this is inferred from sourced growing
  regions rather than drawn from one reference.
- **Establishment:** Field establishment, not frost or drought, is
  reported as the crop's main practical weak point: one grower reported
  only about 40% average establishment (as low as 12% in some cases)
  despite following seeding guidance, and recommends 500 seeds/m² rather
  than the lower minimums some sources cite [41]. Seeding depth and
  base-temperature requirement trade off against each other: a shallower
  3mm depth gave 11% faster emergence, but a deeper 6mm depth lowered the
  base-temperature requirement by 19% in one incubator study [41].
  Germination begins once soil reaches about 38°F (3.3°C) [41]. Fall
  seeding under no-till conditions is reported to work more successfully
  than spring seeding at at least one site [41]. Broadcast seeding (as
  opposed to drilling) should use 1.5-2x the drilled seeding rate,
  followed by a roller harrow or similar implement to press seed into the
  soil [41]. Seeding rate guidance itself varies widely across sources:
  Europe typically 6-8 lb/acre (~6.7-9.0 kg/ha); Montana trials found
  3-5 lb/acre (~3.4-5.6 kg/ha) adequate; Ontario recommends 4-6 kg/ha for
  400-600 plants/m² [41]. Planting depth is about 0.3-0.6 cm [41].
- **Soil pH:** No camelina-specific authoritative range was found with
  confidence; one extension source's pH guidance for camelina is
  explicitly based on canola recommendations, not camelina-specific
  research [42]. One Greek marginal-land trial grew camelina successfully
  on soil with pH <5.5 [31].
- **Salinity tolerance:** Stage-dependent and only partly characterized
  (Finding 8); germination tolerant to about 8 dS/m with only 25% decline
  beyond about 35 dS/m [29]; full-cycle tolerance, especially under
  alkaline/sodic conditions, not yet well studied [29][30].
- **Water use:** Described as one of the most drought-tolerant Brassica
  oilseed species [23]; flowering through seed-filling is the most
  drought-sensitive window [23]. Seasonal crop evapotranspiration
  correlates strongly with seed yield (r² = 0.82) [24]; genotype responses
  to drought vary substantially, with at least one line yielding more
  under drought than well-watered conditions (Finding 8) [23]; deficit
  irrigation up to 75% of full crop ET has been shown to improve
  water-use efficiency in an Algerian trial [45]. A dedicated FAO-56-
  compatible crop coefficient (Kc) curve was developed for camelina by a
  USDA-ARS Arizona study using the dual (basal + soil-evaporation)
  crop-coefficient method [43], and a companion 2010 paper built Kc curves
  from days-after-planting, growing-degree-days, and canopy spectral
  reflectance [44]; the specific numeric Kc-by-stage values from either
  paper were not retrieved beyond their existence and methodology this
  pass. Growing degree days near 1,200 are reported for the full cycle in
  a multi-location trial [3].
- **Pollination:** Predominantly self-pollinating (Finding 2).
  Open-pollinated variety development has used both natural (wind or
  bee) and hand-crossing [8]. Pollination, seed set, and grain filling are
  all noted as dependent on temperature, weather, agronomic practice, and
  pest/disease pressure in one regulatory document, without a quantified
  pollinator-boost figure [8]. No cross-pollination (natural or forced)
  observed between *C. sativa* and *Brassica* species (Finding 3) [10];
  viable hybrids ARE produced with wild *Camelina* relatives (Finding 3)
  [10].
- **Yield basis:** Seed yield, per-hectare across regional trials:
  1,274-2,650 kg/ha in a Chinese five-year study (mean 1,946) [4];
  1.1-2.7 Mg/ha for the top genotype in a European/Canadian multi-location
  trial [3]; around 1 Mg/ha even in the harshest site tested (southern
  Greece) [5]; European marginal-land average 0.94 Mg DM/ha [31]. Oil
  content 29-43% of seed weight, commonly cited around 36-41% in recent
  genotype trials (Finding 1). No single world-average figure was
  located.
- **Cultivars:** Genetic diversity in modern commercial cultivars is
  reported as limited, complicating breeding [9]; the clearest sourced
  cultivar-level distinction is spring vs. winter growth habit, not a
  chemistry or product-type split the way some other oilseed crops split
  by acid profile or market class. Notable named cultivars/accessions:
  **Joelle** (winter-annual type, open-pollinated) had the best winter
  survival among cultivars tested in a 3-season Minnesota trial, averaging
  64% (still a minority surviving) [28], with freeze hardiness still named
  as needing improvement for winter camelina generally [28]. The **'SO'
  series** (SO-40 through SO-110) are spring-type, true-bred
  open-pollinated commercial lines, pedigree-selected from multi-country
  accession crosses [8], used both as oilseed/meal crops and as SAF/
  biodiesel feedstock; no comparative trait-delta data beyond their
  patented-variety status was located. **CS370** is a Sclerotinia-
  resistant accession identified in a 30-genotype disease-screening study
  [32], with resistance tied to a complex-inheritance, cell-wall
  monolignol-production pathway [32].
- **Disease/pest profile:** Highly resistant to blackleg of crucifers,
  confirmed against two canola-virulent races in a Montana trial (small
  sample) [32]. Highly resistant to Alternaria black spot in one study
  [32]. Sclerotinia stem rot resistance is genotype-variable, with complex
  inheritance tied to cell-wall monolignol production; genotype CS370 is
  notably resistant among 30 screened [32]. Resistant genotypes exist for
  brown girdling root rot and downy mildew (*Peronospora camelinae*)
  among *C. sativa* accessions, though downy mildew itself is listed as a
  potential-but-not-yet-observed pest in at least one region [32][33].
  Susceptible to clubroot, white rust, and aster yellows disease [32].
  Can transmit Turnip Yellow Mosaic virus via seed [33]. Flea beetles are
  the most significant insect pest where studied, with measurable natural
  variation in flea-beetle feeding damage across accessions found in a
  dedicated resistance study (not opened beyond the search-snippet level
  this pass) [33].
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window. Harvest timing (percent pod
  ripeness at the single combine pass) is a quality/loss-minimization
  choice rather than a maturity-stage choice selecting between genuinely
  different products. Direct combining at about 75% pod ripeness is the
  recommended harvest-timing balance between seed-yield loss and seed
  quality (protein/oil content) (Finding 5) [14]. Combine setup matters
  more than for most oil crops researched for this project because of the
  seed's tiny size and light weight: slow forward speed (3-4 km/h),
  harvest within 7-10 days of maturity, and very low fan airflow are all
  specifically recommended [13]. Swathing (cutting and windrowing before
  combining) is a fallback mainly used for uneven maturity or summer
  catch-crop systems, not shown to reduce losses versus direct combining
  in a 2024 comparison study, and costs more [13][15].
- **Postharvest storage:** No dedicated camelina seed-storage-life or
  moisture-management study was located this pass, distinct from the
  general small-oilseed storage conventions sourced for some other oil
  crops researched for this project. Given the seed's tiny size and
  documented post-ripening handling sensitivity (Finding 5), other oil
  crops' storage-life figures should not be assumed to apply by analogy
  without a dedicated source.
- **Propagation:** True seed only; no vegetative propagation path was
  sourced or expected for this species. No commercial hybrid seed system
  (CMS, chemical hybridization, or genic male sterility) was found; all
  named commercial varieties located are true-bred, open-pollinated
  selections.
- **Genome:** Allohexaploid, 2n = 6x = 40 (Finding 4), formed ~65,000
  years ago from a hybridization of diploid *C. hispida* (2n = 2x = 14,
  paternal donor) and a tetraploid *C. neglecta*-like lineage (2n = 4x =
  26, maternal donor, recently assembled and named *C. intermedia*)
  [11][12]. No 2C picogram/Gb genome-size figure was located this pass;
  only the ploidy/chromosome-count and subgenome-origin story was
  sourced.
- **Controlled-environment / enclosed space:** Predominantly
  self-pollinating (Finding 2) is a plausible enclosed/off-world oilseed
  advantage without requiring managed pollinators, though open-pollinated
  variety development has used wind/bee-assisted outcrossing as a
  breeding tool, not a production necessity [8]. No footprint, root-
  depth-in-container, or edible-yield-per-m²-per-day figures were sourced.
  No spaceflight or closed-system trials were found.

## Hazards

- Meal glucosinolate load exceeds the recommended monogastric feed-safety
  ceiling at typical processing levels (Finding 6). **TO BE ADDRESSED** —
  a fuller write-up of this Brassicaceae-family glucosinolate livestock
  hazard, consistent with similar glucosinolate hazards already noted
  for other crops in this project, is deferred until this project has a
  place for this kind of health/safety data.
- Highly unsaturated oil (35-45% ALA) is less oxidatively stable than
  high-oleic oils, a genuine storage/rancidity tradeoff against its
  omega-3 value, though no specific shelf-life figure for the crude oil
  was sourced this pass.
- Camelina seed can transmit Turnip Yellow Mosaic virus (Finding 9) [33]
  — a field-disease vector hazard, not a food-safety one.
- No human food-safety issue was identified for the seed, oil, or meal
  themselves in the sources opened this pass.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → pressed for crude oil (food/industrial use) and
  press-cake meal (livestock feed, subject to the glucosinolate ceiling
  above); crude oil is also hydroprocessed industrially into ASTM
  D7566-certified sustainable aviation fuel and into biodiesel (not a
  farm-level process, not detailed further here).
- Historical lamp-oil use (Finding 10) is a pre-petroleum-era historical
  use rather than a current production pathway.

## Open research questions

- No single world-average seed-yield figure was located this pass
  (Finding 1).
- Whether any hybrid seed system (CMS or otherwise) has been developed
  since the sourced patent/variety documents were published was not
  checked (Finding 2).
- A dedicated flea-beetle feeding-damage resistance study across camelina
  accessions exists but was not opened beyond the search-snippet level
  this pass.
- No camelina-specific soil pH range was found with confidence; the one
  commonly-cited range in extension sources is explicitly borrowed from
  canola recommendations, not camelina-specific research.
- No sourced waterlogging tolerance or root-depth figures.
- Two primary FAO-56-compatible camelina crop-coefficient papers were
  identified but their numeric stage-by-stage values were not retrieved
  and opened directly this pass — worth a dedicated follow-up read.
- No light (DLI/shade-tolerance), atmosphere (CO2-response/ethylene-
  sensitivity), soil boron/chloride-class, or enclosed-space footprint/
  edible-yield-per-m² figures were found.
- No 2C picogram/Gb genome-size figure was located, distinct from the
  ploidy/chromosome-count/subgenome-origin story that was sourced.
- Wild *Camelina* relatives (*C. hispida*, *C. neglecta*/*C. intermedia*,
  *C. microcarpa*) are the species underlying the crop's own allohexaploid
  genome and are documented to form viable hybrids with cultivated
  camelina, but no sourced human-gathering or food use for any of them
  was found this pass — a possible future research topic on genetics/
  breeding-relevant wild relatives rather than a wild-food-use topic.
- No dedicated camelina seed-storage-life or moisture-management study
  was located.
- No specific shelf-life or rancidity-rate figure for the crude oil
  itself, despite its notably high polyunsaturated (ALA) content being a
  plausible oxidative-stability concern.
- No sourced isolation distance for camelina-to-camelina seed-production
  blocks, or for separating cultivated camelina from wild *Camelina*
  relatives given their documented hybrid-viability finding (Finding 3).
- No seed-crop-specific seed yield or multiplication-ratio figure distinct
  from the general seed-yield range in Finding 1.

## Estimates and derivations

No DERIVED values were carried forward from this synthesis as reusable
formulas; a rough seasonal-water-requirement placeholder in the source
material was built by multiplying the spring cycle length by a standard
arid/temperate conversion factor, but since no independent total-seasonal-
water-requirement figure exists to check it against for camelina, it is
flagged here as unreliable rather than restated as a derived value — the
Kc-curve papers noted under Water use above are the better path to a real
figure.
