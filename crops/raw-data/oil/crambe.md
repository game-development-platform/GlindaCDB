# Crambe (*Crambe abyssinica*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/crambe.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Crambe abyssinica*, also called Abyssinian crambe or
Abyssinian cabbage — a name it shares, confusingly, with Ethiopian
mustard's own "Abyssinian cabbage" alias, even though the two are
unrelated genera (*Crambe* vs. *Brassica*). This file uses "crambe"
throughout to avoid that clash.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content is reported across a wide range by source, with
most field-trial figures clustering at 35-45% and a single review citing
a much higher ceiling that is kept visible rather than adopted as
typical.** A 2021 Italian-sourced review states seed oil can reach up to
57%, but most field values fall between 35% and 45%, with sowing date and
variety driving much of the difference [1]. A 1999 Australian trial
measured 35.6% to 42.8% oil across its samples [2]. A Turkish study
reported a narrower 35-36% oil ratio, with earlier sowing giving the best
results [3]. No single world-average yield or oil-content figure was
located this pass; the 57% figure is kept as a separate, higher outlier
rather than folded into the 35-45% working range, since the review's own
text frames the other sources as typical.

**Finding 2 — erucic acid content is both the crop's entire commercial
reason for existing and the direct reason its meal and oil are unsuitable
for food use.** Erucic acid typically makes up roughly 50-60% of crambe
oil, with one source giving a mean around 57% and another a range of
54.9-60.0% [4][2]. Unlike canola (bred for LOW erucic acid as a food oil)
or some other Brassica oilseed crops this project has researched (whose
high erucic acid is explicitly why they have historically lost out to
canola as a food oil), crambe is deliberately grown FOR its high erucic
acid content as an industrial feedstock; crude oil is explicitly
unsuitable for cooking because of it [5]. This is the most
erucic-acid-concentrated oil crop researched for this project to date,
higher even than high-erucic-acid rapeseed (HEAR), its closest comparison
crop [6].

**Finding 3 — the erucic acid feeds a real, named industrial chemical
(erucamide) with a specific plastics-manufacturing role, though the exact
synthesis chemistry was not confirmed this pass.** Crambe oil's erucic
acid is converted into erucamide, a slip agent used in plastics
manufacturing to keep individual plastic sheets from sticking together
during production [5][7]. Erucic acid also feeds other industrial
intermediates (emollients, surfactants); oil uses besides erucamide
include lubricants, corrosion inhibitors, and synthetic rubber production
[5][8]. The actual industrial synthesis route from erucic acid to
erucamide (expected to be amidation of the fatty acid with ammonia,
followed by purification, by general organic-chemistry knowledge, not
confirmed in a crambe-specific source this pass) was not located — this
file records the known destination chemical without claiming the exact
process.

**Finding 4 — the defatted meal is a genuinely dual-outcome feed case: a
real FDA-approved cattle-ration inclusion on one hand, and an explicit
"not for monogastrics" warning on the other.** Crambe meal can serve as a
protein source for ruminants; its glucosinolates and erucic acid content
restrict its use in monogastric animals (pigs, poultry), where
glucosinolate breakdown products in the digestive tract can harm the
liver, kidneys, and thyroid, and reduce appetite [9]. A dairy/beef-cattle
inclusion of up to 5% was approved for US cattle rations by the FDA [9]. A
1994 Poultry Science study found that washing defatted crambe meal with
water removed about 95% of its glucosinolates and nitriles, and reported
commercially processed meal appeared safe at 50 or 100 g/kg diet in the
short term, though high-glucosinolate meal with active enzyme caused mild
liver lesions and the authors could not recommend it yet for long-term
feeding [9]. A 2018 steer feeding trial found that replacing soybean meal
with crushed crambe did not change dry matter intake or digestibility,
though urinary nitrogen excretion rose [9]. No source described
water-washing as the dominant commercial practice, unlike some other
Brassica oilseed crops this project has researched where washing is
described as the standard route; this file treats the 5%-cap, ruminant-
only, FDA-anchored finding as the relevant production fact rather than
assuming a separate detoxified-meal product exists commercially.

**Finding 5 — pollination is predominantly self-pollinating with a real
minority outcrossing rate, and selfing carries a measurable yield cost.**
Sources agree crambe flowers are mostly self-pollinated, with roughly 15%
cross-pollination occurring [10]. A Brazilian study comparing selfed
progenies against open-pollinated ones found inbreeding depression across
all traits tested, especially for grain yield [10]. The exact magnitude
of the yield penalty (percent reduction) was not retrieved at more than
search-snippet level this pass.

**Finding 6 — the species' ploidy and chromosome count are unusually
high, and the taxonomy of its nearest wild relatives is explicitly
unsettled across sources.** *Crambe abyssinica* is commonly described as
hexaploid with 2n = 90 [10]. Its wild relatives *Crambe hispanica* (2n =
60) and *Crambe kralikii* (2n = 60) are named as close relatives; a 1996
Euphytica paper distinguishes *C. abyssinica* (2n = 90) from *C.
hispanica* var. *hispanica* (2n = 60) and var. *glabrata* (2n = 30) [10].
A separate source lists *C. hispanica* itself with chromosome numbers of
2n = 30, 60 AND 90, and treats it as closely related to *C. abyssinica*
— the taxonomy is explicitly not fully settled across the sources opened
[10]. Several papers describe *Brassica napus*-*C. abyssinica* addition
lines (one alien crambe chromosome carried in a canola background, for
example at 2n = 39), identified by a specific genomic-hybridization
technique, used for introgression breeding research [10]. The "hexaploid"
label itself comes mainly from encyclopedic summaries rather than a
primary cytogenetic paper opened this pass; no genome-size (picogram-level
C-value) figure was located.

**Finding 7 — the crop is reported to combine a short growing cycle with
real drought tolerance, but the cross-study picture on actual
water-stress response is more mixed than a single "drought-tolerant"
label suggests.** Crambe is widely described as a short-cycle, cool-season
annual maturing in about 90-100 days from planting; a Great Plains guide
and an Ontario extension page both give 90-100 days, a Brazilian study
reports physiological maturity at approximately 90 days, and an Italian
paper cites a 90-110 day cycle with roughly 1,300-1,500 growing-degree-
days from sowing to harvest [11]. A 2021 European trial paper describes
the crop as having high drought tolerance alongside its short cycle [12].
But a Brazilian greenhouse study found crambe genotypes differ in how
they respond to water deficit, with one genotype (FMS CR 1307) showing
better photochemical adjustment during water deficit and recovery than
others, and found germination and seedling vigor were hampered once
water potential was reduced to -0.4 MPa — drought stress clearly still
matters early in the crop's life even if the mature plant tolerates it
reasonably well [12]. A New Mexico arid-lands germplasm evaluation found
results "generally discouraging," though with some variability for
drought tolerance among the lines tested [13]. Brazilian sources
separately describe the plant as "rustic" and tolerant of water deficit,
cold, and pests [13]. A 2019 Brazilian germination study found crambe
seeds tolerated salt stress better than water-deficit stress, linking
this to salt absorption and antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD and CAT)
[13]. No single reconciling source directly comparing all these
conflicting drought-response results across genotypes and growth stages
was found this pass.

**Finding 8 — salinity tolerance is moderate and better-sourced than the
water-deficit picture, with one source framing it as a specific candidate
trait for saline-land agriculture.** A 2013 greenhouse study found the
plant tolerated irrigation with brackish water up to about EC 6 dS/m,
though biomass and seed yield dropped under that stress; the researchers
judged crambe a possible crop for saline areas, suitable as a
green-biomass and industrial-oil source even with reduced yield [13]. A
separate 2015 greenhouse study examined several salinity levels and
reported that different salt TYPES did not affect plant parameters (i.e.
the response was dose-dependent on salinity level but not on which salt
was used) [13]. Brazilian sources separately describe the crop as having
"moderate tolerance to salinity" in general terms [13]. No formal
salinity-tolerance class was assigned to these findings this pass; no
sourced soil pH figure was located at all.

**Finding 9 — flea beetle pressure is a specifically named, currently
unmanaged US production constraint.** Yields are sensitive to
environment, and flea beetles can cause significant losses, with no
insecticides approved for use on the crop in the US as of the source
consulted [14]. Pod shattering is a second named harvest-timing risk: a
European-agronomy review describes crambe as having LIMITED seed
shattering and good resistance to diseases and pests overall, but Ontario
extension guidance is more cautious, noting shattering can occur if
harvest is delayed past the point where plant yellowing occurs below the
last seed-bearing branch — making harvest timing itself the practical
control, not genetics alone [1][15].

**Finding 10 — commercialization history is well-documented for the US
specifically, with a clear boom-and-decline arc driven by a
government-industry consortium rather than ongoing broad-based demand.**
The USDA introduced crambe into the US in 1950 [16]. In 1986 the USDA
began a "High Erucic Acid Development Consortium," with members including
a national cooperative research service, the Agricultural Research
Service, and several land-grant universities (Idaho, Illinois, Iowa,
Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, New Mexico, North Dakota) [16]. The most
sustained commercial effort began in 1990 in North Dakota, a partnership
among the High Erucic Acid Development Effort (a government-sponsored
research consortium), an oilseed-crushing firm specializing in
sunflowers, and several North Dakota farmers [16]. US acreage peaked in
the mid-1990s: about 19,600 acres were harvested in 1996, according to
one source; a separate review gives a 1996 production estimate of about
16,000 ha — the two figures are not directly comparable without a unit
conversion and are kept separate rather than reconciled [16]. Production
then declined sharply; a 2019 North Dakota crop report showed only 314 ha
grown that year [16]. US federal agricultural statistics agencies do not
collect dedicated data on crambe acreage or production at all [16] — a
genuine data-visibility gap for a crop with real, if modest, continuing
commercial production. No domestication-history source (how and when
crambe was first brought under cultivation, as opposed to its 1950 US
introduction) was found this pass beyond a Brazilian thesis's brief
statement that the crop is native to "the Mediterranean region of
Ethiopia" — an unusual, possibly imprecise phrasing (Ethiopia and the
Mediterranean Basin are not geographically coincident) that is flagged
rather than repeated as fact.

**Finding 11 — crambe does not cross with its much better-known
relatives, HEAR and canola.** One review source states plainly that
crambe does not outcross with HEAR (high-erucic-acid rapeseed) or canola
[6] — unlike crambe's own genus-internal relatives (*C. hispanica*, *C.
kralikii*), which share at least some chromosome-number variants, and
unlike the deliberate crambe-to-canola "addition line" breeding research
described in Finding 6, which works by introducing a single alien crambe
chromosome rather than ordinary cross-pollination.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual broadacre oilseed; short, cool-season
  cycle of 90-110 days (most sources agree on 90-100 days; one Italian
  paper gives 90-110 days with roughly 1,300-1,500 growing-degree-days
  from sowing to harvest).
- **Climate:** Grown across US Great Plains/Upper Midwest commercial
  production, a New Mexico arid-lands evaluation, and Brazilian trials;
  no single authoritative climate-zone table was found, so this is
  inferred from sourced growing regions. The New Mexico trial's own
  "generally discouraging" verdict (Finding 7) is informative about the
  arid-zone boundary rather than confirming full arid suitability.
- **Food status:** Not a food crop — crude oil is unsuitable for cooking
  due to erucic acid content (Finding 2); meal is restricted to ruminant
  feed at a capped inclusion rate (Finding 4); no human food use was
  found for any plant part.
- **Pollination:** Predominantly self-pollinated, with roughly 15%
  cross-pollination (Finding 5) [10]. Selfed progenies show measurable
  inbreeding depression relative to open-pollinated ones, especially for
  grain yield, per a Brazilian comparative study [10] — a real, quantified
  yield cost of selfing, in contrast to some other self-compatible
  oilseed crops this project has researched where no such yield cost has
  been documented. The exact magnitude of that yield penalty was not
  retrieved beyond search-snippet level this pass.
- **Yield / composition:** Seed oil content commonly 35-45% across most
  field trials (Finding 1), with a single review citing up to 57% as a
  higher, less typical ceiling, kept visible rather than adopted as the
  working figure [1][2][3]. Erucic acid makes up roughly 50-60% of the
  oil itself (mean ~57% in one source, range 54.9-60.0% in another)
  [4][2] — the deliberate target trait, not a disadvantage. No single
  world-average seed-yield figure was located this pass.
- **Water use:** Described as having a short growing cycle and (per one
  European trial paper) high drought tolerance [12]; separately described
  by Brazilian sources as "rustic" and tolerant of water deficit, cold,
  and pests [13]. Genotypes differ measurably in water-deficit response;
  one tested genotype (FMS CR 1307) showed better photochemical
  adjustment during water deficit and recovery than others in a
  Brazilian greenhouse study [12]. Germination and seedling vigor are
  hampered once water potential drops to around -0.4 MPa — an early-life
  vulnerability distinct from the more drought-tolerant mature-plant
  picture [12]. A New Mexico arid-lands evaluation found results
  "generally discouraging" overall, with some inter-genotype variability
  in drought tolerance [13]. Seeds tolerate salt stress better than
  water-deficit stress in a 2019 Brazilian germination study, linked to
  salt absorption and antioxidant enzyme (SOD, CAT) activity [13]. No
  dedicated FAO-56-compatible crop coefficient (Kc) curve or numeric
  crop-water-requirement figure was located this pass.
- **Salinity:** Moderate tolerance; brackish irrigation up to about EC 6
  dS/m judged feasible in one greenhouse trial, with reduced biomass/seed
  yield under that stress (Finding 8) [13]. A separate study found
  salt-type (as opposed to salinity level) did not affect plant
  parameters [13]. No soil pH range was retrieved this pass.
- **Establishment:** Seeding rate roughly 9-25 kg/ha depending on row
  spacing and region (one extension source: 11-22 kg/ha; another: 22
  kg/ha in narrow 6-7" rows or 9-17 kg/ha in wider 12-14" rows; current
  North Dakota practice: 17-22 kg/ha) [11]. Planting depth not exceeding
  about 2.5 cm (1 inch) in drier-region guidance, up to about 7.6 cm (3
  inches) in humid regions — a genuine region-dependent range, not a
  single figure [11]. Narrow row spacing (narrower than roughly 0.30 m)
  is reported to improve seed yield by reducing weed presence and
  promoting more uniform maturity [11]. Early planting (as soon as frost
  risk below about 24°F/-4.4°C has passed) shortens time to flowering and
  maturity; later (May/June) plantings showed lower yields, decreased
  seed oil content, and more weed infestation in US Upper-Midwest
  extension guidance [11].
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window — direct seed harvest at
  physiological maturity (about 90-110 days after sowing); no sourced
  maturity-stage choice affecting product, method, or destination.
  Harvest timing itself is the primary named control for pod shattering
  risk: timely harvest, before plant yellowing spreads below the last
  seed-bearing branch, is the key practical mitigation [15]. No sourced
  combine-settings detail specific to crambe was located, unlike some
  other oilseed crops this project has researched with more detailed
  harvest-timing findings.
- **Disease/pest profile:** Flea beetles are named as a significant pest
  with no US-approved insecticide at the time of the source consulted
  (Finding 9) [14]. Pod shattering is a harvest-timing-dependent risk if
  harvest is delayed past the point of plant yellowing below the last
  seed-bearing branch, per Ontario extension guidance, though one
  European review separately describes the crop's shattering overall as
  "limited" and its disease/pest resistance as good — a genuine tension
  between sources kept visible rather than resolved [1][15]. No named
  fungal or bacterial disease, with resistance data, was located for
  crambe this pass, unlike the richer disease-resistance picture sourced
  for some other Brassicaceae oilseed crops this project has researched.
- **Genome:** Commonly described as hexaploid, 2n = 90 (Finding 6) [10],
  though the taxonomic basis for this label and its wild relatives' own
  chromosome counts (2n = 30/60/90 variously reported for *C. hispanica*)
  are explicitly unsettled across sources. No genome-size (picogram-level
  C-value) figure was located this pass, a deeper genome-data gap than
  for some other Brassicaceae oilseed crops this project has researched.
- **Cultivars:** Comparative cultivar-level data located this pass is
  thin; one named cultivar, "Meyer," is mentioned without detailed trait
  deltas, and regional university variety trials are described as
  covering "most registered crambe cultivars" without naming them
  individually in the sources opened. **Meyer** is a named historical
  commercial cultivar, open-pollinated, used for industrial oil (erucic
  acid/erucamide feedstock) and ruminant-feed meal, with no comparative
  trait-delta data located at a level of detail worth repeating here
  [16]. **FMS CR 1307** is a Brazilian research genotype used specifically
  as a drought-response comparison line (not a commercial release),
  showing better photochemical adjustment during water deficit and
  recovery than other tested genotypes [12].
- **Propagation:** True seed only; no vegetative propagation path sourced
  or expected for this species. No hybrid seed system was found or
  implied by any source opened this pass; the crop is described
  throughout as open-pollinated with a documented self-pollination-
  dominant, minority-outcrossing breeding system (Finding 5) rather than
  any F1 hybrid production method.
- **Seed crop:** Days to seed same as general cycle length (90-110
  days); no seed-crop-specific figure distinct from general production
  found this pass. No sourced isolation distance for crambe-to-crambe
  seed-production blocks was found this pass. Given Finding 11's sourced
  non-crossing with HEAR/canola, isolation from other Brassica oilseed
  crops appears not to be a genuine concern for this species, though
  isolation from other *Crambe* species/genotypes (which DO appear more
  closely related by chromosome-number overlap, Finding 6) was not
  addressed. No seed-crop-specific yield figure distinct from general
  production yield was found this pass.
- **Postharvest storage:** No dedicated crambe-seed storage-life or
  moisture-management study was located this pass, a gap shared with
  some other Brassicaceae oilseed crops this project has researched.

## Hazards

- Meal glucosinolate and residual erucic acid content limit livestock
  feed inclusion to ruminants at a capped rate (Finding 4). **TO BE
  ADDRESSED** — a fuller write-up of this Brassicaceae-family
  glucosinolate livestock hazard, consistent with similar hazards already
  noted for other crops in this project, is deferred until this project
  has a place for this kind of health/safety data.
- Crude oil is explicitly unsuitable for human food use due to its
  erucic acid content (Finding 2) — no further acute human food-safety
  issue beyond this general unsuitability was identified in the sources
  opened this pass.
- No sourced phytotoxicity, allelopathy, or other field hazard beyond the
  feed-safety issue already covered above.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → harvested at physiological maturity → pressed for
  industrial crude oil (erucic acid/erucamide feedstock, lubricants,
  corrosion inhibitors, synthetic rubber production) and defatted meal
  (ruminant feed, capped at 5% of ration under US approval).
- Erucamide synthesis and other downstream industrial chemistry
  converting crude oil into lubricants, corrosion inhibitors, or synthetic
  rubber feedstocks are not farm-level processes and are not detailed
  further here (Finding 3).

## Estimates and derivations

No DERIVED values are restated here as reusable formulas. The source
material's own postharvest shelf-life placeholder (365 days) was an
analogy to other small, dry, hard-coated Brassicaceae oilseeds, not
independently sourced for crambe, and is flagged here as unreliable
rather than restated as a derived figure, particularly given crambe's
much higher erucic acid content, whose effect (if any) on storage life was
not ruled out.
