# Canola / Rapeseed (*Brassica napus*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/canola.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-29

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Brassica napus* L., the oilseed crop — as distinct
from rutabaga, the same species' root-forming subspecies (*B. napus* var.
*napobrassica*), which this project treats as its own separate crop given
how different the harvested organ and use are, despite the shared
species. "Rapeseed" and "canola" are not two species: canola is
specifically the low-erucic-acid, low-glucosinolate ("double-low") quality
standard bred into *B. napus* (and later *B. rapa* and *B. juncea*)
starting in the 1960s-70s, while "rapeseed" without qualification can mean
either the double-low crop or the older high-erucic type still grown
under contract for industrial oil chemistry (Finding 4). This file keeps
the double-low (canola) and high-erucic (HEAR, industrial rapeseed) lines
as cultivar groups within one crop — the same "shared agronomy, different
end-product chemistry" pattern this project has used for other oilseed
crops with a confectionery/oilseed or similar split — rather than treating
them as separate crops, since the growing method, water/pest/climate
profile, and harvest process are identical and only the seed's chemistry
and market differ.

*B. napus*'s cross-pollination radius and isolation-distance figures used
below were originally sourced during this project's rutabaga research
(the same species, different organ), and are reused here rather than
re-derived, since rutabaga and canola are the same species.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — the double-low ("canola") standard is a named, dated
Canadian breeding achievement, not a vague quality label.** Canadian
breeders began addressing high erucic acid (in the oil) and high
glucosinolates (in the meal) in the early 1960s; a national research
laboratory developed the analytical methods, and breeders at two Canadian
institutions applied them to a world germplasm search [4]. The first
double-low lines were registered as named varieties: **Tower** (a
*B. napus* variety, 1974) and **Candle** (a *B. rapa* variety, 1977) [4].
In 1978 double-low rapeseed was renamed "canola," for "Canadian oil,"
specifically to distinguish it from ordinary high-erucic rapeseed; by
1980 the commercial Canadian crop had largely converted to double-low
varieties, which removed the meal-market constraint that high
glucosinolates had imposed [4]. The first canola-quality *B. juncea*
(tame mustard) variety followed much later, in 2003 [4]. The double-low
standard itself is defined by two separate limits on two separate
tissues: oil under 2% erucic acid, meal under 30 μmol/g total
glucosinolates on a toasted, oil-free basis [4].

**Finding 2 — erucic acid and glucosinolates are two unrelated problems
the same breeding program solved together.** Erucic acid is an
oil-quality issue (a long-chain fatty acid, nutritionally undesirable at
high dietary levels in the pressed oil); glucosinolates are a
meal-quality issue, unrelated to oil quality, but toxic to livestock when
the seed is crushed — breakdown products interfere with thyroid iodine
uptake in non-ruminant animals [4]. Because the two traits sit in
different tissues and are governed by different genetics, they had to be
bred down independently, which is part of why the "double" in double-low
is meaningful rather than redundant.

**Finding 3 — hybrid canola uses the Ogura CMS system, which was itself
engineered from a protoplast fusion between radish and rapeseed.** Because
canola is normally self-compatible and can self-pollinate, hybrid seed
production needs the female parent's own pollen disabled — options
include physical emasculation, chemical gametocides, or cytoplasmic male
sterility (CMS) [3]. The dominant commercial system, Ogura CMS, originated
from protoplast fusion between radish (*Raphanus sativus*) and rapeseed
(*B. napus*) [3] — the same CMS lineage already relevant to this project's
own radish/daikon research (both *Raphanus sativus*), for a completely
different crop and organ. Early Ogura restorer lines carried two
side-effect problems — reduced female fertility and, awkwardly, high
glucosinolate content — which breeders fixed by backcrossing and
selecting low-glucosinolate progeny [3]. A separate refinement combines
Ogura CMS with an apetalous (petal-less) trait, reducing self-shading of
the canopy during flowering [3].

**Finding 4 — HEAR (high-erucic-acid rapeseed) is a real, continuing
industrial-only product line, not a historical dead end.** Pre-canola-type
rapeseed, selected specifically to keep erucic acid high rather than low,
is still grown under contract for industrial oil chemistry — lubricants,
slip agents (erucamide), and nylon-13 precursor feedstock — markets where
canola's low-erucic oil is the wrong chemistry rather than an improvement
[4]. This is a direct structural analogue of a high-oleic-versus-
conventional split this project has documented in another oilseed crop:
one crop, one agronomy, a deliberately opposite breeding target serving
an entirely different market.

**Finding 5 — bee pollination measurably raises canola yield, though the
effect size varies a lot by study, and canola is NOT obligately
insect-pollinated.** Canola is self-compatible and gives good yields
without insect pollination at all [5] — unlike some other oilseed crops'
hybrid crosses, where bee-mediated pollen transfer is a hard requirement.
But bee visitation clearly adds yield on top of that baseline: a
caged-exclusion Quebec study found three hives per hectare gave a 46%
yield gain over no hives at all, with wind-pollinated (uncaged, no bees)
plants gaining somewhat over fully self-pollinated caged plants, and
bee-visited plants gaining the most of the three treatments [5]. A
separate Australian field trial at roughly one hive per hectare found a
smaller but still substantial gain, over 20% (about 400 kg/ha), shrinking
with distance from the apiary [5]. An older oilseed-rape study found only
a 13% gain and explicitly noted that oilseed rape "gives good yields
without insect pollination" [5] — the spread across these three studies
(13% to 46%) is left visible as a real range rather than averaged into one
number, since one source attributes the variation to pollinator density,
bloom-period weather, and possibly the type of canola grown [5].

**Finding 6 — swathing vs. straight-cutting is a genuine farm-level
decision with opposite risk profiles, not just two names for the same
operation.** Swathing (cutting the crop into a windrow before full
dry-down, then combining later) lowers wind-damage and timing-pressure
risk, at the cost of yield and quality if cut too early (immature seed) or
too late (shatter during the swathing operation itself); hail causes
markedly more shatter loss on a standing crop than on an already-swathed
one, and heavy snow can flatten a standing crop [6]. Straight cutting
(combining the standing crop directly, no windrow) saves labour time and
gives roughly equal yield to swathing WHEN timed correctly, but has a
narrower harvest window and rising losses the longer the crop stands after
it is ready — one study found average total harvest losses of 5.5% at
optimal timing versus 17.4% when straight-combining was delayed [6].
Desiccants can speed straight-cut dry-down (applied once 60-75% of seeds
have changed colour) but do not speed actual maturity [6]. Lodged,
unevenly-maturing, or early-frost-risk fields favour swathing; the
swathed-crop benchmark for cutting is about 60% seed-colour change [6].

**Finding 7 — safe storage moisture is lower than the sellable-grade
moisture, and depends on both temperature and oil content.** A national
grading standard's threshold for "straight grade (dry)" canola is 10%
moisture, but the industry council's safe LONG-TERM storage target is 8%
moisture — and even 8% is too high if the grain temperature is 25°C or
above [7]. Based on Australian research, the safe storage moisture target
drops by 0.1 percentage points for every 1.0 percentage point increase in
the lot's oil content [7] — a real, quantified interaction between two of
the crop's own properties (oil content and storage safety).

**Finding 8 — soil pH tolerance is asymmetric and has a sourced linear
yield response on the acid side.** An industry council states that
strongly acidic (below pH 5.5) or alkaline (above pH 8.5) soils reduce
yield potential [8]; a dedicated Oklahoma winter-canola field study found
seed yield decreased LINEARLY below soil pH 5.8 [8] — a more precise,
lower threshold than the general-guidance figure, left visible as the more
specific sourced result rather than silently overwritten by the rounder
number.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual broadacre oilseed, single harvest;
  grown as both a winter annual (fall-sown, overwinters as a vegetative
  rosette) and a spring annual (spring-sown), spanning both season
  categories depending on type.
- **Cycle length:** Not independently sourced to a specific days-to-
  maturity figure for either type this pass; a general oilseed-brassica
  cycle length of roughly 85-100 days from spring sowing to maturity for
  the spring type is common general knowledge, not independently verified
  this pass. The winter type's cycle spans a full overwintering period and
  is not comparable on a simple day-count basis.
- **Climate:** Temperate / cool temperate; winter type additionally needs
  reliable winter cold for vernalization.
- **Composition:** Raw seed composition was not sourced directly; a feed-
  composition table gives raw rapeseed (whole seed, not oil) gross energy
  around 4,100 kcal/kg, with poultry metabolizable energy around 1,500
  kcal/kg [9]. A separate source's rapeseed-oil energy figure was flagged
  by its own search summary as "garbled" and is treated as unconfirmed
  [9]. No single reliable kcal figure for the pressed oil itself was
  established.
- **Pollination:** Self-compatible; gives good yields without insect
  pollination (Finding 5). Insect visitation (honeybees) is optional and
  yield-boosting, not required — a genuinely different pollination
  requirement from some other oilseed crops' hybrid-cross paths, despite
  both benefiting from bee visitation. Hive density in the cited trials
  ranged from about 1 hive/ha (Australian trial, >20% gain) to about 3
  hives/ha (Quebec trial, 46% gain), kept visible as a range rather than
  reconciled to one recommended density since the studies differ in
  geography, cultivar, and methodology [5]. Crosses with all other
  *B. napus* crops (rutabaga, forage rape, forage swede, Siberian kale),
  per the rutabaga-sourced research referenced above.
- **Yield:** No single reliable world-average figure; a review cited a
  global range of roughly 560-2,240 kg/ha, with isolated reports above
  3,000 kg/ha in Chile, Turkey, and parts of Europe in the past decade
  [1]. A Pakistani multi-variety trial found 1,430-2,079 kg/ha [1]. Seed
  oil content ranged 41.46-45.36% across the same trial's varieties [1]; a
  separate national grain-commission average gives 42.6% for the 2024 crop
  year, with 2010-2020 averages consistently at or above 44% [1]. Heat and
  drought during seed fill are the specific cited drag on oil content,
  since oil accumulates during the later stages of grain filling [1].
- **Water use:** A Spanish lysimeter study (2008 and 2012 seasons)
  measured a FAO-56-climate-adjusted mid-season crop coefficient (Kc) of
  1.15 and basal Kcb of 1.11, coinciding with near-complete ground cover
  (fc up to 0.95-0.97) [2]. Separately, an Iranian spring-canola study
  found mean Kc values of 0.72 initial, 1.45 mid-season, 0.66 end-season,
  explicitly noting a significant difference from FAO-56 recommended
  values [2] — the Spanish and Iranian mid-season figures differ by 0.30
  (1.15 vs. 1.45), a real, unresolved cross-study conflict, kept visible
  rather than averaged. A Brazilian 2020-2021 study instead derived Kcb
  per growth stage directly from measured leaf area index and crop height
  rather than using tabulated stage lengths at all [2]. Flowering through
  grain maturity is explicitly identified as the crop's most
  water-sensitive window; water stress in this window "negatively impacts
  yield" [2], not quantified further by that source. No stage-level
  deficit-response curve or seasonal-mm total was found sourced this
  pass.
- **Soil:** Optimal pH is not directly sourced; the lower bound is
  reported two ways — general guidance gives "below 5.5" as
  yield-reducing, while a dedicated Oklahoma field study found a linear
  yield decline specifically below pH 5.8, a more specific, lower-level
  primary-study threshold kept visible alongside the rounder general-
  guidance figure (Finding 8) [8]. Upper bound: above pH 8.5 reduces
  yield potential [8]. Salinity tolerance is reported as "similar to
  wheat... substantially less tolerant than barley" per one source, with
  a separate, much older reference source ranking rapeseed as less
  salt-tolerant than sugar beet and barley but more tolerant than most
  other field crops [8]; neither is a modern, authoritative
  salinity-tolerance table.
- **Storage:** No independently sourced day-count figure for safe
  storage; the sourced figure is a moisture target, not a day-count
  (Finding 7). Safe long-term storage moisture is 8%, lower still if the
  grain is warmer than 25°C or the lot's oil content is above average
  (storage moisture target drops 0.1 percentage points per 1.0-point rise
  in oil content). The 10% figure sometimes cited elsewhere is a national
  grading standard's sellable-grade (dry) moisture threshold, a looser
  short-term figure, not a safe-storage target [7].
- **Flowering / vernalization:** Winter type requires roughly 12-14 weeks
  of cold under controlled conditions; a semi-winter (Asiatic) type needs
  much less, roughly 2-8 weeks; spring type has no vernalization
  requirement at all [3]. Winter type additionally tolerates cold and
  freezing better than spring type during the vegetative overwintering
  phase, a separate cold-hardiness trait from the vernalization-trigger
  trait itself [3]. Spring type is typically grown in North America's
  Northern Plains; winter type is more common in the Southern Plains [3]
  — a real regional-adaptation fact, not independently generalized beyond
  this one sourced comparison.
- **Harvest:** Single stage, single window. Swathing (cutting into a
  windrow before full dry-down, at roughly 60% seed colour change) or
  straight-cutting (direct combining of the standing crop, once seed is
  dry enough to store, with optional desiccant-assisted dry-down at
  60-75% colour change) are the two harvest methods, alternatives to each
  other rather than both used (Finding 6).
- **Genome / origin:** An allotetraploid (AACC genome, 2n=4x=38) formed
  roughly 7,500 years ago by hybridization between *B. rapa* (AA, 2n=20)
  and *B. oleracea* (CC, 2n=18), followed by chromosome doubling; no wild
  *B. napus* population has ever been found, implying the species arose
  under cultivation/human selection rather than from a pre-existing wild
  ancestor [10]. A 2019 study separately suggests winter-type oilseed rape
  may represent the original form of *B. napus* [10]. No 1C picogram
  genome-size figure was found; a 2019 reference-genome assembly length
  (the Darmor-bzh cultivar, approximately 1.13 billion bases) was
  explicitly flagged by the source material itself as "a sequence length,
  not a published 1C value," and that caution is respected here rather
  than silently converting it [10].
- **Cultivars:** The two real, well-documented cultivar groups are
  double-low (canola-quality, oil under 2% erucic acid, meal under 30
  μmol/g glucosinolates) and high-erucic (HEAR, industrial), plus a
  winter/spring/semi-winter (Asiatic) type split driven by vernalization
  requirement — an agronomic-timing distinction rather than a product-
  chemistry one. **Double-low** is the modern commercial standard since
  circa 1980, propagated both as F1 hybrid (Ogura CMS × restorer) and
  open-pollinated, used for food-grade cooking/salad oil, livestock meal,
  and biodiesel [4][9]; founding varieties are Tower (*B. napus*, 1974)
  and Candle (*B. rapa*, 1977) [4]. **High-erucic (HEAR)** is grown under
  contract rather than sold on the open commercial-grain market, not a
  food oil, used for industrial lubricants, erucamide slip agents, and
  nylon-13 precursor feedstock [4]. No sourced list of specific named
  commercial hybrid lines (as opposed to these real category distinctions)
  was found at a level of detail worth repeating here.
- **Propagation / hybrid production:** Hybrid canola (Ogura CMS-based) is
  described across sourced patents as a major, established commercial
  system, alongside continuing open-pollinated double-low cultivation
  [3]; the hybrid share was not independently quantified as a percentage.
  A cytoplasmic-male-sterile line (carrying the Ogura mitochondrial
  sterility allele) is crossed with a nuclear fertility-restorer (Rf-
  allele) line; CMS lines are themselves derived from the original
  radish-rapeseed protoplast fusion lineage [3]. Because Ogura CMS is
  cytoplasmically (not nuclear-gene) inherited, saving seed from a
  commercial F1 hybrid's own harvest gives an unpredictable, largely
  male-sterile result unless the farmer also controls the restorer-line
  cross again — the same structural seed-saving trap documented for
  other CMS-hybrid oilseed crops researched for this project. No named
  maintainer-line (B-line) step analogous to a three-line (A/B/R) hybrid
  system was found described for canola specifically in the sourced
  patents, only the CMS/restorer two-line relationship.
- **Seed crop:** Isolation distance of 400 m (a national pedigreed-seed
  standard) up to roughly 800 m (about half a mile), sourced from this
  project's own prior rutabaga seed-crop research, documenting *B.
  napus*'s cross-pollination radius — reused here rather than re-derived,
  since rutabaga and canola are the same species. Shatter risk at harvest
  is high: straight-cut delay specifically raises shatter losses from
  5.5% to 17.4% in one cited study (Finding 6) [6]. Seed is dried to 8%
  moisture for safe long-term storage, lower if seed temperature is at or
  above 25°C or oil content is higher (Finding 7) [7].

## Hazards

No current food-safety hazard was found for the modern double-low
commercial standard modeled as this crop's primary cultivar group — the
double-low breeding program (Findings 1-2) exists precisely because the
pre-double-low crop's erucic acid and glucosinolate levels were real
human/animal health concerns, but the modern double-low standard has
already had those concerns bred out by regulatory-grade composition
limits. The high-erucic (HEAR) cultivar group (Finding 4) is explicitly
not a food oil for this reason. **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the underlying
erucic-acid/glucosinolate health-concern mechanism was described only at
summary level by the sourced breeding-history material, not independently
verified against a primary toxicology source; a fuller write-up is
deferred until this project has a place for this kind of health/safety
data and a primary source has been located.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain:** seed → swathed or straight-cut harvest → dried to safe
  storage moisture → pressed for crude oil (double-low: food-grade
  cooking/salad oil, biodiesel; HEAR: industrial lubricants, erucamide
  slip agents, nylon-13 precursor feedstock) and press-cake meal
  (double-low: livestock feed; HEAR meal not treated as a normal
  livestock feed ingredient).

## Open research questions

- No confirmed cycle-length (days-to-maturity) figure for either winter
  or spring type; the spring-type ~85-100-day figure is general
  knowledge, not independently sourced, and the winter type's cycle
  length including overwintering was not found at all.
- Crop-coefficient (Kc) values conflict sharply between sources (a
  Spanish lysimeter study's mid-season Kc of 1.15 versus an Iranian
  study's 1.45), with neither treated as authoritative over the other; no
  seasonal total water-use figure was computed from either.
- No reliable kcal/100g figure for the pressed oil itself was confirmed —
  the two figures found are for the wrong tissue (whole seed) or
  explicitly flagged as garbled by their own source.
- No modern, authoritative salinity-tolerance table was found; only a
  general comparative statement and a very old reference-source citation
  exist.
- Blackleg, clubroot, and Sclerotinia stem rot were named as disease
  search targets but returned with no usable detail this pass — a real,
  acknowledged gap, not a "checked and found none" result; needs a
  dedicated follow-up search.
- The erucic-acid/glucosinolate health-concern mechanism behind the
  double-low breeding program is described only at summary level, not
  from a primary toxicology source (Hazards section).
- No canola-specific hydroponic/microgreen viability was researched this
  pass.
- No named commercial hybrid cultivar list was found.
- No seed-crop production-scale figures beyond the reused rutabaga
  isolation distance: no minimum population figure, and no seed-yield-
  per-hectare figure for either the open-pollinated or F1 hybrid
  seed-production path.
- Whether canola hybrid-seed companies use a named maintainer-line step
  (as some other CMS-hybrid oilseed crops do) or a different method is
  unclear from the sources opened.
- No genome-size (1C picogram) figure was confirmed; only a reference-
  genome assembly length exists, and the source material itself warns
  that figure should not be used as a genome-size value.
- No soil chemistry (boron/chloride class, micronutrient demand,
  heavy-metal accumulation), soil biology (symbiont-dependence,
  replant-disease), light (DLI/light-saturation), or atmosphere
  (CO2-response) data were found. Canola is commonly cited in general
  agronomy as a disease-break/rotation crop for cereals, but this specific
  claim was not independently re-sourced this pass.

## Estimates and derivations

No DERIVED values are restated here as reusable formulas; the source
