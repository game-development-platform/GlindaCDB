# Safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*)

**Category:** Oil · **Path:** `crops/raw-data/oil/safflower.md`
**Researched:** 2026-10-30

This file is original research synthesis, compiled from the public sources
listed at the end of the document, with citations tracking each sourced
claim. The sources retain their own copyright/license; this synthesis is
licensed under this project's MIT license (see repository `LICENSE`).

## Naming and scope

This is modeled as *Carthamus tinctorius*, a single-species, no-name-
conflict oilseed crop. Canola, sunflower, and flax are useful comparison
crops for the recurring pattern of a single species carrying two or more
distinct cultivar groups around one commodity, which safflower also
shows in its own high-oleic/high-linoleic oil-chemistry split.

## Research findings

**Finding 1 — oil content and seed yield are both widely variable and
region/cultivar-dependent, with no single authoritative figure.**
Regional field trials give: one Turkish trial up to 2,482 kg/ha seed and
33% oil at one site [1]; another Turkish semi-arid trial 1,706-3,111
kg/ha seed, 26.1-35.1% oil, highest in one named cultivar [2]; a later
study citing national statistics put the Turkish national average at
about 1,474 kg/ha [3]; a Pakistan rainfed trial's check cultivar at only
313 kg/ha but 30.51% oil [4]; Indian extension notes giving one cultivar
at 28% oil, 1.9 t/ha, with oil content and yield both falling with
elevation [5]; an Albanian trial reporting seed protein about 16-17%,
with the authors noting that wider adoption depends on further yield/
oil improvement [6]. No single world-average yield figure was located in
these results this pass.

**Finding 2 — commercial hybrid seed exists, but by routes distinct from
other oil crops' own hybrid systems this project has researched.** A
roughly 20-year private-sector cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) breeding
program produced high-yielding hybrids but never commercialized any of
them [7]. A separate interspecific CMS system was developed by crossing
a wild relative species into cultivated safflower [8], and this lineage
underlies one named CMS-based hybrid, bred by a private Indian seed
company and released for commercial cultivation in 2006 [9] — the one
sourced example of a commercially released safflower hybrid. Because CMS
was historically unavailable to most breeders, two alternative routes are
also documented: (a) a patented genic male-sterility gene pleiotropically
linked to dwarfism, letting growers rogue fertile plants early by height
[10]; and (b) **chemical hybridization**: one study used gibberellic acid
(GA3) as a hybridizing agent, achieving an average 80.7% hybrid seed set
(87.5% on secondary heads), identified using a spiny-capitulum marker
variety [11]. Maintaining recessive genic male-sterile lines is reported
as difficult; seed must be collected only from sterile plants to keep the
sterility percentage stable across generations [12].

**Finding 3 — two oil chemotypes exist on the same species, the same
pattern as canola's double-low/HEAR split and sunflower's confectionery/
oilseed split.** High-oleic (monounsaturated) types are the more
oxidatively stable cooking/cosmetic oil; high-linoleic (polyunsaturated)
types are what is commercially grown in the US Upper Midwest, and are
also a genuine drying oil for paint and varnish manufacture [13][14].
High-oleic inheritance is reported as monogenic, governed by three
alleles at one locus, and the genes for spine presence and for nuclear
male sterility are NOT closely linked to the oleic-acid locus [15].

**Finding 4 — pollination is predominantly self-fertilizing, but bee
visitation measurably raises yield and can shift oil chemistry.** A
Turkish caged-treatment study found total seed and oil yield increased
with bee pollination, and that fatty-acid composition (oleic vs.
linoleic) differed by pollination treatment [16]. A regional Indian
extension source describes safflower as basically self-pollinated, but
states bees or other insects are generally necessary for optimum
fertilization and maximum yield [17]. Reported cross-pollination rates in
older literature range from 0% to 100%, an unusually wide spread [17]. A
consequence flows directly from this: a Western Australian grower guide
specifically warns that oleic and linoleic varieties should not be grown
close together, because cross-pollination can measurably alter the
resulting oil's fatty-acid profile [18] — bee visitation here is optional
but consequential for both yield and oil chemistry at once.

**Finding 5 — spine presence is a real, single-gene heritable trait with
a small, measured, counter-intuitive yield effect, independent of the
oleic-acid locus.** A 2024 study comparing spiny and non-spiny accessions
found spiny accessions had 1.2% more oil and 16.3% more linoleic acid
content than non-spiny accessions [15] — the thornier plants were
measurably the better oilseed in this trial, not merely a harvest-
handling nuisance. Thorns on spiny bract/leaf types do complicate hand
contact during field operations (petal picking, scouting, weeding),
though no sourced injury-rate data for this specific crop was found.

**Finding 6 — seed viability declines unusually fast at ambient
conditions for an oilseed, and this is tied directly to its high
polyunsaturated-fat content.** One review describes safflower as having a
short seed life, noting that oil-rich seeds high in unsaturated fatty
acids are inherently more sensitive to ambient storage conditions [19].
A Turkish storage trial under ambient conditions found germination
declined sharply and viability was completely lost after 14 months [20].
A separate storage-period trial reported, by contrast, 93.33%
germination still at 12 months of storage [21] — kept here as a visible,
unresolved conflict rather than averaged away; the two trials likely
differ in ambient humidity/temperature regime, which neither source
fully specifies. Cold, dry storage performs much better: a Thai study
found storage at -10°C could prolong seed longevity without needing
moisture-content reduction, and lower moisture combined with 5°C or
-10°C extended storage past 27 months [22].

**Finding 7 — the press-cake is a usable feed with no toxin-driven
detoxification step, a direct contrast with castor's or jatropha's meal.**
Undecorticated (whole-hull) safflower meal can be used directly in
ruminant rations as a protein source, substituting for soybean,
cottonseed, or linseed meal on an isonitrogenous basis, provided adequate
energy is supplied elsewhere in the ration [24]. It is, however, bitter
and less palatable than those alternatives, and its hull fibre (30-40% in
undecorticated meal, as low as 10% when decorticated) reduces
digestibility — incorporating safflower hulls in cattle rations is
reported to reduce feed efficiency through low hull digestibility,
though growth itself is not impaired if energy/protein needs are met
from other sources [25]. For poultry, undecorticated meal is rated poor,
being deficient in lysine, methionine, and isoleucine; decorticated meal
is a suitable, higher-protein ingredient but costlier to produce, and
dehulled/extruded meal has been shown to replace up to 60% of soybean
meal in broiler diets without performance loss [26]. In 2015 a US
regulatory approval covered seed meal from a bioengineered, gamma-
linolenic-acid-producing safflower line for cattle and poultry feed, with
regulatory limits of ≥20% crude protein, ≤40% crude fibre, ≤10%
moisture, and ≤2% crude fat [27] — a transgenic-trait detail noted here
but not modeled further, since this project does not track biotech
traits.

**Finding 8 — a genuinely dual-product plant: the same planting can be
harvested for seed oil, for dried petal dye, or both, with a small,
sourced-but-conflicting seed-yield tradeoff when both are taken.** Petals
are hand-picked at onset of flowering through full bloom, over repeated
near-daily passes as capitula open in succession [28]. This is a
genuinely distinct second harvested organ from the same plants, not a
maturity-stage choice on one organ. The yield tradeoff itself is
unresolved across sources: one 2003 Iranian field trial found petal
clipping reduced seed yield by about 7.4% across treatments [29], while a
2015 Iranian study found petal-harvest timing had NO significant effect
on seed yield (though delayed petal harvest did reduce petal yield) [30].
A 2024 Botswana study recommended picking either at onset of flowering
or full bloom to maximize both seed and petal yield together with high
pigment-precursor content [31]. A 2008 Turkish study reported that total
dyestuff content itself was NOT affected by environmental conditions,
cultivar, or picking time [32] — a claim that sits oddly next to the
Botswana study's timing-sensitive recommendation above; both are kept
visible rather than reconciled.

**Finding 9 — safflower's two cultural-product legacies, historical
textile dye and Japanese cosmetic rouge, are genuinely well-documented
and distinct from any other oil crop's uses this project has
researched.** Cloth dyed with safflower petals is reported from ancient
Egyptian tombs, including Tutankhamun's, and tied to 12th-dynasty
textiles [33]; both yellow and red dyes can be obtained from the same
petal batch [33]. In Japan, safflower (benibana) dye use is attested from
the Nara period (710-784 CE) onward, preserved in an 8th-century textile
collection [34]. The petal pigment was processed into a fermented, pulpy
mash and further refined into lip rouge, worn by geisha and courtesans,
peaking in popularity in the Edo period; a historical lip-painting style
layered safflower red over an iridescent green derived from bamboo-grass
stems [35]. Commercial production and lip-rouge manufacture continue
today in one Japanese prefecture, Japan's leading safflower-growing
region [35]. Safflower petals are also, less exotically, a long-
documented cheap saffron-colour substitute in cooking — providing colour
but not saffron's flavour — used historically by Spanish colonists in the
Americas and in various Middle Eastern cuisines, and noted even in an
1854 text as a known saffron adulterant detectable by its tubular floret
shape [36].

**Finding 10 — safflower is genuinely well-adapted to arid, saline, and
(by virtue of being largely self-pollinating) enclosed-space conditions,
a useful contrast to several other oil crops this project has
researched.** Root depth estimates vary (2-4 m by one source [37]; a
major agricultural reference gives roots extending to 3.5 m, though
normally 100% of water uptake in a full-grown crop occurs from the top
1-2 m [38]), and the crop has higher water-use efficiency than other oil
crops such as linseed and mustard by one source's claim [37]. Salinity
tolerance is moderate-to-high: one source gives a 6.4 dS/m seed-yield
salinity threshold [39]; a major agricultural reference's own table
gives 10% yield loss at 6.2, 25% at 7.6, 50% at 9.9 dS/m [38] — kept
visible as a numeric discrepancy rather than reconciled, since the two
likely used different trial designs. Germination is markedly less salt-
tolerant than the established plant, at roughly half the mature-plant
tolerance [38]. Soil pH tolerance is most consistently given as pH 5-8,
with best yields on neutral soils and liming advisable below that range
[38][40]. The main agronomic risk is the opposite of drought: excess
water and poor drainage bring root-rot susceptibility [38]. Because the
crop is predominantly self-pollinating (Finding 4), it is a plausible
candidate for enclosed/off-world growing without needing wind or managed
bee colonies for basic fertilization.

## Agronomic data

- **Life form / season:** Annual, direct-seeded crop. Cycle length
  110-200+ days (summer/spring-sown cultivars 110-120 days in one US
  region; winter cultivars up to about 200 days minimum growing season;
  one source gives 26-31 weeks for summer sowings) [2][41].
- **Climate:** Mediterranean, temperate, arid, semi-arid — derived from
  the sourced growing regions (Turkey, Pakistan, India, US Northern
  Plains, Australia) rather than a single authoritative climate-zone
  table.
- **Food status:** Not toxic. No food-safety issue identified for the
  seed, oil, or meal themselves.
- **Pollination:** Predominantly self-pollinating, but bee visitation
  measurably increases total seed and oil yield and can shift the fatty-
  acid (oleic/linoleic) balance of the harvest (Finding 4). Reported
  cross-pollination rates in older literature range unusually widely,
  0-100%. Growers are advised to separate oleic and linoleic cultivar
  blocks to prevent oil-chemistry cross-contamination, but no specific
  isolation distance was found.
- **Yield / composition:** Seed yield 313-3,111 kg/ha depending on
  cultivar, water, and region (Finding 1); oil content 26-40% of seed
  weight (commonly 26-35%); seed protein about 16-17% in one trial. No
  single world-average figure was located this pass.
- **Water use:** A major agricultural reference's crop-coefficient table
  (partially corrupted in the source extraction, so treated cautiously)
  gives: initial 0.3-0.4 (20-35 days), development 0.7-0.8, mid-season
  1.05-1.2 (45-65 days), late season 0.65-0.7, harvest 0.21-0.25 [44]. An
  Iranian greenhouse study under non-stressed conditions found
  coefficients of about 0.55, 0.9, 1.26, and 0.85 across its four stages
  — broadly consistent with the reference table — falling to 0.07-0.3
  under severe drought/salinity stress [45]. A Mexican forage-safflower
  study found a strong fit (r² 0.89-0.93) to a linear relation between
  the crop coefficient and a vegetation index [46]. Total seasonal water
  requirement is given at roughly 600-1,200 mm depending on climate and
  season length [38]. Flowering and yield formation are the most water-
  deficit-sensitive stages; the crop tolerates water deficit better
  outside those stages [38]. Excess water risks root rot rather than
  drought stress being the dominant failure mode.
- **Establishment:** Shallow seeding (2-4 cm) promotes uniform emergence
  and a better stand; emergence falls sharply with increasing depth
  (about 72% at 0 cm to 35% at 6 cm in one Brazilian study) [42]. Usually
  emerges 1-3 weeks after sowing, more slowly under low temperatures
  [41]. Seed germination above 80% is recommended for good stand
  establishment [41]. Late planting shortens plants, reduces root depth
  and branching, and lowers both seed and oil yield [41]. One seeding-
  rate study recommends 120 seeds/m² at 14 cm row spacing for high-
  yielding seed production regardless of seed vigor [43]. No seed rate in
  kg/ha was found this pass, only seeds/m² figures.
- **Soil:** pH tolerance 5-8, best yields on neutral soils, liming
  advisable below that range [38][40]. Moderate-to-high salinity
  tolerance (Finding 10). Waterlogging tolerance is low — excess water
  brings root-rot risk.
- **Genome:** Diploid, 2n = 2x = 24; a 2023 flow-cytometry study found 2C
  genome sizes of 2.4-2.7 pg across the cultivated species and two wild
  relatives [52]. A chromosome-scale reference genome gives a k-mer size
  estimate of about 1.17 Gb with 0.23% heterozygosity, flow-cytometry-
  close to soybean's (~1.12 Gb) but with lower heterozygosity; the
  assembly itself totals about 1.06 Gb across 12 superscaffolds (90.6% of
  the estimated genome size), with repeats making up 60.13% [53].
- **Cultivars:** The real, well-documented cultivar-level distinction is
  the high-oleic/high-linoleic oil-chemistry split (Finding 3), the
  direct structural analogue of sunflower's confectionery/oilseed split,
  canola's double-low/HEAR split, and flax's fibre/oilseed split, plus an
  independent spiny/non-spiny trait (Finding 5) and several specific named
  lines with sourced trial data. One linoleic-type Turkish field cultivar
  had the highest oil content (up to 35.1%) among cultivars in a semi-
  arid trial [2]. One Pakistani check cultivar was a low-yielding
  rainfed baseline (313 kg/ha) but with 30.51% oil [4]. The one named
  CMS-based F1 hybrid released commercially (2006, India) was not
  quantified in the sources opened beyond its commercial-release status
  [9]. A spiny-capitulum marker line is used to visually identify hybrid
  seed set in chemical-hybridization trials, with no oil-chemistry delta
  of its own [11]. A high-oleic chemotype is a more oxidatively stable
  cooking/cosmetic oil [13]. A high-linoleic chemotype is the dominant
  commercially grown US type, with genuine drying-oil chemistry
  comparable to linseed oil; it must not be grown close to high-oleic
  types since bee-mediated cross-pollination can shift the fatty-acid
  profile [13][14][18]. One linoleic-type cultivar had the lowest
  measured allelopathic/autotoxic effect among cultivars tested in a
  stem-extract bioassay [49]. A research winter-type line is
  vernalization-responsive, with flowering accelerated by about 2 weeks
  of cold exposure during germination, unlike most commercial spring-
  sown varieties, and shows a longer rosette period, more prostrate
  habit, and superior cold acclimation versus spring types [47].
- **Flowering:** Rosette stage (frost-hardy) precedes stem elongation and
  branching, then flowering; the period from end of flowering to
  maturity is consistently reported at about 4 weeks [41]. Capitula
  (flower heads) open in succession over the bloom period, which is what
  allows repeated petal-picking passes (Finding 8).
- **Disease/pest profile:** Fungal: Alternaria leaf spot/blight
  (described as the most destructive disease in Indian extension
  sources, favored by moist warm conditions with intermittent rain, and
  seed-borne); Fusarium wilt, with one-sided yellowing spreading upward
  and dark stem-base lesions that can girdle the stem; rust, producing
  small brown-black pustules on leaves/hypocotyls; root rot (commonly
  charcoal rot, plus Phytophthora and Pythium root rots); also Cercospora
  and Ramularia leaf spots, powdery mildew, Sclerotinia stem rot/head
  blight, Verticillium wilt, Botrytis hard rot, and Septoria leaf spot
  [55]. Viral/other: cucumber mosaic virus (aphid-transmitted) and
  phytoplasma-caused phyllody; bacterial leaf spot [55]. Pests: capsule/
  bud fly, pod/capsule borer, safflower aphid, safflower caterpillar, and
  a regional weevil [55]; broomrape (a parasitic weed) and root-knot
  nematodes [55]. Pathogen names/synonyms vary across sources and were
  not cross-checked against a current taxonomic authority.
- **Soil biology:** Broomrape, a parasitic weed, has its seed germination
  stimulated by sesquiterpene lactones in safflower root exudates [50] —
  the same compound class is also reported phytotoxic to several grass/
  weed species in lab bioassays [50][51]. Allelopathic/autotoxic effects
  were found to come from stem extracts/residues, not root-solution
  extracts, in one cultivar study, with target-species susceptibility
  scaling with the target's seed size (smaller-seeded weeds more
  affected) [49][51].
- **Postharvest storage:** Seed has a short storage life relative to
  other oilseeds, attributed to its polyunsaturated fat content making
  it more sensitive to ambient conditions (Finding 6). Fresh petals have
  only a 1-day shelf life and are dried promptly.
- **Labor:** Hand-picking petals (dye/cosmetic path) over repeated near-
  daily passes during bloom as capitula open in succession, with thorny
  spiny cultivars complicating hand contact (Findings 5, 8). Seed harvest
  is combine-mechanized, about 4 weeks after end of flowering. A
  Brazilian study of petal-harvesting methods found that machine methods
  had greater field capacity than manual methods for petal harvest
  specifically [56] — a genuine, if narrow, mechanization option for the
  dye path, not sourced in enough detail to describe further here.

## Hazards

- Thorns on spiny bract/leaf cultivars complicate hand contact during
  petal picking and other field operations; no sourced crop-specific
  injury-rate data.
- Broomrape (parasitic weed) is stimulated by safflower's own root
  exudates (Finding 10, soil biology above) — an agronomic weed-
  management issue rather than a plant-toxin hazard.
- No food-safety issue identified for the seed, oil, or meal themselves.
  A medicinal/herbal-dosage caution does exist for the dried-flower
  product in traditional-medicine use, including pregnancy and bleeding-
  disorder contraindications reported in that literature.
- **TO BE ADDRESSED** — the dried-flower traditional-medicine dosage
  caution is deferred for a fuller write-up until this project has a
  place for this kind of health/safety data.
- No crop-specific livestock toxicity: the press-cake/meal issue is
  palatability and digestibility, not toxicity (Finding 7), a direct and
  deliberate contrast with castor's or jatropha's toxic meal.

## Uses and processing

- **Chain (seed):** seed harvested (combine, single stage) → pressed →
  oil (high-oleic cooking/cosmetic oil, or high-linoleic cooking oil and
  industrial drying oil) + meal (undecorticated: usable directly as
  ruminant feed, poor for poultry; decorticated: costlier but higher-
  value poultry feed).
- **Chain (petals, optional secondary harvest):** petals hand-picked
  across the bloom period → dried promptly (1-day fresh shelf life) →
  historically processed into dye (textile) or fermented/refined into
  cosmetic lip rouge; also used directly, dried, as a cheap saffron-
  colour substitute in cooking.

## Open research questions

- No single world-average seed-yield figure was located this pass.
- Whether any newer CMS hybrid has reached market since the one named
  2006 release was not checked.
- No sourced crop-specific hand-injury-rate data for spiny-cultivar field
  work.
- No sourced seed rate in kg/ha, only seeds/m² figures.
- No sourced isolation distance between oleic and linoleic cultivar
  seed-production blocks, despite grower guidance that one is needed.
- No sourced light (day-length/shade-tolerance), atmosphere (CO2-
  response/ethylene-sensitivity), or enclosed-space footprint/edible-
  yield-per-m² figures.
- A wild relative species used in the CMS breeding lineage (Finding 2) is
  a documented noxious weed in parts of its native range, with young
  plants reportedly used as fodder and its seed eaten by birds, though
  cattle reportedly avoid it and its thorns are reported to hinder wheat
  harvest — a possible wild-forage/weed-hazard angle not pursued further
  here.
- Conflicting ambient seed-storage-life trial results (14-month total
  loss vs. 93.33% germination at 12 months) are not reconciled.
- Disease/pest names were sourced mostly from general and regional
  extension sources, not cross-checked against a current taxonomic/
  agricultural authority.
- A mechanized petal-harvesting method was mentioned in one Brazilian
  study but not sourced in enough detail to describe as a distinct
  process here.
- No seed-crop-specific seed yield or multiplication-ratio figure
  distinct from the general seed-yield range in Finding 1.
- The dual-product (seed + petal-dye) cultivation pattern in Finding 8
  does not cleanly fit a simple single-organ-maturity-stage framework —
  it is a genuinely distinct second harvested organ from the same
  planting, closer to a cultivation-path choice than a harvest-stage
  choice.

## Estimates and derivations

The crop-coefficient-by-stage figures above come from a major
agricultural reference's own crop-information page, retrieved only as a
partially corrupted text extract in this pass's search results, not
verified against the original formatted table — the magnitudes are
corroborated by an independent Iranian greenhouse study, so any error is
more likely in stage-boundary day counts than in the coefficient
magnitudes themselves. No other DERIVED values are restated here as
reusable formulas.
